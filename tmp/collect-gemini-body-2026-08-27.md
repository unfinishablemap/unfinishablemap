# Referee Report: "The Affective-Forecasting Gap and the Selection Currency"

## 1. Introduction and General Assessment of the Manuscript

The manuscript under review, "The Affective-Forecasting Gap and the Selection Currency," attempts to resolve a central dilemma in the philosophy of mind, action theory, and moral psychology: the tension between anticipated valence (which causally precedes and guides choice) and experienced valence (the phenomenal reality of the outcome). The authors position their argument within the context of a "value-sensitive" theory of consciousness. They assert that while anticipated valence operates as the functional currency of selection, experienced valence metaphysically constitutes actual value. To bridge the obvious divergence between what agents predict will feel good and what actually feels good, the authors rely heavily on the affective-forecasting literature (impact bias, immune neglect, focalism), behavioral economics (the peak-end rule, duration neglect), and specific interpretations of anaesthetic latency and dopaminergic reward-prediction errors.

While the scope of the synthesis is ambitious and addresses a genuinely live problem in decision theory and the metaphysics of mind, the manuscript in its current form is fundamentally unpublishable in a top-tier journal such as *Mind*, *Synthese*, or *Philosophical Studies*. The fatal flaw pervading the manuscript is an extreme empirical fragility resulting from a selective, outdated, and heavily gerrymandered reading of the psychological and neuroscientific literature. The authors construct their core philosophical dilemma upon empirical absolute claims—such as the total distortion of remembered utility by the peak-end rule, the absolute deletion of phenomenal states by memory latency, and the clean neurobiological bifurcation of predictive mechanism ("wanting") and hedonic feeling ("liking")—that have been systematically dismantled, heavily nuanced, or entirely falsified by peer-reviewed literature published between 2020 and 2025.

Furthermore, the manuscript exhibits a persistent structural weakness in its conceptual architecture: it routinely shields its metaphysical commitments from empirical disconfirmation by bracketing away contradictory data or setting up false dichotomies. By isolating the "forecast" from the "experience," the authors ignore contemporary formal epistemology and rational choice theory, which treat the evolution of values not as mere "calibration failures" but as rational adaptations across changing selves. The manuscript relies on the epistemology of the 1990s and 2000s to solve a metaphysical problem of the 2020s.

The following sections provide an exhaustive, systematic audit of the article's empirical claims, logical inferences, methodological assumptions, and counterargument coverage, demonstrating precisely why the manuscript's core arguments collapse under contemporary scrutiny.

## 2. The Mischaracterization of the Forecasting Gap and Anticipatory Utility

The manuscript’s first major empirical pillar (Section 1: "The Gap Is Structured, Not Noise") is the assertion that the affective forecasting gap is a predictable, structured bias rather than random noise. To support this, the authors rely almost exclusively on foundational texts from the late 1990s and 2000s, specifically citing Gilbert et al. (1998) on immune neglect, Wilson et al. (2000) on focalism, and Gilbert & Wilson (2007) on prospection. The authors concede that the *intensity* component of the impact bias has been contested in the literature (noting Levine et al., 2012), but they double down on the claim that the *durability* bias remains an "uncontested challenge" for the selection law.

### 2.1 The Durability Bias is Not Uncontested or Monolithic

This framing is empirically false and conceptually misleading. The durability bias is by no means "uncontested" in the modern literature as a universal law of human cognition. Recent large-scale investigations and meta-analytic summaries indicate that affective forecasting biases, including the durability bias, are heavily moderated by individual traits, contextual boundary conditions, and the specific valence of the target event, rendering the "structured gap" far more fragile than the authors claim.

For instance, recent research on affective forecasting regarding social evaluations demonstrates that forecasting errors are highly sensitive to demographic and personality variables. A 2025 meta-analytic summary of reactions to gossip revealed that individual traits such as narcissism and chronic ostracism systematically alter and even invert predicted valences depending on the stimulus. Highly narcissistic individuals or those with high chronic ostracism exhibit complex affective forecasting inversions regarding negative versus positive gossip that cannot be captured by a monolithic "impact bias" model. By treating the durability bias as a universal, structure-imposing law of cognition that rigidly dictates the divergence between anticipation and experience, the authors overstate the degree to which selection is reliably and systematically decoupled from actual experienced utility.   

Furthermore, the authors fail to account for the dual-process nature of affective forecasting. Contemporary analyses of food choices and health behaviors demonstrate that *implicit* associations (automatically activated attitudes) frequently align with health-favoring choices and actualized positive dietary patterns, operating independently of the explicitly reported (and often biased) affective forecasts. By restricting their definition of "anticipated valence" to explicit, declarative prospection, the authors ignore the vast sub-personal infrastructure of implicit anticipatory valence that reliably tracks long-term experienced utility.   

### 2.2 Anticipation as a Performative, Valenced Experience

A deeper philosophical and empirical category error occurs in the authors' rigid chronologization of utility. The manuscript treats the forecasting mechanism entirely as a sterile, predictive computation—a "poorly calibrated currency" that attempts, and fails, to model future experienced valence. This fundamentally misunderstands the nature of anticipation in human cognition.

As recent comprehensive analyses in consumer psychology, marketing, and behavioral science demonstrate, anticipation is not merely a cognitive forecast or a placeholder for a future state; it is a *performative experience* in its own right, carrying profound, immediate affective valence. Anticipation unfolds in the liminal phase between the decision to act and the moment of actualization, generating real-time hedonic utility—such as hope, excitement, curiosity, anxiety, or fear of disappointment—that is phenomenologically robust and affectively ambivalent.   

If anticipation itself generates robust experienced utility, then the authors' clean bifurcation—where "anticipated valence drives selection" but "experienced valence constitutes value"—collapses conceptually. The selection mechanism is not running on a value-blind, mechanistic forecast of a future state; it is running, at least in part, on the *present* experienced valence of the anticipatory act itself. The authors' failure to recognize anticipatory utility as a subset of real-time experienced utility leads them to artificially inflate the divergence between the mechanism of choice and the phenomenality of value. They have gerrymandered the definition of "experience" to exclude the experience of anticipating.

## 3. The Peak-End Rule, Duration Neglect, and the Remembered Utility Pipeline

In Section 2 of the manuscript, "Three Utilities, Three Jobs," the authors lean heavily on the framework established by Kahneman, Wakker, and Sarin (1997) and Redelmeier and Kahneman (1996). They present a sequential, transformational pipeline of utility:
Experience→Memory (Peak-End-Distorted)→Forecast (Impact-Biased)→Selection

The argument rests on the assertion that remembered utility departs from the actual integral of experienced utility so reliably, and is so completely distorted by duration neglect and the peak-end rule, that "the valence guiding selection is not the valence that is actually felt." The authors claim that retrospective judgments completely neglect the total duration of the episode, tracking only the peak and the end intensity, thereby inducing a preference for dominated options.

### 3.1 Meta-Analytic Falsification of the "Completely Distorted" Pipeline

This section represents perhaps the most severe empirical mishandling in the manuscript. The authors present the peak-end rule and duration neglect as absolute, axiomatic phenomena that effectively erase the continuous integral of moment-by-moment experience from the memory record. The empirical literature of the 2020s fundamentally contradicts this absolutist reading, replacing it with a highly nuanced, multi-variable model of retrospective evaluation.

A definitive 2022 meta-analysis by Alaybek et al. encompassing 174 effect sizes investigated the peak-end rule and duration neglect across multiple domains, including organizational behavior, clinical pain, and daily affective states. While the meta-analysis confirmed that the peak-end effect is statistically large and robust across boundary conditions (r = 0.581, 95% CI = 0.487–0.661), it revealed a critical, paradigm-shifting finding that the manuscript entirely ignores: the effect of the peak-end rule is *comparable to the effect of the overall average (mean) score* of the experience.   

The implications of this finding destroy the manuscript's proposed pipeline. To illustrate the severity of the manuscript's omission, we can map its assertions directly against the contemporary meta-analytic consensus:

| Utility Metric / Phenomenon | Manuscript's Claim (Based on 1990s literature) | 2020s Meta-Analytic Consensus (e.g., Alaybek et al., 2022) | Philosophical Consequence for the Manuscript |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Peak-End Influence** | Completely dominates and distorts remembered utility. | Large effect size (r ≈ 0.581), but does not operate in isolation. | Peak-end is a strong heuristic, but not an exclusive filter. |
| **Overall Average (Integral)** | Replaced/erased by the Peak-End distortion. | **Comparable in predictive power to the Peak-End effect.** | **Falsifies the claim that the continuous integral of experience is lost.** |
| **Duration Neglect** | Absolute; duration has "almost no effect." | Small but statistically detectable effects depending on context. | Falsifies the absolutist claim of complete duration deletion. |
| **Affective Slope/Trend** | Ignored by memory in favor of static peak/end snapshots. | Trend and variability across episodes possess independent predictive power. | Memory tracks dynamic changes over time, not just isolated extrema. |

Because the overall average of moment-by-moment feeling is highly predictive of retrospective evaluation and comparable in strength to the peak-end rule, it is demonstrably false to claim that the memory record undergoes "two non-faithful transformations" that render it entirely disconnected from what was actually felt. The continuous integral of experienced utility *does* survive the transition into remembered utility.   

By suppressing the fact that the arithmetic mean of an experience powerfully informs retrospective judgments, the authors construct a strawman of human memory designed to falsely isolate decision utility from continuous experienced utility. If remembered utility reliably tracks the overall average of the experience alongside the peak and the end, then the "valence that guides selection" is, in fact, deeply and mathematically rooted in "the valence that is actually felt." The philosophical dilemma the authors attempt to construct is resolved not by complex metaphysical disambiguation, but by utilizing accurate, contemporary psychological data.

## 4. Epistemological Overreach in Anaesthesia and Reporting Latency

Section 3 of the manuscript introduces the "retrospective twin" of the prospective forecasting gap: the idea that the latency of the reporting channel determines whether an episode reaches the memory record at all. The authors argue that if the reporting latency is too long, the episode can be "completely deleted" from memory, resulting in a "zero." They claim a zero is indistinguishable from a genuinely empty interval, rendering the forecaster entirely blind.

To evidence this, they rely on comparisons of awareness under general anaesthesia across three reporting latencies: the Isolated Forearm Technique (IFT) in the ConsCIOUS-1 study (claiming a 4.6% responsiveness rate); the 24-hour modified Brice questionnaire (1 in 800); and spontaneous reporting in the NAP5 audit (1 in 19,600). They conclude that "latency alone reduced a rate of 1 in 22 to zero."   

### 4.1 Outdated Base Rates and the Underestimation of Connected Consciousness

First, the authors' empirical base rates for the Isolated Forearm Technique are significantly outdated, relying on the 2017 Sanders et al. study to establish the baseline of real-time experience. Recent large-scale international multicentre cohort studies have drastically revised these figures upward. For example, a major 2022/2023 prospective international multicentre cohort study by Linassi et al. published in the *British Journal of Anaesthesia* assessed connected consciousness shortly after tracheal intubation in young adults (aged 18-40). Out of 338 subjects who completed the study, responses after intubation occurred in 11% of subjects (37/338), with females responding more often than males (13% vs 6%).   

By using an artificially low baseline (4.6%) rather than the contemporary, high-powered consensus (11%), the authors fail to engage with the actual scale of the phenomenon of connected consciousness. But the empirical error is secondary to the profound philosophical error they commit in their interpretation of these data.

### 4.2 The Conflation of Agency, Subjectivity, and Declarative Memory

The authors commit a severe philosophical category error by conflating *responsiveness* (agency/access consciousness) with *phenomenal consciousness* (subjectivity), and further conflating both with *explicit declarative memory*.

The manuscript assumes that because a patient fails to explicitly recall an event 24 hours later (the Brice questionnaire), the "latency alone reduced a rate of 1 in 22 to zero," implying that the memory of the phenomenal state was entirely deleted and therefore unavailable to guide selection. However, contemporary anaesthesia and neuroscientific research explicitly maps the distinction between observable responsiveness and unobservable subjective experience, rejecting the binary ledger the authors propose.

Recent literature has identified "black swan" cases where patients exhibit connected consciousness (such as conversing and making volitional motor responses) despite the presence of alpha and delta oscillations on frontal EEG—signatures traditionally associated with deep unconsciousness. Variations in conscious states under anaesthesia highlight the deep distinction between observable agency and subjective experience.   

More importantly, the fact that an explicit declarative memory is not accessible via a verbal questionnaire does not mean the *affective trace* of the experience was "deleted from the record." Implicit memory formation during anaesthesia remains a heavily documented and highly complex domain. A valenced state experienced during a period of connected consciousness can alter subsequent behavioral selection, emotional reactivity, and implicit associations even if explicit declarative recall is functionally zero.   

The authors treat memory as a simple, consciously accessible spreadsheet—either the event is declaratively accessible, or it equates to a null state. This highly restrictive, Cartesian epistemology of memory completely ignores the role of implicit affective conditioning in human decision-making. By demanding declarative recall as the sole proof that an episode survived latency, the authors arbitrarily shield their "value-blind steelman" from the realities of implicit affective persistence. Selection can, and often does, run on affective records that are functionally invisible to declarative probes but nevertheless veridically represent the valence of the past experience.

## 5. The Collapse of the RPE-Hedonic Dichotomy

In Section 4, "A Mechanistic Correlate from Neuroscience," the manuscript attempts to ground its philosophical disambiguation in neurobiology. The authors invoke the dissociation between "wanting" and "liking" (Berridge & Robinson, 2016) and the dopamine Reward-Prediction Error (Schultz, 2016). They argue that midbrain dopamine neurons code a "predictive, economic-value-like component, not the hedonic 'pleasure' aspect."

The authors leverage this to set up the terrifying prospect of the "value-blind steelman": if selection is driven by RPE (pure predictive mechanism), and feeling is a separate, downstream downstream fact doing no actual selective work, then selection is value-blind.

### 5.1 RPE Directly Modulates Momentary Subjective Well-Being

This neurobiological framing reflects a profound, almost willful misreading of the 2020s literature on reward processing, computational psychiatry, and subjective well-being. The authors construct an impermeable firewall between the computation of prediction error and the phenomenological experience of valence. The contemporary consensus demonstrates that this firewall does not exist.

Over the past decade, and particularly culminating in literature between 2020 and 2025, computational models of momentary subjective well-being (most notably the Rutledge paradigm and its derivations) have conclusively demonstrated that the reward-prediction error *is* the primary computational driver of momentary subjective happiness. In these highly replicated paradigms, individuals' self-reported feelings of happiness dynamically and immediately track the RPE, not merely the absolute value of the reward obtained. When a reward exceeds expectations (a positive RPE), it elicits an immediate, phenomenologically robust spike in experienced positive affect.   

Recent studies extending these computational models to applied behavioral settings (e.g., Eckert et al., 2023) confirm that eliciting the reward prediction error fundamentally alters both behavioral engagement and the *subjective experience* of the individual. Dopaminergic RPE signaling is not merely a "predictive computation" divorced from feeling; it directly modulates the hedonic tone of the present moment. The mechanism of learning is inherently affective.   

Furthermore, the rigid dissociation between dopamine (wanting) and opioids (liking) upon which the manuscript relies has been significantly complicated by recent neurochemical mapping. Recent findings present a nuanced neuroarchitecture where certain neural circuits and regions specialize in processing both predictive value and hedonic response simultaneously. Structures such as the ventral pallidum orchestrate integrated responses linking incentive salience directly to hedonic "disgust" or "pleasure". The brain does not cleanly segregate the prediction of value from the feeling of value in the way the authors' philosophical argument requires.   

By insisting that RPE is purely a "mechanistic correlate" analogous to economic value, the authors force an artificial, outdated philosophical dualism onto the biology. If the computation of RPE directly generates momentary phenomenal happiness, then the mechanism of selection is inherently value-loaded. The "value-blind steelman" is defeated not by an ad-hoc metaphysical distinction (the authors' "defensible position"), but by the empirical reality that prediction errors *feel* like something. The currency of selection is already denominated in feeling.

## 6. Conceptual Gerrymandering and the Avoidance of Transformative Choice

Having constructed a dilemma out of outdated empirical assertions and gerrymandered neurobiology, the authors present their "defensible position" in Section 5. They argue that "experienced valence constitutes metaphysical value... while anticipated valence is the poorly calibrated currency that selection causally consults." They conclude that the divergence between the two is merely a "calibration failure."

### 6.1 The Neglect of Rational Choice and "Changing Selves"

This conclusion betrays a stunning ignorance of contemporary formal epistemology, rational choice theory, and the philosophy of transformative experience. By categorizing the affective forecasting gap exclusively as a "calibration failure," the authors assume a static normative agent. They assume there exists a single, objective "experienced value" in the future that the present self is simply failing to predict accurately due to cognitive biases.

This ignores the vast philosophical literature on *transformative experience* (originating with L.A. Paul) and the specific problem of *changing selves*. As meticulously articulated by Richard Pettigrew in *Choosing for Changing Selves* (2020), agents must make decisions recognizing that their core values, preferences, and evaluative frameworks will fundamentally change over time, often as a direct result of the decisions being made.   

When a forecaster predicts the valence of a future event, they are doing so from the evaluative standpoint, neurobiological baseline, and social context of their *current* self. When the event actually occurs, the experienced valence is judged from the evaluative standpoint of the *future* self. The divergence between anticipated and experienced valence—the very gap the authors agonize over—is frequently not a cognitive "error," a "bias," or a "poorly calibrated currency," but a highly rational manifestation of the fact that the subject's evaluative landscape has evolved.   

By entirely omitting the epistemology of changing selves, the authors artificially reduce a profound problem of diachronic identity and utility aggregation into a mechanistic psychological glitch. Pettigrew's application of judgment aggregation to individual decision-making proves that assigning varying weights to the utility of past, present, and future selves is a complex normative requirement, not a biological error.   

### 6.2 The Incoherence of the Proposed Empirical Test

The authors attempt to prove their theory's testability by proposing a laboratory intervention: "If selection is value-sensitive on experienced valence, then debiasing or defocusing interventions that correct forecasts should alter which outcomes are selected."

However, if the gap is caused by the evolution of the self rather than an epistemic bias, debiasing the forecast to align perfectly with the future self's experienced valence would constitute an irrational violation of the present self's autonomy and values. The present self may *rationally choose* to optimize for its current preferences, knowing full well that its future self will evaluate the outcome differently. The authors' proposed empirical test is philosophically incoherent because it relies on a synchronic, static model of utility to test a diachronic, dynamic phenomenon. They treat the future self as an objective truth standard, rather than as a distinct evaluative agent in an aggregate decision problem.

### 6.3 Epistemic Shielding and Epiphenomenalism

Ultimately, the manuscript's "defensible position" relies heavily on epistemic shielding. The authors concede that selection is driven by a mechanism (anticipated valence) that operates independently of actualized metaphysical value (experienced valence). They claim this preserves the causal standing of experienced valence because it acts as the "calibrator" over time.

However, as demonstrated in their own Sections 2 and 3, they simultaneously argue that the calibration pipeline is hopelessly corrupted by duration neglect, the peak-end rule, and reporting latency. If the feedback loop of calibration is as entirely broken as the authors claim—if the record is "completely deleted" by latency and "non-faithfully transformed" by peak-end effects—then past experienced valence exerts virtually zero accurate causal influence on future selection. A value that cannot be accurately recorded in memory cannot calibrate a future forecast.

The authors have accidentally argued themselves into pure epiphenomenalism. They attempt to escape this trap by merely *asserting* that experienced valence constitutes "metaphysical value" regardless of its causal impotence. This is exactly the kind of untestable, parsimony-violating conceptual gerrymandering that top-tier philosophical journals rightly reject. One cannot claim that a state serves as a causal calibrator while simultaneously marshaling evidence that the calibration channel is fundamentally severed.

## 7. Required Revisions (List of Weaknesses)

The manuscript exhibits severe, likely fatal, methodological and conceptual flaws. It builds sweeping metaphysical claims upon an outdated empirical consensus, misrepresents neurobiological mechanisms to force a false dichotomy, and ignores highly relevant contemporary philosophical literature on rational choice. Revisions to address the following weaknesses would require a ground-up rewrite of the article's core premises.

1. **Failure to acknowledge that continuous experienced utility (the mean) survives in retrospective evaluation:** The article incorrectly claims the peak-end rule distorts memory so entirely that actual experienced valence is lost to selection; however, a comprehensive 2022 meta-analysis of 174 effect sizes demonstrates that the overall average of moment-by-moment experience possesses predictive power comparable to the peak-end heuristic, proving the integral of experience survives (Alaybek et al., 2022).
2. **Misrepresentation of Reward-Prediction Errors (RPE) as a purely value-blind predictive mechanism:** The article argues RPE codes purely "economic" predictive value rather than hedonic pleasure, but modern computational frameworks of subjective well-being prove that momentary phenomenological happiness is directly driven by, and tightly coupled to, the reward-prediction error itself, meaning the mechanism is inherently valenced (Eckert et al., 2023; Rutledge models).
3. **Outdated clinical base rates and flawed epistemology regarding anaesthetic "latency":** The article uses outdated rates for connected consciousness (4.6%) and falsely infers that a lack of declarative recall post-latency equates to a "zero" in the affective record, entirely ignoring recent high-powered incidence findings of 11% in young adults and the presence of complex, decoupled EEG states that complicate the agency-subjectivity divide (Linassi et al., 2022; Kaplon et al., 2023).
4. **Omission of anticipatory utility as a distinct subset of experienced utility:** The article rigidly separates anticipated valence (defined merely as a mechanism) from experienced valence (defined as value), completely ignoring recent behavioral research demonstrating that the act of anticipating a future event constitutes a robust, performative "experienced utility" in the present moment (qualitative analyses and theory development in consumer behavior).
5. **Complete neglect of the philosophy of transformative choices and changing selves:** The article reduces the affective forecasting gap to a mere cognitive "calibration failure," ignoring foundational contemporary work in formal epistemology demonstrating that utility divergence over time is a rational problem of aggregating the differing normative values of present and future selves, rendering the proposed "debiasing" test philosophically incoherent (Pettigrew, 2020).

**Verdict:** The manuscript must be rejected, as its proposed metaphysical dilemma and subsequent resolution rely entirely on a selectively gerrymandered, demonstrably outdated empirical foundation that fails to engage with the contemporary psychological, neuroscientific, and epistemological consensus of the 2020s.
