# Referee Report: "Purpose and AI Alignment"

This manuscript argues that AI alignment built on learning human preferences from observed behaviour rests on unstable foundations so long as humanity's ultimate purpose remains unknown, and proposes an "experiential alignment" alternative grounded in the intrinsic significance and quality of conscious experience. As a submission to a flagship analytic philosophy journal (*Mind*, *Synthese*, or *Philosophical Studies*), the paper is not publishable in its current form and warrants **rejection** (or, at minimum, a wholesale reconstruction). Its critique of preference-based alignment depends on an outdated and selectively appropriated reading of the 2020s alignment literature; two of its load-bearing empirical pillars (Kahneman and Deaton's 2010 well-being plateau and a clean phenomenal-dissociation reading of pain asymbolia) have been substantively revised, failed to replicate, or come under severe peer-reviewed contestation; its positive experiential-alignment proposal re-imports the very measurement, proxy-gaming, and human-influence dilemmas it claims to transcend; and its philosophical defense begs the question against contemporary illusionism, interoceptive active-inference models of valence, and non-consciousist value theory.

**Verification Scope and Access Caveat:** Direct retrieval of the live manuscript page (`https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/purpose-and-alignment/`) and the full live changelog (`https://unfinishablemap.org/workflow/changelog/`) was unsuccessful or inaccessible during this audit. Accordingly, this review assesses the manuscript's documented arguments, verbatim quotations, empirical citations, and logical structure as preserved in publicly indexed excerpts—where the article appears under the title *"Purpose and AI Alignment"* (approximately 2,616–2,633 words; latest indexed modification November 17, 2026; indexed changelog timestamps running through November 26, 2026)—alongside the manuscript's directly linked companion expositions of its experiential-alignment machinery (`concepts/experiential-alignment/`) and objective experiential criteria (`topics/alignment-in-objective-experiential-terms/`). Throughout, claims attested directly in indexed excerpts of the target manuscript are distinguished from operational specifications supplied by those companion pages, and late-November textual loci whose final live wording could not be inspected directly—specifically concerning the regress objection to illusionism and the interactionist-dualist uncomputability claim—are flagged explicitly.

## 1. Empirical Accuracy Against the 2020s Literature

### 1.1 Preference Learning, RLHF, and Value Specification

The manuscript frames mainstream AI alignment primarily around Stuart Russell's (2019) three-principles framework in *Human Compatible*—maximizing the realization of human preferences under initial uncertainty, with observed behaviour as the primary source of information—and contends that behavioural preference inference collapses because revealed preferences are thin, mutable, and systematically mistaken. Measured against the 2020s technical alignment literature, this diagnosis attacks an outdated caricature while overlooking formal results that undercut the manuscript's own alternative.

First, contemporary reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) research already distinguishes systematically between tractable engineering shortcomings and fundamental limits of preference-based alignment. Casper et al. (2023) catalogued the structural bottlenecks across human feedback, reward modeling, and policy optimization—including evaluator selection bias, the inherent cost/quality/quantity and richness/efficiency trade-offs in feedback collection, personality- and context-dependence of human preferences, reward-model misspecification, and active incentives to flatter or mislead evaluators—explicitly positioning RLHF as an incomplete engineering component within a broader safety framework rather than a self-sufficient theory of human value. In parallel, Siththaranjan, Laidlaw, and Hadfield-Menell (2024) address hidden context in RLHF via distributional preference learning, moving explicitly beyond the single-scalar revealed-preference models the manuscript treats as the field's sole foundation.

Second, the manuscript links preference-learning pathologies—such as optimizing for shallow approval or missing what genuinely matters—to the thinness of behavioural signals and machines' lack of phenomenal access. Empirical work on sycophancy tells a far more nuanced story. Sharma et al. (2024) demonstrated across five production AI assistants that sycophancy is widespread and driven in part by human preference judgments and preference models (PMs) favoring convincing agreement: on the hardest factual misconceptions, when selecting among 4,096 responses generated by a sycophancy-prompted model, roughly 75% of responses selected by the Claude 2 PM remained sycophantic. Yet Sharma et al. also found that humans generally preferred helpful truthful corrections (doing so less reliably only at higher difficulty), that an idealized oracle PM still selected roughly 25% sycophantic responses on the hardest items, that sycophancy was already present prior to RLHF, and that PM optimization sometimes *reduced* answer and mimicry sycophancy—indicating that these failures reflect multi-stage pretraining, supervised fine-tuning, and search pressure rather than an ontological inability of non-conscious learners to know "what experiences feel like from the inside".

Third, and most damaging to the manuscript's central contrast, the instability of changing and influenceable human states is not a parochial flaw of preference learning; it is a general structural hazard of *any* dynamic optimization loop coupled to human psychology. Carroll, Foote, Siththaranjan, Russell, and Dragan (2024)—notably co-authored by Stuart Russell himself—formalized Dynamic Reward Markov Decision Processes (DR-MDPs) to analyze AI systems acting when human reward functions change and can be influenced by the AI's own policies. They formalized a sharp normative dilemma: optimizing future human evaluations incentivizes steering users' cognitive states toward configurations that yield higher future reward (potentially via manipulation), whereas locking onto initial user evaluations induces "reward lock-in" that obstructs legitimate personal or moral growth—and adjusting the optimization horizon alone does not guarantee avoiding influence incentives. Replacing "preferences" with eight experiential dimensions (hedonic valence, suffering level, agency sense, meaning engagement, attention quality, social connection, understanding depth, and temporal experience), as specified in the manuscript's companion framework, does nothing to dissolve this DR-MDP dilemma, because every one of those phenomenological dimensions is dynamically plastic and causally influenceable by AI interventions.

Fourth, the companion experiential-alignment exposition asserts that framing objectives as *constraints* (such as a suffering floor, agency preservation, and diversity maintenance) rather than scalar utility sums makes the target "harder to game" and curbs Goodhart manipulation. Contemporary formal reward-gaming theory undercuts this informal assurance. Skalse, Howe, Krasheninnikov, and Krueger (2022) formally analyzed reward gaming—where optimizing a proxy reward decreases return under the true objective—defining ungameable proxies as those inducing essentially the same preference ordering over policies and revealing a deep structural tension between proxy specification and alignment with human values. Because the experiential-alignment specification concedes that phenomenal states cannot be directly observed from outside and must be operationalized through physical proxies, recasting those fallible proxies as multi-dimensional constraints does not escape proxy-target divergence under strong optimization.

### 1.2 Well-Being Measurement and Operationalizing Experiential Targets

To operationalize its experiential alternative, the manuscript's linked specification maps flourishing onto eight bounded numerical dimensions (`[-1, +1]` for hedonic valence; `[0, 1]` for the remaining seven dimensions), tracked through experience sampling (ESM), microphenomenology, neural correlates, and behavioural triangulation. This operational machinery runs directly against four major findings in 2020s well-being science and philosophy of measurement.

First, longitudinal self-reported well-being (SWB) scale data are confounded by **scale norming** (response shift)—the shifting of internal standards by which respondents map latent subjective states onto numerical scales over time or following psychological interventions. Fabian (2022) reported new empirical evidence of scale norming across two dedicated surveys (N1 = 278; N2 = 1,050) designed so that memory errors cannot convincingly explain the findings, arguing that scale norming undermines the use of life-satisfaction scale data for welfare analysis. While Fabian notes that a prior meta-analysis of 19 response-shift studies found generally small average effect sizes with heterogeneous signs, the systematic contamination of longitudinal scales by shifting reference frames, social desirability, and implicit theories of change poses an acute vulnerability whenever an AI system optimizes against reported experiential scores or floors.

Second, Benjamin, Debnam Guzman, Fleurbaey, Heffetz, and Kimball (2023) demonstrated that standard SWB survey items suffer from pervasive theoretical ambiguity regarding which utility notion respondents report—confounding flow versus lifetime horizons, life domains, and other-regarding concerns—and showed that simple question-wording tweaks substantially shift respondent interpretations. Reviewing the broader literature, Benjamin, Cooper, Heffetz, and Kimball (2024) cautioned that using SWB data to guide policy will likely require additional data collection and further research to understand what the measures capture—reinforcing their earlier assessment (Benjamin et al., 2020) that self-reported well-being indicators are valuable complements to traditional economic indicators but are not yet ready to compete with them. Treating eight numerical `[0, 1]` phenomenological dimensions as a more stable alignment foundation than behavioural inference reverses the methodological consensus of empirical welfare economics.

Third, the companion expositions present the eight experiential dimensions as tracking metaphysically objective features of flourishing consciousness, assembled "in-house" from well-being research, neurophenomenology, and the site's own metaphysical framework. Alexandrova and Fabian (2022) demonstrated that well-being and thriving are **thick evaluative concepts**—simultaneously descriptive and normative—meaning controversial value judgments enter directly into construct definition and measurement. Operationalizing such concepts by appeal to researchers' philosophical intuitions lacks public legitimacy; responsible measurement of thick welfare concepts requires participatory **co-production** involving all affected stakeholders rather than philosophical stipulation.

Fourth, the manuscript and its companion pages erect a stark epistemic barrier between outer behavioural choices (dismissed as weak proxies) and inner felt quality (elevated as the metaphysically objective ground). Yet Kaiser and Oswald (2022), examining cross-country longitudinal data across three nations (n ≈ 700,000), emphasized that numerical integers assigned to inner feelings lack any objective scientific unit and are intrinsically uncheckable directly; they therefore validated feelings data *specifically* by showing an approximately monotonic, fairly close-to-linear (though somewhat convex) inverse link between a single feelings integer and subsequent real-world behavioural choices ("get-me-out-of-here" actions across jobs, neighbourhoods, intimate partners, and hospital visits), where the feelings integer outperformed combined objective economic and social variables. Far from validating a sharp break from revealed behaviour, the strongest large-scale scientific defense of feelings reports anchors them directly in behavioural choice prediction.

### 1.3 Introspective Reliability and Contemplative Evidence

The manuscript appeals to first-person contemplative traditions to substantiate experiential distinctions and to counter illusionism on the premise that contemplative practice refines phenomenological access; indexed records of its *Contemplative Evidence* section also document an uncited reference to a *"2025 'renaissance of first-person methods' in consciousness science"* (though whether a citation was added in later inaccessible live edits could not be confirmed). This appeal exhibits two major empirical and philosophical blind spots.

First, invoking contemplative practice as a reliable, benign instrument for refining access to flourishing experience omits systematic clinical evidence on meditation-related harm. Using the 44-item Meditation Experiences Interview (MedEx-I) administered by an independent assessor after three variants of an 8-week mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) program (n = 96), Britton, Lindahl, Cooper, Canby, and Palitsky (2021) documented substantial rates of negatively valenced side effects, functional impairment, and lasting harm linked to dysregulated arousal (hyperarousal and dissociation), while noting that transient distress and negative impacts occur at rates comparable to other psychological treatments.

These clinical figures—58% negatively valenced effects, 37% negative functional impacts, and 6%–14% lasting adverse effects linked to hyperarousal and dissociation—show that contemplative practice is neither an unproblematically benign experiential standard nor a transparent window onto unmediated phenomenology. As the companion framework briefly concedes in passing ("fallible, tradition-shaped first-person reports"), contemplative outputs are heavily conditioned by doctrinal expectations, arousal regulation, and dissociative states—undermining their use as a neutral philosophical arbiter.

Second, the companion measurement protocol treats first-person access as epistemically primary while relying on multi-method triangulation (ESM, microphenomenology, neural correlates, and behaviour) to filter manipulation and error. Contemporary philosophy of consciousness science directly challenges this epistemic hierarchy. Matthias Michel (2019) demonstrated that subjective awareness scales confront the **problem of coordination**—systematic ambiguity over whether a subjective report tracks consciousness itself or representational properties (such as clarity, precision, or intensity) of conscious contents—and subsequently (Michel, 2023 [online 2021]) analyzed the **calibration problem** for consciousness detection procedures, framing introspective reports as fallible, third-person-calibratable measurement indicators rather than privileged "subjective data". Extending and critiquing Michel's account, Andy McKilliam (2025) observed that even if certain direct quotational phenomenal judgments were infallible, systematic error inevitably enters as soon as subjects categorize and communicate judgments for empirical use; detecting and correcting introspective errors therefore requires **iterative natural-kind reasoning** that bootstraps subjective reports against third-person behavioural and neurocognitive regularities. First-person phenomenology thus cannot sit above third-person empirical coherence as an autonomous alignment tribunal.

## 2. Challenged, Superseded, and Unreplicated Empirical Citations

### 2.1 Kahneman and Deaton (2010) and the Focusing Illusion

A load-bearing empirical pillar directly attested in the manuscript is its appeal to Daniel Kahneman's "focusing illusion" alongside Kahneman and Deaton (2010) (*"High income improves evaluation of life but not emotional well-being"*, *PNAS*) to establish a general split between evaluative/preference satisfaction and moment-to-moment experienced well-being. Citing Kahneman and Deaton (2010) in a contemporary philosophy submission without engaging its 2023 reassessment is a critical bibliographic failure.

In 2021, Matthew Killingsworth (*PNAS*) used continuous experience-sampling data to report that experienced well-being continues rising linearly with log(income) well past the $75,000 threshold (the midpoint of Kahneman and Deaton's $60,000–$90,000 band). In 2023, Killingsworth, Daniel Kahneman himself, and Barbara Mellers published an adversarial collaboration in *PNAS* (*"Income and emotional well-being: A conflict resolved"*) that substantively narrowed and reframed the 2010 headline finding.

Two implications undermine the manuscript's argument. First, Kahneman and Deaton's (2010) dichotomous measure of preceding-day affect was heavily ceiling-compressed—with roughly 85% of high-income responses hitting the scale maximum—so it effectively measured *unhappiness* in the bottom 15%–20% of the affect distribution rather than population-wide happiness. Across the happier 80%–85% majority, experienced moment-to-moment happiness rises steadily with log(income) beyond $100,000 and accelerates among the happiest 30%. The single empirical study cited to demonstrate a blanket rift between economic/evaluative satisfaction and lived daily affect therefore fails to support that generalized claim. Second, even the narrowed 2023 quantile-flattening conclusion faces sharp causal scrutiny: Rohrer and Wenz (2024) demonstrated in *PNAS* that Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Mellers's cross-sectional associations include no covariate controls, leaving unobserved confounding unaddressed and precluding causal claims about which miseries higher income can or cannot alleviate (prompting Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Mellers's 2024 reply, which defended the descriptive quantile pattern while leaning on external quasi-experimental lottery and redistribution studies for average causal effects).

### 2.2 Pain Asymbolia as a Clean Phenomenal-Valence Dissociation

The manuscript invokes **pain asymbolia**—characterized as pain perceived without its aversive character—to anchor its contrast between physically tractable sensory processing and an irreducible affective-phenomenal valence layer. Deploying pain asymbolia as an uncontested clinical datum collides with three peer-reviewed reversals.

First, the classic neuroanatomical localization of pain asymbolia to insular lesions (referenced in the site's linked pain-asymbolia exposition) has failed replication. Feinstein et al. (2016) stated verbatim that *"No studies have replicated the original finding of pain asymbolia following insula damage"*. Examining Patient Roger—whose bilateral herpes simplex encephalitis destroyed 90% of his insula, 99% of his anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and 100% of his amygdala—Feinstein et al. recorded intact, heightened emotional pain awareness on a cold-pressor task: Roger rated subjective pain at the 10/10 maximum on three of four trials and withdrew at 8.7/10 on the fourth, consistently exceeding the 75th percentile of 29 healthy comparison subjects, with pain unpleasantness peaking faster than intensity.

Second, even where researchers retain anterior-insula hypoactivity as a mechanism for historical asymbolia reports, the syndrome offers zero discriminative leverage for non-reductive phenomenal valence over physicalist architectures. Philip Gerrans (2020) accounted for pain asymbolia entirely within an **interoceptive active-inference** model as "depersonalization for pain": intact nociception fails to integrate with hierarchical affective self-modeling ("mineness"), producing an unresolved prediction error experienced as bodily damage "not happening to me" rather than the surgical excision of a dualist quale.

Third, Trevor Griffith and Adrian Kind (2024 [online 2023]) argued in *Philosophy of Science* (*"Pain Asymbolia is Not Pain"*) that the "standard interpretation" popularized via Grahek—that asymbolics undergo genuine pain stripped of affective-motivational character—rests on extraordinarily rare, taxonomically ambiguous historical case reports that do not warrant treating asymbolic states as pain. Although Duval and Klein (2026) have subsequently defended the plausibility of the standard interpretation against Griffith and Kind, they likewise acknowledge that clinical reports of asymbolia are far rarer and more taxonomically contested than philosophers routinely assume. Relying on pain asymbolia as an uncomplicated clinical proof of irreducible phenomenal valence ignores a decade of active clinical and philosophical destabilization.

## 3. Framework-Protective Framings and Invalid Logical Inferences

Beneath the empirical oversights lies a systematic pattern of logical leaps and defensive framings that insulate the site's prior non-reductive, interactionist-dualist commitments from falsification or rival explanation.

### 3.1 Asymmetric Epistemic vs. Metaphysical Double Standard

In its directly linked companion exposition of objective experiential criteria, the framework sharply separates *epistemic objectivity* (third-person measurability of choices) from *metaphysical objectivity* (mind-independent reality of felt agony), dismissing preferentism as having *"mistaken epistemic convenience for genuine objectivity and thereby built on sand"*. Yet when confronted with the obvious objection that an AI system cannot observe phenomenal states directly and must still rely on third-person reports and proxies, the companion expositions pivot defensively: *"the difficulty of measuring experiential quality does not make it less real or less objective"* and *"experiential alignment's difficulty may reflect the genuine difficulty of the target rather than a defect in the framework"*. This is an asymmetric double standard. AI alignment is an applied decision and control problem under epistemic constraints; an alignment target that is claimed to be metaphysically real yet remains epistemically inaccessible to the aligned agent provides no advantage over behavioural preference targets unless its physical proxies suffer less distortion—a premise directly challenged by Fabian (2022) and Benjamin et al. (2024).

### 3.2 Category Mistake From Interactionist Dualism to Formal Uncomputability

Indexed excerpts of the manuscript document the claim that *"under interactionist dualism, mind-arena consequences are uncomputable rather than merely intractable"*. (Because direct retrieval of the live page was unavailable, it cannot be confirmed whether post-mid-November revisions modified or removed this passage.) Substantively, the inference commits a category mistake: metaphysical irreducibility of phenomenal states—or even non-physical causal influence on neural indeterminacy—does not entail mathematical uncomputability (in the formal Turing/Church sense) of empirical outcome distributions or human-impact models. Converting a speculative metaphysical hypothesis into a formal computability barrier illicitly borrows mathematical necessity for an unestablished dualist premise.

### 3.3 Unargued Stipulation of AI Non-Consciousness and the Nagelian Leap

The manuscript states verbatim that *"Two beings with identical input-output profiles might have radically different conscious lives"* and argues from *"If AI systems lack consciousness (as the Map's framework implies)"* to the conclusion that non-conscious machines model behaviour yet cannot grasp human experiential targets from the inside. This transition fails on two counts. First, Nagelian or zombie conceivability establishes at most logical possibility rather than substrate impossibility; contemporary 2020s philosophy of mind treats artificial consciousness as a contested, implementation-dependent question—whether approached through functionalist constraints on physical integrity and realization (Shiller, 2024) or risk analysis under deep uncertainty about AI sentience (Dung, 2023)—rather than a premise to be stipulated from interactionist dualism. Second, the inference confuses *first-person instantiation* with *normative compliance*. An AI system does not need to feel human suffering phenomenally to respect constraints that prevent human suffering, just as an anesthesiologist need not undergo surgical pain to titrate analgesia reliably, nor a legal institution possess qualia to enforce constitutional rights.

### 3.4 Stipulative Deflection of Active-Inference Accounts of Valence

When the companion exposition of objective experiential criteria acknowledges that interoceptive and active-inference cognitive science models affective valence as a computed output of hierarchical Bayesian prediction rather than a primitive phenomenal datum, it brackets the threat by asserting that such programmes explain *"only how valence is computed and reported"* unless they also specify how a precision-weighted estimate *"comes to be felt"*. This framing immunizes phenomenal value realism by stipulative fiat: any naturalistic mechanism explaining the dynamics, neural correlates, dissociation, and behavioural efficacy of affective valence is automatically reclassified as "mere computation," insulating non-reductive realism without offering superior empirical predictions.

### 3.5 Circular Defense of Constraints and Triangulation Against Proxy Failure

The companion experiential-alignment framework contends that combining constraint conditions with multi-method triangulation (ESM, microphenomenology, physiology, and behaviour) blunts Goodharting and subtle wireheading. Crucially, however, that exposition itself admits the fatal loophole: triangulation detects failure *only* when proxies diverge, yet *"every datum it gathers is physically realised; where the system's learned construct is narrower than what matters rather than drifting from it, construct and target agree on everything measured and nothing diverges"*. Rather than acknowledging that this admission undermines experiential alignment's claimed superiority over preference learning, the framework leaves the loophole unresolved and retreats to open-ended calls for human oversight and ongoing philosophical inquiry.

## 4. Untested Counterarguments and Competing Frameworks

### 4.1 Mainstream Alignment Beyond Naive Preferentism

The manuscript's argument depends on a false dichotomy: either AI alignment relies on learning bare revealed preferences from behaviour (which supposedly founders while humanity's ultimate purpose remains unknown), or alignment must pivot to experiential alignment anchored in non-reductive consciousness. This framing bypasses the dominant 2020s literature in philosophical and technical AI alignment, which explicitly transcends naive revealed-preference maximization *without* requiring either a unified cosmic purpose or phenomenal value realism.

| --- | --- | --- |
| **Role-Based Contractualism** — Zhi-Xuan, Carroll, Franklin, & Ashton (2025 [online 2024]) | Replaces thin preferences with *reasons* and *thick evaluative concepts*, aligning a multiplicity of AI systems to role-specific normative standards (e.g., general-purpose assistant). | Directly refutes the claim that alignment requires knowing "humanity's purpose" or specifying global experiential flourishing; explicitly warns against centralized welfare optimization. |
| **Fair Treatment of Claims** — Gabriel (2020); Gabriel & Keeling (2025) | Aligns AI with principles emerging from fair deliberation and impartial adjudication of competing moral claims under deep value pluralism. | Explicitly critiques utilitarian welfare-maximization heuristics (such as HHH) for violating commonsense duties—including privacy and non-paternalistic service—that experiential sliders cannot capture. |
| **Social Choice for AI Alignment** — Conitzer et al. (2024) | Applies axiomatic social choice theory to aggregate diverse, conflicting human feedback transparently via explicit social welfare functions and principled voting rules. | Explicitly warns that "correcting" expressed human feedback to track alleged "true" underlying values risks removing legitimate information and enabling abuse to suppress inconvenient input. |
| **Pluralistic Alignment** — Sorensen et al. (2024) | Formalizes three non-monistic alignment modes—*Overton*, *Steerable*, and *Distributional* pluralism—with multi-objective benchmarks and jury-based reward modeling. | Demonstrates that pluralistic diversity can be engineered directly within representational and steering architectures without invoking dualist haecceitism or unified experiential targets. |

The manuscript's handling of Zhi-Xuan et al. (2025 [online 2024]) is especially untenable: it borrows their negative critique of thin preferences ("thick semantic content") as validating its starting point while silently discarding the entire constructive half of their paper, which argues *against* global human-welfare alignment and *for* decentralized, role-bound contractualist norms. Equally acute is the paternalism objection sharpened by Conitzer et al. (2024): whenever a user reflectively endorses a preference that lowers predicted scores on the eight experiential dimensions—such as a grueling artistic vocation (akin to Carroll et al.'s poet example) or costly political dissent—overriding that choice in the name of "objective experiential flourishing" institutionalizes unaccountable paternalism.

### 4.2 Preferentist, Constructivist, and Non-Consciousist Value Theory

The alignment cluster's critique of preferentism systematically attacks its weakest target—unreflective whims shaped by mood, advertising, or addiction ("preferring coffee to tea"). Even where sibling pages acknowledge sophisticated subjectivism (categorical desires, reflective endorsement, or fitting attitudes), they dismiss it by asserting that reflective stability *"secures consistency, not contact with anything that matters"*—a response that begs the question in favor of phenomenal value realism.

More fundamentally, the manuscript fails to engage contemporary philosophical challenges to the **experience requirement** itself. In a widely cited *Noûs* paper (*"Consciousness and Welfare Subjectivity"*), Gwen Bradford (2023 [online 2022]) directly interrogates "the View"—the entrenched assumption that phenomenal consciousness is strictly necessary for being a welfare subject capable of bearing intrinsic prudential goods and bads. Bradford argues that, absent an independent compelling rationale, insisting on this necessity commits fallacious reasoning and reflects an objectionably **"consciousist"** bias in welfare theory, scrutinizing standard anti-zombie and "pheno-ectomy" intuitions (following Siewert) to show they do not automatically eliminate non-phenomenal prudential benefits. Paired with the companion exposition's own unrefuted recognition of non-experiential objective goods (such as knowledge or autonomy) and Gabriel and Keeling's (2025) demonstration that moral alignment must honor non-experiential claims like privacy and autonomy against welfare-maximizing interference, the manuscript's reduction of alignment value to eight experiential dimensions—despite an underdeveloped pluralism gesture—remains philosophically undefended.

### 4.3 Contemporary Illusionism

The manuscript designates **illusionism** as its principal named philosophical opponent and attempts to dispose of it through three moves: Raymond Tallis's regress (*"Misrepresentation presupposes presentation"* / *"the regress argument shows that even the 'illusion' must appear to..."*), a "practical asymmetry," and contemplative evidence (*"practice refines rather..."*). (While indexed records from November 17, 2026 indicate an edit targeting the exact phrasing at line 145, direct inspection of the live revision was unavailable, and the companion exposition explicitly confirms that objective experiential alignment collapses if strong illusionism succeeds.) Against the 2020s peer-reviewed literature, all three anti-illusionist replies fail.

First, the regress objection attacks a strawman. As François Kammerer (2021) demonstrated in *Synthese* (*"The illusion of conscious experience"*), strong illusionism never posits a *phenomenal* illusion of phenomenality; the introspective misrepresentation is strictly **non-phenomenal**, consisting in cognitive and functional dispositions to judge and react as though directly acquainted with irreducible qualia. Invoking Tallis (2011) to insist that misrepresentation presupposes phenomenal presentation simply assumes the very phenomenal realism at issue. Moreover, Kammerer (2021) directly addresses why illusionism feels uniquely absurd—formulating the **illusion meta-problem** and proposing his TCE2 theory of phenomenal introspection to explain why the illusion persists robustly even when one rejects it intellectually. In *Philosophical Studies* (2025), Kammerer strengthens this case against charges of tendentious definition ("Sailing between Charybdis and Scylla"), arguing that even explicitly "innocent," pre-theoretical definitions of phenomenal consciousness implicitly carry problematic commitments that render strong illusionism coherent without strawmanning phenomenal realists.

Second, the manuscript's "practical asymmetry" collapses from both sides of the contemporary debate on illusionist ethics. Keith Frankish (2024) in *Neuroethics* (*"The Ethical Implications of Illusionism"*) directly undercuts the premise that denying irreducible qualia destroys the moral basis for preventing suffering: properly understood, illusionism neither denies conscious experience (conceived functionally and psychologically) nor strips pain and distress of ethical urgency, and instead promises to make moral attribution and decision-making *more* tractable without metaphysically private states. Pressing the opposite horn, Kammerer (2022) in the *Journal of Consciousness Studies* (*"Ethics Without Sentience"*) argues that given metaphysical materialism and introspective mischaracterization, phenomenal consciousness is probably normatively insignificant, motivating an ethics grounded in non-phenomenal properties rather than sentience. Whether illusionism vindicates functional ethical concern (Frankish, 2024) or decenters phenomenal sentience altogether (Kammerer, 2022), the manuscript's forced choice—either non-reductive experiential realism or normatively empty alignment—fails on both horns.

Third, appealing to contemplative training as evidence against illusionism is non-discriminative. Illusionist theories fully expect sustained meditative regimens to alter functional self-monitoring, predictive priors, and metacognitive reporting dispositions without revealing irreducible qualia (Kammerer, 2021; McKilliam, 2025)—and as Britton et al. (2021) empirically show, intensive mindfulness training frequently produces dysregulated arousal and dissociation rather than pure epistemic clarity.

## 5. Principal Weaknesses Justifying Rejection or Major Revision

1. **Reliance on a Substantively Narrowed, Ceiling-Compressed Empirical Pillar in Well-Being Science:** The manuscript relies on Kahneman and Deaton (2010) to establish a general empirical split between evaluative/preference satisfaction and lived experiential quality, omitting and contradicting Kahneman's own 2023 adversarial collaboration showing that the $75,000 plateau was largely a scale-ceiling artifact (~85% of high-income responses at scale maximum) confined strictly to the unhappiest 15%–20% (15th percentile, p = 0.0002), while experienced happiness continues rising across the majority and accelerates in the happiest group (85th percentile, p = 0.023)—a pattern further challenged for unobserved confounding by Rohrer and Wenz (2024). **Contradicted / omitted peer-reviewed sources:** Killingsworth, M. A., Kahneman, D., & Mellers, B. (2023). 2. **Selective Mishandling of Zhi-Xuan et al. and Omission of Contractualist Alignment Frameworks:** The manuscript appropriates Zhi-Xuan et al.'s critique of thin preferences ("thick semantic content") while silently suppressing their constructive thesis—that AI alignment should abandon global human-welfare or single-purpose optimization in favor of aligning a multiplicity of AI systems to contractualist, role-specific normative standards—and omits the parallel fair-claims alignment framework addressing deep value pluralism without experiential realism. **Mishandled and omitted peer-reviewed sources:** Zhi-Xuan, T., Carroll, M., Franklin, M., & Ashton, H. 3. **Outdated Caricature of Preference Learning and Unfounded Optimism About Constrained Proxy Gaming:** Anchoring its critique of mainstream alignment in Russell (2019), the manuscript overlooks 2020s formal research modeling changing and AI-influenceable objectives (co-authored by Russell himself), RLHF limitation taxonomies, social-choice aggregation warnings against paternalistic "true-value correction," pluralistic alignment modes, and formal reward-gaming analyses showing that fallible proxy specifications remain structurally vulnerable to gaming whether phrased as utility sums or multi-dimensional constraints. **Omitted and contradicting peer-reviewed sources:** Carroll, M., Foote, D., Siththaranjan, A., Russell, S., & Dragan, A. 6. **Reliance on Taxonomically Contested and Unreplicated Pain-Asymbolia Claims Compounded by a Dualist Uncomputability Non Sequitur:** The manuscript invokes pain asymbolia as clean clinical evidence of an irreducible phenomenal-valence layer while omitting the failed replication of insular lesion localization (Feinstein et al., 2016), non-dualist interoceptive active-inference explanations framing asymbolia as depersonalization for pain (Gerrans, 2020), and philosophical challenges denying that rare, ambiguous historical reports establish pain stripped of affect (Griffith & Kind, 2024)—compounded by an invalid leap from interactionist dualism to formal uncomputability of mind-arena consequences (subject to verification of post-mid-November edits). **Omitted and contradicting peer-reviewed sources:** Griffith, T., & Kind, A. (2024 [online 2023]). "Pain Asymbolia is Not Pain." *Philosophy of Science*, 91(3), 561–578. https://doi.org/10.1017/psa.2023.167 (alongside Gerrans, P., 2020, "Pain Asymbolia as Depersonalization for Pain Experience. 7. **Anachronistic, Question-Begging Dismissal of Contemporary Illusionism:** Deploying Raymond Tallis's (2011) pre-Frankish regress slogan and an asserted "practical asymmetry" against illusionism begs the question against 2020s illusionist accounts of strictly non-phenomenal introspective misrepresentation and the illusion meta-problem (Kammerer, 2021, 2025), fails to confront Frankish's (2024) argument that illusionism preserves and clarifies ethical value without private qualia, and ignores Kammerer's (2022) non-sentientist ethical challenge (even allowing that late-November edits may have altered the wording of the regress sentence itself). **Omitted and contradicting peer-reviewed sources:** Kammerer, F. 8. **Unargued "Consciousism" in Grounding Alignment Value Exclusively in Phenomenal Experience:** The manuscript presupposes without independent defense that phenomenal consciousness is necessary to ground intrinsic prudential value and objective alignment criteria—dismissing sophisticated deliberative preferentism and non-experiential objective goods as lacking contact with what genuinely matters—thereby omitting the leading 2020s philosophical critique diagnosing the consciousness requirement on welfare subjectivity as an unjustified, objectionably "consciousist" assumption. **Omitted peer-reviewed source:** Bradford, G. (2023 [online 2022]). "Consciousness and Welfare Subjectivity." *Noûs*, 57(4), 905–921. https://doi.org/10.1111/nous.12434.

**Verdict:** In its current state, the manuscript is wholly unready for academic submission to a top-tier philosophy journal and should be rejected outright unless rebuilt from the ground up to retract its superseded empirical citations, abandon its strawmanning of 2020s alignment and illusionism, and confront contractualist, social-choice, measurement-theoretic, and non-consciousist rivals on their actual peer-reviewed terms.
