# External referee report: “Categorical Surprise”

Article reviewed: “Categorical Surprise”
Last substantive modification: 11 September 2026
Recommendation: Major revision

## Executive assessment

The article succeeds as a phenomenological proposal: it identifies an intuitively important contrast between being surprised by an unexpected value within a familiar framework and discovering that the framework itself was inadequate. Its September revision also usefully distinguishes detecting global model failure from constructing a replacement model, and it acknowledges that phenomenology alone cannot decide between causal and epiphenomenal readings. The Unfinishable Map+2The Unfinishable Map+2

It does not, however, establish its stronger conclusions:

- that categorical surprise is a discrete psychological kind rather than an extreme or multidimensional form of model revision;

- that computational systems are structurally incapable of the relevant restructuring;

- that phenomenal vertigo enables rather than merely accompanies restructuring; or

- that this is a specially promising locus for quantum selection.

The central inferential problem is a slide between three very different claims:

- an error signal does not itself contain the model that should replace the failed model;

- a particular learner may lack an adequate replacement in its current repertoire;

- no computational process could construct a replacement representation.

The first is true but unsurprising. The second is sometimes true. The article supplies no argument for the third. Direct computational work on insight, structure learning and conceptual bootstrapping is omitted, including work from 2017 and 2024 that was available well before the article’s latest revision. UCL Discovery+1

My overall verdict is therefore:

The article is an interesting description of one apparent form of experience, but not yet a successful impossibility argument or an independent argument for dualism.

Its strongest defensible conclusion is that existing computational models do not yet capture every aspect of radical human conceptual change. That is considerably weaker than the article’s claim that such change “resists this treatment for structural reasons.”

## 1. The proposed category is not yet empirically established

The article characterises categorical surprise by framework violation, absence of a relevant prior expectation, and subsequent restructuring. It later says both the phenomenology and restructuring “behave like a boundary rather than a slope,” and that careful phenomenological work has not reduced the phenomenon to high-intensity ordinary surprise. The Unfinishable Map+1

Those assertions are under-supported. The article cites no empirical study of surprise or insight phenomenology. Recent work instead suggests that the Aha experience is continuous and multidimensional: surprise, suddenness, pleasure and certainty can vary independently. A preregistered 2024 study found that surprise correlated with metacognitive prediction error, whereas suddenness did not show the same relation and was negatively associated in one analysis. The same literature notes that people do not always experience an Aha moment when producing a new idea. Springer+1

A 2026 review of insight research likewise reports that restructuring is not always sudden, that findings concerning restructuring are equivocal, and that not all experiences classified as insight involve restructuring. Springer

This does not disprove a narrower category of especially radical surprise. But it means that the article currently stipulates rather than demonstrates a natural psychological boundary. It needs to distinguish at least:

- subjective intensity;

- suddenness;

- degree of representational change;

- metacognitive recognition of previous ignorance;

- correctness of the new representation;

- and subsequent behavioural transfer.

At present these are bundled together under “vertigo followed by a click.” A person may feel dramatic surprise without acquiring a better framework, acquire a new framework gradually, or undergo restructuring without a pronounced Aha experience.

### A conceptual problem with “outside the representational space”

The definition also risks becoming self-undermining. If an event literally lies outside every representational capacity of the subject, it cannot be recognised as an event, as novel, or as evidence that a framework has failed. Conversely, once it is represented as “an unknown object,” “an anomaly,” “something my categories cannot accommodate,” or “a possible counterexample,” it is already inside a higher-order representational space.

The article therefore needs to specify which space is exceeded:

- the models currently being entertained;

- the current explicit vocabulary;

- a task-level hypothesis class;

- the learner’s generative grammar;

- or every representational resource the system possesses.

The argument changes radically depending on the answer. Escaping one’s active repertoire is commonplace. Escaping one’s total generative resources is a much stronger claim, and the article offers no evidence that human consciousness does that either.

The red-car example obscures this issue. Someone with no concept of a car can still represent the encounter as an object, artefact, moving thing, dangerous thing or unknown entity. Concept formation need not begin from representational nothingness.

## 2. The computational impossibility claim is not supported

The article’s best move is to concede that a Bayesian system can detect global misfit. It then argues that the misfit signal lacks the content needed to construct the replacement framework. The Unfinishable Map

That observation does not establish a computational limitation. An error signal need not encode its own repair. In ordinary engineered and biological systems, separate processes perform:

- anomaly detection;

- exploratory sampling;

- candidate generation;

- analogical transfer;

- representation search;

- model comparison;

- consolidation;

- and reuse of newly learned abstractions.

The question is not whether the misfit signal itself contains the answer, but whether it can trigger computational processes that generate and evaluate candidate representations.

### Directly relevant work is omitted

Friston and colleagues published a formal active-inference treatment of curiosity and insight in 2017. It explicitly moves from state inference to model selection and structure learning, uses abstract rule-learning simulations, and describes Bayesian model reduction with the hallmarks of Aha experiences. This does not settle every question about radical conceptual change, but it is a direct rival to the article’s central claim. Citing only Friston’s broad 2010 free-energy review while omitting the paper specifically devoted to insight is a serious literature gap. UCL Discovery+1

Zhao, Lucas and Bramley’s computational model of conceptual bootstrapping uses a dynamic conceptual repertoire that caches and reuses products of earlier learning as new conceptual primitives. Across four behavioural experiments involving 570 participants, it reproduced curriculum-order and conceptual garden-path effects. Its underlying generative grammar spans an unbounded concept space and alters the learner’s effective repertoire over time. Nature+1

This model remains implemented in a higher-level formal language. But that is precisely the point the article must confront rather than presuppose away. A computational critic can say that human conceptual change also proceeds through recombination, abstraction, analogy, language, instruments and social scaffolding operating within higher-order capacities. Merely observing that every implementation has some meta-level constraints does not distinguish computers from human minds.

A further active-inference model, published on 17 September 2026, reproduces discontinuous changes in behaviour and confidence, a transient Bayesian-surprise peak and phasic precision changes, interpreting restructuring through Bayesian model reduction and generative replay. It appeared six days after the target article’s latest modification, so its absence is understandable. Nevertheless, it now makes the unqualified sentence “None of these findings currently exists” obsolete. It is a partial, not decisive, counterexample: the model restructures within an experimentally specified task family rather than inventing an entirely new ontology. But it is exactly the sort of account that the article says is not on offer. Springer+1

### The fixed-meta-language objection cuts both ways

The article can preserve a more ambitious claim by saying that all computational restructuring remains inside some antecedently supplied metalanguage. But then it incurs a parity problem:

- What evidence shows that a human subject ever escapes every antecedently available cognitive primitive?

- How could such escape be recognised rather than retrospectively described using the new framework?

- Why should nonphysical consciousness possess representational resources not themselves constrained by some psychophysical structure?

- How does the phenomenal feeling generate the informational content of the new ontology?

Until those questions are answered, the fixed-meta-language observation supports, at most, a general limitation applying to humans and machines alike.

## 3. Gödel and Tarski do not deliver the claimed structural result

The article is commendably more restrained than many invocations of incompleteness: it explicitly says that Gödel and Tarski do not show that a system cannot notice its limits. Nevertheless, it then claims that no system can map its boundaries completely, that restructuring requires such a map, and therefore that the formal results provide the needed bound. The Unfinishable Map

The decisive step is unsupported:

Why would local or even radical restructuring require a complete map of the system’s representational boundaries?

A learner need not characterise everything it cannot represent. It may discover one failed assumption, introduce a new primitive, construct a higher-level abstraction, borrow a representation from another domain, or expand its language in response to a concrete anomaly. None requires exhaustive self-characterisation.

Tarski’s framework actually supplies an obvious physicalist response. Truth for an object language is defined in a suitably stronger metalanguage. That does not provide a final all-encompassing language, but neither does restructuring require one. Hierarchies of object language, metalanguage and stronger metalanguage are analogous to architectures with object-level learning, metacognition and meta-learning. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy

Similarly, Gödel’s results concern formal systems satisfying particular expressiveness, consistency and effective-axiomatisation conditions. They do not establish a theorem that learning systems cannot alter representations, add primitives or move to stronger systems. The Gödel citation itself is bibliographically sound, but the philosophical application exceeds what the theorem establishes. Springer

The article’s reply to the parity objection—consciousness does not solve the regress but “experiences it”—changes the subject. Experiencing a limitation may explain why limitation has a distinctive phenomenal character. It does not explain:

- where the replacement framework comes from;

- why the experience contains information unavailable to computation;

- how it causally reorganises cognition;

- or why a computational metacognitive state could not generate the same report and behaviour.

The distinction between experiencing a problem and solving a problem is especially important here. The argument needs the latter but establishes, at most, the former.

## 4. The causal claim outruns the evidence

The article initially concedes that correlation between phenomenal vertigo and revision does not adjudicate between interactionism and epiphenomenalism. It then asserts that the two “reliably go together,” that no computational account of the pairing is available, and that treating experience as causally idle would make the relation a “systematic coincidence.” The Unfinishable Map+1

There are three problems.

### First, reliable co-occurrence is not established

The article cites no evidence measuring radical representational change and phenomenal vertigo in the same subjects. Existing insight research reports dissociations: Aha dimensions do not invariably co-occur; insight can occur without a strong Aha experience; felt suddenness and restructuring can come apart; and restructuring may unfold gradually. Springer+2Springer+2

### Second, accompaniment would not necessarily be a coincidence

A physicalist identity theorist, functionalist or common-cause theorist need not posit two independently generated events that mysteriously coincide. They can hold that:

- the phenomenal report is the introspectively available aspect of the restructuring process;

- a shared metacognitive mechanism produces both the feeling and the cognitive transition;

- or access-conscious representation causes both behavioural revision and reports of vertigo.

The Map’s own “Consciousness and Cognitive Distinctiveness” article states this point much more accurately. It identifies phenomenal causation, accompaniment and common cause as three readings compatible with the available evidence, and explicitly concedes that comparative data do not defeat the latter two. The Unfinishable Map

“Categorical Surprise” should import that template. Calling alternatives a “systematic coincidence” rhetorically prejudges exactly what remains to be established.

### Third, phenomenology does not supply replacement content

Suppose vertigo is causally active as an alerting, attentional or motivational signal. It still does not follow that phenomenality constructs the new categories. At most it may:

- interrupt habitual processing;

- increase exploration;

- lower confidence in the current model;

- increase cognitive control;

- or motivate continued search.

All these functions have candidate computational descriptions. To establish specifically phenomenal causation, the article would need a dissociation in which phenomenal character varies while the relevant information-processing variables are held fixed, with resulting differences in restructuring success.

This caution is already present elsewhere on the site. “Creative Consciousness” explicitly says that whether felt qualities themselves do causal work, rather than accompanying neural processes that do, is a further claim the cited literature does not supply. The Unfinishable Map

## 5. The quantum connection is appended rather than derived

The article says categorical surprise is where habitual neural processing fails most completely and therefore where “genuinely novel state-selection” and forward-in-time conscious quantum selection matter most. The Unfinishable Map

No bridge is supplied between these propositions. Failure of an existing cognitive model does not imply:

- increased quantum indeterminacy;

- increased causal openness;

- recruitment of a special quantum-sensitive neural substrate;

- or an opportunity for consciousness to choose a useful new representation.

Indeed, selection among physically available states does not by itself solve the content-generation problem. If the brain must already generate the useful candidate states before consciousness selects among them, the original question—where the new representational options come from—returns at the menu-generation stage.

The linked “Consciousness as Intelligence Amplifier” article is more candid: it states that no complete quantum-to-macroscopic amplification chain has been experimentally validated. The Unfinishable Map

The quantum paragraph should therefore be marked as a downstream speculative application of the Map’s mechanism, not as support for the categorical-surprise thesis. At present, one speculative claim is being used to give another speculative claim mechanistic substance.

## 6. Tenet-protective bracketing

The article is unusually transparent about its framework commitments, which is a strength. Transparency, however, does not turn a commitment into a rebuttal.

### Regress parity

The objection is that nonphysical consciousness faces the same representational regress as a physical system. The answer—consciousness “experiences” rather than solves the regress—does not address the challenge to content generation or causal efficacy. It preserves the phenomenological claim while conceding the functional parity. The Unfinishable Map

### Bidirectional interaction

The article concedes that its own evidence does not settle whether phenomenality causes restructuring, then says the issue is settled, if anywhere, by the site’s prior commitment to Bidirectional Interaction. That is legitimate as a statement of framework location, but it means this article supplies no new evidence for bidirectional causation. The Unfinishable Map

### Minimal quantum interaction

Quantum selection is imported after the computational argument rather than independently motivated by it. The article does not establish that categorical restructuring requires, predicts or reveals quantum intervention. The Unfinishable Map

### Limits of Occam’s razor

The article explicitly states that reduction is the more parsimonious account and that the Map declines it because Tenet 5 licenses doing so. This is admirably candid, but it is not an argument against the reduction. A tenet that permits the site to disregard parsimony cannot by itself provide positive reason to believe in a categorical boundary. The Unfinishable Map

### The review system formalises the bracketing

The seventh deep review classifies the regress response as “framework-boundary marking.” More concerningly, it says the continuity objection, regress-parity objection and Buddhist no-self objection are inherited “bedrock disagreements” that should not be re-flagged as fixable flaws. The Unfinishable Map

That procedure risks converting unresolved objections into protected premises. A referee may agree that some disagreements cannot be settled inside the Map’s tenets, but they should remain visible in evaluations of external validity. “Not fixable without changing the framework” is not the same as “answered.”

## 7. Important counterarguments are missing or too quickly dismissed

### The level-relativity objection

A representation can be outside one task-level hypothesis class while remaining generable by a higher-level grammar, program-induction system or meta-learning process. The article repeatedly moves from “not in the current repertoire” to “not computationally generable” without defending that transition.

### The local-repair objection

Framework revision need not involve mapping every representational boundary. Detecting and replacing one assumption can yield far-reaching downstream reorganisation.

### The bootstrapping objection

New concepts may be assembled compositionally from existing primitives and then cached as new primitives. The resulting concept may not have been explicitly enumerated in advance even though its construction was enabled by prior generative resources. Zhao and colleagues provide a direct computational example. Nature+1

### The retrospective-recognition objection

The report “I did not know this was possible” may be made only after the new representation becomes available. The pre-insight state may consist merely of uncertainty, failed search or conflict. The apparent experience of one’s previous representational boundary may therefore be partly retrospective interpretation.

### The unconscious-preparation objection

A conscious click may mark the arrival of a representation generated through earlier unconscious processing, rather than consciousness generating the representation. The article’s phenomenal chronology does not discriminate these possibilities. Contemporary insight research treats restructuring and Aha phenomenology as separable components precisely because their relation remains unsettled. Springer

### The false-Aha objection

A strong feeling of insight or certainty can accompany an incorrect solution. Unless correctness and transfer are measured independently, phenomenal force cannot establish genuine framework transcendence.

### The enactivist and scaffolded-cognition objection

Kuhnian conceptual change is not merely an isolated mind stepping outside itself. It can be distributed across experimental instruments, notation, language, collaborators and accumulated anomalies. The article cites Kuhn’s social-scale scientific revolutions and then transfers their structure to individual phenomenology without examining the role of those external scaffolds. Kuhn’s chapter X is indeed about revolutions as changes of world view, but this historical account does not establish that phenomenal consciousness causes an individual’s conceptual restructuring. University of Chicago Press+1

### The open-world objection

A system can represent “unknown type,” reserve probability mass for novelty, detect distribution shift, or initiate exploratory search without already possessing the final category. That may be less dramatic than the article’s target case, but it blocks the inference from absence of a specific prior category to absence of any computationally usable response.

## 8. Falsifiability is welcome but presently too elastic

The article’s falsifier section is one of its better features. It names computational restructuring, continuous phenomenology and apparently nonconscious artificial categorical surprise as possible challenges. The Unfinishable Map

The criteria nevertheless permit substantial goalpost movement.

A computational system that changes its internal ontology can always be described as remaining within a still larger fixed meta-space. Unless the relevant representational level is specified in advance, no implementation could count as success. Conversely, human conceptual change is not subjected to the same demand: the article does not show that humans generate concepts outside every pre-existing higher-order resource.

Likewise, “without anything plausibly described as consciousness” makes the AI test dependent on the Map’s prior consciousness criteria. A sufficiently capable system could be declared conscious precisely because it passed the test, preventing the result from falsifying the proposed link.

The phenomenological falsifier also sets an unnecessarily high bar. The relevant alternative need not show “no qualitative discontinuity” in every case. Evidence of continuous distributions, mixed cases, gradual restructuring and dissociation between surprise and restructuring already weakens the claim that categorical surprise is a single natural kind. Such evidence exists. Springer+1

Finally, “None of these findings currently exists” is too categorical. Computational accounts of insight and conceptual bootstrapping existed before September 2026, while the 17 September active-inference model is now an especially direct partial challenge. The article can reasonably argue that none demonstrates unrestricted ontology invention, but it can no longer say that no relevant computational restructuring result exists. UCL Discovery+2Nature+2

## 9. Citation audit

### Chalmers 1996 — bibliographically sound, inferentially limited

The 1996 date for the original Oxford publication of The Conscious Mind is defensible. Chalmers supports the general explanatory-gap argument: a functional description does not by itself explain why a process is accompanied by experience. He does not establish that representational restructuring is noncomputable or that phenomenality causes it. The reference therefore supports the article’s modest qualitative-gap point, not its structural-computational conclusion. Google Books

### Friston 2010 — accurate but insufficient and stale for the claim made

The article details—Nature Reviews Neuroscience 11(2), 127–138—are correct. But this is a broad review of the free-energy principle, not the strongest relevant source on insight or structure learning. The omission of Friston et al. 2017 is particularly serious because that later paper formally addresses curiosity, insight, model selection and structure learning. PubMed+1

### Gödel 1931 — bibliographically correct, philosophically overextended

The journal, volume and page range are correct. The problem is not attribution but entailment: incompleteness does not show that a computational learner cannot change representational schemes, extend its language or move to a stronger system. Springer

### Kuhn 1962 — accurate reference, analogical rather than evidential support

The publication date and the reference to chapter X, “Revolutions as Changes of World View,” are appropriate. Kuhn supports the description of scientific revolutions as changes in what scientists take the world to contain. He does not provide evidence that the associated phenomenology performs causal cognitive work, and his unit of analysis is substantially social and historical. University of Chicago Press

### Tarski — definite bibliographic error and overbroad formulation

The article cites:

Tarski, A. (1936). “The Concept of Truth in Formalized Languages.”

That year is wrong for that work. Tarski’s Polish truth monograph appeared in 1933, the German version in 1935, and the English translation appeared in the 1956 collection. Tarski’s 1936 publication was the distinct paper on the concept of logical consequence or “following logically.” McMaster Experts+3Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+3Google Books+3

The accompanying theorem statement is also too broad. Tarski does not show that every formal language simply cannot define truth. The result concerns sufficiently expressive languages under particular adequacy conditions; Tarski also constructed truth definitions for various formal languages using stronger metalanguages. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+1

This error is methodologically significant because the September deep review explicitly carried forward a citation ledger classifying “Tarski, A. (1936), ‘The Concept of Truth in Formalized Languages’” as “real-correct.” The Unfinishable Map

## 10. Integration with linked articles

The article has extensive internal linking, but the links currently produce more framework reinforcement than evidential integration.

### “Surprise, Prediction Error, and Consciousness”

The parent article makes even stronger claims: that genuine surprise requires consciousness, that wherever there is real surprise there is experience, and that the surprise spectrum contains discontinuities quantitative models cannot explain. Its categorical-surprise section repeats the outside-vantage argument without the target article’s later concessions. The Unfinishable Map+1

This parent now needs to be brought into line with the more calibrated target article—and both need the empirical insight literature.

### “Predictive Processing”

The linked overview correctly says that predictive processing, by itself, need not make claims about subjective experience. But it largely treats PP as a fixed-model prediction-error framework and does not incorporate the most relevant active-inference work on insight and structure learning. The Unfinishable Map

Its later move from precision weighting to quantum selection similarly goes beyond the evidence: the fact that a computational description does not settle phenomenality does not imply that quantum selection supplies the missing attentional or agential mechanism. The Unfinishable Map

### “Consciousness and Cognitive Distinctiveness”

This is the best-calibrated neighbouring article. Its three-way distinction among phenomenal causation, accompaniment and common cause should be imported almost verbatim as a methodological template. It also states plainly that the site’s preference for phenomenal causation follows from its bidirectional-interaction commitment rather than being forced by comparative evidence. The Unfinishable Map

### “Creative Consciousness”

This article recognises that failed third-person discrimination is equally compatible with a phenomenal difference and with there being no underlying difference to detect. It also concedes that the literature does not establish that felt qualities themselves perform the causal work. “Categorical Surprise” is materially less cautious when it says the phenomenal signal enables restructuring. The Unfinishable Map+1

### “Consciousness as Intelligence Amplifier”

The amplifier article appropriately labels its AI implication a hypothesis rather than proof and acknowledges the lack of an experimentally validated quantum-to-macro chain. “Categorical Surprise” should inherit both qualifications rather than presenting framework transcendence as the place where quantum selection “matters most.” The Unfinishable Map

### “Consciousness-Selecting Neural Patterns”

This page treats selection as an unsettled proposal and acknowledges live objections concerning randomness and causal interpretation. Linking to it can show where the Map would locate a mechanism conditional on the tenets, but it cannot provide independent evidence that categorical surprise recruits that mechanism. The Unfinishable Map

### P-D1

The explicit acknowledgement that categorical surprise belongs to the same premise-sharing cluster as Mary, zombies and the explanatory gap is excellent. It prevents the article from being counted as independent cumulative evidence for dualism. That calibration should be made more prominent: the article is best understood as a phenomenologically vivid application of an existing anti-physicalist argument, not as a new formal proof. The Unfinishable Map

## 11. Assessment of the changelog and review methodology

The public changelog is a substantial strength. It exposes corrections, dependency problems and failed verifications that most AI-assisted publishing workflows would leave invisible. The seventh review of “Categorical Surprise” also caught a genuine contradiction introduced by a recent calibration pass: one passage conceded computational detection of global misfit while another continued to deny it. The Unfinishable Map

The same records nevertheless demonstrate a systematic risk: repeated internal reviews are not independent when they inherit the same citation ledgers, framework assumptions and earlier conclusions.

For this article:

- six previous reviews did not prevent the central contradiction;

- the seventh review incorrectly marked the Tarski citation “real-correct”;

- the formal-theorem-to-restructuring step was left as a merely “watched seam”;

- and major objections were classified as bedrock disagreements that future reviews should not re-flag. The Unfinishable Map+1

The broader changelog records the same failure mode elsewhere. One six-review chain converted an unverified purported quotation into a page-number issue and repeatedly treated the attribution as sound until an independent full-text check established that the quotation was fabricated. The Unfinishable Map

Another page had been declared converged after nine reviews even though its upstream tenet dependencies had changed more than eighteen times, leaving three high-severity inconsistencies stranded in the supposedly stable article. The Unfinishable Map

The methodological problem is therefore not AI generation as such. It is correlated review under shared priors:

- later models inherit earlier models’ classifications;

- “convergence” suppresses fresh scrutiny;

- citation checks sometimes establish existence rather than source-to-claim entailment;

- and tenet-level objections can be administratively removed from the defect queue.

A genuinely external review must remain able to say that a framework commitment leaves an objection unanswered, even when changing the answer would require changing the framework.

## Final verdict

“Categorical Surprise” contains a worthwhile philosophical intuition: some encounters appear to occasion more than ordinary parameter correction, and the phenomenology of disorientation and subsequent understanding deserves focused study. The September revision has improved the article by separating anomaly detection from replacement-framework construction and by admitting that phenomenology does not independently prove causation.

Its present argument nevertheless turns an open research problem into an impossibility claim. It does not establish a discrete phenomenal kind, does not engage the principal computational accounts of insight and conceptual bootstrapping, misuses incompleteness results to impose an unnecessary complete-boundary-mapping requirement, and then relies on the Map’s tenets to bridge from distinctive experience to nonphysical causal intervention.

The article should be retained, but repositioned as a research hypothesis and discriminating-problem specification, not as evidence that computational restructuring is structurally impossible.

## Concrete potential improvements

- Revise “Categorical Surprise” into three explicitly separated theses:
Phenomenological thesis: some framework failures feel distinct from ordinary surprise.
Functional thesis: some learning episodes modify representational dimensions or primitives rather than parameters.
Dualist thesis: phenomenality causally contributes to that modification.
Give each thesis its own evidence and confidence level; do not allow support for the first to flow automatically into the third.

- Operationalise “outside the possibility space.” Specify whether this means outside the active model repertoire, task-level hypothesis class, explicit concept vocabulary, generative grammar or total architecture. State in advance which level a computational counterexample must change.

- Replace the binary ordinary/categorical taxonomy with a provisional multidimensional model. Include representational distance, suddenness, subjective surprise, metacognitive uncertainty, correctness, degree of retrospective reinterpretation and transfer to new tasks. Treat a discrete categorical subtype as an empirical possibility rather than an established fact. Springer+2Springer+2

- Add a serious rival-model section to “Categorical Surprise.” It should engage Friston et al. 2017 on active inference and insight, Zhao et al. 2024 on conceptual bootstrapping, and Doulfoukar, Pezzulo and Stuyck 2026 on computational Aha dynamics. Explain precisely what each model achieves and what, if anything, remains outside its scope. UCL Discovery+2Nature+2

- Remove the claim that restructuring requires a complete map of representational boundaries, unless a formal argument can be supplied. Replace it with the weaker and defensible question of how a learner generates new representational primitives after local model failure.

- Demote Gödel and Tarski from supporting premises to a boxed cautionary analogy. State that incompleteness limits exhaustive formal self-characterisation but does not establish noncomputability of concept learning or framework revision.

- Correct the Tarski reference to something such as “Tarski 1933/1935, English translation 1956,” and distinguish it from the separate 1936 logical-consequence paper. Update the September deep-review record that incorrectly marked the current entry “real-correct.” Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+2Google Books+2

- Withdraw or qualify “the phenomenal signal enables the reorganisation.” Replace it with a set of competing possibilities: causal trigger, motivational amplifier, metacognitive readout, accompaniment or common effect. Identify evidence that would distinguish them.

- Import the three-reading structure from “Consciousness and Cognitive Distinctiveness.” Make phenomenal causation, accompaniment and common cause the standard analysis whenever a Map article infers causal efficacy from a phenomenology–performance correlation. The Unfinishable Map

- Rewrite the “systematic coincidence” argument. Acknowledge that identity, realisation and common-cause theories do not posit an accidental correlation between independent events. The Map may reject those theories, but it must argue against them rather than characterise them as unexplained coincidence.

- Move the quantum-selection discussion into a clearly labelled speculative subsection. State that categorical surprise neither demonstrates quantum involvement nor identifies a neural locus of causal openness. Require a separate bridge theory connecting representational restructuring to the proposed quantum interface.

- Revise “Surprise, Prediction Error, and Consciousness.” Remove or qualify the universal claim that wherever there is genuine surprise there is consciousness, and replace its stronger outside-vantage argument with the target article’s detection-versus-repair distinction. Add the multidimensional Aha and metacognitive-prediction-error literature. The Unfinishable Map+2The Unfinishable Map+2

- Update “Predictive Processing.” Add the active-inference literature on curiosity, insight, structure learning and Bayesian model reduction. Distinguish basic predictive coding from the broader family of hierarchical, structure-learning and meta-learning models rather than treating PP primarily as parameter updating. UCL Discovery+1

- Reconcile “Creative Consciousness” with “Categorical Surprise.” The former correctly concedes that existing literature does not show felt qualities themselves doing causal work; the latter should not assert that causal contribution without new evidence. The Unfinishable Map

- Revise “Consciousness as Intelligence Amplifier” and “Consciousness-Selecting Neural Patterns” to mark downstream dependency. Links from categorical surprise should state that these pages develop conditional mechanisms assuming bidirectional interaction; they do not independently support the premise that phenomenal experience causes restructuring.

- Replace citation-existence checking with claim-level entailment checking. For every externally checkable sentence, record the exact source passage, whether it supports the full claim or only part of it, and whether the claim is empirical, philosophical, analogical or framework-dependent.

- Introduce expiry dates for negative-existence claims. Statements such as “no computational account is on offer” and “none of these findings currently exists” should automatically return to the review queue after 60–90 days and after any relevant new publication.

- Make review convergence dependency-sensitive. A page should lose converged status when linked tenets, positions or mechanism articles change materially. The changelog already demonstrates that static-file convergence misses upstream conceptual drift. The Unfinishable Map

- Do not exempt “bedrock” objections from future external review. Tag them as framework-level disagreements, but continue testing whether later articles accidentally present framework-relative conclusions as externally established results.
