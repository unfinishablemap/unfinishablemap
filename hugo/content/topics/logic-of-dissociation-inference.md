When brain damage abolishes one capacity and spares another, and a second patient shows the reverse, neuropsychology has a double dissociation. The Unfinishable Map leans on such patterns across a cluster of articles, reading the syndromes as a map of the mind-brain interface's channels. This article examines the step those articles take before any question of interface versus production arises: what a dissociation licenses about mental architecture at all.

The methodological debate since Caramazza (1986) supports a graded answer, set out as [three tiers](#three-tiers) (explained below). Dissociations robustly show that capacities can fail separately. They support a functional distinction only as an inference to the best explanation, against the rival models that have actually been tried. Reading off discrete components or channels needs further [bridge assumptions](#bridge-assumptions) (explained below), such as transparency, locality and subtractivity, which are disputed and are weakest for measures that depend on reports of awareness.

The result calibrates the Map's own inference and argues neither against dualism nor for it. The interface reading and its functionalist rival both presuppose the step to components, so neither gains here; what changes is the grade of the channel vocabulary. The Map's fifth tenet, that simplicity misleads when knowledge is incomplete, applies with particular force, because one of the field's leading methodologists, Tim Shallice, called the isolable-subsystem reading "a simple explanation of the findings" (Shallice 1991).

## The Classical Inference and Its Bridge Assumptions {#bridge-assumptions}

A single dissociation, task A impaired and B spared, may only mean that A is harder. The classical remedy, usually credited to Teuber (1955), is the double dissociation: if another patient shows the reverse, difficulty alone cannot explain both.

Shallice (1991) also separates *classical* dissociations, where the spared task is performed within the normal range, from *strong* ones, where the better task "is still below the normal range even though the contrast between the better and worse performed tasks is striking". The double-dissociation criterion presupposes comparable measures, with each task tested in both patients. The blindsight–neglect pairing that the Map's [dissociation hub](/topics/neurological-dissociations-as-interface-architecture/) treats as a double dissociation contrasts two syndromes that both lose awareness, rather than crossing two tasks over patients on one scale, and so falls short of it.

Caramazza (1986) argued that "given certain assumptions, only the single-case method allows valid inferences about the structure of cognitive systems from the analysis of impaired performance". Coltheart (2017) names those assumptions "fractionation, modularity, transparency and universality", and Shallice (2015) calls them "the bridge principles" for such inferences. Roughly: damage can impair parts of the system selectively; the system has separable parts; impaired performance reveals which part was damaged; and the architecture is shared across people.

Transparency is the crux. Shallice (1991) writes that dissociations can generate theory "only if the relation between damage to a subcomponent and impaired task performance is fairly transparent". Two related assumptions travel with it. Farah (1994) names *locality*: neuropsychologists "generally assume that the effects of brain damage are local, that is, that the nondamaged components of the architecture continue to function as they did before the damage". *Subtractivity*, which Shallice calls "the subtraction approach", treats impaired performance as the normal system minus its damaged parts; Shallice grants that "certain observed impairments cannot be understood merely by subtracting one or more components from the normal system".

The classical position never treated these assumptions as guaranteed. Shallice, defending the method, wrote that "The presence of dissociations in no way entails the existence of isolable subsystems". "Dissociations can arise from damage to other types of architecture," he went on, naming continuous processing spaces, overlapping processing regions and coupled systems, and they "can also occur from impairments to different levels or modes of operation of the same subsystem".

## Selective Deficits Without Separable Components

Plaut (1995) damaged a connectionist network that pronounces words via meaning "and yet has no separable components", and obtained a double dissociation between concrete and abstract word reading. The network does have functional specialisation, but the specialisation behind the dissociation "is not transparently related to the network's structure, as modular theories assume". Plaut presents this as a violation of Caramazza's transparency assumption. Lesions of the same site and severity also produced different outcomes, which "raises specific concerns about the interpretation of single-case studies".

Plaut's conclusion is limited. The dissociation still does "throw useful light" on the network's organisation, he holds, borrowing Shallice's phrase, given assumptions about its computational principles. What fails is the default: "cognitive neuropsychologists cannot assume that the cognitive system must be modular in order to account for observed dissociations among brain-damaged patients."

Bullinaria and Chater (1995) found that such effects can depend on scale. Their small networks trained on rules and exceptions showed double dissociation without modularity, but in "sufficiently larger and more distributed networks ... double dissociations are not observed", so their small-network cases "are merely artefacts of scale". In that context, they conclude, connectionism "appears to create no additional problems for the traditional neuropsychological inference." Whether demonstrations of Plaut's kind survive at realistic scale remains disputed.

Other single-system routes need no network. Dunn and Kirsner (1988) showed that "it is possible to posit single processes that mimic both single and double dissociation", and proposed *reversed association* as a sounder test. Chater (2003) gave three constructions. Two tasks may share all machinery except one tiny component each, like a prawn allergy and a peanut allergy in one digestive system. They may share components used to different degrees, as when javelin and shot-put blisters fall on different parts of one hand. Or they may use identical components with different backup strategies, so that "the double dissociation may arise purely from the differences between backup strategies".

Chater concludes that, "At this high level of generality", double dissociation "cannot be used as an inference principle for uncovering the modularity of the cognitive system". He keeps a role for the data: a single dissociation "may be a powerful constraint" when "whole classes of cognitive model cannot reproduce that single dissociation". Van Orden, Pennington and Stone (2001) press harder. Citing Shallice (1988), they hold that the practice of revealing dissociations "is predicated on modularity being true", and they question the utility of assuming it: in reading research, modularity's failure to converge perpetuates "unending fractionation into more modules".

Richard Gregory, whom Shallice cites for the transparency worry, states it with a radio analogy, the image the Map's hub uses for the opposite conclusion: "If you've got a radio and you take part of it out, remove it, the radio might howl ... but it doesn't mean that that part in the radio was a suppressor for howling." Removing a part leaves "a modified total system" (Gregory 2006), though Gregory still grants that localisation is "very, very broadly" correct. On a simulated microprocessor, Jonas and Kording (2017) found transistors whose removal abolished exactly one game, yet "a given transistor is obviously not specialized for Donkey Kong or Space Invaders".

## Non-Local and Non-Subtractive Lesion Effects

Farah (1994) argued that the locality assumption "is probably not correct in general", and that inferences can proceed instead on the assumption that processing "is graded, distributed, and interactive". In her three worked cases, among them the selective loss of knowledge about living things, the inferences become more plausible once locality is dropped.

Anatomy points the same way. The term *diaschisis* was "coined by von Monakow in 1914" for changes distant from a focal lesion, and Carrera and Tononi (2014) report that its connectional form, altered connectivity between regions remote from the lesion, "seems to relate more consistently to the clinical findings" than the focal form, especially after stroke in motor and attentional networks. Boes et al. (2015) found that "heterogeneous lesions producing similar symptoms share functional connectivity to specific brain regions involved in symptom expression", so a symptom can localise to a network rather than to the damaged site. The specificity of their lesion network mapping is now disputed. Van den Heuvel et al. (2026) argue that it maps local changes onto "the same nonspecific properties" of the normative connectivity data; Boes, Fox and colleagues reply, in a preprint, that specificity testing supports it (Siddiqi et al. 2026). The diaschisis evidence above predates the method and does not rest on it.

Degeneracy cuts the other way. Where several sufficient systems can each perform a task, Price and Friston (2002) note, the redundancy cannot be detected in patients "because their performance might not be impaired when only one degenerate system is damaged". The absence of a dissociation therefore does not show the absence of separate components.

A third family of models changes what a lesion is taken to be. Parr, Rees and Friston (2018) recast syndromes within Bayesian accounts of brain function: "neuropsychological deficits can be thought of as false inferences that arise due to aberrant prior beliefs (that are poor fits to the real world)". They relate their taxonomy of pathological priors to failures of neuromodulation and to disconnection syndromes. On this view a lesion alters a parameter of a hierarchical model rather than removing a box. Fotopoulou (2014) applies the approach to anosognosia and calls for "a dynamic, non-modular neuropsychology". Both are physicalist programmes. They enter here only as models of what damage does; predictive processing as a rival to dualism is the subject of [predictive-processing-and-dualism](/topics/predictive-processing-and-dualism/).

## The Defence: Abduction, Scale and Comparative Standing

The method's defenders concede the logical point and contest the practical one. Shallice (1991) knew "of no area in which inferences from neuropsychological dissociations were at one time in conflict with those from the normal literature and were later shown to be artifactual". He names three lines of inquiry that could in principle choose among architectures (how syndromes relate to one another, how strong individual dissociations are, and how they map onto experiments with normal subjects), though, to his knowledge, none had yet been tried for that purpose. Classical dissociations also resist the worry that a reorganised system is being studied, since "there is no reason a reorganised system should produce the same quantitative level of performance as the original one".

Coltheart (2017) reviews Caramazza's four assumptions and concludes that "all four remain defensible today". Machery (2012) compares dissociation inferences with the function-to-structure inferences of cognitive neuroscience and argues that the function-to-structure inferences "have a poorer epistemic standing". Davies (2010) states the inference's status plainly: "arguments from double dissociation to separate modules work by inference to the best, not the only possible, explanation". Connectionism, he argues, widened the range of candidate explanations, and, given assumptions that go beyond cognitive psychology, lesion-location findings "may enhance the value of double dissociation in shifting the balance of support between cognitive theories".

None of the sources reviewed here holds that a dissociation by itself entails a component. Caramazza's validity claim is conditional on his assumptions, Shallice denies entailment, and Davies makes the step an inference to the best explanation. The live disagreement concerns how strong the case is in a given instance, which depends on which rivals have actually been modelled.

## Three Tiers of What a Dissociation Licenses {#three-tiers}

The following tiers are the Map's construction from these sources, not a scheme any one author proposes.

1. **Differential vulnerability (observed).** Capacity A can be lost while B is spared, and in another patient the reverse. This tier needs only good measurement, and it is strongest for classical dissociations on comparable measures. Most of the Map's clinical cases earn it.
2. **Functional differentiation (abductive, model-relative).** No member of a specified class of models can produce the pattern without some functional distinction. The tier is earned to the extent that rivals have been modelled and have failed: a single process with non-linear performance, a resource or difficulty artefact, a criterion shift, a backup strategy, degeneracy, diaschisis, or a change of prior or precision. Where no rival has been tried, the tier is asserted rather than earned.
3. **Discrete components and channels (bridge assumptions required).** Reading a dissociation as the loss of one part needs transparency, locality, subtractivity and universality. Every claim about a "channel" sits at this tier.

The interface-versus-production question sits above tier three. The hub article's verdict that both readings "predict the same dissociation phenomenology" stands, but both readings presuppose tier three. Where tier three is not earned, a channel table is one admissible decomposition among several. The Map's [four-level chain](/apex/phenomenal-output-causal-machinery-dissociation/), which separates separability from mechanistic independence and grants that functional accounts supply the latter within one substrate, marks the same gap; the tiers give the argument for it.

None of this makes dissociations uninformative. Tier one is untouched, and it is what most arguments from dissociation need: experience depends on differentiated brain function. A robust double dissociation in a large system may still indicate some separation (Bullinaria and Chater 1995), and because of degeneracy a failure to find a dissociation does not show unity.

## Why Consciousness Dissociations Sit Lowest

The measures that matter most to the Map are the weakest at tier three. Summarising the last five chapters of his book, on knowledge, visual attention, supervisory operations, memory and consciousness, Shallice (1991) writes: "On the whole, the methodological scruples of the earlier sections are abandoned, and a dissociation is assumed to represent the operation of a damaged subsystem." He then offers "a functionalist account" of findings such as blindsight, aspects of prosopagnosia and the split-brain syndrome. A leading defender of the method thus marks these higher-level domains, consciousness among them, as where it runs without its safeguards.

Awareness is measured by report, and report brings decision criteria and confidence into the measurement. Phillips (2021) argues that blindsight is degraded conscious vision left unacknowledged "due to conservative response biases", so that it "does not reveal any dissociation between performance and awareness". Michel and Lau (2021) reply that in some cases awareness is "clearly impaired relative to the corresponding visual sensitivity". Whichever side is right, the dispute shows how a criterion shift can create or remove an apparent dissociation of awareness; the [blindsight](/concepts/blindsight/) article weighs the case. Most of the cluster's consciousness dissociations, from anosognosia to the split brain, likewise rest on what patients report. In [tonic immobility](/topics/tonic-immobility-and-awareness/#collection) the motor side is checked against body sway, while the awareness beside it rests on report given minutes to decades later.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)**: The dissociation literature engages this tenet most directly. Shallice called the isolable-subsystem reading "a simple explanation of the findings"; Van Orden and colleagues argue that modular decomposition fails to converge; Davies places the inference among inferences to the best explanation, judged against "the criteria of probability and explanatory value", and on the Map's reading simplicity enters through those judgements. The tenet holds that simplicity misleads when knowledge is incomplete, as knowledge of how a damaged brain reorganises plainly is; applied to the Map's own evidence, it counsels against reading channels off syndromes. A framework that invokes Occam's limits against physicalist [parsimony](/concepts/parsimony-epistemology/) owes the same caution to a parsimony inference that happens to favour its interface vocabulary. Ruling out a one-channel model is the kind of tier-two claim the literature supports; a count of six channels is a tier-three claim.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)**: The dissociation evidence never discriminated dualism from functionalism, as the hub article concedes, so the Map's compatibility-grade verdict for dualism is unaffected; the addition here is that the premise both readings share is itself abductive. If lesion effects are non-local, as Farah argues and Gregory's "modified total system" suggests, then a lesion changes the whole brain-side input to consciousness rather than cutting one line while the rest of the signal continues. The [filter-theory](/concepts/filter-theory/) gloss in [disconnection-neuroscience](/concepts/disconnection-neuroscience/), "the channel is broken, the broadcast is intact", is a locality claim made across the interface, and it inherits that assumption's contested standing.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)**: The ascending/descending split in the Map's channel table is a tier-three decomposition, one the tenet is compatible with rather than one the clinic delivers. The case for descending causation rests elsewhere, chiefly on [motor selection](/concepts/motor-selection/), as the hub article says.

**[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction)**: A claim that the interface's components are finite, enumerable and individually disableable presupposes transparency and locality, so it sits at tier three. Dissociation evidence alone cannot fix how far the interface extends.
