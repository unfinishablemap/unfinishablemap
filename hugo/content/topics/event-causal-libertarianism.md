Libertarianism about free will is a genus with three species. All libertarians share two commitments—determinism is false, and some agents sometimes act freely and are morally responsible—and divide over a third question: what role does indeterminism play, and where does the freedom-grounding source of the action sit? The standard taxonomy, canonised in the Stanford Encyclopedia's "Incompatibilist (Nondeterministic) Theories of Free Will" and in the survey chapters of Kane's *Oxford Handbook of Free Will* (2002; 2nd ed. 2011), sorts libertarians into **event-causal** (free actions are nondeterministically caused by agent-involving mental *events*, with no agent-as-substance cause added), **agent-causal** (the agent, an irreducible substance, is a cause over and above any event), and **non-causal** (free actions are basic and uncaused). This survey gives the event-causal wing—Robert Kane's self-forming actions above all—the detailed treatment it deserves, then follows the **luck** and **disappearing-agent** objection that bears hardest on it to locate the Map among the options.

The taxonomy is shared-ground philosophy of action. It carries no commitment to dualism, and nothing in the three-way carving favours the Map's view. The Map occupies one position within the taxonomy (broadly agent-causal, developed at [agent-causation](/concepts/agent-causation/)), and its claim that event-causal libertarianism succumbs to luck is a *framework-relative* argument, not a proof. Readers should meet all three species on their own terms before that location is drawn.

## The Shared Ground

Every libertarian holds that determinism is false and that free will is real. Where they part company is the answer to: *what is the ultimate source of a free action, and what work does indeterminism do?* One useful frame runs across the three answers. Event-causal libertarianism tries to secure free will from indeterminism *without* invoking agent-causation. Agent-causal libertarianism holds that indeterminism among events is not enough, so an irreducible agent-cause is required. Non-causal libertarianism holds that a free action needs no positive causal story at all.

The division cross-cuts the more familiar [source-versus-leeway distinction](/concepts/source-versus-leeway-incompatibilism/). Source and leeway concern *what freedom requires* (ultimate origination, or open alternatives); the event/agent/non-causal split concerns *what does the causing* at the moment freedom is exercised. A theorist fixes a position on both maps.

## Event-Causal Libertarianism

On the event-causal view, a free action is nondeterministically caused by agent-involving mental *events*—the agent's having certain desires, beliefs, reasons, and intentions. The simplest such account differs from a good event-causal *[compatibilist](/concepts/compatibilism/)* account only by adding that the relevant causation is indeterministic. Crucially, the agent is not an additional cause over and above these events; the agent's bringing about a choice reduces to states and events involving the agent. This is the reductive, broadly naturalist wing, and its proponents include Robert Kane (as his earlier work was usually classed; see [below](#a-hybrid-at-the-seam)), Laura Ekstrom, Alfred Mele (as a defended-but-not-endorsed model), Mark Balaguer, and Christopher Franklin.

### Kane's Self-Forming Actions

Kane's model, developed in *The Significance of Free Will* (Oxford University Press, 1996) and refined in his later replies to the luck objection, locates the indeterminism at **self-forming actions** (SFAs)—the difficult, character-shaping moments when an agent is *torn* between competing visions of what to do or become: the moral course against ambition, present desire against a long-term goal. In these torn decisions the agent makes an indeterminate effort of will, and it is undetermined whether the effort succeeds. From 1999 until his final book Kane "doubled" the effort for cases of moral conflict: the agent makes *two simultaneous efforts*, one toward each option, so that whichever way the decision falls she succeeds at something she was actively trying to do. Because the indeterminism sits inside the agent's own effort, whichever way it resolves the outcome is something the agent willed and rationally endorsed.

Kane calls this **plural voluntary control**: the agent is such that, whichever option is chosen, the choice will be rational, voluntary, and the agent's own doing—endorsable either way. SFAs need not be frequent. Many free actions may flow deterministically from a character that earlier SFAs forged, so responsibility for ordinary actions traces back through the character to the undetermined self-forming ones. This is how Kane secures *sourcehood* on what is usually read as a purely event-causal budget: the agent is the ultimate source because the character doing the later determining was itself formed in undetermined efforts that were fully the agent's.

### Ekstrom's Coherentist Account

Laura Ekstrom's *Free Will: A Philosophical Study* (Westview Press, 2000) defends an indeterminist event-causal view built on a *coherence* theory of self-determined action, modelled on Keith Lehrer's coherence theory of knowledge. A *preference* is a desire formed by critical evaluation against the agent's conception of the good; preferences count as the agent's own when they cohere with her other preferences and acceptances, and the agent just *is* that coherent set together with the evaluative faculty that forms it—no irreducible agent-cause is added. The 2000 account is *deliberative*: the indeterminism sits in the formation of the preference, which then deterministically causes the decision. Ekstrom (2019) later criticised that placement and moved to a *centered* account, locating the indeterminism in the immediate causation of the decision itself—the same placement Kane and Balaguer use—so that the agent's control is exercised at the moment of choice rather than upstream of it.

### Balaguer's Naturalist Version

Mark Balaguer argues that whether we have libertarian free will reduces to an *open scientific question* about the causal histories of certain neural events. His "torn decisions" do not require Kane-style competing efforts of will; what matters is whether the neural process realising the decision is genuinely undetermined in the right way. On this reading libertarianism becomes an empirical hypothesis about the brain rather than a metaphysical posit, which distinguishes Balaguer's minimalism from Kane's richer psychology of effort.

### A Hybrid at the Seam

Kane's mature view sits at the seam of the taxonomy, and his final book counts it as a fourth species. He now rejects the event-causal *label*, insisting that his theory involves no event-causal *reduction* of agency, and the Stanford Encyclopedia's authors note that it is unclear whether he denies that agency is always fully realised in causation by events. *The Complex Tapestry of Free Will* (2024) affirms agent and event causation together, "and neither is reducible to the other", in an "agent-causal/event-causal, or AC/EC, view" on which the agent is "an information-responsive complex dynamical system, exercising teleological guidance control" over some of its own processes. Kane wants the extra factor without an agent-*substance* of the classical kind, and he affirms agent causation of a naturalised kind: the agent "brings it about that the appropriate mental states or events cause the decision" (p. 156, quoted in Palmer 2025), which Palmer tentatively reads as Dretske's *structuring* causation, with the mental events as triggering causes.

Critics reply that luck survives the hybrid. Haji (2022) argues that Kane's combination of "non-traditional agent causation with indeterministic event causation" does not circumvent the luck objection. Palmer reports that the book also drops the doubled effort, agreeing with critics such as Clarke that trying to make two incompatible choices at once is practically irrational: the agent makes one effort, and if it fails no choice is made at that time. Following Haji, he finds the old chance worry in this revision too, since the effort could have failed and left no decision at all.

## Agent-Causal Libertarianism

Agent-causal libertarians (Roderick Chisholm and Timothy O'Connor, with Randolph Clarke as a guarded expositor) hold that a free action must be caused by the **agent as a substance**, not reducible to causation by prior events or mental states. Chisholm's founding distinction contrasts **transeunt** (event) causation with **immanent** (agent) causation; O'Connor's *Persons and Causes: The Metaphysics of Free Will* (Oxford University Press, 2000; ISBN 9780195133080) gives the leading modern defence, grounding agent-causal power in an emergent capacity of persons. The Map develops this wing—and its own place in it—at [agent-causation](/concepts/agent-causation/) and does not re-derive that content here; what matters for the taxonomy is that this species answers luck by making the agent, rather than a prior event, the settler of the outcome.

## Non-Causal Libertarianism

Non-causal libertarians (Carl Ginet, Hugh McCann, Stewart Goetz) hold that free actions need not be caused by anything—neither by events nor by an agent-substance. Freedom is a *basic*, uncaused feature of action. Ginet (1990) locates an action's activeness in an "actish phenomenal quality," the intrinsic way it seems to the agent that she is directly making the action happen; McCann stresses irreducible intrinsic intentionality. E. J. Lowe sits on this wing's border with the agent-causal one: he defends substance causation and a non-Cartesian substance dualism, but holds that free volitions are uncaused, spontaneous exercises of a rational power, so the Stanford Encyclopedia files his account with the non-causal theories (Lowe 2008). This is the least populated wing, largely because it struggles with two demands: how the agent exercises *active control* (an event with the right "actish" feel could be induced by direct brain stimulation, with no genuine agency), and how an action is done *for a reason* when reason-explanation looks causal. Standard agent-causal views face the second demand as well, since they too deny that the agent's reasons cause the free choice ([agent-causation-and-acting-for-reasons](/topics/agent-causation-and-acting-for-reasons/)).

## The Luck and Disappearing-Agent Objection

The **luck objection** bears hardest on the event-causal wing, and understanding why is the pivot of the whole taxonomy. If, at the moment of choice, it was genuinely undetermined which way the agent would choose, then imagine, with van Inwagen (2000), rolling the universe back to that instant and replaying it. In some replays she chooses A, in others B, with the *same* prior events. What accounts for the difference between the replays? Nothing about the agent—the prior events were identical. So the difference looks like a matter of chance, and chance does not ground responsibility.

Derk Pereboom's **disappearing-agent** formulation sharpens this into a general objection to *all* event-causal libertarianism. With the causal role of the antecedent events already given and yet the outcome undetermined, *nothing settles* whether the decision occurs—so the agent does not settle it. The agent "disappears" from the moment of settling. The objection bites the event-causal picture hardest because that picture, by design, admits no cause beyond the events; when the events leave the outcome open, the theory's own reductive commitment guarantees there is nothing left to do the settling.

Kane replies on two fronts. First, in a genuinely indeterminate effort of will, "exact sameness" across rollback worlds may not be well-defined, so the thought experiment can be accused of smuggling in a determinacy the model denies. Second, by plural rationality: whichever way the torn decision resolves, it issues from the agent's own effort and is rationally endorsable, so it is a willed self-formation rather than mere chance. Critics (Clarke, Mele) reply that an effort can make the resulting choice free only if the effort is itself free, which the model leaves unexplained, and that a choice *voluntary* either way still leaves unsettled *which* way it goes—that residue is the luck. The Map's own reply to luck—consciousness *selecting* the outcome rather than receiving it—is developed in full at [luck objection](/concepts/quantum-indeterminacy-free-will/) and is not repeated here; what the taxonomy adds is the map of *which* libertarian species the objection targets and why.

## The Intelligibility Objection

Agent-causal libertarianism answers the disappearing agent by making the agent the settler, but it pays for the answer with the **intelligibility objection**: can causation *by a substance*, rather than by an event, be made coherent at all? The orthodox view is that causation relates events; an enduring substance seems the wrong kind of relatum, and critics ask how an unchanging agent produces a change *at a particular time* without a triggering event—and if there is a triggering event, event-causation is back. Defenders reply with neo-Aristotelian substance causation as ontologically fundamental (Lowe, O'Connor, Jacobs and O'Connor) and with causal pluralism, on which substance and event causation are equally fundamental and interdependent, so that a change in the agent can accompany her causing without competing with it. Each wing, then, buys an answer to one objection at the cost of exposure to another: event-causal views are naturalistically respectable but vulnerable to luck; agent-causal views answer its disappearing-agent form but owe an account of substance causation. The rollback form is pressed against them too (van Inwagen 2000; Mele 2006): replay an agent's choice and ask what explains her causing this decision rather than the other, when everything before it was the same. Agent-causalists reply that the difference is the agent's exercising her free will differently; the Map treats that settling as a primitive whose modal structure is owed rather than supplied ([free-will](/topics/free-will/)). [The rollback argument](/concepts/rollback-argument-against-agent-causation/) sets out van Inwagen's replay, the replies to it, and why every reply the Map has found ends in that primitive.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Map locates itself in the **agent-causal** wing, and reads event-causal libertarianism as its principal *rival within libertarianism*. The two agree that determinism is false and free will real; they diverge over whether any irreducible agent-cause is needed. The Map's considered view is that the event-causal attempt to get free will out of indeterminism *without* agent-causation succumbs to the luck objection—the undetermined event is not settled by anyone—and that agent-causation, on the Map's substance-bearing reading, is required to close the gap. This is the Map's distinctive move, and it should be read as a framework-relative judgement rather than a demonstration: the taxonomy is neutral, and the strongest rival replies (Kane's indeterminate efforts and plural rationality, Franklin's (2011) rebuttal of four formulations of the luck argument) remain live for those who weight the naturalist commitments more heavily than the Map does. Kane's AC/EC view is a naturalistic rival on that last point: it claims to close the gap with agent causation of a naturalised kind and no agent-substance of the classical kind, and the Map takes a different step without claiming to have refuted it.

**Tenet 1 (Dualism)** enters at the agent-causal level, not at the taxonomy. As [agent-causation](/concepts/agent-causation/) records, the [Dualism tenet](/tenets/#dualism) is itself neutral between substance and property dualism; the substance-leaning that agent-causation needs is a commitment of the Map's framework, and a reader who accepts the tenet but rejects substance-bearing agents parts company at the agent-causal step. The event-causal wing is, from the Map's side, what dualism-neutral libertarianism looks like when it declines that step.

**Tenet 3 (Bidirectional Interaction)** is why the Map prefers the agent-causal wing, though the tenet alone does not exclude the event-causal one: it commits only to outcome-selection, which a conscious contribution fixed by prior mental states would also satisfy. Reading that selection as the agent *originating* the difference is the Map's further, agent-causal step ([where-the-substance-commitment-enters](/concepts/where-the-substance-commitment-enters/)). Non-causal libertarianism, by denying that the agent causes the action, gives the Map no causal purchase at all; event-causal libertarianism supplies causation but, on the Map's reading, leaves the crucial outcome unsettled by the agent. The Map's [quantum interface](/concepts/quantum-indeterminacy-free-will/) is the concrete proposal for *where and how* an agent-cause makes contact with the physical: a conscious selection at the interface is the agent's own settling of an otherwise-undetermined outcome, which is precisely the settling the disappearing-agent objection finds missing in the event-causal picture. This gives the Map part of an answer to the intelligibility objection too: it says where substance causation touches physics, though whether a substance can be a cause at all still rests on the substance-causation defences above, and the location is itself a hypothesis, not a proof.

**Tenet 5 (Occam's Razor Has Limits)** governs how the Map holds the position against Pereboom. Tenet 5 is no answer to the luck objection itself, which the Map presses against the event-causal wing above. What it governs is Pereboom's further claim that we have no positive reason to posit agent causation, a parsimony judgement the Map can decline to treat as decisive—while conceding, symmetrically, that declining buys the Map no positive support. His [wild-coincidences argument](/topics/the-manipulation-argument-and-hard-incompatibilism/) against agent causation is not a parsimony point and stays open. The Map adopts the agent-causal reading on independent grounds (its coherence with the other tenets), and the taxonomy's role is to name its neighbours and rivals, not to establish that its condition is met.
