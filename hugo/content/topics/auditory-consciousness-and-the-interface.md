Philosophy of perception is overwhelmingly a philosophy of vision. Colour, the inverted spectrum, blindsight, change blindness, the Molyneux problem—the canonical examples are visual, and the [Map's own treatment of perception](/topics/dualist-perception/) concedes that "while vision dominates philosophical discussions, perception extends across modalities." This article develops the auditory case, because hearing supplies four kinds of evidence the visual case lacks or only weakly displays: experience that constitutively unfolds in time rather than presenting a frozen scene, a perceptual organisation problem (auditory scene analysis) that isolates binding from spatial layout, qualities—pitch and timbre—that are vividly determinate yet appear to carry no intrinsic spatial character, and a dissociation literature (tinnitus, congenital amusia, auditory agnosia) in which each of those qualities comes apart from its usual moorings. None of these phenomena, on the reading offered here, *settles* dualism over physicalism, which has accounts of each. What they may do is sharpen what any theory must accommodate, and they suggest the Map's [interface](/concepts/mind-matter-interface/) reading extends cleanly beyond the visual paradigm that shaped it.

This piece is about audition *as a perceptual modality and interface case*. It is distinct from [the phenomenology of musical understanding](/topics/phenomenology-of-musical-understanding/), which concerns aesthetic normativity and non-propositional meaning in music specifically; here the subject is ordinary hearing—voices, footsteps, a kettle, a single sustained note.

## The Temporal Unfolding of Heard Sound

Vision presents a scene that is, to a first approximation, available all at once. You can fixate a static image and the visual content persists; you can pause and inspect. Sound has no static configuration to inspect. A heard melody, a spoken sentence, even a single tone, exists only as it unfolds. The [perception article](/topics/dualist-perception/) marks the contrast in one line—"an imagined melody can be paused where a heard one unfolds with perceptual authority"—and that line repays development.

The asymmetry it names is real. An imagined melody is under voluntary control: you can hold a note, skip ahead, replay a bar, or freeze the sequence at will. A *heard* melody arrives on the world's schedule. You cannot pause the trumpet; you cannot hold the third beat while you examine it; the sound is gone the instant it sounds and is replaced by the next. Hearing therefore presents its objects with what we might call temporal authority: the content is not yours to arrange. This is arguably one face of the broader [temporal structure of consciousness](/concepts/temporal-consciousness/)—the heard present seems to be a span, not an instant (William James's "specious present"), within which succession is itself experienced rather than, on this reading, merely inferred.

The physicalist has a ready account: auditory cortex implements temporal integration windows, echoic memory buffers the recent signal, and predictive models project the next event. Nothing here forces a dualist reading. What the auditory case *does* is make vivid that perceptual consciousness is not generically snapshot-like. A theory built entirely on the visual snapshot—consciousness as a rendered still image—will mis-describe hearing. The Map's interface framework, which treats conscious experience as constituted at the boundary between physical processing and phenomenal presentation, is not committed to snapshots; it can, on this reading, represent a presentation that is intrinsically durational. That is an accommodation, not a proof.

## Auditory Scene Analysis as a Binding Case

The [binding problem](/topics/the-binding-problem/) is usually posed visually: how do separately processed features—colour here, motion there, shape elsewhere—come to be experienced as one bound object? Vision lets the spatial layout do much of the work; features at the same retinal location are plausibly the same object. Audition strips that crutch away. Sounds from different sources arrive superimposed in a single pressure wave at each ear, and the auditory system must decompose that one signal into distinct perceived sources without a spatial map to sort them by.

This is the problem Albert Bregman named **auditory scene analysis** (Bregman, 1990). His framework distinguishes two grouping operations: *sequential* integration, which connects sounds over time into a single ongoing stream representing one source (the recurring melody line, one speaker's voice), and *simultaneous* integration, which partitions the spectral content arriving at one instant into concurrent sounds, each with its own qualities. These can compete: a frequency component may be heard either as the continuation of an earlier stream or as part of a concurrent sound. The "cocktail party" achievement—following one voice in a noisy room—is sequential streaming succeeding against competition.

Auditory scene analysis is valuable for the binding discussion precisely because the grouping cannot be read off a spatial scene. Frequency proximity, harmonicity, common onset, and continuity do the organising work, and the result is a perceived sound *object* that no single point in the signal corresponds to. Bregman's own account is computational and broadly physicalist; he did not argue for any dualist conclusion, and the streaming phenomena have been modelled successfully without one. The point for the Map is structural: scene analysis suggests that phenomenal grouping is not parasitic on spatial co-location, which would mean a theory of binding cannot reduce binding to "features at the same place." On the [capability-division](/concepts/capability-division-in-vision/) reading the Map applies to vision, the brain-side capabilities (frequency analysis, onset detection, harmonic grouping) may be taken to deliver organised candidate streams, while the experienced unity of *hearing one sound* appears as the mind-side contribution. The auditory case generalises that division beyond the visual modality where it was first stated—again, as an accommodation the framework permits, not a phenomenon that compels it.

## Non-Spatial Pitch and Timbre Qualia

Visual qualia are saturated with space. Colour is colour *of a region*; shape is spatial by definition. Auditory qualities are different. Sounds have pitch, timbre, and loudness, but—on a standard view in the philosophy of perception—these qualities "lack intrinsic spatial characteristics" (O'Callaghan, *Auditory Perception*, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy). We do localise sound sources, but localisation is computed from interaural timing and level differences; it is not carried within the pitch or timbre itself the way extension is carried within a colour patch. A pitch can rise or fall, but "up" and "down" here are not literal spatial directions—they are a metaphor laid over an ordering that is, at root, a matter of periodicity.

That computed localisation still has to arrive somewhere, and where it arrives is not an auditory map of its own but the single cross-modal arena described by the [3D world representation problem](/topics/three-dimensional-world-representation-problem/)—the arena in which a slamming door is heard from where the door is seen. Audition therefore occupies an odd position. Its qualities carry no intrinsic spatial character, yet its localisation output is bound into a spatial framework it does not itself supply. The non-spatiality claim concerns pitch and timbre; it leaves audition's contribution to the felt spatial world untouched.

Two features make auditory qualia especially clean test cases for the irreducibility of [qualia](/concepts/qualia/). First, the **missing fundamental**: a complex tone lacking any spectral energy at its fundamental frequency is still heard at that fundamental's pitch. The perceived quality does not correspond to a frequency present in the stimulus—pitch is computed, not read off. Second, **timbre** is the quality by which two sounds of identical pitch and loudness still differ—a violin and a flute on the same note. Timbre is multidimensional and resists reduction to any single physical parameter, yet it is immediately and determinately present in experience.

The physicalist treats all of this as more perceptual computation: periodicity extraction yields the missing-fundamental pitch; spectral-envelope and temporal-fine-structure analysis yields timbre. That account is live and well supported, and nothing in the missing fundamental tells against it. What the non-spatial auditory qualities arguably do is press the [explanatory gap](/concepts/explanatory-gap/) in a register vision cannot. It is no accident that the canonical statement of the gap—Nagel's question of what it is like to be a bat (Nagel, 1974)—reaches for an *auditory* creature whose world is built from echolocation; the felt character of a heard quality is where the "what it's like" pressure is most easily isolated from any external object-property to identify it with. With colour one can be tempted to locate the quality in the surface—to say yellowness just *is* a reflectance. Pitch and timbre seem to have no such external home: there is no "fundamental frequency" sitting in the world to identify the missing-fundamental pitch with, and no single physical magnitude that plausibly identifies timbre. The quality appears determinate in experience while being conspicuously non-identical to any one stimulus property. That may make audition a sharp illustration of why qualia resist reduction—an illustration, the Map holds, rather than a demonstration.

## Where Hearing Comes Apart: Tinnitus, Amusia, Auditory Agnosia

Audition has an unusually rich dissociation literature, and three cases bear directly on the claims made above. Each is given in the same register as the rest of this article: the physicalist account of each is credible and is adopted here.

**Tinnitus — a percept with no stimulus.** Tinnitus is "an auditory phantom sensation (ringing of the ears) experienced when no external sound is present" (Eggermont & Roberts, 2004). Most cases follow hearing loss from noise exposure or ageing, and the mechanism the literature converges on is *release*: damage to the cochlea or to auditory projection pathways downregulates intracortical inhibition, and the resulting rise in spontaneous firing and neural synchrony through the central auditory pathway is what generates the phantom (Eggermont & Roberts, 2004; Shore, Roberts & Langguth, 2016). It belongs to a family with phantom limb pain and the visual hallucinations of Charles Bonnet syndrome, all read as maladaptive plasticity following damage to the peripheral nervous system (Painter et al., 2018). The framing is easy to get wrong and worth getting right. What the deafferented system does is emit content once the input that constrained it is gone. The phantom reconstructs nothing the lost input would have carried, and no hearing is recovered by it—which is why *release* is the right word for the mechanism and repair is the wrong one.

Maladaptive central plasticity is a *physicalist mechanism*, well described and adopted here without reservation—the same concession the [chemosensory](/topics/chemosensory-consciousness-and-the-interface/) and [tactile](/topics/tactile-consciousness-and-the-interface/) companions make about the limbic bypass and the C-tactile system. What tinnitus adds is a limiting case of this article's central claim. The section above argued that pitch is computed rather than read off the signal; the missing fundamental has no fundamental in the stimulus. Tinnitus is the endpoint of that line—a determinate auditory quality, often with a pitch the patient can match on a tone generator, and no stimulus whatever.

**Amusia — the quality goes missing while hearing does not.** Congenital amusia is a lifelong deficit in music perception not explained by hearing loss, brain damage, intellectual deficit or lack of exposure, and it "affects mostly the melodic pitch dimension" (Peretz, Brattico, Järvenpää & Tervaniemi, 2009). The largest prevalence study to date puts it at 1.5%, a substantial downward revision of the 4% figure earlier work relied on (Peretz & Vuvan, 2017). The deficit is strikingly narrow: amusic adults show severe deficiencies in processing pitch variations while processing and recognising "speech, including speech prosody, common environmental sounds and human voices, as well as control subjects" (Ayotte, Peretz & Hyde, 2002).

What sharpens the case is what electrophysiology found underneath it. Amusic brains *do* track quarter-tone pitch differences, showing an early right-lateralised negative response; what distinguishes them from controls is "the limited awareness of this ability" (Peretz et al., 2009). The pitch difference is registered without arriving in experience as the determinate quality the section above took as its datum. That is the shape of the [capability division](/concepts/capability-division-in-vision/) the Map reads off [blindsight](/concepts/blindsight/)—a brain-side discrimination surviving without a corresponding mind-side presentation—recovered in the auditory modality, and in exactly the qualities this article argues have no external object-property to be identified with. The reading is not forced. Peretz and colleagues offer their own: the neural pitch representation "cannot make contact with musical pitch knowledge along the auditory-frontal neural pathway," a connectivity story requiring nothing non-physical. Both readings fit the data, and the Map takes the case as an accommodation rather than a result.

**Auditory agnosia — hearing without recognising.** Selective brain damage produces a family of auditory disorders—cortical deafness, pure word deafness, auditory agnosia and phonagnosia—in which sound is heard but not identified, and which taken together "suggest a modular architecture analogous to models of visual processing that have been derived from studying neurological patients" (Polster & Rose, 1998). Audition, like vision, is organised into partly separable processing streams (Rauschecker & Scott, 2009). This bears on the [capability-division](/concepts/capability-division-in-vision/) material the article borrows from vision: the division of perceptual labour it describes is not an artefact of visual anatomy, because a system built differently, and without a retinotopic map to structure it, comes apart along recognisably parallel lines.

The three cases together do the work the rest of the article does and no more. Each has a mechanistic account in good standing, none requires a non-physical perceiver, and a physicalist can read the whole set as evidence about processing architecture and lose nothing. What they show is that the auditory qualities treated here are dissociable—from the stimulus, from awareness, and from recognition—so any theory of perceptual consciousness owes an account of each dissociation. The interface reading has one. Having an account is not the same as being established by it.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The auditory distinctives above are framed throughout as phenomena the dualist/interface reading *accommodates*, not as evidence that settles the dispute. The physicalist has a credible account of each—temporal integration windows, computational scene analysis, periodicity-based pitch extraction, maladaptive central plasticity for tinnitus, disrupted auditory-frontal connectivity for amusia, stream-selective damage for the agnosias—and those accounts are taken seriously here and, where relevant, adopted. The Map's claim is the weaker, defensible one: a theory of consciousness modelled on the visual snapshot will systematically mis-describe hearing, and the interface framework, by contrast, extends to audition without strain.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)**: Auditory qualities arguably sharpen the case that [qualia](/concepts/qualia/) are not identical with physical properties, because pitch and timbre appear to have no external object-property to be identified with—the missing fundamental has no fundamental in the signal, and tinnitus is the limiting case, a determinate heard quality with no signal at all. This may make audition a clean illustration of the [explanatory gap](/concepts/explanatory-gap/), while leaving the physicalist's computational reply standing—which in the tinnitus case is a strong one, since maladaptive central plasticity accounts for the phantom without remainder at the level of mechanism. The Map reads perceptual qualities as constituted at the [interface](/concepts/mind-matter-interface/) between physical processing and conscious experience; the auditory case suggests that reading is not merely a visual artefact.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)**: The active, directed character the [perception article](/topics/dualist-perception/) attributes to seeing applies equally to hearing—we *listen to* a sound, attend selectively to one stream, foreground a voice. The Map interprets this attentional selection in auditory scene analysis as consonant with bidirectional interaction: phenomenal attention participates in which stream is foregrounded. Tinnitus offers a variant of the same interpretive hook—Shore, Roberts and Langguth (2016) note that "emotional and attentional state could be involved in the development and maintenance of tinnitus via top-down mechanisms," and the hedging is theirs and is kept here. This is an interpretive alignment, not a finding; the epiphenomenalist can re-describe the same selection as wholly neural, and the Map's commitment rests on its broader [interaction framework](/topics/predictive-processing-and-dualism/) rather than on the auditory case alone.

The Map does not claim audition settles dualism. It claims audition is evidence any adequate theory must accommodate, and that a framework shaped by vision alone seems incomplete until it does.
