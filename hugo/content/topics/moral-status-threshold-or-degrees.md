Moral status, on the Map's account, has one threshold and no degrees. Whatever has the capacity for valenced experience — experience that feels good or bad to its subject — has moral status equally with every other bearer; everything that varies between bearers (welfare range, the intensity and richness of experience, what death would take) is graded as *stakes*, which carry all the differential treatment hierarchists put into status. The register holds this as [P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2). In the field's vocabularies it is Kagan's *unitarianism* (2019), DeGrazia's *Unequal Interests Model* (2008), the *threshold conception* of the Stanford Encyclopedia's "Grounds of Moral Status" entry, and Wendler's thesis that there are no *status-enhancing* properties (2023); its published defenders reach it without any view about what minds are, and the Map holds it on their grounds too. The rivals of record are Kagan's limited hierarchy, DeGrazia's sliding scale, and McMahan's two tiers — equal consideration below a line, respect above it.

What the Map adds is framework-dependent. The standard objection to any threshold — a status-conferring capacity comes in degrees, so a line drawn on it is arbitrary (SEP §4; McMahan 2008; Floris & Timmer 2025) — defeats, in the Map's judgement, every threshold drawn on a *scalar* capacity, the personhood tier included. The Map's threshold survives only because it is drawn at the *presence of an experiential locus* rather than at a magnitude, a reply available under Tenet 1 and unavailable under illusionism or integrated information theory ([explained below](#presence)). The verdict-changing case — two patients, one anaesthetic dose, stakes fixed by construction — is worked in [the enhancement article](/topics/ethics-of-cognitive-enhancement-under-dualism/#enhancement-and-moral-status) and not re-run here.

## Where the Map Sits in the Field {#taxonomy}

Kagan separates *standing* — counting at all — from *status* — counting in a particular way; unitarians hold that everyone with standing has the same status, hierarchists deny it. His arithmetic makes the disagreement exact: "the greater the status of a given individual, the more value there is in any given unit of welfare obtaining for that individual" (2019, p. 109, via Schukraft 2020). The Map denies that second factor: all the multiplication goes into welfare range, none into status.

DeGrazia's *Unequal Interests Model* — equal consideration, differing interests — is the same view, and he supplies its test: whether it is "equally problematic to inflict suffering on persons and to inflict it on nonpersons" (2008, p. 188). Whether the model already counts as degrees of status is terminological: the SEP says yes (§5.3); Rossi (2010) argues that equal consideration is incompatible with unequal status; the Map's usage is Rossi's.

This article adopts Wendler's triad: *status-conferring* properties bring a being into the circle, *action-guiding* properties fix what is owed to it in a situation, and *status-enhancing* properties, if any existed, would make one bearer's comparable interests count for more than another's. Degrees of status require the third kind, and Wendler argues that none exists (2023). The Map holds one status-conferring property — the capacity for valenced experience, read phenomenally under [P-MS1](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms1) — many action-guiding ones, graded through the value pluralism of [P-VS4](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs4), and none of the third kind. Sebo's review of Kagan states the verdict — "everyone who has moral standing has it equally" — and accepts its consequences (2021). Neither Sebo nor Wendler needs dualism, and the Map does not claim they share it.

## Why Equal: Four Routes and the One the Map Takes {#equality}

The register's consistency argument fixes a reading without arguing for it, and Singer's principle states equality without deriving it. The Map's primary affirmative argument is the one Wendler generalises from Rachels's moral individualism (2004): take any property proposed to raise status and ask what work it does. Either it changes what its bearer has at stake, in which case it is action-guiding and the threshold view already counts it, or it does not, and what is left is the bare claim that the same interest counts for more because it is hers. The enhancement pair is one instance; this is the general form.

Regan's categorical inherent value — "Inherent value, then, belongs equally to those who are the experiencing subjects of a life" (1985) — has the right shape, subject-of-a-life being a presence property, but its equality is stipulated, which the SEP says a threshold theorist may not rest on. Korsgaard's *Fellow Creatures* (2018) argues that importance is a tethered property — to be important is to be important to someone — so there is no perspectiveless standpoint from which humans could be found to matter more than animals (Bernstein 2019). That fits the Map's [phenomenal value realism](/topics/phenomenal-value-realism/), on which value is value *for* a locus, and the Map adopts it as the ground beneath the translation argument: nothing survives translation because there is no standpoint outside the subjects from which one good could outweigh another comparable good. Korsgaard's route is Kantian; the convergence is on the conclusion. Carter's *opacity respect* (2011) grounds basic equality in a range property plus a refusal to evaluate persons' varying capacities. The Map declines it as a ground of status: opacity respect is a norm about agents, and the Map's agent-side norms live in the [responsibility gradient](/topics/responsibility-gradient-from-attentional-capacity/), the desert column [the invertebrate article](/topics/ethics-of-consciousness-invertebrate-question/#moral-status-without-moral-agency) keeps apart from status and stakes.

## The Arbitrariness Objection at Full Strength {#arbitrariness}

The SEP states the objection in its own voice: "Once the importance of some feature, such as the capacity to value, is highlighted, it seems that not only the possession of the capacity but also how well one can exercise it is morally relevant to one's status" (§4).

McMahan, its named threshold advocate, lists seven challenges to his own threshold, "all of which I consider to be quite powerful" (2008, pp. 95–97). The second is the sharpest: "Because the relevant capacities are matters of degree and lack sharp boundaries, the difference between an individual just above the threshold and an individual just below it cannot be the difference between the possession of some capacity and the failure to possess that capacity." Floris and Timmer (2025) turn this into a dilemma: rejecting the reply that thresholds are merely vague, they defend a *moderate discontinuity* view — a threshold marks a moderate rather than a radical shift in status — and conclude that there is no principled way to hold both the moral equality of persons and their superiority over animals.

The Map concedes all of it for any threshold drawn on a scalar capacity, the personhood tier included. What it does not concede is the premise every party shares: that the status-conferring property comes in degrees.

## A Threshold That Is Not a Cut on a Scale {#presence}

The Map's status-conferring property is the presence of an experiential locus — someone for whom things are good or bad, now ([a locus, not a career](/concepts/no-self-objection-to-phenomenal-value/#maps-reply)). McMahan's second challenge — that the difference across the line cannot be between possessing a capacity and lacking it — describes, for the Map's one property, exactly what the difference is. Floris and Timmer's premise — that some degree of the property has an incrementally lower degree that fails to ground status — has no instance, because being a subject does not come in increments; the shift from no subject to a subject is radical because it is a shift of kind, not of amount. A unitarian without Tenet 1 must defend the threshold as Buchanan defends his, by conceding a fuzzy lower boundary and calling it vague — the reply Floris and Timmer reject.

The reply has a price. Lee (2023) argues that whether consciousness comes in degrees is theory-relative, and that most theories entail it does; DeGrazia presses it directly: "it seems natural to say that different sorts of sentient creatures have differing degrees of capacity to experience feelings, providing support once again for the sliding-scale model" (2008, p. 193). The Map's answer distinguishes degrees of *experience* — intensity, richness, temporal grain — from degrees of *being an experiencer*. The first are conceded and are all stakes variables; the second is denied, since a locus is present or it is not. The Map's own [degrees-of-consciousness](/concepts/degrees-of-consciousness/) page opens "Consciousness is not binary" and invokes Tenet 5 against treating consciousness as on or off; it grades what a subject has — level, richness, access — and its finding that no single scalar of "how conscious" may exist tells against any threshold drawn on such a scalar. Whether a subject is present at all is the prior question, the [phenomenal-sorites-problem](/concepts/phenomenal-sorites-problem/), where ontic vagueness is the live rival and the Map's resistance is recorded as motivated by Tenet 4 rather than established; the threshold is exactly as secure as that resistance.

The reply fails in two places. Under [illusionism](/concepts/illusionism/) there is no phenomenal property to be present; the criterion reverts to functional organisation, which is scalar, and the objection returns in full. Under [integrated information theory](/concepts/integrated-information-theory/) any system with Φ above zero has some experience and Φ varies continuously, so being an experiencer is a magnitude and a threshold on it a cut on a scale.

## Buchanan and the Enhancement-Status Symposium {#buchanan}

[P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2)'s literal question — does enhancing the capacity that confers status raise status? — has a debate of its own, where the Map's verdict was published first: "Merely augmenting the characteristics that make a being a person doesn't seem to be the sort of thing that could confer higher moral status" (Buchanan 2009, p. 359). Buchanan's ground is respect-based, though, and he argues that only such a ground can carry a threshold: "if having a good is what confers moral considerability, then having a higher good or being capable of greater well-being should always be relevant to how one ought to be treated," so an interest-based view "seems ill suited" to a threshold concept (p. 361). The Map's ground is interest-based, so this is the objection it most needs to answer.

The answer is that "relevant to how one ought to be treated" names the stakes column. Buchanan's own contrast — relevance to treatment against bearing on degree of status — is the separation the Map draws, and the translation argument says all the relevance goes there. Among the replies, Douglas (2013) argues that Buchanan's response fails, grants that status-raising enhancement is possible, and answers the harm worry distributively; DeGrazia (2012) likewise holds post-persons possible. The Map's verdict is Buchanan's and its ground is not. Under the Map the question has two forms: whether an intervention moves a subject across the threshold or changes the *number* of subjects — a census question ([moral census opacity](/concepts/moral-census-opacity/#counting)) the register does not answer.

## One Threshold, Not Two {#one-threshold}

McMahan's "two-tiered" account governs killing below "the 'threshold of respect'" solely by concern for the victim's time-relative interests, and killing above it "by a requirement of respect for their inherent worth" (2008, p. 94). The lower tier — on the time-relative interest account (2002), a death's badness is discounted by the victim's psychological unity with the goods lost — is the literature's most worked account of a stakes variable carrying the killing intuitions; the Map's position is that tier extended to everyone with standing, with the respect tier deleted.

Three arguments say the second line cannot be held. McMahan's own challenges attack the threshold of respect, and his three-tier refinement — an intermediate status whose constraints vary with capacity (pp. 98–99) — concedes that the line does not hold. Kagan, a hierarchist, rejects restricting deontological status to persons because the capacities that ground it come in degrees and many animals have them to some degree (ch. 8, as Sebo summarises it). Floris and Timmer's dilemma reaches the same result from the egalitarian side. Jaworska and Tannenbaum's relational person-rearing account (2014) is a two-factor account in Wendler's sense, and Timmerman and Fischer (2019) argue it yields implausible consequences once combined with the accounts of flourishing it needs.

The coma verdict and the refusal of a respect tier are one commitment. Where the capacity for valenced experience is irreversibly gone, intrinsic status goes with it and what remains owed is owed derivatively ([sentientism](/concepts/sentientism/)). A respect tier would give that patient a ground of status the capacity's loss does not touch; declining it is what a single status-conferring property means, and the residue is the cost of having no second ground.

## The Residue {#residue}

The separation relocates Nozick's utility monster rather than blocking it: equal consideration of equal interests weights a subject whose intensity ceiling has been raised, and an unbounded ceiling is an unbounded weight (the Map's extension of an argument Nozick aimed at aggregation). Chappell (2021) argues that intuitions about the monster reverse when it starts from a baseline of massive suffering, so the worry is symmetric: an enhanced subject whose *suffering* ceiling has been raised is not obviously a monster. The distributive form — Vallentyne (2005); Kagan's argument from distribution (ch. 3) — says a unitarian who accepts a priority principle must favour the worse-off mouse; on the enhancement pair it favours the unenhanced patient, which the enhancement article finds no less plausible. The residue belongs to aggregation, where interests are summed whoever's they are, and Kagan's multiplier answers it only by reintroducing the factor the translation argument removed.

One flank stays open. Where morality is non-aggregative — each subject's claim a separate constraint rather than a summand, as with Nozick's side constraints — equal status is action-guiding by construction and the separation is at its least verbal, since constraints do not translate into stakes. Kagan argues (chs. 7–9) that deontologists too must accept hierarchy, or unitarian constraints forbid killing a fish to save a human. The Map has no worked deontology and does not borrow Kagan's; whether one threshold with graded stakes can be married to constraints is open.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)** turns the threshold from a cut on a scale into a presence. The verdict needs no metaphysics; the reply to McMahan and to Floris and Timmer needs the phenomenal reading of the criterion ([P-MS1](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms1)), on which "capacity for valenced experience" names a property a locus has or lacks rather than an organisation that shades off, and stands or falls with the tenet.

**[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)** supplies, through indexical identity, the claim that a subject is a countable thing — why "how many subjects" is a question ([moral-census-opacity](/concepts/moral-census-opacity/)) and "how much subject" is not — and the Map's resistance to ontic vagueness, which is as strong as the non-deflationary subject the tenet presupposes; the threshold inherits that dependency.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)** binds twice. The degrees-of-consciousness page invokes it against treating *how much* experience there is as on or off, and this article agrees; it also forbids invoking simplicity for the claim that the *presence* of a subject is binary, which rests on Tenet 1 and the anti-vagueness commitment — where those are motivated rather than established, so is the threshold.

**[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction)** and **[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)** do not bear on the verdict; where the threshold *falls* ([the invertebrate article](/topics/ethics-of-consciousness-invertebrate-question/)) is a separate question.
