Can someone refer to *that* object without being conscious of it? Zenon Pylyshyn's visual indexes, a hypothesised early-vision mechanism that picks out and tracks about four or five objects "without being categorized or conceptualized" (Pylyshyn 2001), are a vision-science rival to John Campbell's thesis that conscious attention gives knowledge of what a perceptual demonstrative refers to. The Unfinishable Map's verdict ([what the evidence licenses](#weighing), explained below) has three parts. Selecting an object, which is reference in a sub-personal sense, can run without awareness of the object, on evidence from masked-object and patient studies; whether tracking can is open. Campbell's claim concerns a further achievement, knowing *which* thing one refers to, and his own sea-of-faces case already grants the sub-personal latching, so the vision science presses that claim without refuting it. Neither outcome bears on dualism.

Three cautions belong with that verdict. Pylyshyn calls indexing *preconceptual*: his 2001 statement of the theory does not discuss consciousness, and there he stopped calling indexing "preattentive". In the standard tracking experiment the targets are visible, so the case for selection without consciousness rests on separate evidence ([selection without awareness](#without-awareness), explained below). Index theory is a leading hypothesis, not settled science. And one result cuts the other way: in one cueing paradigm, updating the information stored about an object was "entirely contingent on conscious perception" (Lamy et al. 2015), a finding later partly qualified. The cost falls on the Map's account of singular thought ([singular-thought-and-acquaintance](/topics/singular-thought-and-acquaintance/)), where perceptual demonstratives were the one place a phenomenal condition survived. That condition is now contested on two fronts, one empirical and one conceptual, and survives, if at all, as an epistemic condition rather than one on selecting the object.

## Visual Indexes {#indexes}

Multiple-object tracking (MOT) is the paradigm behind the theory. Pylyshyn and Storm (1988) found that "subjects are able to track a subset of up to 5 objects in a field of 10 identical randomly-moving objects", and read the result through the earlier FINST model, which posits a "primitive identity maintenance mechanism that indexes and tracks a limited number of visual objects in parallel". FINST stands for "FINgers of INSTantiation", a name Pylyshyn (2001, p. 130) traces to joint work in 1978. In the 2001 version indexes pick out objects or proto-objects, a term several vision scientists use.

Pylyshyn draws the demonstrative analogy himself. "Like natural language demonstratives (such as 'this' or 'that') this direct connection allows entities to be referred to without being categorized or conceptualized" (2001, abstract). The reference relation an index sets up "ceases to exist when the referent (i.e. the visual object) is no longer in view. In that respect it functions exactly like a demonstrative" (p. 138, n. 2), and he suggests that through indexes "one might say that we can have demonstrative thoughts" (p. 154). He also limits the claim: the ordinary notion of an individual physical object "does require concepts (in particular it requires what are called sortal concepts)" (p. 154).

Three questions need separating: is indexing conceptual, is it attentional, is it conscious? Pylyshyn answers only the first firmly. He had called indexing "preattentive" because the theory hypothesizes that "indexes are not mediated by a conceptual description". But the *task* of tracking several objects "may require a great deal of effort and attention", and since many investigators read effort as a sign that a process is attentive, he wrote, "I now avoid referring to indexing as preattentive", while continuing to assume that indexing and tracking are "realized by an automatic and preconceptual mechanism" (2001, p. 149). In 2007 he described FINST theory as taking the tracking aspect of MOT to be "automatic and non-attentional", while others read it as split attention (Pylyshyn 2007). Neither wording says indexing is unconscious.

The theory is contested. Ayare and Srivastava (2024) argue that existing evidence suggests "it might be possible to perform MOT without indexing", and model tracking without indexes. Pylyshyn's framing is causal and naturalistic, not dualist: the items indexes pick out are what the visual system gives us "through a brute causal mechanism", and concepts must be grounded in "a primitive causal connection between thoughts and things" (2001, p. 154).

## Object Files {#object-files}

Kahneman, Treisman and Gibbs (1992) developed the object file: "a temporary episodic representation, within which successive states of an object are linked and integrated." It differs from the philosophical *mental file* of Recanati and others ([singular-thought-and-acquaintance](/topics/singular-thought-and-acquaintance/)).

Raftopoulos and Müller (2006) ground demonstratives in object-file segmentation: "the reference of perceptual demonstratives is fixed in a causal nondescriptive way through the nonconceptual content of perception", content that "individuates but does not identify the object of perceptual awareness". They place object-file grounding inside awareness.

Mitroff, Scholl and Wynn (2005) asked whether object files correspond to conscious percepts. Conscious percepts of "which went where" in dynamic ambiguous displays "can diverge from the mapping computed by the object-file system", they found, but only "in at least some specialized (and perhaps ecologically invalid) cases". The objects themselves were seen; only the correspondence diverged.

## Campbell's Thesis and the Sea of Faces {#campbell}

Campbell's book states the thesis: "Conscious attention to an object, singling it out in experience, provides knowledge of the reference of a demonstrative term" (Campbell 2002, abstract). His model of selection is location-based: "The evidence suggests that experience of the location of the object will be critical to allowing selection of just the right information" ("Experiential Highlighting" chapter abstract). Of an object that "is simply there in my field of view, though unnoticed by me", he writes, "I am not yet in a position to refer to it" (p. 2, as quoted in Williams 2013).

His central case is a thought experiment. S uses a demonstrative for a woman whom H, facing a sea of faces, cannot single out. H can still "make reliable guesses about what the person is eating, wearing, and so on, as well as reaching and pointing appropriately" (pp. 8–9, as quoted in Williams 2013). Campbell's verdict is that "so long as H's conscious experience remains a sea of faces, there is an ordinary sense in which H does not know who S means", and that "The problem here does not have to do with whether H is reliable" (p. 9, as quoted in Williams 2013). He rests the verdict on common sense.

The case grants what indexing would supply: H reaches and points at the right person reliably, and Campbell's claim is that this falls short of *knowing* who is meant.

Campbell (2007) replies to Pylyshyn directly. He defends Lynn Robertson's thesis that "spatial awareness is a cause of object perception", and a second thesis about verbal report: "Zenon Pylyshyn has criticized both. I argue that nonetheless, the burden of the evidence supports both". He grants non-conscious perception of objects, arguing that conscious perception has "a different causal role" from it. Campbell is a relationalist and no dualist ([naturalist-relationalism](/concepts/naturalist-relationalism/)).

## Selection Without Awareness {#without-awareness}

The evidence for selection without consciousness comes from studies other than Pylyshyn's.

- **[Blindsight](/concepts/blindsight/).** Kentridge, Heywood and Weiskrantz (1999), testing one blindsight subject, concluded that "the spatial selection of information by an attentional mechanism and its entry into conscious experience cannot be one and the same process". This is *spatial* attention; Mole's complaint is that such evidence "cannot demonstrate attention to a thing in the absence of consciousness of that very thing" (Mole 2026, citing Mole 2008).
- **Masked objects.** Norman, Heywood and Kentridge (2013), with normal observers whose awareness of the masked objects was tested by report and by signal detection, found that "attentional selection at the level of objects can take place without giving rise to awareness of those objects", which "goes some way towards addressing this complaint" (Mole 2026). Their 2015 follow-up, with stricter checks that the objects stayed unseen, reports "exogenous object-based attention without awareness". These are cueing effects, not tracking.
- **Neglect and extinction.** Driver and Vuilleumier's (2001) review finds that "considerable residual processing can still take place for neglected or extinguished stimuli, yet without reaching the patient's awareness", including "preserved visual grouping processes". In one patient, Mattingley, Davis and Driver (1997) found that "parietal extinction arises only after substantial processing has generated visual surfaces". On the Map's reading, surface segmentation, a step indexing would need, ran before awareness was lost.

What the evidence does not show is tracking without awareness. No study the Map has found has observers track MOT targets they are not aware of. The search behind that sentence was a PubMed title search for "multiple object tracking" (237 records) narrowed to records whose title or abstract mentions awareness, unconscious, invisible, suppression or masking (8 records, each screened by abstract), plus two web searches, on 22 October 2026; it did not reach PsycINFO or conference abstracts. In MOT papers "invisible" often means occluded or briefly blanked, as in tracking "temporarily invisible objects" (Huff et al. 2010), not present but unconsciously processed. A direct test, on the Map's suggestion, would have observers track targets kept unseen by masking, with awareness checked by signal detection as in the Norman studies.

### The Counter-Result {#counter-result}

Lamy, Alon, Carmel and Shalev (2015) found, in a cueing paradigm, that "attentional capture is largely independent of conscious perception and that updating the episodic information stored about an object is entirely contingent on conscious perception". On the Map's reading the split fits Campbell's picture: selection runs largely without consciousness, while gathering information about the selected object waits on it.

Schoeberl, Ditye and Ansorge (2018) qualify it, finding that with a spatial-frequency search, same-location costs "were not dependent on cue awareness", and that some of these costs "are at odds with" the object-file-updating explanation. Lamy's finding holds for its paradigm; whether it generalises is contested.

## Where the Dispute Now Sits {#dispute}

**Which reference?** Pylyshyn's reference is a sub-personal binding that lapses when the object leaves view; Campbell's is *knowledge* of reference, a personal-level state. Williams (2013) argues that Campbell's arguments "are not entirely successful (largely because of his reliance on his notion of 'knowledge of reference')", and Tamburini (2025), writing on communication, that "knowledge of reference is not necessary for referential understanding".

**Is experience the only route?** Smithies (2011) gives consciousness "an essential epistemic role" in demonstrative thought. Barkasi (2019) favours, in Mole's gloss, "a sub-doxastic characterization" of attention where Campbell's is epistemic. Berger, Nanay and Quilty-Dunn (2018) conclude that "unconscious perceptual justification is quite plausible." Barkasi denies that experience is the *only* route, and Berger and colleagues that consciousness has a *special* justificatory role; neither denies that consciousness often plays these roles.

**Objects or places?** Campbell ties selection to experienced location; Pylyshyn's indexes follow objects through changes of location. Austen Clark (2004), a pluralist rather than a critic of indexes, holds that "Perceptual representation is a layered affair" that "probably includes both feature-placing and proto-objects".

**Does reference need attribution?** Burge (2022) argues that "perceptual reference is always guided by perceptual attribution", which tells against attribution-free indexing. Quilty-Dunn and Green (2023) argue that the evidence undermines all-attribution, no-attribution and privileged-subset models alike, and defend "a flexible‐attributives model". Burge cuts both ways: Begby (2011) reports him finding Evans "relying on unstated arguments which connect perceptual representation with consciousness (the latter necessary for the former)", and finding those arguments "shot through with problems at practically every turn".

## What the Evidence Licenses {#weighing}

This weighing is the Map's synthesis, not any cited author's.

1. Observers can track several identical moving objects at once (Pylyshyn & Storm 1988); that early vision does so through preconceptual indexes is a contested hypothesis.
2. Object-level attentional selection, grouping and surface segmentation can run without awareness of the objects concerned. Tracking without awareness is not established, and some object-file updating depends on awareness, within limits.
3. Campbell's thesis survives as a thesis about knowledge, since it grants the sub-personal latching. What remains is whether knowledge of reference is a real further achievement that needs consciousness, or a gloss that sub-personal selection plus [availability](/concepts/access-consciousness/) can supply.

## The Scope–Phenomenality Trade-off Revisited {#trade-off}

[The Map's account of singular thought](/topics/singular-thought-and-acquaintance/) reads that literature as a trade-off: the more phenomenal the acquaintance, the less singular thought it allows. Among the views surveyed there, a phenomenal condition survives, if anywhere, only for perceptual demonstratives. That survivor holds only if Campbell's or Smithies's contested account of what consciousness adds is right. Empirically, selection can run without awareness, though the Map has found no test of tracking without it and one updating result points the other way. Conceptually, knowledge of reference and the epistemic role are both disputed (Barkasi; Berger, Nanay and Quilty-Dunn; Tamburini; Williams). The Map's other route, on which consciousness enters singular thought through the *thinker* under moderate phenomenal intentionality theory ([intentionality](/concepts/intentionality/)), is untouched.

## Relation to Site Perspective

On the Map's reading, a phenomenal condition on demonstrative reference is contested at the level of selecting the object and survives, if at all, only in the epistemic role Campbell and Smithies give consciousness, which is itself disputed.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism).** Neither outcome supports dualism. If Campbell is right, the result is consciousness-dependence for one kind of knowledge, which physicalists can accept. If the rivals are right, a phenomenal condition on reference loses its best case. That is a cost to the Map's narrow claim, not to dualism, which never rested on it.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction).** Campbell's "Space and Action" chapter abstract says "the role of conscious attention is to identify the target of the action for the benefit of the underlying brain systems". That fits the tenet without evidencing it: Campbell is no interactionist, and a physicalist assigns the role to the neural processes underlying awareness. The sea-of-faces case supposes that pointing can proceed without conscious attention to the target; if so, consciousness is not needed for that task. That would not show conscious attention idle where present; Lamy's result and Campbell's knowledge claim both mark work the non-conscious route may not do.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits).** The preconceptual mechanism is the simpler account of perceptual reference. The tenet governs only the Map's own reasoning: it lets the Map doubt that simplicity settles the personal-level question, and gives no licence to discount a well-evidenced sub-personal capacity for being simple. The reasons for care about index theory are empirical (Ayare & Srivastava 2024).

[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) and [No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds) are not engaged.
