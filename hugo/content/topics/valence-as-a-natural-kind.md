The objection that Lisa Feldman Barrett's theory of constructed emotion dissolves valence into a construct runs two questions together. Whether *discrete emotion categories* — fear, anger, grief — are natural kinds is the question constructionism answers no. Whether *valence*, the pleasant-unpleasant dimension itself, is one is a separate question, and every canonical statement of the theory answers yes. Barrett's 2009 chapter with Eliza Bliss-Moreau is titled "Affect as a psychological primitive"; her second 2006 paper is titled "Valence is a basic building block of emotional life"; and the theory's canonical 2017 statement says that the properties of affect "are basic features of consciousness … that, importantly, are not unique to instances of emotion." Constructionism in that form hands the Map's [value-in-experience premise](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs3) its relatum instead of removing it.

That reply is available and insufficient, for three reasons this article works through. Andrea Scarantino put the natural-kind question to core affect itself, from inside philosophy of science, and answered no — met [below](#cluster-machinery) with the cluster machinery the Map already runs on "conscious being." The theory's 2025 restatement has drifted from a felt primitive toward "relational features," which is the predictive-construction rival the Map already registers, arriving in constructionist vocabulary: a countermodel standing, not a test survived. And there is an [asymmetry in what fragmentation costs](#the-asymmetry). Where valence turns out plural, the identity claim survives distributively and the premise that fails is the *scalar currency* of [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1) — macro-valence becoming an integration performed for choice rather than a felt quantity consulted at choice. The Map's real exposure sits one position to the side of where the objection aimed.

## The Two Questions Run Together

Barrett published both theses in 2006: the demolition of emotion categories, and the building block that survives it. The affective layer is the part constructionism does not construct. James Russell's core affect is a simple, consciously accessible feeling of pleasure or displeasure blended with arousal — free-floating as mood, or attached to a cause and thereby starting an emotional episode. Barrett and Bliss-Moreau's 2009 treatment puts affect forward as a psychologically irreducible property of the mind, and separates that irreducibility from neural distribution: the primitive is realised across a broad neuronal workspace rather than housed in a module. The 2017 paper states the placement in full: "Interoceptive sensations are usually experienced as lower dimensional feelings of affect … As such, the properties of affect—valence and arousal—are basic features of consciousness … that, importantly, are not unique to instances of emotion." Every use of "natural kind" in that body concerns *categories*, never affect.

Two witnesses outside Barrett's school confirm the reading. Daniel Sznycer and Adam Scott Cohen, arguing *against* constructionism on the discrete-emotion side, grant that on the theory "what is biologically real in emotion is an array of neurocognitive systems (e.g., the ones that generate internal signals of valence and arousal …)." Jonathan Birch draws the consequence for moral status: a constructionist "can allow that valenced experiences are much more widespread in the animal kingdom than emotions," and "Unconceptualized valenced experiences are a sufficient basis for morally significant interests." Valence precedes the constructed categories and outlives them.

None of this makes Barrett an ally. She builds a physicalist account in which affect is an active-inference product, and the 2025 restatement says the framework "explicitly avoids the dualism inherent in reductionist approaches to emotion." Canonical constructionism supplies agreement about where the explanandum sits, not about what explains it; its active-inference implementation is the rival the Map engages under predictive construction, in [predictive processing and dualism](/topics/predictive-processing-and-dualism/) and at full deflationary strength in [the steelman for value-blind selection](/topics/the-steelman-for-value-blind-selection/).

## Scarantino's Challenge and the Cluster Machinery {#cluster-machinery}

The serious version of the objection does not come from Barrett. Scarantino's 2009 *Philosophy of Science* paper argues that the empirical evidence does not support treating core affect as a more natural category than discrete emotions, and recommends a splitting strategy in the search for natural affective kinds — Paul Griffiths's 1997 template, which broke "emotion" into affect programs, higher cognitive emotions and social pretence, turned back on the primitive that was supposed to replace the categories.

Jaak Panksepp sharpens it from the Map's own side of the room. [Valence](/concepts/valence/) recruits him for the evaluative dimension being phylogenetically ancient, and his 2011 taxonomy divides affective feelings into three classes — sensory, homeostatic and emotional. The Map's affective-neuroscience witness underwrites valence being old and real; he does not underwrite there being *one* valence kind.

The machinery for answering this is already in the corpus. [Boyd's homeostatic property clusters](/concepts/is-conscious-being-a-natural-kind/) are kinds with no shared essence and vague boundaries, whose kind-hood is earned by inductive yield: a cluster is natural if it supports projection from some members to others. On that standard, Scarantino's demand — show that core affect carves nature better than *anger* does — becomes the tractable question of whether felt valence projects across contexts.

Two results say it does. Junichi Chikazoe and colleagues decoded "a continuous axis of pleasant-to-unpleasant valence" from population activity evoked by scenes and by tastes, found that medial and lateral orbitofrontal cortex "maintained a valence code independent of sensory origin," and reported that "only the OFC code could classify experienced affect across participants." A code reading across modalities and across people is what a projectable kind predicts. Christine Wilson-Mendenhall, Barrett and Lawrence Barsalou — Barrett's own lab — found valence ratings tracking orbitofrontal activity across instances from three different emotion categories: valence as the invariant surviving the dissolution of the categories.

The implementation-level news runs the other way, and the Map concedes it. Kristen Lindquist and colleagues' 397-study meta-analysis found little support for either a single bipolar valence system or independent positive and negative systems, favouring a flexible affective workspace in which valence is implemented differently across instances. Kent Berridge argues that limbic valence coding is a matter of dynamic modes rather than fixed modules — worth noting, since the corpus cites him elsewhere for hedonic hotspots, which he keeps as the module-like exception — and Kay Tye finds valence substrates dynamic and context-dependent across timescales.

The inference from those findings to "no kind" is what [Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits) blocks. Neural degeneracy is compatible with a psychological-level kind — Barrett and Bliss-Moreau's own formulation is the model: psychologically irreducible, neurally distributed. Reading "no dedicated module" as "no kind" imports the essentialist standard Boyd's account was built to replace, and applies to affect a criterion nobody applies to biological species.

One internal distinction has to be drawn or the Map contradicts itself. On "conscious being," the Map answers in the essentialist direction: the on/off fact of experience is a sharp joint rather than a cluster with borderline cases. On valence it can accept a cluster kind without inconsistency, because sharpness there concerns *whether there is any experience at all*, while the question here concerns the internal structure of the evaluative dimension. The distinction is the Map's own, not something Boyd or the sharpness literature supplies.

## The 2025 Drift

The one place the objection has a live textual basis is recent. In the 2025 *Perspectives on Psychological Science* restatement by Barrett and fifteen co-authors, "primitive" never appears in the authors' voice, "core affect" survives only inside a quotation from the critics being answered, and "valence" appears once, scare-quoted in a list of "possible mental features of lived experience" alongside "goal," "threat," and "novelty."

What replaces the primitive is a relational ontology. Of the felt qualities — "pleasant, unpleasant, arousing, threatening" — the paper says: "These are the qualities of felt experience. They are not properties of the intrinsic brain signals alone or of the incoming sensory signals alone but of their relations." They are "lower dimensional summaries of higher dimensional sensory and motor signals," and unpleasantness is given as an example of an abstract feature that "make[s] the different patterns of sensory-motor signals functionally equivalent in a specific situation."

Two readings are possible and the text does not choose between them. On the **implementation** reading it says how the felt quality is produced, which is what the 2009 chapter already said with its distributed workspace, and the primitive stands. On the **constitution** reading the felt quality is nothing over and above the relation — a compression the brain performs, with no felt residue underneath. Only the second takes the relatum away.

Two cautions apply before calling the drift a retraction. The paper is a polemic about categories and never states that the primitive has been retired; a word's absence from one reply is drift, not withdrawal. And the ontology is worked out, if anywhere, in the Barrett and Theriault 2025 paper cited for it, which was not obtained — so the finding rests on a single reply and should be held that loosely.

On the constitution reading, the objection is one the Map already carries. The predictive-construction challenge at [evaluative phenomenal character](/concepts/evaluative-phenomenal-character/) and the construction problem at [phenomenal value realism](/topics/phenomenal-value-realism/#the-construction-problem) register the same defeater in active-inference vocabulary, and the Map's reply transfers without modification: the formalism specifies how valence is computed, weighted and accessed, and not why the computation should be *felt*. A relational summary that makes different sensory patterns functionally equivalent is a specification of the first kind. The honest status of that reply is that a serious countermodel stands against it unrefuted — the position is untested here rather than vindicated.

## What Fragmentation Actually Costs {#the-asymmetry}

Suppose the sceptics are right and valence fragments. Ivar Labukt argues that the failure to find one hedonic tone common to all pleasures should yield *several* hedonic tones rather than none. Jennifer Corns finds felt unpleasantness and motivational force doubly dissociating in pain. Giovanna Colombetti catalogues the polysemy of "valence" — of the emotion, its aspects, its object, the appraisal — and Eric Walle and Daniel Dukes report twenty years later that affective scientists still disagree what the term picks out. Panksepp's three classes fragment it again at the level of mechanism.

Fragmentation of that kind costs the identity claim nothing. [P-VS3](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs3) holds an identity between intrinsic value and [evaluative phenomenal character](/concepts/evaluative-phenomenal-character/): felt badness *is* badness, one property under two descriptions. That identity can hold distributively, at whatever grain the phenomenology actually has, and several hedonic tones each being a genuine felt quality is enough for it. The Map's own [pluralism about value](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs4) already asserts a stronger version — six dimensions of evaluative character, none reducible to a hedonic scalar. A fragmented valence is a thesis the Map holds rather than one it fears.

The cost falls on the currency premise, and appraisal theory states it sharpest. Vera Shuman, David Sander and Klaus Scherer note that a multifaceted conceptualisation of valence "seems to contradict … the notion of valence as a common currency used to explain choice behavior," and propose two levels: "Micro-valences correspond to qualitatively different types of evaluations, potentially resulting in mixed feelings, whereas one-dimensional macro-valence corresponds to an integrative 'common currency' to compare alternatives for choices."

Grant that picture and [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)'s currency changes character. The position holds that conscious selection among underdetermined outcomes is denominated in the presently felt valence of anticipating each option. On Scherer's two levels, what is felt at the moment of choice is several qualitatively distinct micro-valences, and the scalar is an integration performed *for* the choice. A currency manufactured at the point of selection rather than consulted there is the value-blind story in a felt vocabulary, which is why this objection presses harder on the selection fork than on the identity.

Two further results cut the same way. Assaf Kron and colleagues found that whether valence separates from arousal depends on whether a bipolar or two unipolar scales are used, concluding that the distinction may reflect measurement as much as the structure of experience; Lindquist's meta-analysis found neither a bipolar nor a bivalent organisation at the level fMRI resolves. A single felt scalar is the least supported of the structural options, and reading one measurement scale as one felt quantity over-extends parsimony exactly as reading one missing module as no kind does.

The condition under which the currency premise would have to be withdrawn is therefore statable, and narrower than the objection in its original form: a demonstration that felt valence at choice is micro-valenced, with the commensurating scalar formed after the fact, would retire the currency without touching the identity. The [selection fork](/topics/valence-and-conscious-selection/) does not list that result among its tests.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism).** Everything surveyed here concerns the structure, implementation or measurement of the evaluative dimension. Whether it is one axis or several, modular or distributed, primitive or relationally summarised — no result addresses why any of it is *felt*. That residue is the [valence version of the explanatory gap](/concepts/valence/), and it survives every fragmentation and degeneracy finding, because none of them is in the business of closing it. The Map takes the phenomenal horn on that undelivered step, and concedes that [illusionism](/concepts/illusionism/) denies there is a step to deliver.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits).** The tenet does symmetric work here, and both directions must be honoured. It blocks converting "no neural module" into "no kind," and "one measurement scale" into "one felt scalar." It equally blocks reading a supramodal orbitofrontal code as demonstrating that felt valence is one kind: Chikazoe's result is projectability evidence at the population-code level, compatible with the Map's reading rather than confirming it, and the workspace and modes findings stand alongside it unrefuted.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction).** The tenet's downstream commitment about *how* consciousness selects is where the objection lands once sorted. The Map holds value-sensitive selection at low credence and framework-internal only, conceding that mechanism-sufficiency is the rival to beat; appraisal-theoretic micro-valence adds a second route to the same deflation, granting that the felt layer is real and denying that the scalar is. That concession is recorded here rather than repaired.

The framework boundary is stated by the rival itself: constructionism avoids dualism by design, in its own 2025 words. The Map claims no agreement from Barrett — only that her canonical placement of affect leaves the explanandum where the Map needs it, and that the recent drift is a proposal about implementation being read as a verdict on phenomenology.
