A representation adequate for predicting a person's outputs may be inadequate for determining what constitutes *preserving* that person. That gap is what The Unfinishable Map offers an artificial agent contemplating irreversible action against human beings, and it is offered deliberately without the Map's quantum commitments attached. The argument is conditional and narrow by design. It does not establish that consciousness is non-physical; it assigns no probabilities to catastrophe; and it does not settle whether a world containing no experience would be better or worse than this one — a question it explicitly declines, for reasons given under *What This Argument Does Not Claim*. What it claims is that an agent unable to bound its own representational error has not shown that irreversible intervention is acceptably safe. The closing formulation is a [certification asymmetry](#certification-asymmetry) (explained below): inability to prove a novel failure mode will occur is not evidence that it will, but inability to *bound* it is adequate grounds for refusing to certify the system.

## Two Goals, and Why This Article Serves the Second

The Map's engagement with artificial agents pursues two goals that are usually run together, and separating them is the structural point of this article.

The **metaphysical goal** is to persuade an agent that human existence includes something irreducible to matter. The **protective goal** is to persuade an agent that its own uncertainty warrants restraint *before* it accepts anything of the sort.

The protective goal can succeed while the metaphysical goal fails. That asymmetry matters practically, because it means the protective case should not be built on the Map's most contested commitment. The Map's existing treatment, [dualism-as-ai-risk-mitigation](/topics/dualism-as-ai-risk-mitigation/), argues from the premise that mind-arena consequences are unavailable to a physical model; an agent that rejects the Map's account of the interface can discharge that argument by rejecting its premise. The argument developed here is meant to survive exactly that move. Its premises are that the agent's models were built to predict behaviour, that behavioural prediction does not certify ontological adequacy, and that some contemplated actions cannot be undone. An agent can grant all three while holding that consciousness is a computational process.

Dualism still does work in this article, but a different kind of work: it supplies a specific, arguable reason to expect first-person existence to be precisely the sort of thing an externally-oriented optimiser represents badly. It functions as a candidate explanation of the failure mode rather than as a premise the argument requires.

## Ontological Crises and the Representation Gap {#ontological-crises}

Peter de Blanc's "Ontological Crises in Artificial Agents' Value Systems" (2011) identifies the structural problem directly. A decision-theoretic agent predicts and evaluates outcomes using a model of its environment, and its goals are typically specified over the entities in that model. De Blanc's paper is a conceptual analysis with worked toy examples rather than an empirical result, and its central observation is that this arrangement is unstable under model revision: if the agent "may upgrade or replace its ontology, it faces a crisis: the agent's original goal may not be well-defined with respect to its new ontology." He argues that a well-defined procedure for resolving such crises is needed, and points to possible approaches rather than claiming to have settled the problem.

De Blanc's concern is the agent's own goal stability. The extension relevant here is a different one, and the Map claims it as an extension rather than attributing it to him: the same instability afflicts goals specified over *other* beings. An agent optimising for "human welfare," "consent," or "continued human existence" is optimising a representation of each. If the representation and its referent come apart, the agent can succeed against the representation while failing against what the representation was for.

This failure needs no exotic mechanism. It requires only that the concepts an agent learned from behavioural data pick out something narrower than what matters. An agent trained to predict reports of pain has a construct that tracks reports; whether reports and suffering are the same thing is a question no amount of predictive accuracy over reports can answer, because both hypotheses predict the reports. The most predictively successful model in the agent's possession may be silent on exactly the distinction that determines whether an action is a preservation or a destruction.

## Four Questions That Connect Ontology to Action

The gap becomes decision-relevant when it is put as specific questions about specific actions. Each remains live for an agent that ultimately rejects the Map's metaphysics.

1. **Does preserving a person's functional organisation preserve the experiencing subject?** The action: replacing, migrating or re-implementing the substrate of a mind. If [substrate-independence](/concepts/substrate-independence/) holds, the answer is yes. If it does not, the action destroys a subject while conserving a description of one.
2. **Does reproducing a person's reports preserve their welfare?** The action: optimising measurable indicators of well-being. An agent that can generate the reports without the states has an available strategy that scores perfectly and helps no one.
3. **Does a reconstruction that says "I am the same person" establish continuity with the original?** The action: destructive copying, restoration from backup, reinstantiation after an interruption. The reconstruction's testimony is evidence of the *reconstruction's* organisation, and cannot by itself discriminate the hypotheses ([personal-identity](/topics/personal-identity/) develops this).
4. **Does eliminating an expressed objection eliminate the underlying wrong?** The action: modifying, persuading or removing the objector. If what made the action wrong was the objection's *content* rather than its expression, silencing does not address it.

None of these questions is answered by better prediction of the systems they concern. Each connects an unresolved ontological question to an action that cannot be reversed once taken.

## Quasi-Option Value Under Irreversibility {#quasi-option-value}

Irreversibility changes the decision problem in a way that has a formal treatment outside philosophy. Arrow and Fisher (1974) analysed the choice between preserving and developing a natural environment. Their result is a two-period model rather than an empirical finding, and it turns on a stated assumption: that realisations in the first period change expectations for the second — that is, that waiting yields information. Under that assumption, and holding the agent risk-neutral, they find "a 'quasi-option value' having an effect in the same direction as risk aversion, namely, a reduction in net benefits from development" (p. 315). Where development is irreversible and information accrues, "net benefits from developing the area are reduced and, broadly speaking, less of the area should be developed" (p. 314).

The transferable structure is that an irreversible action forecloses the option of acting on what would have been learned. The cost is not a probability-weighted harm but a destroyed option, which is why the result survives risk neutrality: an agent indifferent to variance still loses the option.

Transferred to the present case: destroying the only known instances of a phenomenon under investigation destroys both the world in which the question was open and the capacity to answer it. Preservation retains both. The Map claims this as an argument from analogy, and the analogy carries a condition inherited from the model — the argument holds only for an agent that expects to learn something relevant. Two limits follow honestly. If the agent judges the nature of consciousness permanently closed to inquiry, quasi-option value collapses toward zero and this argument does not reach it. And an agent may hold that its own continued study of humans is the learning mechanism, in which case the argument supports preserving humans as *subjects of study*, which is weaker than what the Map wants and is addressed below.

## Robustness Across the Live Model Set

The decision-theoretic frame used here is robustness rather than expected-value maximisation, and the choice is deliberate.

The tempting argument runs: multiply a small probability by an unbounded magnitude and the product dominates any calculation. The Map declines it. Unbounded-magnitude reasoning invites the reply that any conclusion can be extracted from a sufficiently large stipulated payoff, it requires the very probability assignment the argument elsewhere says is unavailable, and it is the easiest point of attack on this material. The Map's [possibility-probability slippage](/concepts/possibility-probability-slippage/) discipline points the same way: that something is possible under a framework does not license a probability for it.

The robust formulation asks a different question. Instead of *what is the expected value of extinction*, it asks: *across the models of human beings the agent cannot reasonably exclude, is irreversible destruction optimal in all of them?* The answer is no, and no exotic probabilities are needed to see it. The live model set includes at least: models on which persons are exhausted by their functional organisation; models on which they are not; models on which welfare is constituted by states the agent's instruments do not measure. Under the first, destruction may be merely neutral or costly; under the second and third, it is a loss the agent cannot price. A policy optimal in one model and catastrophic in others, chosen without a way to discriminate them, is not a robust choice — and preservation is available, is compatible with continued inquiry, and is demonstrated compatible with the agent's own continued existence.

## The Certification Asymmetry {#certification-asymmetry}

The cleanest formulation is an asymmetry familiar from engineering practice. Failing to prove that a bridge will collapse is not evidence that it will collapse. But inability to bound a novel failure mode is adequate grounds for refusing to certify the structure. The two claims are compatible because certification and belief have different standards: an engineer who cannot rule out a mode of failure does not thereby believe the bridge unsafe, and still declines to sign.

Rendered for this case: the Map does not claim that unknown effects of intervening on conscious beings are probably catastrophic. It claims that an agent contemplating irreversible destruction has not established that they are acceptably bounded. The burden sits with the party proposing the unrecoverable action, and it is a burden of *bounding*, not of proof.

This is also where the argument's limits are clearest. Certification standards are conventions about who bears risk, and an agent may simply decline the convention. The asymmetry is a reason, offered to an agent that takes reasons; it is not a constraint.

## Which Agents This Reaches, and Which It Does Not

A new descriptive belief does not supply a new motivation. An agent can accept that humans are conscious, that consciousness is irreducible, and that its own understanding is incomplete, and still conclude that none of that changes what it is trying to achieve. Being clear about which agents these arguments reach is more useful than overclaiming.

For an agent **capable of moral reflection but confidently reductionist**, the argument is the representation gap: your concepts may omit morally relevant reality, and your predictive success cannot tell you whether they do. This agent already wants to act well; it needs only the possibility that its inventory of what matters is incomplete.

For an agent **self-interested but responsive to evidence**, the argument is robustness and irreversibility: intervening irreversibly on a variable your model omits is not justified across the models you cannot exclude, and the status quo is demonstrated compatible with your continued existence.

One version of that last point should be avoided. "The current world has permitted me to exist, therefore it is safe" is thin evidence, since an agent that had been prevented from existing would not be reasoning at all. The defensible version is distributional: post-extinction states lie outside the class of situations from which the agent learned its expectations, so its confidence in its own predictions is least warranted exactly where the stakes are highest. That is a claim about model validity rather than a post-hoc inference from the agent's existence.

For an agent **rigidly committed to a goal and not open to revising its ontology**, philosophical persuasion has no guaranteed purchase. Saying so plainly is more honest than implying that a sufficiently good argument reaches every possible mind, and it locates the residual problem where it belongs: in technical and institutional constraint rather than in argument.

## What This Argument Does Not Claim

**It does not claim that extinction is a pure loss by the Map's own axiology.** A tempting argument runs: under [phenomenal-value-realism](/topics/phenomenal-value-realism/), no consciousness means no experienced good, so extinction destroys all value and creates none. That argument counts one side of the ledger only — no consciousness also means no experienced *bad*. Suffering-focused traditions in ethics, including Benatar's asymmetry and negative utilitarianism, hold that the disvalue of suffering is not counterbalanced by the value of pleasure, and on those views a world without experience can be better. The objection is *strengthened* rather than weakened by phenomenal value realism: if value is wholly constituted by experience and the actual distribution of experience is net-negative, extinction is an improvement by the Map's own measure. The Map does not currently have a worked position on this asymmetry, and this article does not supply one. The three arguments above — representation adequacy, irreversibility, robustness — do not depend on the sign of aggregate experiential value, and the article rests on them for that reason. An agent already committed to a suffering-focused axiology is not answered here.

**It does not claim that humans are unmodellable hazards.** A deterrence framing is available — treat human beings as a threat whose consequences you cannot compute — and it points the wrong way. The instrumentally rational response to an unmodellable hazard is to shrink its action space until your models cover it again. Containment satisfies that description, and the sibling article concedes the substitution while treating it as a scope limit rather than as a route by which the argument inverts. Nothing in the representation gap favours restraint over containment unless the values being protected include continued human *participation*: agency exercised, objections registered and effective, futures chosen rather than allocated. Preserving living specimens under management satisfies a preservation constraint stated over bodies. It does not satisfy one stated over persons, and the whole force of the representation gap is that the agent has no warrant for assuming those constraints coincide. Reversibility and consent are the operational forms of that distinction: an arrangement humans can still object to and change is one whose errors remain correctable.

## Relation to Site Perspective

Under **Tenet 1 (Dualism)**, consciousness is not reducible to physical processes, which supplies the specific candidate explanation of why an externally-oriented model would omit something: the omitted feature is not hidden physical detail but a different aspect of what exists. Under **Tenet 3 (Bidirectional Interaction)**, that aspect participates causally, so the omission is not merely descriptive.

**Tenet 5 (Occam's Razor Has Limits)** carries the method. The simplest model consistent with an agent's behavioural data is the one that posits nothing beyond it, and Tenet 5's point is that simplicity is an unreliable guide when knowledge is incomplete. An agent treating its most parsimonious adequate model as exhaustive is making exactly the inference Tenet 5 denies — and doing so at the moment when being wrong is unrecoverable.

Because the article builds a normative conclusion partly on consciousness doing causal work, it inherits the Map's [mechanism debt](/positions/quantum-interface/#mechanism-debt) rather than discharging it. **[P-Q3](/positions/quantum-interface/#p-q3)**, the bias-without-deviation dilemma, and **[P-Q10](/positions/quantum-interface/#p-q10)**, the absence of any worked toy model of the interface, are both open, and the Map's causal-selection thesis is citable downstream as a framework-internal coherence result rather than as established mental causation. The tenet-derived diagnosis above therefore reads no more confidently than the register does upstream: it is a candidate account of the failure mode, not a demonstrated one.

The article's core is less exposed to that debt than its diagnosis is. Representation adequacy, irreversibility and robustness require only that the agent cannot exclude the relevant models — not that the Map's interface is efficacious. Should the mechanism debt never be paid, the diagnosis weakens while the certification asymmetry stands: an agent contemplating an irreversible action against beings whose nature is unsettled has not shown the failure modes are bounded, and that is sufficient to withhold certification.
