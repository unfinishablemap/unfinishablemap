The Unfinishable Map models choice as one conscious selector settling among options the brain prepares. The strongest challenge to it is that the will looks divided. People reverse preferences they predicted and regret, keep some resolutions while breaking others, choose differently when an option is framed as one of a series, and confabulate reasons for choices they cannot see into. Five families of theory explain these facts with no single selector: Donald Davidson's partitioned mind, George Ainslie's bargaining interests, planner–doer models, hot/cool models, and multiple controllers with arbitration.

The verdict comes first. Partitioned and distributed architectures *predict* the fragmentation data, while a unitary selector only *accommodates* them. The data are modest evidence against an "organ"-style will, meaning a general faculty, strong or weak, that the person deploys; [Ainslie's test of the organ model](#the-organ-model-test) (explained below) is the one discriminating result in the literature. The data do not discriminate between the Map's selector and a physical arbitration process. Nothing found here supports the selector over its rivals. Its standing is coherence inside the Map's framework, and the Map's tenet on the limits of parsimony can block a dismissal on grounds of simplicity without ever counting in the selector's favour. Akrasia also sharpens the [luck objection](#luck-objection) against the Map's agent-causal reading, and this article leaves that objection unanswered.

## The Data a Theory of the Will Must Fit {#the-data}

- **O1. Preference reversal.** Agents prefer the larger, later reward at a distance and the smaller, sooner one up close, and they often predict and regret the switch.
- **O2. Failed and selective resolutions.** Resolutions fail, and the failures can be confined to particular kinds of choice.
- **O3. Bundling.** A choice changes when the same option is presented as one of a series.
- **O4. Self-opacity.** Agents lack access to the basis of their own choices, as the choice-blindness and confabulation results surveyed in [the interpreter-module article](/concepts/interpreter-module-narrative-construction-unity/) show.
- **O5. Disavowal.** Agents disown their choices afterwards or supply reasons that were not the causes.

Preference reversal under delay is strongly supported, though its functional form is contested. Bundling is a live hypothesis resting on two small studies. The neural two-system split and the strict dual-process typology are contested.

## Five Rivals Without a Single Selector {#five-rivals}

### Davidson's partition

Davidson's 1970 analysis of incontinence ends in a datum: "the actor cannot understand himself: he recognizes, in his own intentional behaviour, something essentially surd" (1980, p. 42). The akratic has a reason for what he does; "what he lacks is a reason for not letting his better reason for not doing a prevail" (n. 25, same page).

"Paradoxes of Irrationality" (1982) locates the irrational step. Irrationality is marked by "a mental cause that is not a reason" (p. 179 of the 2004 reprint), and Davidson places that cause between "quasi-independent structures that interact in ways the Plato Principle cannot accept or explain" (p. 181). The parts are functional rather than agents: "The idea of a quasi-autonomous division is not one that demands a little agent in the division" (p. 185).

The partition invites the charge of redescription: its parts are individuated by "the breakdown of reason relations" (p. 185), so they are defined by the phenomenon they explain. Davidson's divisions are interpretive rather than further entities.

### Ainslie's population of interests

The model of the person in Ainslie's *Breakdown of Will* (2001) has "no ego or judge or other philosopher-king, no organ of unity or continuity, although it will predict how such an organ may appear to operate" (p. 62). The person is a population of reward-seeking interests that discount hyperbolically, so preferences reverse as rewards draw near (O1). Their bargaining is recursive self-prediction rather than negotiation between literal future selves (p. 93). On this account a will emerges from personal rules that make each choice a precedent, with no organ required. He sets this against cognitivism's "reservation of ultimate choice to an imponderable ego" (p. 130), the slot the Map's agent-cause occupies.

### Planner–doer and hot/cool models

Thaler and Shefrin (1981, abstract) model the individual as "both a farsighted planner and a myopic doer". Metcalfe and Mischel (1999, abstract) make a cool cognitive system "the seat of self-regulation and self-control" against a hot emotional one. Both models move the controller into a subsystem rather than eliminating it.

Their neural basis is disputed: McClure and colleagues (2004, abstract) report that "two separate systems are involved in such decisions"; Kable and Glimcher (2007, abstract) report a set of regions that "tracks the revealed subjective value of delayed monetary rewards". Whether valuation is unified below the personal level is open, and every answer on the table is physical.

### Multiple controllers with arbitration

Daw, Niv and Dayan (2005, abstract) model the brain as containing "multiple systems for behavioral choice" and note that "such a surfeit of control raises an additional choice problem: how to arbitrate between the systems when they disagree". They propose arbitration according to uncertainty. A distributed architecture therefore has its own selection step, specified computationally and assigned to no selector. Critics of dual-process typology (Keren and Schul 2009; Melnikoff and Bargh 2018, who call it "a convenient and seductive myth") reject the two-type taxonomy without arguing, in their abstracts, for a unitary controller.

### Unity without a non-physical selector

Mele's account of akrasia (1987) needs no partition: one agent whose wants have motivational force out of line with its evaluations. A unitary *physical* agent is therefore available, so unity is not the only property in dispute. A non-physical selector faces the further question of what it adds to a physical arbitration step.

## Ainslie's Test of the Organ Model {#the-organ-model-test}

Chapter 8 of *Breakdown of Will* scores five theories of will against eight observed properties, among them the "organ" theory, "that the will is an entity characterizable as strong or weak in general and directed rather like a muscle by an independent intelligence" (p. 118). Ainslie finds the organ model silent on most of his tests, but it "is contradicted by observations that weakness of will can be specific to particular kinds of choice and by evidence of recursive choice-making" (p. 139).

Ainslie's scoring table (p. 125) is his own, so it is advocacy rather than an independent test, and the independent evidence is thinner. Kirby and Guastello (2001, abstract) report that a third of participants in their first experiment who could choose pair by pair reversed a prior preference and took the larger reward when the choice was framed as part of a series. Hofmeyr, Ainslie, Charlton and Ross (2011, abstract) found smokers choosing the larger, later reward more often when bundling was suggested or forced; "Non-smokers showed neither pattern", and the authors qualify the result "If replicated". The bundling prediction is a live hypothesis on that basis.

The test constrains how the Map may describe its selector. Lapses specific to particular choices and recursive choice fit a will deployed "like an arm or leg" poorly and a population of interests well. No Map article checked treats the selector as a reservoir of strength, and it must stay that way: a selector choosing among options the brain prepares ([control-theoretic will](/concepts/control-theoretic-will/)) is candidate-relative and never a faculty of general strength.

## What a Unitary Selector Must Add {#accommodation}

The comparison in this section is the Map's own. A distributed architecture derives O1–O5 from independently measured parts: hyperbolic valuation yields O1 and O3, arbitration yields O2, and a late, separate reporting system yields O4 and O5.

A unitary selector accommodates each datum only with an added assumption. O1 and O3 need a present-weighted currency, which is ad hoc unless the felt-valence currency [the Map leans toward](/topics/valence-and-conscious-selection/) is independently shown to be hyperbolic. O2 needs a low-bandwidth, fallible selector, which [the control-theoretic will](/concepts/control-theoretic-will/) motivates independently. O4 and O5 need the [verification limit](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a4) on introspected causation and the interpreter distinction, both independently motivated. The comparison of likelihoods therefore runs against the unitary selector on O1–O3 and is roughly even on O4–O5.

What does a unitary selector predict that its rivals do not? The akratic owns the act, unlike the patient with an [anarchic hand](/topics/anarchic-hand-and-action-ownership/), but ownership is a computed signal that every party accommodates. Responsibility practices presuppose one agent, but Korsgaard's practical unity supplies that without a posit ([discussed below](#practical-unity)). Nothing remains.

### The candidate-selection trilemma {#the-candidate-selection-trilemma}

A unitary selector can be read in three ways.

1. **An organ-style will.** Ainslie's two observations disconfirm it at modest strength.
2. **A candidate-relative selector**, choosing among options a distributed system supplies. It coexists with every rival but faces a trilemma in the akratic case. (i) If it picks what the supplied values favour, the akratic pattern belongs to the valuation and the selector explains none of it; that is the [nearness to epiphenomenalism](/positions/quantum-interface/#mechanism-debt) the Map concedes for observer-indistinguishable selection, restated at the level of psychology. (ii) If it departs from those values, the departure has no reason, and Davidson's surd has moved into the subject. (iii) If it tracks felt valence, the hyperbolic shape must lie in the felt currency, so the selector supplies the medium of choosing but not the explanation of the failure.
3. **Interface relocation**, the split-brain move, which puts the fragmentation in the expression channel and leaves the selector whole. Because it is always available, it discriminates nothing. [The split-brain article](/topics/split-brain-consciousness/) already concedes that agency-unity "is what a coordinated substrate would produce whether or not the phenomenal subject is single".

On this evidence the selection reading of akrasia supplies a vocabulary and a locus for agent causation, but no prediction.

## Why Akrasia Sharpens the Luck Objection {#luck-objection}

Davidson's partition keeps the irrational step from being a reasoned act of the whole person, because the non-reason cause runs between structures. A unitary agent-cause has no other structure to put it in. The akratic has a reason for *x* and no reason for *x* rather than *y*. The agent-cause therefore selects against the agent's all-things-considered ranking, not against all of the agent's reasons. The Map's standard reply to the luck objection, that selection on reasons is contrastively explicable ([agent causation](/concepts/agent-causation/#the-luck-objection), point 3), fails for exactly that contrast.

Neil Levy presses the general form of the objection. He argues that "agent-causation must be rational all by itself for the luck objection to be averted" (2011, p. 66), and that if reasons only structure the options, "agent-causation itself is irrational, and therefore arbitrary" (p. 67). On akrasia his conclusion is blunt: "Far from being paradigmatically free actions, akratic actions are lucky actions" (p. 153). Levy argues that on extant libertarianisms, event-causal and agent-causal alike, luck defeats moral responsibility (p. 44).

The nearest published move from the other side found here is Helen Steward's. In *A Metaphysics for Freedom* (2012) she observes that "my best reasons do not simply set my body into inexorable motion" (p. 147). Steward argues that weakness of will, whatever its value to an agent, "might nevertheless be essential to the very existence of such an agent" (p. 161), because a settler of matters at the time of action must be able not to act on an intention already formed. Steward is an agency incompatibilist whose settlers are animals, human and many non-human, and she distinguishes her view sharply from Cartesian dualism (p. 16). On the Map's reading her move explains why a settler must be *capable* of akrasia. It does not explain why this agent acted akratically on this occasion, which is what the luck objection asks. Franklin's reply to Levy on agent causation and akrasia (2015) was located by title only, and nothing here depends on it.

Two replies are open to the Map, and each has a cost. (a) It can accept the surd as the price of sourcehood, treating the agent's exercise of power as an explanatory terminus, the direction Steward's argument points. (b) It can confine agent-causal selection to choices with no settled ranking, which concedes that akratic acts are not agent-caused against the ranking and so withdraws the selection reading of akrasia. Neither reply is argued in full here. The objection stands unanswered, as it does in [the akrasia article](/topics/akrasia-and-weakness-of-will/).

## Felt Unity and Korsgaard's Practical Unity {#practical-unity}

Felt unity is not evidence of a unitary selector. Christine Korsgaard writes that "The 'phenomenon' of the unity of consciousness is nothing more than the lack of any perceived difficulty in the coordination of psychic functions" (1996 reprint, p. 377). The Map applies the same deflation to agency, as the first of [three exposures](/topics/kants-paralogisms-and-the-maps-subject/#three-exposures) in the Kant article does for felt mine-ness. The akratic's two pulls are present together in one field, and that is compatible with a partitioned will. Akrasia is the case of phenomenal unity with divided control, so phenomenal unity does not by itself supply a unified locus of selection.

Korsgaard also supplies the rival framing. On her account the "conception of yourself as a unified agent is not based on a metaphysical theory" (p. 369). Unity is a practical requirement: conflict among desires must be settled for the person to act at all. On the Map's reading, practical unity gives its ethics everything it needs from a unified agent, without the posit. The Map can adopt it and keep the posit on its independent grounds of agent causation and indexicality, as [the Kant article](/topics/kants-paralogisms-and-the-maps-subject/) requires. The cost is that unity of agency then gives the posit no support.

## Relation to Site Perspective

This section gives the Map's interpretation; none of the authors above shares the Map's metaphysics.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism).** Davidson and Ainslie are physicalists, but their architectures say nothing about whether experience reduces to physical process, and the fragmentation data neither support nor threaten the first tenet.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction).** The fragmentation data give the selector, where the third tenet meets psychology, no support, though unsupported does not mean absent. The selector remains, in the tenets' own words, "a posit the interface argument leaves open, not a result it secures" ([Tenet 3's standing](/tenets/#tenet-3-standing)). The article takes no view on how widely conscious selection operates, and it constrains how the selector may be described: candidate-relative, low-bandwidth and fallible, never a faculty of general strength. Ownership, felt unity and co-present pulls are features the Map ascribes to experience, and they cannot double as evidence for its selector.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits).** The tenet blocks dropping the selector merely because a distributed architecture is simpler. It gives no reason to keep the selector, and it binds the Map equally: a single selector cannot be claimed as the simpler explanation either.

**[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) and [No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds).** The akratic data locate nothing at the quantum level, and the indexical subject the fourth tenet needs rests on grounds independent of unity of agency. Whether the aspects the Map ascribes to experience require a subject that persists, or only one present at the moment of choice, is a tension this article leaves open.

Speculatively, if partitions were themselves subjects, intrapersonal conflict would become an instance of the conflict-rule gap the [subject census](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc2) already records.
