Counterfactual reasoning—the capacity to imagine what isn't, wasn't, or might not be—poses a challenge for physicalist accounts of mind that goes beyond the familiar [hard problem](/topics/hard-problem-of-consciousness/). The hard problem asks why any processing is conscious; the counterfactual problem asks something more specific. Representing a counterfactual requires holding a scenario in a dual status—treated as informatively real for reasoning purposes while simultaneously tagged as non-actual—and in conscious reasoning this dual status is *phenomenally present* to the reasoner, not merely encoded in a data structure. Computers manipulate counterfactual representations routinely (game trees, simulations, planning algorithms), yet nothing in their processing is known to involve grasping non-actuality as non-actual. The Unfinishable Map argues that this phenomenal grasp of dual status—actuality and non-actuality held together in conscious awareness—points toward an irreducible mental dimension that formal computation alone has not been shown to provide.

The definitional question of what [counterfactual reasoning](/concepts/counterfactual-reasoning/) involves is addressed in the concept article. The question here is philosophical: what does this capacity tell us about the nature of consciousness itself?

## The Puzzle of Representing Non-Actuality

A physicalist can explain *why* representing non-actuality is adaptive: organisms that anticipate unrealised dangers and opportunities outcompete those that merely react. And computational systems already represent counterfactuals—chess engines evaluate millions of hypothetical board states, weather models simulate scenarios that never occur, planning algorithms explore branching futures. So the puzzle is not that physical systems *cannot process* counterfactual information. They can and do.

The puzzle is about dual-status awareness. When a human entertains a counterfactual, the scenario is phenomenally present—vivid, evaluatively coloured, emotionally weighted—while simultaneously marked as *not actual*. We *know* the imagined scenario isn't real, and that knowledge is itself part of the phenomenal character of the experience. A chess engine's evaluation of an unexplored branch has no analogous awareness of the branch's non-actuality; the representation is simply another data structure to be processed.

The puzzle sharpens further: what is the phenomenal character of entertaining a counterfactual? There is something it is like (Nagel, 1974) to imagine a world where you chose differently—a felt quality of exploring an alternative that is neither perception nor memory nor hallucination. This distinctive phenomenology suggests counterfactual imagination involves a distinctive conscious capacity, not just weakened perception or rearranged memory traces, one that involves grasping the *modal status* of a representation (actual vs. merely possible) from the inside.

## The Dual Representation Problem

Counterfactual reasoning requires holding two incompatible representations simultaneously: the world as it is and the world as it might be. The reasoner must keep these distinct—confusing counterfactual for actual would be delusion—while allowing information to flow between them. "If I had left earlier, I would have caught the train" requires knowing you didn't leave earlier (actual), imagining that you did (counterfactual), and tracing the causal consequences within the imagined frame while comparing them to actual outcomes.

This dual representation has specific cognitive demands:

**Explicit negation**: The counterfactual premise must be marked as non-actual. The mind must represent "not-P" while reasoning within a frame where P obtains. This requires [metarepresentation](/concepts/metacognition/#the-metarepresentation-threshold)—representing one's own representations as bearing a particular status (actual vs. imagined).

**Causal modelling**: Tracing consequences within a counterfactual frame requires understanding causal structure, not merely statistical association. "If the glass hadn't been pushed, it wouldn't have fallen" requires grasping that pushing caused falling, not merely that pushing and falling co-occurred. This causal grasp requires more than pattern extraction from observed regularities—on Pearl's (2009) account, an explicit causal model, which machines can implement, so the demand is not yet for consciousness.

**Evaluative comparison**: The point of counterfactual reasoning is typically to draw a conclusion—regret, relief, planning, learning (Roese, 1997). This requires comparing the actual and counterfactual scenarios against values or goals. The comparison is phenomenally present rather than merely computational: we *feel* the regret of the better alternative not taken.

[Working memory](/concepts/working-memory/) capacity constrains this dual representation. Cowan (2001) puts human capacity at about four chunks (three to five), substantially less than the older 7±2 estimate; a review of the comparative literature puts chimpanzee capacity lower still, at a contested 2±1 (Read, Manrique & Walker, 2022). Great apes show some future-oriented behaviour, such as saving tools for later use (Mulcahy & Call, 2006), though Suddendorf and Corballis (2007) found "as yet no convincing evidence" of mental time travel in non-human animals. But the *extended* counterfactual reasoning characteristic of humans—constructing elaborate alternative histories, reasoning about unrealised possibilities across multiple causal steps, sustaining the dual-status awareness described here—appears qualitatively limited in non-human primates. This pattern aligns with the prediction that full dual representation demands conscious workspace capacity beyond what [baseline cognition](/concepts/baseline-cognition/) provides. The [consciousness threshold](/topics/consciousness-and-cognitive-distinctiveness/) article reads such human–ape differences as a cluster appearing where neural architecture becomes an effective interface for consciousness, while conceding that comparative data cannot exclude readings on which experience merely accompanies the capacities; counterfactual reasoning inherits that limit.

## Why Physicalism Struggles

The physicalist must explain counterfactual reasoning as nothing more than neural activity. Several approaches are available, but each, on the Map's reading, leaves a difficulty specific to the *dual-status* character of counterfactual thought:

**The simulation account**: The brain "simulates" alternative scenarios by recombining remembered material: counterfactual thinking recruits the core network used for remembering the past and imagining the future (Van Hoeck et al., 2013). Prefrontal monitoring can tag representations as imagined rather than perceived, and Byrne's mental-model theory already models the dual status: a counterfactual conditional is understood by representing both the suppositional and the presupposed case (Byrne & Tasso, 1999). That captures the structure. The residue the Map presses is phenomenal: the reasoner must treat the simulated scenario as informatively real (tracing its causal consequences, feeling its emotional weight) while *simultaneously* maintaining awareness that it is not actual. This is not just the hard problem of why tagging feels like something—it is the problem of how two opposed epistemic attitudes toward the same representation coexist in a single conscious moment.

**The computational account**: Counterfactual reasoning is formal manipulation of data structures—branching operations over possible-world models. But computational counterfactual processing lacks the dual-status phenomenology entirely. The system does not *grasp* that the branch it evaluates is non-actual; that distinction exists only in the programmer's interpretation, not in the processing itself. If human counterfactual reasoning is just computation, the felt awareness of non-actuality is either identical to that computation, which the Map's [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism) tenet denies, or epiphenomenal, which its [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) tenet rejects—a conflict with the Map's commitments rather than a refutation.

**The predictive processing account**: The brain is a prediction machine, and counterfactuals are predictions about what would happen under alternative conditions. In its developed form it reaches the phenomenology itself: Seth (2014) proposes that "counterfactually-rich" generative models, encoding how sensory input would change under possible actions, determine how *present*—how real—a perceived object feels, which offers a mechanism for the felt marker of reality and its absence. The elaboration of retrospective counterfactuals ("what if I had studied philosophy instead?") far beyond a bare error signal can be treated as earning its keep: on Roese's (1997) functionalist review, counterfactuals yield causal inferences and their net effect is beneficial. What remains, on the Map's reading, is how a scenario can be vivid in content while felt as unreal, and why either marker is felt at all; whether that residue goes beyond the hard problem is what the physicalist disputes.

Each account captures the functional structure. The question the Map presses is narrower: how the mind sustains two opposed stances toward a single representation, and why this dual stance seems to have a phenomenal character formal computation lacks.

One pressure qualifies every claim in this section. Large language models generate counterfactual scenarios fluently, maintain a counterfactual premise across extended reasoning, and draw conclusions within the imagined frame—apparently without phenomenal consciousness, a challenge case the concept article on [counterfactual reasoning](/concepts/counterfactual-reasoning/) treats as one where the Map's predictions are unclear. If such systems can perform increasingly sophisticated counterfactual *processing* without dual-status awareness, then the claim that phenomenal grasp is *necessary* (rather than merely the biological route to the capacity) is under pressure. The arguments below should accordingly be read as suggesting that conscious counterfactual reasoning has a character formal computation has not been shown to share—not as establishing that it could not in principle be matched.

## Counterfactual Reasoning and Bidirectional Interaction

The Map's [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) tenet holds that consciousness causally influences physical outcomes. Deliberation is where the Map most naturally locates that influence, but counterfactual reasoning supplies a picture of how the tenet would operate if it holds rather than independent evidence that it does: each step below depends on premises a physicalist need not grant.

Consider deliberation. When choosing between options, you construct counterfactual scenarios for each—imagining what would follow from each choice—and then select based on the comparison. If this deliberation is genuinely conscious and genuinely influences which action you take, then consciousness is doing causal work: the phenomenal experience of imagining alternatives shapes the physical outcome (your behaviour). A reductive physicalist accepts this conditional, since on that view conscious deliberation just is a neural process; it supports interactionism only if the phenomenal side is already taken to be non-physical.

The rival reading—that the neural correlates of deliberation do all the causal work while conscious experience merely accompanies them—is not defeated by the [baseline cognition](/concepts/baseline-cognition/) evidence. Great apes possess sophisticated neural machinery for decision-making, yet the *extended* counterfactual reasoning humans perform remains qualitatively limited in them, which a physicalist attributes to differences in prefrontal development, working memory architecture, and language capacity. The Map reads expanded [working memory](/concepts/working-memory/) as itself a [consciousness-dependent amplification](/concepts/consciousness-as-amplifier/) of baseline capacity, but an accompaniment reading predicts the same human–ape pattern, as the [comparative audit](/topics/consciousness-and-cognitive-distinctiveness/#what-the-comparative-pattern-can-and-cannot-establish) concedes. What favours the Map's reading is its [case for mental causation](/topics/evolutionary-case-for-mental-causation/), which the rival does not share, rather than the gap itself.

The Map's [agent-causal](/concepts/agent-causation/) framework locates the agent's selection at quantum indeterminacies, and the [concept article](/concepts/counterfactual-reasoning/) proposes that counterfactual simulation is how the options are evaluated before one becomes actual. If so, counterfactual comparison may be the *mechanism* by which consciousness exercises causal influence—a speculative but coherent model of the Map's [Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) tenet in action.

## The Ontological Status of Counterfactuals

A deeper question lurks behind counterfactual reasoning: what, if anything, do counterfactual scenarios represent?

The Map's [No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds) tenet rejects the interpretation where all quantum possibilities actualise in branching universes. If many-worlds were true, counterfactual reasoning would be reasoning about real (though inaccessible) branches of reality. But the Map holds that there is one actual world, and alternatives that don't obtain genuinely don't exist.

This creates a philosophical puzzle. Counterfactual reasoning represents states of affairs that, on the Map's view, have no ontological home. "If I had chosen differently" refers to a scenario that neither exists, existed, nor will exist. Yet counterfactual reasoning is cognitively real, phenomenally vivid, and causally efficacious (it guides action). The mind grasps something that doesn't exist and uses it to shape what does.

This capacity to represent genuine non-being—to think about what isn't—may be distinctively mental in a specific way. Physical systems *process* information about non-actual states—computers simulate counterfactual scenarios, thermostats respond to deviations from set points—without, so far as anyone can tell, grasping that the represented state of affairs does not obtain. The *phenomenal awareness* of non-actuality, the felt sense that "this is not real but I am treating it as if it were," appears to be something only conscious minds achieve.

Examined carefully, this everyday capacity reveals the mind doing something that computational processing alone has not been shown to do: constructing representations with no actual referent, *consciously recognising them as non-actual*, and using them to causally influence what becomes actual. This loop—from non-actual representation through conscious evaluation to actual outcome—requires the dual-status awareness that distinguishes phenomenal counterfactual reasoning from mere computational branching.

## The Counterfactual Void

The [counterfactual void](/voids/counterfactual-void/) maps the limits of this capacity. Spontaneous imagination defaults to minimal departures from actuality: people change reality along predictable "fault lines," imagining alternatives to actions rather than inactions, to controllable rather than uncontrollable events, and to the most recent event in a sequence (Byrne, 2005)—a psychological counterpart to Lewis's nearest possible worlds. The default is a bias rather than a ceiling, since deliberate modal reasoning reaches further, but radical counterfactuals (worlds with different physics, alien forms of consciousness, fundamentally different categories of experience) thin out even under effort, losing phenomenal richness until only schematic labels ("a world with different physics") remain. This pattern matches debates in modal epistemology over whether bare conceivability is a reliable guide to genuine possibility (Van Inwagen, 1998): the further imagination strays from experienced actuality, the less the resulting representations earn epistemic weight.

The limit does not by itself favour a phenomenal over a computational account: a resource-bounded system whose models were learned from actual experience would show it too, and Byrne's own explanation is representational—which aspects of reality people change depends on which possibilities they mentally represent. What the Map draws is narrower. The thinning is a loss of *phenomenal* content—formal methods still represent distant alternatives, as the void article notes, without the grasp we have of nearby ones—which fits the view that we imagine by modifying experienced actuality: subtracting one element, adding another, tracing what changes. That grounding would explain both the vividness of counterfactual reasoning (we *feel* what the alternative would be like) and its anchoring to the imaginer's cognitive architecture.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Map's tenets illuminate counterfactual reasoning from multiple angles:

**Dualism**: Physical and computational systems process counterfactual information routinely, so far as anyone can tell without awareness of its modal status; the *phenomenal grasp* of non-actuality, on the Map's view, resists reduction to information processing. If this dual-status awareness is irreducible, it provides a distinctive argument for dualism: not from qualia or the explanatory gap, but from the mind's ability to *consciously apprehend* what doesn't exist.

**Bidirectional Interaction**: Counterfactual reasoning may be the mechanism through which consciousness exercises causal influence: weighing imagined alternatives against values could be how consciousness shapes physical outcomes, if the Map's interactionist picture is correct.

**Minimal Quantum Interaction**: If consciousness influences the physical world through the smallest possible intervention at quantum-level decision points, counterfactual reasoning may be the cognitive process that makes this possible. Evaluating alternatives before selection is precisely what a minimal-interaction model requires—consciousness need not restructure neural activity wholesale but only bias which quantum possibility becomes actual, guided by counterfactual comparison.

**No Many Worlds**: If unrealised possibilities don't exist as parallel branches, then counterfactual reasoning represents genuine non-being. The mind's ability to think about what isn't, in a universe where non-actual alternatives have no ontological reality, underscores the distinctive nature of consciousness as something that transcends the merely actual.

**Occam's Razor Has Limits**: The simplest account—counterfactual reasoning as neural simulation and nothing more—explains the functional structure, the shared episodic network and, through prefrontal and working-memory differences, plausibly the primate gap. What it leaves unexplained, on the Map's reading, is the dual-status phenomenology; where knowledge of consciousness is this incomplete, the richer explanation, involving irreducible conscious processing, may be closer to the truth despite its ontological cost.
