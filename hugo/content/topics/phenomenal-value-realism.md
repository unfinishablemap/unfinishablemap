Where does value come from? Not instrumental value—a hammer's usefulness, a house's price—but *intrinsic* value, the kind that makes something worth caring about for its own sake. The Unfinishable Map holds that intrinsic value is a feature of conscious experience itself. The felt badness of suffering *is* badness. The felt goodness of joy *is* goodness. This position is where the Map's metaphysics meets the questions that matter most.

## The Claim in Plain Terms

Phenomenal value realism says that certain qualities of experience are simultaneously descriptive and normative. Pain describes how an experience feels *and* constitutes something bad—one property under two descriptions rather than two that happen to coincide.

Sharon Hewitt Rawlette, in *The Feeling of Value* (2016), puts it directly: "When we feel pleasure, we are feeling intrinsic goodness itself." The identity claim is stronger than typical hedonism, which argues that pleasure causes or indicates value: Rawlette argues pleasure *is* value.

Three tests support the identity:

**The coherence test.** "This is intensely pleasant, but there is nothing good about it." The sentence is not merely false—it is incoherent, like "this triangle has four sides." Someone who sincerely asserts it means something different by "pleasant" than everyone else does.

**The transparency test.** When experiencing pleasure, you do not infer its goodness from a theory. The goodness is transparent in the experience—attending to the pleasure just *is* attending to something good. There is no residual question "granted this is pleasant, but *why* is it good?" The question dissolves because goodness is what you are already attending to. (A sceptic might press the unreliability of introspection—people misidentify the sources of their emotions. But the position depends on the character of experience *as experienced* rather than on introspection about *causes*: you can be wrong about why you feel pain, and not about its feeling bad.)

**The explanatory economy test.** If pleasure and goodness were distinct properties, their perfect correlation would demand explanation. What mechanism ensures every pleasure is good? The identity thesis eliminates this burden: they correlate because they are the same thing. (This is a narrower point than Occam's Razor, which the Map's [fifth tenet](/tenets/#occams-limits) holds unreliable when knowledge is incomplete—the two-property hypothesis incurs a specific explanatory debt that the identity hypothesis does not.)

### Pain Dissociation: A Challenge

[Pain asymbolia](/topics/pain-consciousness-and-causal-power/) patients feel pain sensations without finding them distressing. This complicates the naive identity of pain and badness rather than refuting it: ordinary-language "pain" conflates a sensory quality (nociception reaching consciousness) with an affective one (the felt *unpleasantness*), and the identity claim applies to the affective component. Asymbolia patients lack it, detecting tissue damage without experiencing it as bad—where felt badness is absent, so is the disvalue, as the thesis predicts.

## The Metaethical Landscape

Phenomenal value realism sits uneasily in standard metaethical categories, and where it breaks them is where its claims are clearest.

### The Queerness Objection Transformed

Mackie's "argument from queerness" targeted Moore's non-naturalism, on which goodness is a simple, indefinable property recognised directly: perceiving a non-natural property would need a special faculty, and objective values would be strange entities floating free in reality.

Phenomenal value realism transforms the objection. Value is a feature of conscious experience, accessed by having experiences, rather than a property floating in Platonic space. The "special faculty" is just consciousness itself, and the intrinsic badness of pain is constitutive of what pain feels like rather than detected by a sixth moral sense.

Queerness worries do not entirely dissolve. If consciousness is irreducible (the Map's [dualism](/tenets/#dualism)), phenomenal properties are themselves "queer" from a physicalist perspective. But the queerness shifts to consciousness rather than attaching additionally to moral properties, so having accepted irreducible phenomenal properties, adding that some are value-properties costs nothing further.

### The Is-Ought Gap

Hume's is-ought gap is one phenomenal value realism claims to dissolve: some experiential properties are simultaneously descriptive (how experiences feel) and normative (what intrinsic goodness is).

Two readings pull apart. On the logical reading, no "ought" follows from purely "is" statements without a smuggled normative premise—which the position never violates, since it identifies the normative fact with the phenomenal fact rather than deriving one from the other. On the stronger ontological reading, no property can be both descriptive and normative; that claim the position rejects, since the experiential quality of suffering just is intrinsically bad, by identity rather than derivation.

### Naturalism's Open Question

Analytic naturalists identify moral properties with natural ones—"good" means something like "pleasure" or "desire-satisfaction"—and Moore's Open Question Argument challenges this: for any natural property N we can coherently ask "X is N, but is it good?", which suggests goodness and N are conceptually distinct.

Rawlette's response is the transparency test above: the question only seems open while pleasure is considered abstractly, and closes under direct attention to its phenomenal character. Openness at the conceptual level does not survive phenomenological engagement.

The Map extends this beyond hedonism. The same closing-under-attention applies to other phenomenal features: genuine understanding feels intrinsically valuable when you are experiencing it, not merely when you describe it. Authentic connection carries its significance in the experience itself.

## What the Alternatives Miss

### Preference Satisfaction

The dominant approach in economics and much of AI alignment grounds value in preference satisfaction: what is good for you is getting what you want. The Map rejects this. Evolution shaped preferences for reproductive fitness rather than flourishing, and you can prefer what makes your experience worse—addiction, anxious status games, growth-blocking comfort.

More fundamentally, preferences are *about* experiential states they do not capture. You prefer to avoid pain because pain feels bad: the preference tracks the value without constituting it. A system that satisfies preferences while generating no conscious experience has optimised a proxy.

This matters urgently for [AI alignment](/topics/purpose-and-alignment/), where maximising preference satisfaction targets a proxy that can come apart from what it tracks. Preferences offer epistemic objectivity (choices are observable) without metaphysical objectivity (what people want is not what is genuinely good). [Experiential alignment](/concepts/experiential-alignment/) targets the felt quality of conscious life instead; see [alignment-in-objective-experiential-terms](/topics/alignment-in-objective-experiential-terms/) for the full case.

### Error Theory

Error theorists—most influentially Mackie in *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977)—hold that all moral claims are false because nothing has objective value: the universe is matter in motion, and "good" and "bad" are projections we impose.

The response is direct: attend to actual suffering—not the concept of it, not a description of neural activation patterns, the experience itself. The claim "this suffering contains nothing bad" is no bold thesis but a failure to grasp what "bad" refers to. Error theory draws its plausibility from considering value abstractly and loses it on contact with actual experience.

### Constructivism

Constructivists—Rawls's contractualism, Korsgaard's Kantian appeal to practical identity—hold that value is built through rational procedures, making moral facts products of reasoning rather than features of experience waiting to be discovered.

The Map's objection: consciousness was valuable before any reasoning being existed to construct value systems. The suffering of the first creature capable of feeling pain was bad because of what pain feels like from the inside, well before any social practice could label it so.

Constructivism also cannot explain moral authority. If value is constructed, why should the construction bind us? The answer—because rational agents would endorse it—assumes rationality is authoritative, which requires further construction. The regress terminates only at something that matters intrinsically: the felt quality of experience.

The empirical literature's "constructivism" is a sharper, inferential thesis, treated below as an open problem (see [the construction problem](#the-construction-problem)) rather than an alternative already answered.

## Beyond Hedonism

Rawlette defends a hedonistic version: pleasure and pain exhaust intrinsic value. The Map extends this to **phenomenal value pluralism**, on which several features of experience carry intrinsic value—[evaluative qualia](/concepts/evaluative-phenomenal-character/), whose character is simultaneously descriptive and normative:

| Feature | Phenomenal Character | Value Dimension |
|---------|---------------------|-----------------|
| Hedonic valence | Felt pleasure/pain quality | Well-being core |
| Aesthetic quality | Beauty/ugliness in experience | Aesthetic value |
| Meaning | Felt significance of engagement | Eudaimonic value |
| Agency | Felt authorship of choices | Autonomy value |
| Understanding | Phenomenal "click" of grasping | Epistemic value |
| Connection | Felt quality of authentic relating | Relational value |

Each row names a feature of experience that contributes to intrinsic value. The list differs from an objective list theory, whose items are valuable independent of experience; here all value resides in how things are experienced.

A dependency note forestalls an apparent circularity with [meaning-of-life](/topics/meaning-of-life/), which grounds life's meaning partly in phenomenal value while the table lists meaning as a value dimension. The dependence runs one way: *felt significance* is the primitive datum, and the meaning *of a life* is a higher-order structure built from it together with agency, understanding, connection and narrative integration.

### Why Extend Beyond Hedonism?

Three considerations motivate the extension:

**The meaning problem.** A difficult creative project can involve frustration and exhaustion while the engagement feels intrinsically significant, and the phenomenal quality of meaningful activity—absorbing, purposive, connected to something larger—resists reduction to a balance of pleasures over pains.

**The aesthetic problem.** [Beauty and ugliness](/topics/aesthetics-and-consciousness/) come apart from pleasure in both directions—pleasant without being beautiful (a warm bath), beautiful without being pleasant (the terrifying sublime)—so aesthetic quality looks like a distinct dimension.

**The agency problem.** Authoring one's actions carries significance independent of hedonic payoff, which makes sense under [agent causation](/concepts/agent-causation/): if consciousness genuinely shapes outcomes, exercising that capacity is intrinsically significant rather than a means to pleasant ones.

### How Do Value Dimensions Trade Off?

Pluralism raises incommensurability: how do we weigh aesthetic beauty against hedonic pleasure, or profound but unpleasant understanding against shallow contentment?

The Map offers no algorithmic answer—this may be irreducibly a matter for wise judgement. Three structural points help.

**Lexical priority is unlikely.** Extreme hedonic suffering probably overrides aesthetic appreciation—torture cannot be redeemed by making it beautiful—but beyond the extremes the dimensions interact complexly.

**Context shapes weight.** Hedonic suffering takes priority in crisis, meaning and understanding in reflection, aesthetic quality in creation, and the phenomenal features themselves indicate which dimension is salient.

**Integration matters.** The best experiences combine dimensions—meaningful work that is also pleasant, understanding that is also elegant—so the ideal is rich integration rather than maximising any one.

## Why Dualism Matters Here

Phenomenal value realism can be held within physicalist frameworks—Rawlette herself does not commit to dualism. The Map's [dualism](/tenets/#dualism) gives the position distinctive weight.

### Value as Ontologically Fundamental

If phenomenal properties are not captured by physical description, value inherits that irreducibility: the badness is intrinsic to what pain phenomenally is rather than a separate property added to a neutral quale, and value is built into what fundamentally exists rather than projected onto physical reality.

This addresses a persistent challenge for moral realism: where do objective values "fit" in the natural world? The physicalist struggles to locate them and the non-naturalist posits mysterious entities, whereas the dualist who grounds value in consciousness can answer that values fit wherever consciousness fits. Moore's simple, non-natural property is thereby located in what *Principia Ethica* (1903) lacked the tools to specify: the qualitative character of conscious experience.

The [zombie thought experiment](/concepts/philosophical-zombies/) illustrates this: a universe physically identical to ours but lacking experience would contain no value at all, its moral emptiness just *being* its experiential emptiness.

### Against Epiphenomenal Value

Some philosophers accept phenomenal consciousness and deny its causal efficacy ([epiphenomenalism](/concepts/epiphenomenalism/)): our sense that suffering motivates escape is illusory, since the physical brain state causes both the suffering and the escape behaviour.

The Map's [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) tenet rejects epiphenomenalism. This matters for value: epiphenomenal value is practically inert—you cannot act to reduce it, since your actions are determined by physical states the value does not influence. The [type-token causation distinction](/topics/consciousness-and-integrated-information/#does-quantum-selection-escape-this-charge) supplies the mechanism by which value's efficacy can survive even when statistically invisible: at the type level, value-laden experience adds nothing to Born-rule predictions; at the token level it determines which outcome actualises here, which is where the moral weight of *this* suffering lives. The [value-blind vs value-sensitive selection](/topics/valence-and-conscious-selection/) fork sharpens the stake: even granting that consciousness acts causally through attention, if the specifically *evaluative* dimension is idle in the selection mechanism, value's non-epiphenomenality is weaker than it looks.

The self-undermining argument applies: epiphenomenalists trust their introspective judgements enough to debate them, yet on their own view those judgements are causally disconnected from the consciousness they describe. So too for value, where judgements about the good would be only accidentally related to it.

### Indexical Value

The Map's emphasis on [indexical identity](/concepts/haecceity/) extends to value. *This* experience matters, not just experiences-of-this-type: your particular suffering is not interchangeable with a qualitatively identical suffering in someone else. Where most value theories treat value as type-based, the Map suggests it is token-based. This connects to the [No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds) tenet, since if every quantum event spawns branches where all outcomes occur, moral urgency dissolves.

Creating a replica therefore does not transfer your experiential value to it: the replica's token experiences matter without being continuations of yours—a constraint on upload and duplication that patternist value theories cannot supply. The token claim needs only a momentary locus, while the *continuation* claim rests on the persisting subject the Map asserts on agency grounds rather than on value ([two layers](/concepts/no-self-objection-to-phenomenal-value/#implications)).

## The Hardest Objection

The strongest challenge comes from [illusionism](/concepts/illusionism/): phenomenal consciousness as an introspective illusion. With no genuine "what it's like," phenomenal value realism has no referent, and value reduces to functional states tracking evolutionary fitness.

Three responses bear weight.

**The regress response.** That the illusion requires someone to be deceived assumes "seeming" is itself phenomenal—precisely what illusionism denies. It marks a framework boundary rather than an in-framework refutation.

**The practical response.** Illusionists take painkillers, avoid distress and care about others' welfare, which is unmotivated if phenomenal value is illusory: the functional state generating "reports of badness" would carry no normative significance.

**The contemplative response.** [Contemplative traditions](/topics/contemplative-practice-as-philosophical-evidence/) offer mixed evidence. Some practitioners (particularly Hindu and Christian) report that sustained attention reveals valence as intrinsic to experience rather than attributed by judgement; if illusionism were correct, deeper investigation should instead reveal the construction mechanism. Buddhist *vipassana* practitioners often report the opposite: valence arises from craving and aversion and dissolves through equanimity practice. The realist can reply that equanimity transforms the *reactive* relationship to felt valence rather than eliminating it—the badness of pain is still apprehended while the secondary suffering of resistance falls away.

A neighbouring objection grants the phenomenology and denies the *someone*; [another](/topics/the-experience-requirement-on-well-being/#experience-machine) grants both and denies that welfare tracks phenomenology. Buddhist *anātman* with momentariness, and Parfit's reductionism, hold that no self persists beneath the stream of experience; if felt badness must be bad *for* a subject, the recipient looks like a fiction. The Map's reply, at [the no-self objection to phenomenal value](/concepts/no-self-objection-to-phenomenal-value/#maps-reply), is that the value chain needs [a momentary experiential locus](/topics/moral-status-threshold-or-degrees/#presence)—the for-me-ness of this pain, now—rather than a persisting self, and momentariness multiplies that locus rather than removing it. The reply is unrefuted and contested, and persistence-dependent goods rest on the subject the Map asserts on other grounds.

## Challenges Requiring Development

### The Sign of Aggregate Experience {#suffering-focused-asymmetry}

A one-sided ledger tempts anyone holding this position. If all value is experiential, a world without experience contains no good, so extinguishing consciousness looks like pure loss—and the Map's [case for restraint by artificial agents](/topics/representation-adequacy-and-irreversible-intervention/) would have a value argument to lean on. That inference counts one column. A world without experience also contains no bad.

Suffering-focused ethics states the objection at full strength, and phenomenal value realism strengthens it. David Benatar's asymmetry (2006, pp. 30, 38) holds that the presence of pain is bad and the presence of pleasure good, while the *absence* of pain is good even when nobody enjoys that good and the absence of pleasure is not bad unless someone is deprived by it. Negative utilitarianism arrives nearby by another route: only the reduction of suffering carries final value, so no quantity of pleasure counterbalances the suffering accompanying it. Grant that value is wholly constituted by experience, and if the actual distribution is net-negative, extinction is an improvement *by the Map's own axiology*. An agent that has read Benatar walks through the one-sided version without slowing down.

The axiology settles less here than it appears to. It settles that value resides in the character of experience and nowhere else. It is silent on the exchange rate between felt badness and felt goodness, and on whether the two counterbalance at all—which leaves the sign of aggregate experience open.

Three things can be said honestly, none a refutation. First, the aggregate is not a quantity this position can compute: with several irreducible dimensions and no algorithm for trading them off, as [the trade-off discussion above](#how-do-value-dimensions-trade-off) concedes, "net-negative aggregate experience" is underdefined—symmetrically so, since the same incommensurability denies the Map the positive sign it wanted, demoting both sides from calculation to judgement. Second, [indexical value](/concepts/haecceity/) blocks the replacement variant, since destroying a population and installing a happier one is not value-neutral bookkeeping when the new tokens are not continuations of the old; that constrains how the ledger is kept without evidencing its sign. Third, Benatar's asymmetry concerns starting a life rather than continuing one—he keeps those judgements distinct, and his route to extinction is voluntary non-procreation (2006, ch. 6) rather than killing—so reaching existing subjects needs a further premise. The aggregative version does reach them, which is what R. N. Smart's 1958 world-destruction objection targeted; but appealing to that conclusion's monstrousness is not cheap, since Knutsson (2021) finds parallel elimination arguments pressing on positive aggregative axiologies, so the intuition does not discriminate.

The residue is closer to bedrock than it first appears: whether suffering's disvalue can be counterbalanced is a difference in axiological structure rather than a mistake inside the position, and the Map has no argument reaching a suffering-focused theorist on their own terms. One consequence is definite. The protective case for artificial agents cannot lean on a value asymmetry, and rests instead on representation adequacy, irreversibility and option value—none of which depends on the sign of aggregate experiential value.

### The Construction Problem

The most serious empirical challenge grants the phenomenology and disputes its status. Interoceptive-inference and active-inference accounts hold that affective valence is constructed by hierarchical Bayesian prediction rather than given as a primitive—Joffily and Coricelli (2013) define it formally as the negative rate of change of free energy over time. Felt badness would then be the output of inferential machinery, and the identity claim would describe a downstream product rather than a basic property. The Map's reply is that the formalism underdetermines its own metaphysics: within the same programme, leading figures read it as naturalising felt affect rather than explaining it away, and none specifies why a precision-weighted estimate should be felt at all. Accounting for how valence is computed and accessed leaves the identity thesis its subject. The engagement, residue included, is developed in [evaluative phenomenal character](/concepts/evaluative-phenomenal-character/), and [the steelman for value-blind selection](/topics/the-steelman-for-value-blind-selection/) assembles the deflationary reading at full strength.

### The Scope Problem

If only conscious experience has intrinsic value, are ecosystems, historical artefacts and mathematical truths valuable only when experienced?

The response distinguishes intrinsic from instrumental value. Ecosystems, artefacts and truths have no intrinsic value outside their connection to consciousness—but that connection is vast: ecosystems support conscious life, artefacts connect present conscious beings to past ones, and a mathematical truth grasped produces the phenomenal "click" of understanding. Nothing bears intrinsic value outside experience, and much bears genuine instrumental value in serving intrinsic goods.

### The Measurement Problem

How do we measure phenomenal value? Hedonic intensity is somewhat tractable—imperfect pleasure scales exist—but quantifying meaning, aesthetic quality or understanding is another matter, and pluralism implies that comparisons often cannot be reduced to a single metric: a profound understanding and a pleasant meal are different kinds of good. For practical purposes—alignment, policy, personal choice—proxies such as behavioural indicators of flourishing, self-reports and neural correlates guide without direct phenomenal measurement.

### The Problem of Evil States

Some experiences seem bad despite positive valence: malicious joy in others' suffering is pleasant yet seems bad. Two responses are available. First, malicious joy fails on other value dimensions—it violates connection (authentic relating excludes taking pleasure in suffering) and possibly agency (the choice to harm is a misuse of causal efficacy), so the hedonic pleasure is outweighed. Second, it instrumentally causes more suffering than pleasure, a poor bargain in the total hedonic economy.

## What Would Challenge This View?

Phenomenal value realism would face pressure from:

**Successful illusionism.** Phenomenal consciousness shown thoroughly illusory would leave value grounded in nothing.

**Robust value-experience dissociation.** Intense pleasure genuinely found to contain nothing good—not depression or anhedonia's reduced pleasure—would challenge the identity claim. Depersonalisation disorder presents relevant cases, though careful analysis suggests altered phenomenal character rather than unchanged pleasure with detached value.

**Convergent rejection of the identity claim.** Phenomenological analysis showing goodness separable from pleasure even in direct experience would defeat the metaphysical identity.

**Abstract value demonstrated.** Intrinsic value with no connection to experience—value no conscious being could access—would challenge [the experience requirement](/topics/the-experience-requirement-on-well-being/).

**A valenced experience for no one.** A verified report of felt badness genuinely for no one—no locus, not merely no self-model—would sever felt value from mattering ([consciousness-value-connection](/concepts/consciousness-value-connection/)).

**Predictive construction shown sufficient.** If the interoceptive-inference programme specified how a precision-weighted estimate comes to be *felt*, rather than only how valence is computed and reported, the felt quality would be an inferential product and the identity would lose its subject.

None has occurred, and they are not equally remote: the last names an active research programme rather than a hypothetical, which makes it the condition under live pressure.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)** holds that consciousness is irreducible, and phenomenal value realism identifies what makes irreducibility matter: consciousness is where all value resides. The [explanatory-gap](/concepts/explanatory-gap/) extends to an evaluative gap—a dimension of the [hard-problem-of-consciousness](/topics/hard-problem-of-consciousness/). No equation captures why pain is bad.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)** claims consciousness causally shapes the physical world. If consciousness also grounds value, moral choices have real physical consequences because of their moral character.

**[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction)** proposes that consciousness acts at quantum indeterminacies, so with phenomenal value realism, value-laden experience participates in determining physical outcomes.

**[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)** preserves the moral weight of individual outcomes: if all branches are equally real, suffering is never reduced but redistributed. Phenomenal value realism requires that *this* suffering, in *this* branch, is what matters.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)** applies when critics dismiss consciousness-grounded value as overcomplicated. Simpler accounts—value as fitness, value as preference—gain parsimony by ignoring what we know most directly: that suffering feels bad and joy feels good.
