Decision science has studied, since at least 2001, the input the Unfinishable Map names as its candidate selection currency: the presently felt valence of anticipating each option ([P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)). Its names for that input are immediate and anticipatory emotions, integral affect, risk-as-feelings, the affect heuristic and, for carried-over mood, incidental affect. Its findings fit the currency without evidencing it. Feeling toward an option, reported just before choosing, predicts the choice beyond anticipated emotions and probability. But it was measured, not manipulated, and related measures track the options' value, as a value-blind mechanism predicts. The affect heuristic defines its affect as experienced "with or without consciousness", so its effects favour neither a felt nor an unfelt reading.

Where affect has been manipulated, the record is uneven. Induced sadness raises what people pay for goods, a replicated effect. In the largest test the Map found, though, effectively induced emotions left financial risk-taking unmoved (Dong et al. 2026, n = 7,000). That result partly meets a disconfirmation condition the Map wrote for [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1) itself ([explained below](#battery)). Affect-scaled probability weighting reversed in Many Labs 2, and the trait fear–anger effect on risk replicated for judgement but not for preference. None of this shows felt valence idle. [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1) stays at low credence, held on prior commitments, with the value-blind rival undefeated.

## The Field's Names for Felt Affect at Choice {#vocabulary}

The constructs differ in timing, in object and in whether they are felt. Loewenstein, Weber, Hsee and Welch (2001) define anticipatory emotions as "immediate visceral reactions (e.g., fear, anxiety, dread) to risks and uncertainties", as against anticipated emotions, forecasts of later feeling. Peters (2006) separates integral affect, "positive and negative feelings about a stimulus", from incidental affect, "mood states that are independent of a stimulus but can be misattributed to it".

## What Was Measured: Option-Directed Affect Predicts Choice {#measured}

Schlösser, Dunning and Fetchenhauer (2013) asked people, immediately before a risky choice, how they felt about each option. Across four studies these immediate emotions predicted decisions beyond anticipated emotions and subjective probability. Young, Shuster and Mikels (2019; N = 32 and 24) found affect toward the sure option, probed before each framed decision, partly mediating the framing effect where the anticipated-affect mediator did not. Both designs are set out in [the affective-forecasting gap](/concepts/affective-forecasting-gap/#the-disambiguation-the-map-must-make).

Related measures track value. Grimani et al. (2024) found that "anticipatory and anticipated emotions responded very similarly to changes in value for the sure gains in both studies". Their emotion questions came "After each decision", so they measure feeling about a choice just made. Pham's (2007) review puts it generally: "Integral emotional responses are often used as proxies for values".

Where option values must be learned, expected feeling dominates. In an experience-based gambling task, Jäger et al. (2020) found "a guiding function of expected valence for decisions", and no predictive power for current affect once both were modelled. Their current-affect item, "Please rate how you are feeling now.", asks about global mood rather than an option, so it does not contradict Schlösser or Young like for like.

Davis, Love and Maddox (2009) reverse the arrow: "We find that emotions are associated with responses as opposed to stimuli." On their emotions-as-outcome account, anticipatory affect can be produced by deciding rather than fed into it. Their task had "no economic valence", they measured skin-conductance arousal rather than felt valence, and they call the result "not a refutation of the theory as a whole".

## Whether the Field's Affect Is Felt {#felt}

Much of this literature does not need affect to be conscious, and its central text says so. Slovic, Finucane, Peters and MacGregor define affect as goodness or badness "experienced as a feeling state (with or without consciousness)", drawn from tags "consciously or unconsciously associated with the representations" (Slovic et al. 2007, reprinting their 2002 chapter). Finucane, Alhakami, Slovic and Johnson (2000) found the inverse relation between judged risk and judged benefit "strengthened when time pressure was introduced". Efendić et al. (2022) replicated the paper's information experiment; the Map's search for replications of its time-pressure experiment found none. Since the definition admits unfelt affect, an unfelt valuation tag would yield the same speed and substitution as a felt one.

A regulation study that did target affect at choice set feeling aside. Sokol-Hessner et al. (2009) had people choose gambles "as if creating a portfolio", which "uniquely reduced both behavioral loss aversion and arousal to losses relative to gains". Of emotion's components they wrote, "we focus on the latter 3 components", which exclude subjective feeling. The instruction also broadens choice bracketing, a reframe that could lower loss aversion with no felt mediator.

The field's talk of epiphenomena concerns models. Loewenstein et al. describe a tradition in which feelings at decision "are seen as epiphenomenal—that is, not integral to the decision-making process". That concerns what a model includes, not the [metaphysical thesis](/concepts/epiphenomenalism/). Pfister and Böhm (2008) describe a cool route by which valence enters choice unfelt: "reducible emotions are mentally represented as beliefs about the decision's consequences, not as momentary feelings."

## What Was Manipulated: Incidental Affect and Risk {#manipulated}

The standard manipulation induces a mood, usually with a film clip, before an unrelated decision. Lerner, Small and Loewenstein (2004) found disgust "reducing selling and choice prices and eliminating the endowment effect", while sadness lowered selling prices and raised choice prices. Garg, Williams and Lerner (2018) replicated the sadness effect on choice prices (original d = 0.49, replication d = 0.40). They did not test disgust or selling prices.

For risk the record is weaker. Lerner and Keltner (2001) reported that "fearful people expressed pessimistic risk estimates and risk-averse choices, angry people expressed optimistic risk estimates and risk-seeking choices." Their choice data came from trait fear and anger, measured rather than induced; their one induction study measured risk perception. A registered replication of the three trait studies (Lu, Efendić & Feldman 2026, N = 780) supported the appraisal account of risk optimism "but not for risk preference and risk optimism for negative events". Over 114 effect sizes, Marini (2023) finds a "causal impact of fear on risk aversion, albeit to a small extent", and "average null effects" for happiness, sadness and anger.

The largest test the Map found is Dong, Dreber, Johannesson and Kilicgedik (2026): n = 7,000 on Prolific, a detailed pre-analysis plan, five emotion conditions and a no-induction control. The manipulations "worked as intended with large manipulation effects on the targeted emotions". Yet "we find no evidence of causal effects of incidental emotions on risk-taking", "at least in this setting." The manipulation check was a separate condition rating the clips "without the risk task", so choosers' felt state was inferred.

Whether incidental results transfer to integral affect is itself in doubt. In a preregistered meta-analysis of eight experiments (n = 5,336), Ferrer and Ellis (2021) found that "incidental anger decreased risk perceptions, whereas integral anger increased them". They judge that generalising from incidental to integral emotions "may not be advisable". Their outcomes were perceptions and intentions, not choices, and they call the evidence preliminary. The mood-as-information effect behind misattribution is weak in its home domain too: of nine tests of mood on well-being judgements, most "resulted in nonsignificant effects" (Yap et al. 2017). Carryover onto valuation has replicated. Carryover onto risk preference is small, fear-specific or absent in the best-powered tests.

## The Risk-as-Feelings Signature Did Not Replicate {#signature}

Risk-as-feelings predicts one signature of a felt input: "feelings about risk are largely insensitive to changes in probability, whereas cognitive evaluations do take probability into account" (Loewenstein et al. 2001). Rottenstreich and Hsee (2001) supplied the classic demonstration: "An affect-poor prize was preferred over an affect-rich prize under certainty, but the direction of preference reversed under low probability." Many Labs 2 repeated their first study, a kiss from a favourite film star against $50 (Klein et al. 2018). The original effect was d = 0.74 (N = 40). In the aggregate replication (N = 7,218), 47% preferred the kiss when unlikely and 51% when certain, a difference "in the direction opposite the prediction of the hypothesis" (d = −0.08).

The surviving support compares domains. Pachur, Hertwig and Wolkewitz (2014) found people paying "less attention to probability information" for affect-rich medical side effects than for monetarily equivalent losses. Affect-richness is there confounded with everything else separating health from money. No study the Map has found, in searches of the affect-rich choice, probability-neglect and replication literature, measured felt affect as the mediator of this gap.

## Valence or Appraisal? {#valence-or-appraisal}

Where incidental affect moves judgement or choice, the operative variable is often appraisal content rather than valence. Fear and anger, both negative, push risk in opposite directions. Appraisals "of certainty and control moderated and (in the case of control) mediated the emotion effects" (Lerner & Keltner 2001). Disgust and sadness part company on choice prices (Lerner et al. 2004), and a meta-analysis of 28 experiments finds effects depending "on appraisals other than valence" (Bartholomeyczik, Gusenbauer & Treffers 2022). Pfister and Böhm (2008) doubt "that the totality of experiences, including complex cognitions, can be collapsed onto one hedonic scale of simple affect", and see in ambivalence and hard trade-offs "different currencies with frequently unknown exchange rates".

## Bearing on [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1) and the Test Battery {#battery}

The Map tests [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1) against a three-row [dissociation battery](/concepts/affective-forecasting-gap/#the-disambiguation-the-map-must-make), collated under [P-VS2](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs2).

*Debias forecast (checked).* Nothing here adds to this row, which discriminates nothing: every model predicts that a changed forecast changes choice.

*Shift present affect; forecast, learned value fixed.* This literature is the field's attempt at the middle row. Loewenstein et al. state the same demand: if feelings and cognitive evaluations had the same determinants and consequences, "anticipatory feelings would not be required as an intervening construct". Incidental induction holds learned value fixed in expectation by random assignment. But it measured no forecast, and it checked the manipulation separately or between subjects. Of the regulation studies the Map found, Sokol-Hessner et al. alone targeted affect at choice rather than induced mood, and left feeling unmeasured. The Map searched the field's vocabulary (immediate, anticipatory, integral and incidental emotion, affect heuristic, emotion regulation, risky choice), the forward citations of Schlösser et al. and Young et al., and the replications and meta-analyses cited here. No study it found manipulated option-directed felt affect at choice, with a within-participant check on felt valence, while measuring and holding fixed both forecast and learned value.

The Map has already said what a null here would mean. Its account of [misattribution](/concepts/miswanting/#misattribution) commits it to this: choice left unmoved by induced incidental affect, with learned value and bodily state held fixed, would count against [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1). Dong et al. is a large instance, in financial risk-taking, of choice left unmoved by effectively induced incidental affect, with learned value held by randomisation. Bodily state was not held fixed, since an induced emotion moves the body too, and the choosers' felt state was inferred from a separate condition. The Map therefore reads Dong et al. as a partial hit on its own disconfirmation condition, confined to risk preference, and counts it modestly against [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1) there. Marini's meta-analysis points the same way, as does Lu et al.'s replication for trait emotion.

Ferrer and Ellis offer an exit the Map declines. If incidental induction is a poor proxy for integral affect, it is equally poor for the positive results, Garg et al.'s sadness effect included. The Map chose incidental induction as its test; narrowing it to integral affect after this result would be a post-hoc rescue, and any narrowing must precede further results and apply to positives and nulls alike.

*Shift learned value; affect, forecast fixed.* No study in the same searches manipulated this row. Its nearest observational analogue, Jäger et al.'s learning task, shows the rival's profile: expected valence carries most weight, current mood least.

The appraisal findings bear on [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)'s micro-valence condition. If felt valence at choice proved to be several qualitatively distinct evaluations, with the common scalar integrated *for* the choice rather than consulted at it, the currency premise would retire while [P-VS3](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs3) stood ([what fragmentation costs](/topics/valence-as-a-natural-kind/#the-asymmetry)). Same-valence emotions acting oppositely point toward that condition without meeting it: the effects come from incidental or trait affect, and the fear–anger contrast replicated for judgement, not preference.

No confidence band moves. The misattribution condition has taken a partial hit. A live countermodel (Davis et al.) stands against reading any pre-choice measure as an input. For the middle row with integral affect, nothing has come in. [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1) remains at low credence, grade D, held on prior commitments.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)**: Feeling toward an option that predicts the choice is what the tenet, read through [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1), leads one to expect. It fits the tenet without evidencing it, since the value-blind rival predicts the same correlation, with feeling as a correlate of learned value or an output of deciding. Nor do the incidental-affect nulls show felt valence idle. They count modestly against [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1) in risk preference, but they concern mood carried into a choice, a limit the sadness result shares, and leave felt anticipation of the options untested.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)**: The affect heuristic's affect is defined "with or without consciousness", so its findings neither support nor threaten the tenet. Using this literature for a *felt* currency would need a bridging argument that the literature does not supply.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)**: The tenet governs the Map's own reasoning, not what the studies show. The rival explains every result here without an experiential term, and the Map may decline that simpler account only by argument: its prior commitment to [phenomenal value realism](/topics/phenomenal-value-realism/), not a datum from this literature. Nor does the tenet license discounting the Dong et al. null while crediting positive results.
