If the brain produces consciousness the way a generator produces electricity, then cutting the fuel supply should dim the lights proportionally. Where the measurement is best, it does: in patients with disorders of consciousness, awareness tracks cortical glucose metabolism closely, as a production account predicts. The departures lie elsewhere. Under anaesthesia, unresponsiveness is reached from very different metabolic starting points; after cardiac arrest, some survivors report vivid experience; and in two dying patients organised gamma activity rose as oxygen supply failed—a finding that would strain even sophisticated production models if it marked experience, which the recording cannot show. The Unfinishable Map argues that these departures are compatible with [filter theory](/concepts/filter-theory/)—the brain constrains and channels consciousness rather than generating it, and metabolic crisis disrupts the constraint rather than the source. The evidence does not prove filter theory, but it identifies specific cases that production models accommodate through further hypotheses, some supplied by the researchers themselves.

## The Production Model's Prediction

The dominant physicalist position treats consciousness as a product of neural computation, which requires metabolic energy. Pepperell (2018) articulates this directly: consciousness is a physical process caused by the organisation of energetic activity in the brain. If this is correct, reducing metabolic supply should reduce consciousness—perhaps not in strict proportion, since network models allow graceful degradation and threshold effects, but broadly tracking metabolic decline. The prediction is directional: less fuel, less consciousness, with no room for paradoxical intensification. Network-level production accounts (global workspace, integrated information) loosen this further: what matters is which networks are functioning, not total fuel. The evidence below is sorted by which version it troubles—several findings press only on the crude metabolic-rate version, and the intensification cases press on both.

This prediction is testable. Multiple clinical and experimental settings create precisely the conditions needed: brains operating under severe metabolic constraint, with both metabolic rates and consciousness levels measurable. The first result fits even the crude version; several others do not, and the thinnest may fit neither.

## Five Lines of Evidence

### The Metabolic Threshold

Stender et al. (2014) used FDG-PET scanning to quantify cortical glucose metabolism in patients with disorders of consciousness. Vegetative state (unresponsive wakefulness) patients averaged 42% of normal cortical metabolic activity; minimally conscious patients averaged 55%. The diagnostic boundary fell close behind: the likelihood of a minimally-conscious rather than vegetative diagnosis crossed over at a cortical metabolic rate of roughly 44% of normal, and cortical metabolism alone classified the two groups with up to 83% accuracy.

The 44% figure is a statistical classifier derived from clinical data, not a precise biological switch, and the study measured diagnostic state rather than long-term recovery. The authors read their data as a graded correlation: "a tight correlation exists between level of conscious awareness and energy turnover in the brain", and because most patients' values fell between the two group averages they call the finding "consistent with a gradual transition between the states". A follow-up in 131 patients reported that "42% of normal cortical activity represents the minimal energetic requirement for the presence of conscious awareness" (Stender et al., 2016). In this setting, then, awareness tracks cortical metabolism down to a floor, which is what a production account predicts; the Map's page on [covert consciousness](/topics/covert-consciousness-and-cognitive-motor-dissociation/) says the same of the 2016 result. The Map's filter reading can redescribe the floor as a channel's minimum operating requirement, but nothing in these data favours that description over the authors' own.

### Psychedelic Enhancement and the Metabolic Record

Carhart-Harris et al. (2014) proposed the entropic brain hypothesis: the richness of conscious states tracks the entropy of neural dynamics rather than the level of activity. The imaging behind it found that psilocybin produced only *decreases* in cerebral blood flow and BOLD signal, maximal in default mode network hubs, and that the size of the medial prefrontal decrease predicted the intensity of the subjective effects (Carhart-Harris et al., 2012). Blood flow and BOLD signal are not metabolic rate, however. FDG-PET, which measures glucose metabolism directly, found the psilocybin state metabolically *expensive*: a global rise in cerebral glucose metabolism, most marked (around 25%) in frontomedial cortex and anterior cingulate (Vollenweider et al., 1997).

The psychedelic case therefore does not show consciousness intensifying as metabolic supply falls. It shows experiential intensity failing to track the default-mode *signal* while overall metabolism rises—more fuel alongside more vivid experience, which even the crude production model would expect. The entropic brain hypothesis, itself a physicalist account, predicts the finding directly. The [filter reading](/topics/psychedelics-and-the-filter-model/)—the DMN normally constrains consciousness into a narrow, ego-structured channel, and psychedelics loosen that constraint—survives only by separating what the DMN *does* from how much the brain *spends* ([the caveat in full](/concepts/filter-theory/#the-psychedelic-paradox)). Of the five lines of evidence here, this one discriminates least; it is included because it is often cited as metabolic evidence for the filter model, and it is not.

### Agent-Specific Anaesthesia Profiles

Montupil et al. (2023) distinguish at least three states under anaesthesia—unconsciousness, disconnected consciousness, and connected consciousness—and place ketamine anaesthesia, with its dreamlike internal experience, in the disconnected category. The agents' metabolic profiles differ sharply. Propofol produces a dose-dependent reduction in cerebral metabolic rate—on the order of tens of percent, deepening toward burst-suppression—and typically produces unconsciousness. Anaesthetic doses of S-ketamine, by contrast, leave whole-brain oxygen and glucose metabolism essentially unchanged while raising blood flow (Långsjö et al., 2005), yet still abolish responsiveness. Unresponsiveness is reached from very different metabolic starting points, and which state results depends on *which* networks are disrupted rather than on how much metabolism is lost.

That is anomalous only for the crude production model; network-level accounts predict network-specificity outright. [Anaesthesia research](/topics/anaesthesia-and-the-consciousness-interface/) adds a further observation: under propofol and sevoflurane, individual brains become less distinguishable from one another in their functional connectivity, an effect reversed on recovery (Luppi et al., 2025). The filter reading takes this as metabolism sustaining the brain's *channelling structure* rather than consciousness itself; a production reading takes it as the loss of the activity that constitutes an individual's experience. These data do not choose between them.

### Near-Death Experiences During Metabolic Crisis

Between 10% and 20% of survivors of in-hospital cardiac arrest report [near-death experiences](/concepts/near-death-experiences/)—episodes of vivid phenomenology often described as "realer than real" (Mashour et al., 2024). These occur during acute physiological crisis: hypoxia, failing circulation, collapsing metabolic supply. The neurobiological paradox is stark. If the brain produces consciousness, rich subjective experience during metabolic failure is difficult to explain. If the brain filters consciousness, reduced filtering during breakdown predicts exactly this kind of intensification.

The timing question remains contested—whether NDEs occur during the period of flat-line EEG or during recovery—but preliminary findings of organised gamma activity during cardiac arrest (discussed next) suggest the dying brain is capable of neural signatures associated with consciousness, even as its metabolic foundation crumbles.

### Gamma Surges in Dying Brains

Xu et al. (2023) recorded the brain activity of four comatose dying patients before and after ventilatory support was withdrawn—the prelude to cardiac arrest. Two of the four showed rapid, marked surges of gamma-frequency oscillations as the heart failed: cross-frequency coupling between gamma and slower oscillations *increased*, interhemispheric connectivity in gamma bands *strengthened*, and the activation concentrated in temporo-parieto-occipital junctions, regions associated with conscious experience, dreaming, and out-of-body experiences. The authors attribute the surge to oxygen deprivation—"global hypoxia markedly stimulated gamma activities in two of the patients"—and note that "both of these patients had a history of seizures". They are explicit about what the recording cannot show: the activation "is suggestive of elevated conscious processing in these patients, it does not demonstrate it", because none survived to say whether anything was experienced, and "it remains possible that this neurophysiological surge is epiphenomenal or pathological"—their words for the surge being a by-product of the dying process. Per the Map's own [evidence quality standards](/topics/clinical-evidence-quality-standards-consciousness-research/), two positive cases in one small study warrant cautious language, not strong inference.

In those two patients, gamma power and connectivity rose above the pre-withdrawal baseline as oxygen supply failed, rather than falling. The authors' own account is physiological—hypoxia stimulated the activity—and a production model can adopt it as it stands. The Map's filter reading is that the filter is breaking down and what had been constrained is briefly released. That reading is compatible with the recording, which cannot favour it: nothing shows that experience accompanied the surge.

## The Filter Model's Explanation

The [filter or transmission theory](/concepts/filter-theory/)—originating with William James (1898) and developed by Bergson, Huxley, and contemporary researchers like Edward Kelly—proposes that the brain constrains consciousness rather than producing it. Metabolic energy sustains the constraint, not the consciousness. This framework is compatible with each of the five lines of evidence:

- **The metabolic threshold**: The channelling structure has a minimum operating requirement, below which it cannot maintain coherent filtering—a floor under a graded correlation, which production accounts predict and the filter reading only redescribes.
- **Psychedelic DMN reconfiguration**: Loosening the brain's primary constraint network produces intensified experience—though, given the rise in overall metabolism, this fits production accounts equally well.
- **Agent-specific anaesthesia profiles**: Different agents disrupt different components of the filtering architecture, producing different consciousness effects that do not track metabolic reduction.
- **NDEs during metabolic crisis**: Reduced filtering allows broader conscious access, experienced as vivid, expansive awareness.
- **Gamma surges in dying brains**: The filter is failing, and consciousness briefly breaks through with reduced constraint—though the recording comes from two patients and cannot show that any experience accompanied it.

The production model can accommodate each finding individually—perhaps gamma surges are death throes (the specific proposal: anoxic depolarization from Na+/K+ pump failure transiently generating high-frequency activity before terminal silence, the anoxic relative of the spreading depolarizations recorded in dying human cortex—Dreier et al. 2018; see [near-death-experiences](/concepts/near-death-experiences/) for the full engagement), perhaps NDEs are reconstructed memories. Network-level production accounts already predict the metabolic floor and the psychedelic and anaesthetic patterns, so the pressure concentrates on the intensification cases and on terminal lucidity (discussed below). Accommodating those requires auxiliary hypotheses that complicate the model's original simplicity. The filter model accounts for the pattern from a single principle—metabolism sustains constraint, not consciousness—though this unification must be weighed against the filter model's own risk of unfalsifiability (addressed in the section on what would challenge filter theory).

## Chronic Versus Acute Metabolic Failure

The intensification cases above involve *acute* metabolic disruption—rapid, global changes to brain energy supply. A contrasting pattern emerges in [neurodegenerative disease](/topics/consciousness-and-neurodegenerative-disease/), where metabolic efficiency erodes chronically through neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and protein aggregation. In these conditions, consciousness does not intensify; it degrades progressively, with cognitive faculties peeling away in a characteristic order (episodic memory first, phenomenal awareness last).

The filter model offers an account of both patterns—though the ease with which it accommodates opposite outcomes warrants scrutiny (see the falsifiability section). Acute metabolic collapse disrupts the filtering apparatus globally and suddenly—the constraint lifts, and consciousness may briefly expand. Chronic degeneration destroys the interface structure itself—the hardware through which filtered consciousness is expressed. The distinction between *filter dissolution* (acute) and *interface degradation* (chronic) explains why not all metabolic reduction enhances consciousness, resolving what would otherwise be an asymmetry problem for filter theory.

[Terminal lucidity](/topics/terminal-lucidity-and-filter-transmission-theory/) bridges the two patterns. Patients with severe neurodegeneration—massive neuronal loss, widespread cortical atrophy—suddenly recover mental clarity, memory, and personality shortly before death; the phenomenon is documented across a review and case collection by Nahm et al. (2012). Batthyány and Greyson (2021) documented 124 cases in which more than 80% showed complete cognitive remission, with the majority of patients dying within hours to days of the lucid episode. The evidence is largely retrospective caregiver report, but the production model faces a real puzzle: if the neural substrate for complex cognition has been extensively destroyed over years, it is unclear what machinery produces a final episode of coherent consciousness. On the filter model, dying may involve a final acute disruption of whatever filtering capacity remained, briefly releasing consciousness through a degraded but still partially functional interface. Terminal lucidity is the chronic case suddenly behaving like an acute one, and the [theoretical implications](/topics/terminal-lucidity-and-filter-transmission-theory/) are explored in detail elsewhere on the Map.

## What Would Challenge the Filter Model

A theory that explains everything explains nothing. The filter model's flexibility—acute disruption loosens the filter, chronic degeneration destroys the interface—could be accused of accommodating any possible metabolic-consciousness relationship. This concern is legitimate, and the Map should specify what metabolic patterns would be genuinely difficult for filter theory to explain.

**Proportional degradation across all timescales.** If consciousness tracked metabolic rate in a smooth, proportional curve—across both acute and chronic conditions, with no threshold effects, no paradoxical intensification, and no agent-specific variation—the filter model would lose its explanatory advantage. A simple production model would suffice, and the additional ontological commitment of filter theory would be unjustified.

**No gamma surges or organised activity in any dying brain study.** If large-scale replications of the Xu et al. paradigm consistently found no organised neural activity after cardiac arrest—across hundreds of patients, multiple labs, varied conditions—the filter model's prediction of "constraint release" during acute metabolic collapse would be disconfirmed. The current evidence (n=2 positive cases) is too thin to confirm or disconfirm; what matters is the direction future evidence takes.

**Anaesthetic uniformity.** If each agent's effect on consciousness were predictable from its metabolic reduction alone, this would undermine the filter model's claim that different agents disrupt different filtering components. The production model's simpler prediction—less fuel, less consciousness—would be vindicated.

**Psychedelic enhancement with rising metabolism.** This condition is already partly met: FDG-PET finds psilocybin raising frontomedial glucose metabolism (Vollenweider et al., 1997), which undercuts any version of the filter model that ties intensification to *metabolic* suppression of the DMN. The filter reading survives only in the weaker form that ties it to the DMN's constraining function rather than its energy budget; replication showing DMN-hub metabolism rising in step with subjective intensity would press on even that form.

**Terminal lucidity without proximity to death.** If episodes resembling terminal lucidity occurred routinely in neurodegenerative patients at random points in disease progression—not concentrated near death—the filter model's explanation (dying disrupts remaining constraint) would lose its specificity. The pattern would look more like random fluctuation than systematic filter dissolution.

These are not exotic scenarios. Several are empirically testable with current methods. Specifying them converts the filter model from a framework that accommodates any result into one that makes distinguishable predictions—which is what a theory worth defending requires.

## Hibernation and the Lower Bound

Torpor presents an instructive limit case. Hibernating animals reduce metabolic demand to as low as 2% of basal metabolic rate (Cerri, 2017), and cortical EEG amplitude collapses toward isoelectric in the deepest torpor. At first glance, this supports the production model: almost no metabolism, no consciousness. Cerri himself is more cautious: the cortical activity of torpor differs from that of sleep, coma, and anaesthesia and "resembles a sort of 'slowed wakefulness'", leaving it "uncertain whether residual consciousness may still be present."

Hibernating animals also periodically arouse to euthermia—brief, energetically expensive returns to normal body temperature and metabolic rate—before re-entering torpor. The purpose of these arousals remains debated, and the leading proposals are physiological: immune restoration, recovery of sleep debt, synaptic regrowth, clearance of metabolic by-products. The filter model can add a speculative gloss—that continuous suppression is tolerable for the body but not for the consciousness-brain interface, which needs periodic metabolic maintenance—but the arousal pattern gives that gloss no evidential support over the physiological accounts, and a production model faces no special puzzle here. Hibernation marks the lower bound; it does not discriminate between the readings.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The metabolic evidence connects to three of the Map's [tenets](/tenets/).

**Dualism**: If consciousness were a direct function of the brain's metabolic output, its relationship to metabolic supply should be straightforward—more fuel, more consciousness; less fuel, less consciousness. In disorders of consciousness that is roughly what is found. The agent-specific anaesthesia profiles and the reports and recordings from dying patients depart from it, and the Map reads those departures as compatible with the dualism tenet rather than as evidence against production as such: the findings that would strain network-level production accounts most—the intensification cases—are also the thinnest, and their authors do not claim they show experience.

**Bidirectional interaction**: The Map's framework of [interactionist-dualism](/concepts/interactionist-dualism/) proposes that consciousness acts through quantum indeterminacy in neural systems. Metabolic constraint may affect the brain's quantum interface differently from consciousness itself. If metabolic energy maintains the decoherence-preventing conditions that allow [quantum-level interaction](/concepts/quantum-consciousness/)—a possibility the Map explores alongside the still-contested [evidence for quantum effects in neural tissue](/topics/quantum-biology-and-neural-consciousness/)—then metabolic collapse could paradoxically alter the interaction dynamics—not by eliminating consciousness but by changing the interface through which it operates. On this speculative reading, the gamma surges in dying brains may represent consciousness acting through a destabilised but not yet destroyed quantum interface.

**Occam's razor has limits**: The production model's apparent simplicity—consciousness is what brains do when adequately fuelled—holds where the measurement is best, in disorders of consciousness, and is strained only at the edges. Explaining the intensification cases and terminal lucidity within a production framework requires auxiliary hypotheses. The filter model, while ontologically less parsimonious (it posits consciousness as non-reducible), accommodates all five lines under a single principle—though three of them (the metabolic floor, psychedelics and anaesthesia) fit production accounts at least as well. [Tenet 5](/tenets/#occams-limits) bars the Map from converting that unification into an economy claim in its own favour: it is a claim about explanatory coverage, not about simplicity.
