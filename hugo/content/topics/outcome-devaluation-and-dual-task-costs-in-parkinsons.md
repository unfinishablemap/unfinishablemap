The Map's account of Parkinson's disease turns on a dissociation: self-initiated movement fails most, externally cued movement is comparatively spared. Two readings compete over which control system the dopamine loss disables. The Map's *willed-deficit* reading says selection of self-initiated action fails; the *habitual-control* reading of Redgrave et al. (2010) says automatic control fails and patients fall back on effortful goal-directed control. [paradoxical-kinesia](/topics/paradoxical-kinesia/) found that kinesia itself cannot decide between them and named two tests that could: outcome devaluation and dual-task cost. The verdict is that the outcome-devaluation test, which that page called "the standard behavioural signature of habit," comes out *against* the habitual reading: in the two direct patient studies located as of 2026-09-30, habit formation was not impaired and goal-directed control was what tracked disease severity (de Wit et al. 2011; Mi et al. 2021), and a computational decomposition found model-based learning impaired off medication and restored on it while model-free learning was untouched (Sharp et al. 2016, abstract). The dual-task test is mixed: patients do lose motor automaticity (Wu and Hallett 2005, 2008), but when baseline task demand is controlled their gait costs track controls' except for step width (Rochester et al. 2014), and external cues *reduce* dual-task interference (Rochester et al. 2005), the reverse of what the kinesia page's extension of the habitual reading predicted.

The answer to *which control system fails* is therefore domain-indexed. In instrumental learning with reward outcomes, goal-directed control is what dopamine loss impairs and habit is spared or over-weighted. In motor performance, automaticity is what patients lose. Neither result transfers cleanly to the other domain, and the instrumental literature's "goal-directed" is a different construct from the willed-deficit reading's "self-initiated movement" ([explained below](#the-construct-gap)). For the Map this restores parity between the two readings where [paradoxical-kinesia](/topics/paradoxical-kinesia/) had given the habitual reading "the modest edge"; it does not lift the Map's reading above *compatible with* the evidence, and it leaves the question of whether goal-directed control is *felt* exactly where it was.

## Why These Two Tests Were Named

The two readings agree about the observable asymmetry and disagree about its mechanism, so any discriminating test must probe the mechanism directly. Outcome devaluation does this for habit. A response is habitual, in the operational sense the cognitive science of habit uses, when it persists after the outcome it once earned stops being valued; a goal-directed response tracks the outcome's current value (see [philosophy-of-habit-under-dualism](/topics/philosophy-of-habit-under-dualism/) on outcome-insensitivity as the defining marker). If dopamine loss damages habitual control, patients should be *more* outcome-sensitive than controls on a well-practised task; if it damages willed or goal-directed selection, they should be *less*. de Wit et al. (2011) state the habitual reading's prediction in their own terms, as a proposal for an extensive-training paradigm: if stimulus-response habit formation "through extensive practice" is impaired in Parkinson's, patients "should paradoxically outperform control subjects on a subsequent outcome devaluation test."

Dual-task cost probes the other side. Redgrave et al. (2010) predict that a gradual replacement of automatic control by effortful goal-directed processing should render patients' task in hand "increasingly subject to interference from other goal-directed tasks." The kinesia page extended this to cued movement: if cued walking runs on the goal-directed route, "it should degrade under a concurrent attention-demanding task more in patients than in controls." Both predictions are clear enough to fail.

## The Outcome-Devaluation Test

Two direct devaluation or slips-of-action studies in patients were located as of 2026-09-30; a third may exist under other vocabulary. Both point the same way.

**de Wit, Barker, Dickinson and Cools (2011)** tested thirty patients (fifteen on medication, fifteen after eighteen-hour withdrawal) and fourteen controls on an instrumental conflict task with congruent, standard and incongruent discriminations, followed by an outcome-devaluation test. The incongruent condition is the one where a goal-directed strategy causes response conflict and a learner who can lean on direct stimulus-response associations does better. The result, in the authors' abstract: patients "are able to rely on direct stimulus-response associations when a goal-directed strategy causes response conflict, suggesting that habit formation is not impaired. If anything our results suggest a disease severity-dependent deficit in goal-directed behavior." The congruence effect did not differ between groups; there was no group effect on goal-directed response-outcome knowledge; within patients, devaluation-test accuracy correlated negatively with motor severity (r = −.37). Medication status had no significant effect, though the on-medication group tended to do worse (p = .08), which the authors read as a severity-matching artefact. The authors connect the goal-directed deficit to "a shift from internal to external control in PD", which is the same asymmetry the Map's dopamine article describes, now read as a goal-directed rather than a habit deficit.

**Mi, Zhang, McKeown and Chan (2021)** ran the de Wit task plus a slips-of-action test in twenty patients tested in the practically OFF state and twenty controls. Instrumental learning was slower in patients but was acquired with practice. In the expression test, where the two systems compete, patients "responded less for valuable outcomes" and "responded more often to stimuli that were associated with devalued outcomes", with worse performance at higher motor severity, later Hoehn-Yahr stage and longer disease duration. The authors' summary: patients "exhibit an impairment in engaging the goal-directed system with a relatively excessive reliance on habitual control in the expression of acquired learning." That is the mirror image of the habitual reading's prediction. Mi et al. reconcile their result with the Redgrave lineage's anatomy by way of Hernandez et al. (2019, abstract), on which "frequent reliance on habitual performance may be a critical functional stressor" for the dopamine projection; the lineage's anatomical claim survives, its behavioural prediction does not.

**The prior evidence on the other side** is Knowlton, Mangels and Squire (1996, abstract), whose patients "failed to learn the probabilistic classification task, despite having intact memory for the training episode." This is the result Redgrave et al. cite for impaired habit learning. It is a learning result on a probabilistic feedback task, not a devaluation or slips test, so it measures feedback-driven acquisition rather than outcome sensitivity. de Wit et al. and Mi et al. both replicate slower instrumental acquisition in patients while finding intact or dominant habit expression, so the two literatures may be measuring different things. Redgrave et al. (2010) knew the de Wit result and replied that "conflict-induced habit formation may be different from that induced by extensive training." The reply is available; it leaves the direct devaluation test, the one the kinesia page named, pointing against their reading.

## Model-Based Learning and Dopamine Manipulation

The reinforcement-learning decomposition of the same distinction gives the same answer. **Sharp, Foerde, Daw and Shohamy (2016, abstract)** ran a two-step task in patients ON and OFF medication and in controls: "Surprisingly, we found no effect of disease or medication on model-free learning. Instead, we found that patients tested OFF medication showed a marked impairment in model-based learning, and that this impairment was remediated by dopaminergic medication." Model-based learning also correlated with working memory. The sample sizes, effect sizes and medication-order controls are in the unread full text, and de Wit et al. (2011) found no comparable remediation on the devaluation test.

Dopamine manipulation in healthy volunteers runs the same way in both directions. **de Wit et al. (2012)** depleted dopamine precursors (phenylalanine and tyrosine) in healthy volunteers (fourteen per group). Depletion had no effect on stimulus-response learning or on the outcome-devaluation test itself, but "when goal-directed and habitual systems competed for control in the slips-of-action test, APTD tipped the balance towards habitual control." The effect was "restricted to female volunteers", and the authors report it as robust to menses status and pill use; the result is therefore a female-only finding until replicated in men. **Wunderlich, Smittenaar and Dolan (2012, abstract)** report the converse: "increased dopamine levels promote model-based over model-free choice."

A methodological point follows: the devaluation test proper was unaffected while the competition test moved, so outcome sensitivity and habit dominance under competition are separable measures. de Wit et al. (2011) found a severity-linked goal-directed deficit without a group difference; Mi et al. (2021) found excess habit under competition. Those are consistent and run in the willed-deficit reading's direction, but they are two measures, not one.

## The Dual-Task Test

The motor-domain statement of the habitual reading is Wu, Hallett and Chan (2015, abstract): patients "have difficulty in performing learned motor skills automatically", and among the mechanisms are failure to shift skills to the sensorimotor striatum and "use of attentional control and/or compensatory efforts to execute movements usually performed automatically in healthy people."

**Automaticity operationalised by dual task.** Wu and Hallett (2005, abstract) recruited fifteen patients, excluded three who could not achieve automaticity, and trained the remaining twelve and fourteen controls on finger sequences, defining automaticity as performing the sequence under a concurrent task. Twelve of the fourteen controls performed both sequences automatically; all twelve retained patients managed the simpler sequence and only three the more complex one, and patients showed wider cortical and cerebellar recruitment during automatic performance. The conclusion: "Parkinson's disease patients can achieve automaticity after proper training, but with more difficulty." Wu and Hallett (2008, abstract) found that only three of fifteen patients, against twelve of fourteen controls, managed a complex dual task after practice, and attributed the difficulty to "limited attentional resources, defective central executive function and less automaticity in performing the tasks." That three-way attribution matters: the authors themselves do not isolate automaticity loss from executive decline. Wu et al. (2015, *Cerebral Cortex*, abstract) add the imaging correlate: "In PD patients, attention induces a shift from the automatic mode back to the controlled pattern within the striatum." On self-paced motor tasks, then, patients lose automaticity, in count-of-patients terms and with a stated executive confound.

**Gait against controls.** Dual tasking reliably degrades walking in Parkinson's: a meta-analysis of nineteen studies found "a strong negative effect of dual tasks on walking performance (SMD = -0.68)" irrespective of baseline speed or secondary-task type (Raffegeau et al. 2019, abstract). That is a within-patient effect with no patient-versus-control contrast in the abstract, so it does not test the Redgrave prediction, which needs patients to degrade *more* than controls. Kelly, Eusterbrock and Shumway-Cook (2012) assert that patients "consistently demonstrate greater dual-task walking deficits than healthy, age-matched individuals," summarising O'Shea, Morris and Iansek (2002, abstract) as gait-speed costs of −18 to −19% in patients against −7% in controls. Yogev et al. (2005, abstract) found the opposite for speed: "In both groups, gait speed decreased in response to dual tasking, in a parallel fashion. For the PD group only, gait variability increased compared to usual walking", which they read as rhythmicity regulation becoming attention-demanding in patients. The largest baseline-controlled comparison located, Rochester, Galna, Lord and Burn (2014, abstract), with 121 incident patients and 189 controls and single-task demand equalised on both tasks, found that "PD and controls responded similarly to the dual-task for all gait characteristics except for step width and step width variability," a postural exception they call a Parkinson's-specific coordination deficit, with no correlation between interference and cognition, motor deficit or executive function in either group. The literature does not license the general claim that patients show larger dual-task costs than controls; no control-contrast meta-analysis was located as of 2026-09-30, and the best-controlled sample favours similar costs with a postural exception.

**Cueing under dual task.** The kinesia page's extension predicted that cued walking, if it runs on the goal-directed route, should degrade more under concurrent load. Rochester et al. (2005, abstract) found the reverse in twenty patients: "The use of auditory cues during a dual task involving gait reduced the interference effect on the task", with a 19% step-length gain. Cues unload rather than load. That fits a bypass story and also one on which cues make the goal-directed route cheaper; what it does not fit is the specific prediction the kinesia page stated.

**Freezing.** The one place the dual-task test cleanly favours the habitual reading is freezing of gait. Spildooren et al. (2010, abstract), testing fourteen freezers, fourteen non-freezers and fourteen controls OFF medication, found freezing in 37.5% of 180° turning trials and none in straight-line walking, with "360° turning in combination with a dual-task" as "the most important trigger for freezing." Vandenbossche et al. (2012, abstract) frame freezing as "a de-automatization disorder," with non-freezing patients compensating by switching to increased cognitive control. The willed-deficit reading makes no specific prediction here, so the row stands to the habitual reading's credit.

## The Two Readings Against the Evidence

| Test | Willed-deficit reading predicts | Habitual reading predicts | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outcome devaluation, well-practised task | Patients *less* outcome-sensitive | Patients *more* outcome-sensitive | No group difference in outcome sensitivity, severity-linked goal-directed deficit (de Wit 2011); patients less goal-directed and more habitual under competition (Mi 2021) |
| Model-based vs model-free learning | Model-based impaired | Model-free impaired | Model-based impaired OFF, remediated ON; model-free spared (Sharp 2016, abstract) |
| Dopamine manipulation in healthy people | Less dopamine → more habit | Less dopamine → less habit | Less dopamine → more habit (de Wit 2012, females only); more dopamine → more model-based (Wunderlich 2012, abstract) |
| Dual-task cost, self-paced motor tasks | No specific prediction | Larger in patients | Larger, in count-of-patients terms (Wu & Hallett 2005, 2008, abstracts); confounded with executive capacity by the authors' own account |
| Dual-task cost, gait vs controls | No specific prediction | Larger in patients | Mixed: larger (O'Shea 2002 via Kelly 2012), parallel with PD-specific variability (Yogev 2005), similar except posture (Rochester 2014); all abstract-only except Kelly |
| Cued walking under dual task | Cues bypass; cost should not rise | Cues recruit goal-directed route; cost should rise | Cues *reduce* interference (Rochester 2005, abstract) |
| Freezing under dual task | No specific prediction | Predicted | Dual task plus turning is the strongest trigger (Spildooren 2010, abstract) |

The three instrumental rows reverse the habitual reading's edge; the freezing row and the self-paced motor row support it; the gait row is mixed; the cueing row cuts against the specific extension made in [paradoxical-kinesia](/topics/paradoxical-kinesia/). The structural point is that the two tests belong to different task domains. A single-system verdict, in either direction, is not licensed by this literature. Redgrave et al. (2010) themselves posed the question in almost these terms, asking whether patients' difficulties initiating goal-directed performance "are due to the primary disease state (that is, the loss of dopamine from goal-directed circuits" or arise "because goal-directed control is overwhelmed by the absence of automatic control routines." The instrumental evidence answers the first way for reward learning; the motor evidence leaves the second open for movement.

## The Construct Gap

The central caveat is that the instrumental literature and the willed-deficit reading are not measuring the same thing. "Goal-directed" in de Wit, Mi and Sharp means sensitivity of a learned response to the current value of its outcome, or model-based planning over a learned task structure: response-outcome knowledge, exercised in a button-press task with reward outcomes. "Self-initiated" in the Map's reading of the dissociation means a movement generated without an external trigger, the construct the kinematic and cueing studies manipulate. The two overlap, since both contrast with cue-driven, outcome-insensitive control and both degrade with disease severity, but they are not identical. None of the instrumental tasks measures self-initiated *movement*; none of the gait studies measures outcome sensitivity. A patient could in principle retain outcome-sensitive choice while losing the capacity to start a chosen movement without a cue, or the reverse. So the devaluation result restores the willed-deficit reading to parity by defeating the rival's prediction, and it does so by showing that the system the rival said was spared is the one that fails. It does not show that what fails is the selection of self-initiated action, and any article that reports the devaluation evidence as confirming the Map's reading has substituted one construct for the other.

## Stated Limits

Fifteen of the twenty-one sources consulted were retrieved as abstracts only; they are marked "(abstract)" at first mention and in the References, and nothing beyond abstract content is asserted for them. The full texts of de Wit et al. (2011, 2012), Mi et al. (2021), Redgrave et al. (2010) and Kelly et al. (2012) were read selectively for the passages quoted, not end to end. The count of two direct devaluation or slips studies in patients is a search result as of 2026-09-30, not a systematic review. The depletion result of de Wit et al. (2012) held in females only. No meta-analysis contrasting patients with controls on dual-task cost was located. Foerde (2018), the likeliest single review of the human-patient habit evidence, could not be retrieved and is listed under Further Reading as unread. Effects of levodopa and deep-brain stimulation on automaticity, and the animal 6-OHDA devaluation work, were not surveyed. None of the authors surveyed addresses the metaphysics of control: Redgrave, de Wit, Mi, Sharp and Shohamy, Wu and Hallett, and Rochester write about physically realised control systems, and none is cited here as endorsing the Map's reading of the dissociation.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**Tenet 3 stays at *compatible*, and gains parity.** The [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) tenet holds that consciousness causally influences the physical world, and the Map's own tenet text records that the interface argument shows this influence to be *available* without showing it to be *actual* ([the tenet-3 standing note](/tenets/#tenet-3-standing)). Nothing here moves it. A goal-directed deficit that dopamine replacement remediates is a dopamine-dependent goal-directed system, which non-reductive physicalism with mental causation predicts as readily as the Map's selection-interface reading does. On the compatibility-versus-support boundary of [evidential-status-discipline](/project/evidential-status-discipline/), the devaluation evidence is compatible with the Map's reading and does not support it over the rival, because the rival's *anatomy* survives and only its behavioural prediction failed; the Map names no discriminator the physicalist reading fails to predict. What the evidence does is remove the anatomical rival's claim to the discriminating test, so the Map's reading returns to equal footing where the kinesia page had placed it a step behind. Parity is the tier reached: *compatible, not supported*.

**Tenet 5, narrowly.** [Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits) warns against preferring the simpler account because it is simpler. The relevant application is small: the habitual reading's appeal partly rested on an anatomically tidy story (posterior putamen loss, therefore habit loss), and the direct behavioural test did not honour it. That cautions against inferring function from lesion locus, which cuts against any tidy story including the Map's; it licenses no preference for the Map's reading, and the Map draws none.

**Nothing for Tenet 2, and the felt-ness question untouched.** None of these data bear on the [Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) mechanism, on Tenet 1 or on Tenet 4. The disagreement between the Map and the physicalist rival still lies where [dopamine-and-the-unified-interface](/topics/dopamine-and-the-unified-interface/) places it: in why goal-directed control is *felt* as effortful, which neither devaluation tests nor dual-task costs address. On the habitual reading what survives in Parkinson's is attended, effortful control, where the Map locates [felt effort](/concepts/mental-effort/); the devaluation result complicates that comfort, since in reward learning the attended goal-directed system is the one that weakens with severity. Which of these is closer to conscious selection as the Map means it is a question the [construct gap](#the-construct-gap) leaves open.
