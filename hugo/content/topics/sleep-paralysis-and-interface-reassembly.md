The brain does not wake by replaying sleep onset backwards. Measured on laboratory clocks, its components rejoin waking coordination at different rates: the thalamus re-establishes its waking coupling fastest, the anterior cingulate changes on a different and non-monotonic schedule, and sensorimotor cortex shows no change in coupling timing at all across the measured window (Wang et al., 2024). Whole-brain modelling agrees: "falling asleep and waking up are two asymmetric processes" (Stevner et al., 2019). Sleep paralysis is the case in which the mis-ordering becomes conspicuous from the inside — waking self-knowledge has returned, many experiencers perceive their actual bedroom, and yet the motor channel stays closed and dream-generation keeps running.

The Unfinishable Map reads these findings as the ascent-side complement to [hypnagogia](/topics/hypnagogic-phenomenology-and-interface-modulation/): the consciousness-brain interface that disassembles channel by channel at sleep onset reassembles channel by channel on waking — on separate clocks, and not in reverse order. The claim's status belongs up front: the paralysis mechanism is well characterised and physical, the hallucinations' content is well explained by threat-vigilance and body-map accounts the Map concedes in full, and every finding below is compatible with a production model in which different neural generators simply restart at different rates. What the Map stakes out is narrower — the order and independence of channel restoration is real, measured, and direction-sensitive, and accounts that treat consciousness as switched on by a single arousal variable accumulate per-case amendments that a multi-channel interface treats as expected structure. The argument concerns where explanatory debts fall, not anything physicalism cannot in principle absorb.

## The Ascent Is Not the Descent Reversed

Two independent lines of third-person evidence separate the descent into sleep from the ascent out of it.

The first is whole-brain trajectory. Stevner et al. (2019) fitted a hidden Markov model to simultaneous EEG-fMRI from 57 participants falling asleep, recovering the brain's paths among whole-brain network states across the wake–NREM cycle. Their conclusion: "Our results confirm that falling asleep and waking up are two asymmetric processes, leading to two separated transition modules of whole-brain network states during wakefulness, with one more likely to occur after consolidated sleep." Wakefulness before sleep and wakefulness after sleep are different network neighbourhoods; the path out of sleep does not retrace the path in.

The second is hysteresis. The Map's [anaesthesia article](/topics/anaesthesia-and-the-consciousness-interface/) documents neural inertia: the brain needs less drug to stay unconscious than to become unconscious, and emergence runs on different circuitry from induction. Kim, Moon, Mashour and Lee (2018) argue that this asymmetry is a general property of coupled brain networks rather than a pharmacological artefact: in their simulations and empirical comparisons, "the topographic similarities during emergence are always higher than during the induction path at the same connection strength", and hysteresis "is not a pharmacokinetic attribute, but a fundamental neurobiological process that stabilizes states of consciousness". That generality bridges the anaesthesia and sleep wings: if the loss-and-recovery asymmetry is a network property, the nightly sleep-wake transition should show the same directional structure — and the trajectory evidence above indicates it does. Kim and colleagues explain it entirely within coupled-oscillator dynamics, with no reference to consciousness; the Map cites their result as measured structure, not as support for its interpretation.

## Channels Come Back on Separate Clocks

Sleep inertia — the groggy interval after waking — supplies the most direct measurement of staggered restoration. Wang et al. (2024) recorded simultaneous EEG-fMRI through nocturnal sleep in 21 healthy subjects, then ran three five-minute resting-state scans at twenty-minute intervals after awakening, tracking the time-lag at which EEG vigilance signatures couple to regional blood-oxygen signals. The thalamus recovered fastest: its alpha-vigilance peak-correlation lag shrank from roughly eight seconds just after waking to roughly 2.5 seconds by the third scan. The anterior cingulate also changed significantly across sessions, but non-monotonically — its lag ran earlier through the second scan before settling back to roughly six seconds by the third. Sensorimotor cortex showed no significant change in coupling timing across the entire measured window. This extends an older PET observation that the thalamus and brainstem are the first regions to restore normal blood flow on awakening (Balkin et al., 2002).

Two features give the finding its weight. It is third-person: the staggering is measured on laboratory clocks rather than reconstructed from retrospective report — the standing weakness the [hypnagogia article](/topics/hypnagogic-phenomenology-and-interface-modulation/) flags against its own disengagement sequence. And it is channel-shaped: what recovers at different rates is the coordination of specific subsystems, not a general arousal level, with motor-adjacent cortex last — the channel whose delayed return sleep paralysis makes phenomenally obvious.

The finding is neutral between filter and production readings: a production model can hold that different generators restart at different rates, and nothing in the coupling data forbids it. What the data do rule out is the single-dial picture, in which one scalar recovery variable brings the whole system back on one clock.

## Sleep Paralysis: The Mis-Ordering Experienced from Inside

In sleep paralysis, a person surfaces into full awareness while the REM-sleep motor blockade is still engaged. Sleep medicine characterises it as a dissociated state — elements of REM persisting into wakefulness — whose episodes "usually happen in the transition from REM sleep to the waking state" (Sodré et al., 2023). The paralysis mechanism is well understood: during REM, descending brainstem systems inhibit spinal motoneurons via GABA and glycine, and in sleep paralysis this atonia outlasts the arousal that should have terminated it. Aggregating 35 studies (N = 36,533), Sharpless and Barber (2011) estimate that 7.6% of the general population experiences at least one episode, with far higher rates among students (28.3%) and psychiatric samples. Nothing about the state is mechanistically mysterious, and the Map's use of it implies no such thing.

What the state supplies is a channel decomposition available from the inside. During an episode, environmental perception can be partially open — experiencers commonly report seeing their actual surroundings. [Metacognition](/concepts/metacognition/) is substantially restored: sufferers know they are awake, know they cannot move, and recall the episode immediately afterwards. The motor channel is fully closed. And the imagery and threat systems are still running in dream mode, producing the sensed-presence and pressure phenomena catalogued in the next section. Channels that ordinary waking presents as a unit come apart, and their restoration order is experienced as wrong: awareness first, world-access partial, motor control last.

The state has begun to acquire an electrophysiological profile, though on very small samples. Mainieri et al. (2021), recording episodes in five experiencers, found the EEG spectrum during sleep paralysis "intermediate between wakefulness and REM sleep in the alpha, theta, and delta frequencies", with theta rhythm present in 70.8% of scored mini-epochs during paralysis against 89.7% in REM and 21.2% in wakefulness. Their own conclusion is that the brain during sleep paralysis "is not in an awake but in a dreaming state" — a dreaming brain co-present with waking self-knowledge. Herrero et al. (2025), in a study whose title labels it exploratory (seven participants analysed, two sleep-paralysis episodes), found theta reduced relative to both REM and stage 1 in both episodes and low-gamma increased in both, with beta increased in one of the two; the authors state that the sample size "may affect the generalizability and statistical robustness of the findings". The calibrated summary: a distinctive spectral configuration has been reported in two very small studies — enough to motivate the multi-channel description, not enough to declare sleep paralysis a third state of consciousness.

Philosophy of mind has begun to reach a similar structure from the phenomenological side. Alcaraz-Sánchez (2024) locates states of minimal awareness — "clear light" sleep is the worked case, situated against its neighbouring transitional states — in a multidimensional space of phenomenological dimensions (richness of content, bodily awareness, passage of time, attentional focus, self-revelation) rather than at points on a single wakefulness axis. The framework is a metaphysically uncommitted research heuristic; the convergence with the Map's multi-channel reading is structural, and is claimed here only as that.

## Conceding the Content

The best-developed science of sleep paralysis concerns what the hallucinations depict, and the Map concedes this ground in full. Cheyne, Rueffer and Newby-Clark (1999) sorted sleep-paralysis hallucinations into three factors — an Intruder cluster (sensed presence, fear, auditory and visual hallucination), an Incubus cluster (chest pressure, breathing difficulty, pain), and a cluster they labelled Unusual Bodily Experiences (floating and flying sensations, out-of-body experience, bliss), the grouping Cheyne's later work with Girard renames vestibular-motor — and offered an explicitly dual account: neurological generation of the raw phenomena plus cultural construction of their interpretation. Jalal and Ramachandran (2014) explain the intruder as a projection of the subject's own body-map, generated when multisensory processing of body and self around the right superior parietal region runs without confirming afferent input. The cultural record shows the same neural event dressed locally: the Jinn of Islamic tradition, blamed by 48% of Egyptian sufferers (Jalal, Simons-Rudolph, Jalal & Hinton, 2014); the witch-like Pandafeche of Abruzzo (Jalal, Romanelli & Hinton, 2021); the Old Hag of Newfoundland (Hufford, 1982).

These accounts explain the content well, and cultural variance in what is depicted is a decisive reason to stake nothing on it. The Map's claim survives the concession because it never rested there: a hypervigilant threat system and an unanchored body-map explain what an immobilised, half-woken brain hallucinates, but they presuppose rather than explain the timing structure — phenomenal awareness and metacognition back online while motor control and body-schema anchoring are not. The mis-ordering is the datum; the imagery is downstream.

The same small literature bears on the Map's treatment of [out-of-body experiences](/topics/out-of-body-experiences/): Herrero et al. (2025) also captured two out-of-body episodes during sleep, which showed increased delta and theta with reduced alpha and reduced fronto-central beta relative to wakefulness, while low-gamma moved in opposite directions in the two episodes — sleep-OBE electrophysiology to set beside the self-model-dislocation reading that article defends, on the same two-episode footing.

## Hypnagogia and Hypnopompia: A Default, Not a Finding

Should hallucinations at waking differ from hallucinations at sleep onset? The field's position is an equivalence convention rather than an established result. Ghibellini and Meier's 2023 review of the hypnagogic state defines the terms this way: "Hypnopompic, on the other hand, refers to the very same phenomena occurring at sleep-offset" — and then folds both under a single term for the remainder of the review. Cheyne and colleagues' questionnaire work likewise treats hypnagogic and hypnopompic experiences as a single class of hallucinoid experiences (Cheyne, Newby-Clark & Rueffer, 1999). The treat-as-one convention is the literature's working default, adopted in the near-absence of targeted comparison: hypnopompia is far less studied than hypnagogia, and a targeted (non-exhaustive) search in August 2026 found no dedicated study of hypnopompic neural correlates published since 2023.

One hard quantitative asymmetry does exist. In a representative UK sample of 4,972 people, "thirty-seven per cent of the sample reported experiencing hypnagogic hallucinations and 12.5% reported hypnopompic hallucinations" (Ohayon et al., 1996) — a threefold gap that runs the wrong way for a recall account, since hypnopompic events immediately precede full waking and should be the better remembered. Either the ascent generates less hallucinatory content than the descent, or it encodes report less well; both readings are direction differences. The same survey's authors suggest hypnopompic hallucinations may be the better indicator of narcolepsy among respondents reporting excessive daytime sleepiness — a hint that the two directions do not behave identically even clinically.

There is a historical irony here. "Hypnopompic" was coined by Frederic W. H. Myers — the psychical researcher whose transmission theory the Map's [terminal-lucidity article](/topics/terminal-lucidity-and-filter-transmission-theory/) treats as a founding statement of the filter tradition — as the complement to Maury's 1848 "hypnagogic" (Ghibellini & Meier, 2023). The founder of the filter tradition thought the departure of sleep deserved its own word; the field that inherited the word treats it as a synonym.

The Map registers this as an open question and a prediction, in that order. The question — are the two transitions phenomenologically distinct? — is under-researched rather than settled. The prediction follows from the reassembly reading: if channels close in one order at sleep onset and reopen in a different order on different clocks at waking, hypnagogic and hypnopompic experience should differ in structure — in which channels contribute imagery, how much metacognition accompanies it, how episodes end. The hysteresis and staggered-restoration evidence gives principled reason to expect direction differences; direction-controlled phenomenology could confirm or refute them.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)**: The reassembly evidence extends to the sleep-wake boundary the channel architecture the Map reads across anaesthesia, dissociation, and dying — coupling components that restore independently, in direction-sensitive order — and sleep paralysis adds its first-person face: awareness present while access and control channels lag. Per [direction-of-interface-change](/concepts/direction-of-interface-change/), this is a further view of the same architecture, not an additional independent confirmation: counting the sleep-side and anaesthesia-side asymmetries separately would inflate the case exactly as the [common-cause null](/project/common-cause-null/) warns. The signatures constrain substrate-symmetric production accounts — recovery does not retrace degradation off a direction-blind vulnerability profile — while production models absorb each finding by paying a per-case cost in direction-specific machinery.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)**: In sleep paralysis the channel that returns early is the high-engagement one — metacognitive self-knowledge, the capacity to observe, evaluate, and afterwards report — while the channels through which consciousness acts on the world return last. The anaesthesia wing documents the same inversion pharmacologically: executive function returns before simpler sensorimotor capacities during emergence. A reading on which consciousness participates in reassembly through its most active channels first fits this ordering; a reception-only reading must treat the ordering as a fact about substrate readiness alone. The evidence permits both; the Map notes which it expects.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)**: "Waking is falling asleep in reverse" is the simple story, and it fails three separate empirical tests: prevalence (transition hallucinations are reported roughly three times as often on the descent), network dynamics (two separated transition modules; staggered coupling recovery), and thresholds (hysteresis). Parsimony applied ahead of evidence would have endorsed the reversal picture.

## What Would Challenge This View

1. **A single recovery variable.** If the staggered coupling recovery of sleep inertia proved fully derivable from one scalar variable — a uniform vascular or neuromodulatory gradient reaching different regions at different times for reasons of anatomy alone — the staggering would lose its architectural significance.

2. **The sleep-paralysis signature dissolving.** The spectral findings rest on five and seven experiencers respectively. If larger samples showed sleep-paralysis EEG to be a simple mixture of wake and REM epochs rather than a distinctive configuration, the partial-reassembly description would revert to phenomenology alone.

3. **Demonstrated hypnagogic-hypnopompic equivalence.** The reassembly reading predicts direction differences in transitional phenomenology. If direction-controlled comparison demonstrated structural equivalence — and the Ohayon prevalence gap proved a reporting artefact — the prediction would have failed where it is most directly testable.

4. **Hysteresis confined to pharmacology.** If natural sleep-wake transitions proved, on direct measurement, to show no hysteresis — thresholds and trajectories symmetric in both directions — the bridge Kim et al.'s network-generality argument licenses would fall, and the sleep evidence would detach from the anaesthesia wing's.
