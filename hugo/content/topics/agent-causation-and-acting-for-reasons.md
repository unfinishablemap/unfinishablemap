The Unfinishable Map holds that a conscious agent settles which of several physically open outcomes becomes actual, and that she does so *for reasons*. Agent-causal libertarianism has a standing difficulty with that phrase. If the agent, rather than her recognising a reason, causes the choice, something else must make it a choice made for *this* reason rather than another she also had. The literature offers three worked answers. On the non-causal answer (Roderick Chisholm, Timothy O'Connor), reasons do not cause the choice; in O'Connor's version they explain the agent-caused intention through its content and causally shape the agent's propensities. On Randolph Clarke's integrated answer, the agent and her having reasons are joint causes of the choice, though Clarke doubts that the substance causation it needs is possible. On E. J. Lowe's answer, free volitions are uncaused and the operative reason is the one the agent chose to act on.

This article sets out the three answers and their costs, and records that the Map holds pieces of all three without having chosen. It adds one observation of its own, in [the section on the Map's selector](#the-integrated-shape) (explained below): the Map's default selection, which keeps public outcome statistics Born-exact, already has the shape of Clarke's view wherever the agent's reasons are realised in brain states. The article does not establish that the Map's selection is in fact guided by reasons. That would need the thesis that token-level selection is genuinely causal, which the Map cites only as a coherence result inside its own framework ([the mechanism debt](/positions/quantum-interface/#mechanism-debt)).

## The Problem: Which Reason Was Operative? {#the-problem}

Donald Davidson (1963) argued that reasons explain actions by causing them. An agent often has several reasons that would each rationalise what she does, yet may act on only one, and Davidson held that only a causal relation marks the difference: the operative reason is the one that caused the act.

Agent-causal libertarians cannot take this answer over unchanged. "Standard agent-causal views deny that events such as the agent's recognizing certain reasons cause any free action (or whatever event the agent directly causes when she acts freely)" (Clarke, Capes and Swenson 2021, §3.1). If the agent alone causes the choice, Davidson's criterion is unavailable. The free-will entry, co-written by O'Connor, names the cost: such libertarians "must explain how reasons can explain and motivate action without causing it; and this has turned out to be no easy task" (O'Connor and Franklin 2022, §2).

The Map inherits the problem. Its [trilemma-of-selection](/topics/trilemma-of-selection/) has consciousness bias which option crosses threshold "for reasons that matter to the agent", and [reasons-responsiveness](/concepts/reasons-responsiveness/) says the reasons guide the outcome without determining it. Neither says how a reason guides a selection that the agent, and no event, makes.

## Three Worked Answers {#three-answers}

### The Non-Causal Answer: Content and Structured Propensities {#non-causal}

The free-will entry places Chisholm and O'Connor (2000, ch. 5) among agent-causalists who deny reasons any direct causal role in a free action (O'Connor and Franklin 2022, §2). On O'Connor's account, as the incompatibilism entry reconstructs it, "agents do not cause free decisions; rather, a free decision is a causally complex event, consisting of the agent's causing her coming to have a certain intention". Roughly, a desire explains the decision when the intention the agent causes is an intention to act here and now in order to satisfy that desire (§3.1, adapting O'Connor 2000: 86). The operative reason is the one the intention's content refers to, and no reason need cause anything. The chapter's abstract describes "an internal, non-contrastive explanatory link between a prior motivational state and the intention-in-action that the agent causes" (O'Connor 2000).

Reasons are not idle, though. The same abstract adds "a causal notion of 'structured propensities'" to explain why some undetermined actions are likelier than others, and John Martin Fischer reports O'Connor treating reasons as "structuring conditions on purposive action" (Fischer 2001, citing O'Connor 2000: 95). Andrei Buckareff reads the chapter as non-causal: "The reasons direct action, but they do not cause the action" (Buckareff 2003). Both readings hold: reasons do not cause the choice, but they causally shape the agent's tendency to make it.

The incompatibilism entry records two objections. Richard Feldman and Buckareff (2003) argue that the content requirement is unnecessary, since a desire can truly explain a decision whose intention is not about it. The second is that a feature of the effect, the intention's content, carries the explanation while the reason causes nothing; the entry traces this to the requirement "that only the agent cause what is directly agent-caused in a free action" (§3.1). O'Connor's agent causation does not rest on substance dualism: "agent-causal theories do not necessarily require substance dualism, but may require causal powers and properties that are ontologically emergent" (O'Connor 2011, abstract).

### The Integrated Answer: Two Causes of One Choice {#integrated}

Clarke keeps the causal theory of reason-explanation and adds the agent. The abstract of "An Integrated Agent-Causal Account", chapter 8 of *Libertarian Accounts of Free Will*, objects that O'Connor's view "fails to provide for the reason-explanation of free actions" and offers an account that "integrates agent causation (as a requirement for free action) with an event-causal theory of action" (Clarke 2003). Erik Carlsson's review gives the structure: "a free action has a nondeterministic conjunctive cause, one conjunct of which is the agent herself, and the other is her having certain reasons, etc. Each conjunct is a necessary, but not (even in a nondeterministic sense) sufficient cause of the action" (Carlsson 2004).

Such a view "can provide the same accounts of acting for a reason and of reason-explanation as can event-causal theories", while the agent conjunct secures origination (Clarke, Capes and Swenson 2021, §3.1); Clarke co-wrote that entry, so this is his own voice, not independent corroboration.

Clarke is the view's architect, not its advocate. His chapter 9 judges that it adequately characterises free will "Assuming that all it requires is possible", and chapter 10, on substance causation, concludes "there are, on balance, good reasons to reject this possibility" (Clarke 2003). In Carlsson's summary, "He concludes that the possibility of free will is doubtful, given broad incompatibilism" (Carlsson 2004).

### The Uncaused-Volition Answer {#uncaused-volition}

Lowe's *Personal Agency* (2008) defends a non-Cartesian substance dualism and a substance-based account of causation on which rational agents are "unmoved movers, or initiators of new causal chains" (book abstract). Yet Lowe does not hold that agents cause their free volitions. Meghan Griffith's review is explicit: "Like some noncausalists, Lowe posits uncaused volitions and denies that reasons are causes"; because agents cause by acting, they "cannot cause their own volitions on pain of regress" (citing Lowe 2008: 7); and "As uncaused acts, volitions are not even partly caused by reasons" (Griffith 2009). The incompatibilism entry files Lowe with the non-causal theories (§1), and Lowe places his view "midway between volitionism and classical agent causalism" (Lowe 2008, ch. 7 abstract).

Lowe's reply to Davidson is that "the reason for which the agent acted is the reason which the agent chose to act upon" (Lowe 2008: 183, as quoted in Griffith 2009). Choosing fixes the operative reason. And free choices lack objective probabilities: "With chance events we can assign objective probabilities. We cannot do so with free choices" (Griffith 2009, summarising Lowe 2008: 190–195).

## What the Map Currently Holds {#current-mix}

The Map has adopted none of the three answers, and its pages use pieces of each.

- [agent-teleology](/concepts/agent-teleology/) says the gazelle's reason "is a conscious state that causally contributes to the outcome": the integrated answer, with the reason-state as a partial cause.
- [trilemma-of-selection](/topics/trilemma-of-selection/), [reasons-responsiveness](/concepts/reasons-responsiveness/) and [agent-causation](/concepts/agent-causation/) answer the luck objection by saying that selection on reasons makes the choice intelligible, and leave [why one option rather than another](/concepts/rollback-argument-against-agent-causation/) to the agent's settling. Clarke's contrastive reason-explanation of undetermined choice is causal: he argues that "even contrastive rational explanations are available for some nondeterministically caused actions" (2003, ch. 3), while the abstract of O'Connor's chapter calls his link "non-contrastive". The Map's reply is of the non-contrastive kind.
- The [agency register](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a2) ([P-A2](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a2)) grounds authorship in immanent causation "in Chisholm's sense" and takes only substance causation from Lowe, who denies that the agent causes her volitions at all. On the free-will entry's classification Chisholm denies reasons a direct causal role.
- [philosophy-of-action-under-dualism](/topics/philosophy-of-action-under-dualism/) borrows Davidson's causal skeleton and Lowe's volitionist structure but makes the volition agent-caused, which Lowe denies. Lowe accepts the skeleton's downstream half, from willing to movement, and rejects its upstream half, from reasons to choice.

These pieces make different choices in different places, and no Map text yet says which governs the "for" in "selects for reasons".

## Where the Map's Selector Has the Integrated Shape {#the-integrated-shape}

This section is the Map's own analysis, not a claim any source above makes.

On the Map's default reading of [Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction), conscious selection keeps outcome frequencies Born-exact "in every publicly conditionable context; only conditionals on the conscious state itself are free" ([P-Q2](/positions/quantum-interface/#p-q2)). The brain prepares options whose weights physics fixes, and consciousness settles which becomes actual.

Insofar as the agent's reasons are realised in brain states, those states are publicly conditionable, so outcome frequencies given them must be Born-exact. Whatever the reason-states contribute, they contribute through the weights: they make some options likelier, and none becomes actual without the selection. That is Clarke's architecture: agent-involving events cause the choice nondeterministically, the agent's settling is needed for any of them to go through, and neither is sufficient.

Insofar as the reasons enter instead as conscious content, they act through the free conditionals. The [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) tenet has consciousness bias quantum indeterminacy "toward patterns that express its content". A recognised reason that belongs to that content shapes the agent's selecting tendency without being a separate event-cause of the choice. That is nearer O'Connor's structured propensity, with the content-directed bias doing the structuring.

The two routes can coexist, and no Map text says which carries the reasons. The first makes the Map an integrated agent-causalist by construction. The second puts the reasons exactly where the mechanism debt is owed: a content-directed bias that leaves every public statistic intact is the bias-without-deviation problem of [born-preserving-causal-efficacy](/apex/born-preserving-causal-efficacy/), met again at the level of reasons.

## What Each Answer Would Cost the Map {#costs}

### The Integrated Answer {#costs-integrated}

The integrated answer gives the Map event-causal reason-explanation, including the contrastive kind its luck reply forgoes. On the Map's reading it also opens a reply to Derk Pereboom's wild-coincidences objection. Pereboom (2014) argues that if agent-causes settled outcomes governed by physical statistics, the long-run match between free choices and those statistics would be a coincidence. If the frequencies are the reason-events' own propensities, the agent's settling has no frequency of its own to match. Published replies contest the objection on other grounds (Runyan 2018; Taggart 2021), and on the Map's present texts the cost stays undischarged, as [the-manipulation-argument-and-hard-incompatibilism](/topics/the-manipulation-argument-and-hard-incompatibilism/) records. Three costs stand against these gains.

1. **No extra control over the event-cause.** Carlsson objects that the "integrated account does not provide for any more control over the occurrence of the action-causing events, than does an event-causal account" (Carlsson 2004). If the reasons fix the probabilities, the agent's distinctive contribution is the settling alone. The Map's case against [event-causal-libertarianism](/topics/event-causal-libertarianism/) would then rest on origination, on the "greater control than can be secured by any event-causal libertarian account" that Clarke credits to the integrated view only if it is possible (Clarke 2003, ch. 9), and not on contrastive explicability, which event-causal libertarians already have. That fits the event-causal article's verdict that agent-causal views answer the disappearing-agent form of the luck objection, not luck as such. The wild-coincidence reply and this objection are then two faces of one trade: the less the agent shifts the frequencies, the less there is to coincide and the less control she adds.
2. **Partial reasons and competing causes.** If the reasons are necessary but not sufficient, "The most we can say is that the agent acted partly for reasons" (Carlsson 2004). With two distinct causes, the free-will entry asks whether the agent might cause one decision while her reasons "simultaneously cause an incompatible decision" (O'Connor and Franklin 2022, §2). On the Map's architecture the reasons only weight the options, so the second worry may not arise; that reply is the Map's, and untested.
3. **The probability objection.** Among Clarke's grounds for doubting substance causation is that "any cause must be of a category individuals of which can affect the probabilities of their effects before those effects occur" (Clarke 2003: 203, as quoted in Carlsson 2004), which substances, being undated, seem unable to do. An agent-cause that leaves the probabilities to the reason-events falls under that exclusion, so the integrated Map would have to reject the premise. It has not yet argued against it.

### The Non-Causal Answer {#costs-non-causal}

Keeping the agent as the sole cause fits [P-A2](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a2)'s appeal to Chisholm and avoids competing causes. It leaves the luck reply intact, since that reply already forgoes contrastive explanation and needs only the non-contrastive kind O'Connor's link supplies. It owes answers to the two objections to O'Connor above, and the wild-coincidence cost stays where it is.

### Lowe's Answer {#costs-lowe}

Lowe's answer is the cleanest: choosing fixes the operative reason, and no event competes with the agent. Two features sit badly with the Map. Lowe denies that free choices carry objective probabilities, while [P-Q2](/positions/quantum-interface/#p-q2) keeps Born frequencies for the physical outcomes a choice realises. And since his volition is not even partly caused by reasons, a reason could make a difference only as the content of a choice already made. Whether that meets the tenet's content clause is open.

## The Content Clause, Not the Evidence Premise {#content-clause}

The Map's commitment to the efficacy of reasons comes from the tenet's clause that consciousness biases indeterminacy "toward patterns that express its content". If the reasons an agent recognises belong to that content, their content makes a difference to which outcome becomes actual.

It does not come from the efficacy-of-evidence premise in [the acquaintance article](/topics/acquaintance-without-causation/#content-efficacy). That premise is stated for "a downstream belief, inference or assertion" held or made *from* evidence, and says nothing about choices made *for* reasons. Extending it to choice would be a parity step the Map has not taken, and Lowe's thesis that reasons for action "must differ fundamentally from those that properly qualify as reasons for belief" (Lowe 2008, ch. 10 abstract) is one principled ground for declining it. At most, the premise adds parity pressure toward a causal "for".

The clause narrows the options without deciding among them. All three answers give recognised reasons some role in what becomes actual: as partial causes, as structuring conditions on a propensity, or as the content of an uncaused choice. The clause clearly rules out only a selection to which what consciousness presents makes no difference, the bare interactionism the acquaintance article finds no better than epiphenomenalism at using and voicing experience.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)** is the tenet engaged, at the level of compatibility rather than evidence. Each answer is compatible with outcome selection by consciousness, since each lets the agent's activity fix which outcome becomes actual. Reading selection as the agent originating the outcome is already a step beyond the tenet ([where-the-substance-commitment-enters](/concepts/where-the-substance-commitment-enters/)); choosing how reasons enter is a further step, which the Map has not taken. The integrated shape described above is a fit with a framework whose causal-selection thesis is citable only as an internal coherence result, so it is no evidence for the integrated view.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)** does not decide the question. O'Connor builds agent causation on emergent powers without substance dualism, while Lowe, the substance dualist, is non-causal about volitions.

**[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction)** fits the integrated reading best, on the Map's analysis, since the non-physical contribution is then only the settling; the same feature produces Carlsson's objection.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)** blocks a shortcut. The non-causal answer posits one cause of the choice and the integrated answer two, but the Map cannot prefer the first for having fewer causes. The choice has to turn on what each answer explains and what it costs.
