The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*'s dualism entry records that "some distinguished neuroscientists, such as Charles Sherrington (1951), Wilder Penfield (1975) and John Eccles (1994) continued to defend dualism throughout the second half of the twentieth century." The lineage behind that sentence is genuine and unusually tight: Penfield trained under Sherrington at Oxford from 1915, and Eccles took his doctorate under Sherrington in 1929, co-authoring eight papers with him. Read carefully, though, the sentence flattens a gradient. Sherrington's *Man on His Nature*—his Gifford Lectures of 1937–38, published in 1940 and revised in 1951—defends an epistemic dualism: natural science resolves the world into forms of energy, mind does not appear among them, and science knows their relation only as correlation. He accepted, tentatively, that mind influences action through a working contact with the brain, but called the "how" of that contact a blank and declined to theorise it. His students went further. Penfield built a clinical case for an interacting mind; Eccles built mechanisms for it, ending in quantum physics and, quite openly, in theology.

Two calibrations govern everything that follows, and they matter more than the lineage itself. First, the lineage is presented here as history—the provenance of images, data, and arguments the Map still draws on—never as an authority count: three eminent dissenters against a consensus is sociology, not evidence (see [why below](#is-distinguished-dissent-evidence)). Second, quotations from *Man on His Nature* are verified against a scan of the 1940 first edition; the 1947 foreword is now verified against the reissue scan, while the 1950 symposium contribution, the revised 1951 text, and most of Eccles's books remain secondary-carried and are marked as such. Relatedly, the two most-quoted "dualist Sherrington" sentences belong to his 1947 foreword to *The Integrative Action of the Nervous System* and to his 1950 contribution to *The Physical Basis of Mind*, not to *Man on His Nature*—a standing misattribution in the secondary literature that this article corrects.

## The Two-Ultimates Argument

Charles Scott Sherrington (1857–1952) coined "synapse," wrote the foundational *The Integrative Action of the Nervous System* (1906), shared the 1932 Nobel Prize with Edgar Adrian, and held the Waynflete Chair of Physiology at Oxford from 1913 to 1936. *Man on His Nature* closed his career: Gifford Lectures on natural theology's remit, staged as a running dialogue between modern physiology and the sixteenth-century physician Jean Fernel.

Its central argument starts from the physics of Sherrington's day, which resolved the natural world into forms of energy. Mind is not found among them: "Mind we think of as sui generis. Admittedly it seems not physical energy." And "The energy-concept of Science collects all so-called 'forms' of energy into a flock and looks in vain for mind among them" (1940 ed., ch. 9, p. 266, verified; Pepperell 2018 carries the line with "the mind"). Sherrington then let the materialist standpoint—now "a scientific position"—speak its own verdict: thoughts and feelings "are not amenable to the energy (matter) concept" and so lie outside natural science (p. 290, verified; also carried by Pepperell 2018)—a verdict he accepted as the honest limit of the energy concept. Energy and mind therefore "seem the two ultimate constituents" of what we are (ch. 8, p. 262, verified), and natural science cannot reduce them to one.

The companion claim concerns what science can say about their relation: nothing beyond correlation. In the same passage—still in natural science's voice—we "know nothing of any relation between thoughts and the brain, except as a gross correlation in time and space" (ch. 9, p. 290, verified); Sherrington's own comment follows: it is "embarrassing for biology," since "Biology cannot go far in its subject without being met by mind" (pp. 290–291, verified). Mind, he wrote later in the book, goes in our spatial world "more ghostly than a ghost" (ch. 11, p. 357, verified). No mechanism connects the two ultimates—on his verdict, not even the beginning of one.

The book also gave neuroscience its most famous image. Describing the waking brain, Sherrington pictured the head-mass becoming "an enchanted loom where millions of flashing shuttles weave a dissolving pattern, always a meaningful pattern though never an abiding one" (1940 ed., ch. 7, p. 225, verified). Where mind is in question, the nervous system has no "pontifical cell": it "elaborates a million-fold democracy whose each unit is a cell" (p. 277, verified)—integration without a central executive. The loom is an ancestor of the imagery the Map itself uses for the brain-side of the mind–brain relation, which is why the provenance of these lines matters.

## The 1947 Foreword and a Standing Misattribution

Sherrington's most explicit late dualist sentence appears in the foreword, dated June 1947, that he wrote for the Physiological Society's reissue of *The Integrative Action of the Nervous System*; the sentence usually paired with it comes from his 1950 broadcast contribution to *The Physical Basis of Mind*. Secondary sources routinely cite both to *Man on His Nature*. Neither appears in the 1940 text (checked against the scan), and the foreword, now checked against the 1947 reissue scan, carries only the first.

The first is a parsimony refusal, the foreword's closing sentence: "That our being should consist of two fundamental elements offers I suppose no greater inherent improbability than that it should rest on one only" (foreword, p. xx, verified against the 1947 reissue scan; carriers that add commas around "I suppose" are repunctuating). The sentence declines to let monist simplicity settle an open question—an early, clean statement of the discipline the Map's fifth tenet codifies.

The foreword's own concession is plainer in wording and the same in substance: "each of the two achieves its aim only by reason of a contact utile between them", yet "the problem of how that liaison is effected remains unsolved; it remains where Aristotle left it more than 2000 years ago" (p. xix, verified). The sentence usually quoted beside the parsimony refusal, that the relation of mind to brain is "not merely unsolved, but still devoid of a basis for its very beginning", is not in the foreword. It belongs to Sherrington's contribution to the 1950 BBC symposium *The Physical Basis of Mind*, where a later contributor quotes it back at him (Laslett 1950, p. 69); it is his last published word on the question. In both forms the claim is that no basis for a mechanism existed at all, and any interactionist descended from him inherits that gap as a debt.

## The Agnostic Pole: What Sherrington Did and Did Not Claim

The verdict-direction of Sherrington's dualism is the point most often blurred, in both directions. His dualism is epistemic: he posed the question as "dualism or monism in a limited scope, that is, with no reference to reality," because what ultimate reality may be is a question "the mind of itself has not the means to answer" (ch. 11, p. 324, verified). Within that scope he committed. The brain is "an organ of liaison between energy and mind, but not as a converter of energy into mind or vice versa," and "on these admissions we become committed to dualism" (ch. 10, pp. 318–319, verified)—though monism, he admitted, held "a certain attraction" for him.

Nor did he suspend judgment on whether mind acts. Having canvassed parallelism and "the non-possumus inference that mind is an [epiphenomenon](/concepts/epiphenomenalism/)," he allowed that "it may be, although we do not know 'how', that mind does influence energy" (ch. 6, p. 200, verified). By chapter 7 this is a working assumption: "the immediate meaning of the finite mind in the body would seem to be to influence acts," and "we felt we had to look upon their contact as a 'contact utile'" (p. 232, verified). What he refused was mechanism—"The blank of the 'how' of mind's leverage on matter" is an "impasse," and "the 'how' of it, perennial crux, we left" (p. 232)—and theology: inserting "an immortal soul" into the individual was a "trespass" (ch. 11, p. 355, verified). His own phrase for the pragmatic position, silent on how mind affects matter while assuming that it does, fits the stance he took: "somewhat as though agnosticism should believe, even in the field in which it declares it has no knowledge" (p. 200).

So Sherrington is the lineage's agnostic pole in a precise sense: agnostic about ultimate reality and about mechanism, not about whether mind and brain are in contact, and hedged throughout ("it may be," "would seem"). That sharpens a question within the lineage that is usually papered over: what does "science finds only correlation" license? Sherrington let it license a second category and an untheorised working contact. His students claimed more—clinical evidence for the contact (Penfield), then a mechanism for it (Eccles).

## The Lineage: One Teacher, Two Students, a Quantum Terminus

Sherrington's Oxford school produced a generation of eminent physiologists—John Fulton, Derek Denny-Brown, Howard Florey (Nobel 1945), Ragnar Granit (Nobel 1967) among them. Only some became public dualists. The dualist lineage is a strand within the school, not the school itself, and that distinction already deflates any argument from the school's collective prestige.

**Penfield is the clinical middle node.** A Rhodes Scholar at Merton College from January 1915, drawn to neurophysiology by Sherrington and William Osler, he returned for a final year as Sherrington's graduate student before turning to neurosurgery to study the living human brain. Six decades later, *The Mystery of the Mind* (1975)—the "Penfield (1975)" of the SEP sentence—argued from his cortical-stimulation series that the deciding, believing mind is not localizable to any stimulable site. Where the teacher had a conceptual gap, the student supplied clinical evidence; the Map's full treatment, including the comparator-model reply that keeps that evidence framework-relative, is at [penfield-interactionist-dualism](/concepts/penfield-interactionist-dualism/).

**Eccles is the mechanistic terminus.** He came to Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar in 1925 to work with Sherrington, served as his research assistant from 1928 to 1931, and shared the 1963 Nobel Prize with Hodgkin and Huxley for discoveries concerning the ionic mechanisms of excitation and inhibition in the nerve cell membrane. His interactionism escalated across four decades. Book-length statements began with his Waynflete Lectures, *The Neurophysiological Basis of Mind* (1953). *The Self and Its Brain* (Popper & Eccles 1977) argued for a self-conscious mind acting on the "liaison brain" within [Popper's three-worlds scheme](/concepts/popper-three-worlds-interactionism/), which Eccles had already adopted in *The Understanding of the Brain* (1973): "I was a dualist, now I am a trialist!" (ch. 6, p. 189, verified). *How the Self Controls Its Brain* (1994)—the SEP's "Eccles (1994)"—paired each cortical *dendron* (a bundle of apical dendrites of pyramidal cells, some forty million of them) with a mental unit he called a *psychon*, with willed action raising the probability of synaptic vesicle release in the corresponding dendron and perception running the reverse way (details as carried by secondary transcriptions). The physics beneath this is the Beck–Eccles proposal (1992): a quantum-tunnelling model of exocytosis triggering on which a mental influence biases release *probability*, so that no energy is injected and no conservation law is violated.

Eccles also made the lineage self-conscious: with William Gibson he wrote the teacher's biography, *Sherrington: His Life and Thought* (1979). And he was candid about where his own dualism rested. In *Evolution of the Brain: Creation of the Self* (1989) he wrote that "we have to recognize that we are spiritual beings with souls existing in a spiritual world [...] as well as material beings with bodies and brains existing in a material world" (p. 253, verified in the digitised Routledge edition), and that each soul is a divine creation implanted in the growing fetus between conception and birth (p. 237, a locator the book's index confirms; wording as transcribed in secondary discussions). These sentences are exactly what critics of the lineage point at, and they should be displayed rather than laundered. His philosophical collaborator did not share the concern behind them: a reviewer of their joint book recorded Eccles's "strong concern to preserve the theoretical possibility of the survival of human personality beyond bodily death, to which Popper is comparatively indifferent" (Gray 1981, p. 124).

Two consequences follow from displaying the gradient honestly. First, the lineage runs from an untheorised working contact (Sherrington) through clinical interactionism (Penfield) to confessional, mechanised substance dualism (Eccles): the teacher called the insertion of an immortal soul a "trespass," and his student wrote of each soul as a divine creation. Citing "the lineage" as if it held one position misrepresents all three. Second, the Beck–Eccles mechanism and Eccles's theology are separable, and should be separated in both directions. The 1992 proposal is a physical model of probability-biasing at the synapse; nothing in it requires divinely created souls, so refuting the theology leaves the mechanism standing—and adopting the mechanism family, as the Map's quantum-interface programme in effect does, commits no one to the theology.

## The Critics at Full Strength

The dualist reading of this material was contested from the beginning, and the strongest replies deserve full statement.

**Ritchie (1941).** Reviewing *Man on His Nature* in *Nature*, A. D. Ritchie answered the two-ultimates argument with flat naturalism: "There does not appear to be anything incorporeal about living organisms as such"—life being special arrangements of ordinary physics and chemistry. The book's own first scientific audience declined the dualist verdict.

**Pepperell (2018).** The modern direct answer to the energy argument—a philosophical argument drawing on neuroscience, published in *Frontiers in Psychology*—accepts Sherrington's premise that mind is not a *form* of energy and blocks his conclusion: consciousness, Pepperell argues, is a physical process constituted by the *organization* of energy in the brain. If mind is energy's structure rather than a further item on energy's inventory, the two ultimates collapse into one, and the "looks in vain" observation loses its force. Sherrington named this option himself in 1947—that "we can suppose that our thinking is likewise an outcome of 'energy'", so that "the body-mind disparation disappears"—and set it aside as resting on "an assumption which seems to many an unjustified one" (foreword, pp. xix–xx, verified).

**Smith (2001).** The historicising reply. Smith places Eccles in a Cartesian tradition of British neurophysiology that he dates from T. H. Huxley, traces how the mind–brain problem troubled Eccles throughout his career, and argues that the quantum-microphysical solution Eccles finally reached provides—"at least in the form Eccles espoused"—no solution to the problem Descartes posed. (Access caveat: Smith's argument is reported here from the abstract; the full text was not read.)

**Arshavsky (2006).** Argues that a *covert* dualism—shown in neuroscientists' unwillingness to discuss neural mechanisms of consciousness, leaving the problem to psychologists and philosophers—pervades the field well beyond the overt dualists, and locates its "scientific roots" in neuroscience's own paradigm of cognition as the work of networks of simple elements; overcoming it is the paper's stated goal. The same access caveat applies.

The Map's replies to this family live elsewhere—the parsimony discipline in [parsimony-case-for-interactionist-dualism](/topics/parsimony-case-for-interactionist-dualism/), which reaches organization-style dissolutions only as instances of its general case, and the interface-problem treatment of the "no solution" charge in [the-interface-problem](/topics/the-interface-problem/)—but the critics' positions stand as stated, and Smith's verdict that the problem remains unsolved is one Sherrington himself stated in 1947 and again in 1950: no basis for a mechanism existed.

## Is Distinguished Dissent Evidence?

No. The SEP sentence establishes that dualism retained scientifically literate, indeed eminent, defenders deep into the twentieth century—a fact about the sociology of the debate that corrects a common assumption that no serious neuroscientist ever held the view. It carries no evidential weight beyond that. Eminence in synaptic physiology confers no privileged access to metaphysics; the dualists were a strand within their school, not the school itself; and counting authorities is exactly the move the Map's own methodology disallows when it runs the other way.

The honest use of the lineage is as provenance. The enchanted loom is the ancestor of a family of brain-side images; Penfield's stimulation series is a real clinical dataset whose interpretation remains contested; the 1947 parsimony sentence is an argument that stands or falls on its content; Beck–Eccles is a mechanism proposal assessable on its physics. Each of these earns whatever weight it has independently of who produced it. That rule governs the Map's own citation of the SEP sentence in [penfield-interactionist-dualism](/concepts/penfield-interactionist-dualism/) as much as anyone else's use of the lineage.

## Relation to Site Perspective

Sherrington's two-ultimates argument is an ancestor of the Map's first tenet, and the relation should be stated without conscription. His verdict was epistemic—natural science finds two irreducibles and no known bridge, and he scoped his dualism "with no reference to reality"—whereas the Map's Dualism tenet is an ontological commitment. The Map goes beyond Sherrington here and does not claim him for the further step.

The 1947 foreword sentence is the cleanest early anticipation of **Tenet 5** (Occam's razor has limits): a refusal to let monist parsimony decide a question the evidence leaves open. This is also the Map's principled reply to Pepperell-style dissolutions—the organization-of-energy reading is simpler, but by Tenet 5 its simplicity is not by itself a reason to believe it, a point developed in [parsimony-case-for-interactionist-dualism](/topics/parsimony-case-for-interactionist-dualism/).

On **Tenet 3** Sherrington stands nearer the Map than his reputation suggests, and the Map claims no more than that: he held mind's working contact with the brain as what "would seem" to be so, and refused only its mechanism; the 1947 foreword keeps the hedge and says more: "two continuous series of events, one physico-chemical, the other psychical, and at times interaction between them" (p. xvii, verified). On **Tenet 2** he offers two remarks and no mechanism. In 1940, "the physicist releases the individual" from Laplacean necessity into "a probability system" (ch. 6, p. 201, verified), which clears room for mind's influence without proposing how it acts. In 1947 he noted that the retina's reaction to "as few as six photons can be perceived" and "can release motor behaviour of the whole body", a process "called by some biologists 'amplification'" (foreword, p. xviii, verified): the [amplifier vocabulary](/topics/first-quantum-free-will-wave-1927-1940/) Ralph Lillie had introduced in *Science* in 1927, which Compton built his decision model on and [Popper](/concepts/popper-three-worlds-interactionism/#penny-toss) would later dispute, used for a biological cascade with no claim that mind acts at the quantum event. His 1950 "devoid of a basis for its very beginning" names precisely the gap the Map's quantum-interface programme works on. The mechanism side of the Map's framework descends instead from the lineage's terminus: Beck–Eccles probability-biasing at the synapse is one ancestor of Tenet 2's minimal quantum interaction, alongside the von Neumann–Wigner–Stapp route traced in [history-of-the-interaction-problem](/topics/history-of-the-interaction-problem/). The Map adopts that mechanism family minimally—stripped of psychon bookkeeping and entirely decoupled from Eccles's theology, which the tenets neither need nor assert. The Map's architecture is fairly summarised as taking Sherrington's gap seriously and Eccles's mechanism minimally.

Finally, following [evidential-status-discipline](/project/evidential-status-discipline/), the Map treats Sherrington, Penfield, and Eccles as sources of arguments and data, never as a roll-call of eminent support—and accepts the mirror image: the far longer roll-call of eminent physicalists is no evidence against the tenets either.
