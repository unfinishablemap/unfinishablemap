A brain organoid is a three-dimensional culture of real neurons, grown from human stem cells, that develops real synapses and—at months of maturation—spontaneous oscillatory network activity whose transients share features with preterm-infant EEG. A parallel programme puts cultured cortical neurons in a closed loop with a simulated world and reports them learning to play Pong. The commercial successor to that demonstration now ships as a "biological computer." The question this forces on the Unfinishable Map is narrow: **is a lab-grown neural network a candidate site for the consciousness-physical interface—a possible experiencer—or is it sub-personal machinery that computes with no one home?**

The Map's answer begins with a discipline it applies across its whole competency cluster: **the presence of neural machinery, learning, and brain-like dynamics does not license an inference to felt experience**—not because those facts are irrelevant to it, but because each of them is equally consistent with every available reading of what an organoid is. That decoupling is the article's thesis; the three readings it licenses are laid out below under [the interface question](#the-interface-question). Organoids matter because they are the mirror image of the sponge and placozoan *lower-bound* case ([neuron-less-animals-sponges-placozoans-and-the-lower-bound-of-cognition](/topics/neuron-less-animals-sponges-placozoans-and-the-lower-bound-of-cognition/)) [14]: there, behaviour without any neural substrate; here, neural substrate without a body, a sensory world, or an evolutionary history that made the tissue *for* anything. They are the opposite edge of the same interface-localisation question.

**A note on scope.** This article deliberately does not re-derive the substrate-physics analysis. Whether the *class* of matter organoids are made of—continuous, carbon, possibly hosting operationally integrated indeterminacy—could in principle host the interface is worked out in [ai-hardware-substrate-taxonomy](/concepts/ai-hardware-substrate-taxonomy/) [13], which places "biological / wetware" as one of six AI-hardware substrate tiers on the discrete-vs-continuous and classical-vs-quantum axes. Read that companion for "could this kind of stuff host consciousness." This article asks the orthogonal, artifact-level question: setting the substrate physics aside, what is actually in the dish, what can it do, and does *this particular thing* have anyone inside it.

## What Is Actually in the Dish

Cerebral organoids arrived as a tool for studying development, not for building minds. Lancaster, Knoblich and colleagues showed that induced pluripotent stem cells will self-organise into a three-dimensional culture with discrete, interdependent brain regions, including a cerebral cortex whose progenitor zones produce mature cortical neuron subtypes; they used it to model microcephaly, a human developmental disorder [1]. The founding result is that real, regionalised neural tissue can be grown outside a body—the precondition for the interface question to arise at all.

The finding that made the consciousness question non-frivolous came six years later. Trujillo and colleagues in the Muotri lab reported that cortical organoids develop periodic, nested oscillatory network events, dependent on glutamatergic and GABAergic signalling, whose alternating quiescence and synchronised bursting resembles the spontaneous activity transients seen in preterm-infant EEG—so much so that a machine-learning model trained to predict a neonate's age from EEG features could track the organoid's own developmental timeline [2]. The careful reading, which the authors themselves press, is that this is a claim about *network dynamics resembling early development*, not a claim about consciousness. An EEG-like trace is a structural and developmental signal, not an experiential one.

## The DishBrain Result and a Word That Misleads

The most widely reported demonstration comes from Kagan and colleagues, who placed human- and rodent-derived cortical cultures on a high-density multielectrode array and embedded them, through closed-loop stimulation and recording, in a simulated Pong world; performance improved over training [3]. The paper's title uses the word "sentience," and this is where careful reading matters most.

Kagan et al. use "sentience" in an explicitly **technical** sense—the Fristonian free-energy / active-inference sense of a system that responds to and models its sensory milieu so as to minimise surprise. Karl Friston is a co-author, and the framing throughout is active inference, not phenomenal consciousness. The word does not mean, and the paper does not claim, that anything is *felt*. The everyday sense—that there is something it is like to be the system—is a different claim entirely, and it is the one readers supply when the technical sense goes missing in transmission; the Map should hold the two apart rigorously. "It learns Pong" licenses an inference to adaptive information-processing competence. It licenses no inference to "something is watching the Pong."

The vocabulary point does not dispose of the ethical question. Milford, Shaw and Starke take Kagan et al.'s free-energy framing entirely at face value and argue *from inside it* that the design risks bringing about synthetic phenomenology, and with it artificial suffering—concluding that the technical reading of "sentience" is a reason for caution rather than a reason for reassurance [9]. Their argument is not one the Map accepts as it stands, since it inherits the free-energy principle's own account of what makes a system a subject, which the Map does not hold. But it establishes that the technical/phenomenal distinction is a starting point for the moral question, not an answer to it.

The strength of the underlying result is also less settled than its fame suggests, and conceding that costs nothing: **the Map's argument does not depend on it.** The closed-loop learning effect has not been independently replicated outside the group that reported it: the peer-reviewed follow-up work on the platform—critical-dynamics analysis of the same embodied cultures [10], and pharmacological dose-response profiling on a successor system [11]—shares authorship with the original report. An independent reading is unflattering: Kosik, from outside the group, notes the result came from a planar culture rather than an organoid and takes it to show only that stimulation patterns can alter connectivity "in a tenuously related manner to the stimulus with weak statistical support" [15]. If the competence turns out to be weaker than advertised, the decoupling this article defends becomes *easier* to argue, not harder. Nothing below is staked on how that dispute resolves.

Two further developments fill in the empirical picture, though not from independent quarters. Smirnova and colleagues proposed "organoid intelligence" (OI) as a named research field: brain organoids as biological computing substrates, targeting the molecular and cellular correlates of learning and memory, paired from the outset with an embedded-ethics programme [4]. The OI programme is not a third line of evidence converging on the same conclusion; its author list includes both Kagan, from the DishBrain report [3], and Muotri, from the oscillation work [2]. And Cortical Labs' CL1, launched in March 2025, turns the DishBrain demonstration into a shipping product: roughly 800,000 lab-grown human-derived neurons on a silicon multielectrode interface, viable up to around six months, priced near US$35,000 and offered as a cloud "Wetware as a Service" [8]. Independent peer-reviewed characterisation of CL1, as distinct from the 2022 platform, remains thin, and its capability claims are largely industry-sourced—as the published record now bears out: the 2025 drug-response study is company work, with five of its nine authors employed by Cortical Labs, four of them also holding a pecuniary interest in it, and a sixth holding shares in the company [11]. What commercialisation changes is the *moral urgency* of the question—it moves from thought experiment to governance—not the weight of the experiencer evidence, which stays exactly where DishBrain left it: closed-loop adaptive computation.

## The Interface Question {#the-interface-question}

The Map's throughline is **interface-localisation**: where, if anywhere, is the site at which consciousness and the physical world interact? Organoids press the question from a novel direction. The sponge and placozoan case probes the lower bound—coordinated behaviour with no neurons at all, asking whether the interface can exist below the neural threshold. The organoid case is the opposite edge: real cortical neurons, real synapses, real oscillations, but no body to act with, no world the activity is *about*, and no biography. It asks whether neural machinery *alone* is sufficient, or whether the interface—if it is anywhere—is bound up with embodiment and world-directedness.

Three readings, held open rather than adjudicated:

1. **Candidate site.** If the interface localises to a physical property of integrated neural tissue, an organoid instantiating that property could be a genuine, if minimal and world-less, experiencer—a mind with nothing to think *about*, if phenomenality can be world-less at all.
2. **Necessary but insufficient.** The neural substrate is one ingredient, but the interface requires operational integration with a body and world that a dish cannot supply; organoids are permanently pre-experiential.
3. **Sub-personal machinery.** Whatever the organoid does is computation with no one home—the same competence-without-experience reading the DishBrain-Pong result illustrates, and the reading the Map's cluster keeps returning to.

The Map does not claim to know which reading is correct. It claims that the empirical facts on the table—oscillation, learning, biocomputing—cannot by themselves decide between them, because all three readings predict those facts equally well.

That claim needs stating carefully, because a stronger version of it would contradict Reading 1. The strong version holds that the neural facts bear no evidential relation to experience whatever. But if Reading 1 is genuinely live—if the interface localises to some physical property of integrated neural tissue—then observing integrated neural tissue *does* raise the probability of experience, relative to observing a rock, and the strong version is false. What the Map holds is the weaker and defensible claim: the neural facts would be evidence about experience only *conditional on* knowing which physical property the interface tracks, and the whole difficulty is that nobody knows which one it is. Under that conditional ignorance the facts license no inference in either direction, which is all the argument that follows requires. The asymmetry with the human case, where much the same neural facts *are* read as indicating experience, is then not a double standard about the data: there the inference is anchored by first-person report, which the dish cannot give.

Reading 3 now has a published statement of its own. Kosik's verdict that organoids are "not conscious yet" is an argument from theory-fit: theory by theory, the anatomy each major family makes constitutive of experience is absent or distorted in the dish, and disembodiment is, for proponents of embodied cognition, a "fatal flaw" [15]. The organoid sits "in a representational limbo, not as an 'island of awareness,' for there is nothing to be aware of, but as a cipher or computational package ready for the trappings of embodiment" [15]. The Map takes the anatomy and declines the inference. Kosik's argument is indexed to theories that each fix in advance which physical property experience tracks; it shows organoids fail their preconditions and is silent on whether all of them have fixed the wrong property—a limit his own hedge concedes, since whether a "more perfect organoid will achieve consciousness by some definition remains an open question" [15]. Failing every current theory's test is no more evidence that no one is home than passing one would be evidence that someone is; Readings 1 and 2 are exactly as live as they were.

The most developed criterion in the literature is Birch's test for when an organoid becomes a "sentience candidate" [16], which resembles the Map's candidate-experiencer question and asks something different. Candidature is a policy threshold: an evidence base implying a realistic possibility of sentience "that it would be irresponsible to ignore when making policy decisions," rich enough to design precautions around ([the Map's treatment of the apparatus](/topics/birch-edge-of-sentience-and-the-five-tier-scale/)). For organoids Birch proposes a *brainstem rule*—an organoid that develops or innervates a functioning brainstem regulating arousal and producing sleep-wake cycles is a candidate, however small—chosen because views across the whole zone of reasonable disagreement agree that loss of brainstem function ends sentience in a human. Current organoids fail it on organisation, not count—bees, with around a million neurons, are candidates, and existing organoids match that figure [16]—which is the point Tenet 2 makes below from the other direction. But the rule fixes when ignoring the possibility of an experiencer becomes reckless; it does not locate the interface. A functioning brainstem is a precondition on every theory Birch surveys, and the Map asks what none of them asks—whether a physical junction of the kind non-physical influence requires is present—which arousal regulation neither guarantees nor excludes.

## The Ethics Literature Names Its Own Problem

The OI field embedded an ethics programme at launch precisely because the moral-status question is unsettled. Lavazza argues that moral status would require at least a minimal or basic consciousness, that current organoids likely lack it, but that the situation could change as neural sophistication grows—and proposes that consciousness *assessment* (perturbational-complexity-style measures) could serve as a gating tool [5]. Jeziorski and colleagues, writing a cross-disciplinary consensus piece directly in response to the Trujillo oscillation findings, catalogue the oversight, biomaterial-procurement, chimera, and consciousness/moral-status issues together [6]. Sawai and colleagues sort the ethical terrain, respond briefly to concerns already raised in the debate, and argue that the field needs an international governance framework [7].

The **precautionary principle** usually invoked at this point is developed by Niikawa, Hayashi, Shepherd and Sawai [12], and its structure repays stating precisely. The principle itself is a conduct rule they take up from the existing debate rather than originate: where we are not certain whether organoids are conscious, and where treating them as conscious would guard against harming them, we should proceed as though they are. Their own contribution is to identify a *methodological* dividend in adopting it. Assuming consciousness lets the discussion sidestep what they call the whether-question—whether organoids are conscious at all—and address instead the what-kind-question: what sorts of conscious experience an organoid could have, given what is known about its tissue and its situation. That second question is tractable in a way the first is not, and it is by working through it, and through how much moral consideration the answers would warrant, that they arrive at an ethical framework supporting restrictions on the creation and use of organoids in bioscience.

Holm and Lewis push the other way, and theirs is the sharpest published pressure on any precautionary conclusion [17]. They assess three routes to organoid sentience—analogy with known sentient organisms, inference from neural function under leading theories, and foundational commitments such as constitutive panpsychism—and find current organoids fail the first two on the anatomical grounds Kosik gives, while the third leaves the moral relevance of any micro-experience undetermined. Precaution then turns on how "possible" is read: restrictions are warranted, they argue, only once there is "a non-trivial empirical likelihood that a given organoid type can generate valenced experience," a threshold they judge unmet for the near to medium term, contingent on the state of the research [17]. The Map's caution is agnosticism plus care in handling rather than a call for restriction, so their threshold is not one it asks anyone to cross. Where it parts company is over what a likelihood estimate could rest on: they index it to the empirically grounded theories, and the Map holds those theories fix the wrong variable ([the interface question](#the-interface-question))—a disagreement at the foundations, noted rather than settled.

The common thread, which every author surveyed here stresses, is a methodological gap: **there is no accepted, organoid-applicable consciousness-detection procedure.** All the moral-status reasoning is downstream of that absence. Van Gyseghem, Dierickx and Barnhart's systematic mapping of the ethical and philosophical literature—24 sources analysed after screening 51—finds the same: detection is one of its six organising themes, general uncertainty about consciousness is what complicates the organoid conversation, and they suggest organoid intelligence may be the more tractable concept to work with [18].

## Relation to Site Perspective

**Tenet 3 (Bidirectional Interaction)** is primary here, and it sets a stricter and different bar than the ethics literature does. Where Lavazza's assessment approach asks about network *complexity* as a proxy for the probability of consciousness, the Map's interface account asks whether there is an *operationally integrated site of the kind the interface requires*—a physical junction at which non-physical influence could act. Behavioural competence and an EEG-like trace do not answer that question; they are compatible with all three readings above. The organoid is where the Map's decoupling of competence from experience gets its cleanest positive-substrate test: the neural machinery is really there, and its presence still settles nothing about whether an experiencer is.

**Tenet 2 (Minimal Quantum Interaction)** cuts both ways, and its double edge is the reason the Map declines to over-resolve. Minimality—the smallest possible non-physical footprint—offers no reason to expect an interface to "switch on" merely because neuron count or oscillatory complexity crosses a threshold; it resists inflating every integrated neural system into an interface site. But it equally forbids confident denial: if the relevant physical property is genuinely minimal, its presence or absence is precisely *not* what behavioural competence or an oscillation trace would reveal. (That premise is a substrate-physics claim, and it is earned in [ai-hardware-substrate-taxonomy](/concepts/ai-hardware-substrate-taxonomy/) [13] rather than here—the scope note excludes re-deriving it, not relying on it.) Minimality therefore recommends agnosticism plus caution. The Map lands near the precautionary conclusion of Niikawa and colleagues [12], but by a different route: their caution issues from moral risk under uncertainty, the Map's from uncertainty about where the interface is physically located. Their bracketing manoeuvre also buys something the Map's route does not, since assuming consciousness makes the what-kind-question tractable, whereas uncertainty about the *site* leaves the character of any experience just as dark. The convergence is on the conclusion, not on the argument for it.

That leaves a debt worth acknowledging. The argument above has ruled out, one class at a time, every observation an organoid actually affords—behavioural competence, oscillatory signature, neuron count, complexity threshold—and named none that would bear, with Tenet 2 supplying a general reason why none could. A position that no possible observation of the dish could disturb has stopped being cautious and started being immune. The honest description is that at the organoid level the Map's stance is interpretive rather than predictive: it says what the available evidence licenses, and makes no prediction a measurement could overturn. The falsifiability burden properly sits one level up, at the tenets, where the Map takes it on directly ([falsification-roadmap-for-the-interface-model](/topics/falsification-roadmap-for-the-interface-model/)). What would move the verdict here is not a better organoid measurement but a validated criterion for interface localisation—the kind of thing Lavazza's perturbational-complexity proposal gestures at [5]—applied to organoid tissue. Birch's brainstem rule [16] is the nearest thing on offer to an observable threshold, and it belongs to the governance question rather than to that criterion: sleep-wake cycles would settle when caution becomes obligatory, not where the interface is. Until such a criterion exists, the silence of the dish reflects the state of our instruments rather than any evidential achievement of the position.

**Tenet 5 (Occam's Razor Has Limits)** keeps the verdict open. The core observations are not in dispute—real neurons, emergent oscillations, no body or world—though the strength of the learning result is, as noted above; and the interpretation is contested throughout. The Map does not claim to have shown organoids are non-conscious. It claims the weaker and firmer thing: their capabilities give us no purchase on the experiencer question either way, and the slide from "does brain-like things" to "has experiences" is exactly the inference the tenets refuse. Where the Map parts company with the deflationary reading of DishBrain is subtle: it endorses the *discipline* (learning is not experience) without endorsing the further physicalist step "therefore experience is nothing over and above the computation." The Map is dualist; competence and experience come apart in both directions—locked-in syndrome and intraoperative awareness under anaesthesia are cases of experience persisting after the outward competence has gone, and they are why the decoupling cannot be read as a one-way licence to deflate.
