Anton syndrome is the clinical condition in which a blind patient, usually after damage to both occipital lobes, sincerely says they can see and describes surroundings they cannot see. It looks like a refutation of first-person authority. The Map's verdict is narrower. Anton leaves standing the Map's strongest first-person claim, the near-incorrigible [Layer 1](/topics/phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence/) judgement that experience is occurring at all, because that claim is global and Anton patients are conscious. What fails is a *modal* claim, "I can see". If no visual experience stands behind it, which no case on record excludes, it is also a false modal existence claim, "I am having visual experience"; claims at that grain therefore cannot be counted as near-incorrigible under pathology, and they carry the fallible Layer-2 grade of authority rather than Layer-1 grade. The case also fixes where authority attaches: to *seemings*, not to *reports*. A sincere report of seeing does not certify that a visual seeming stands behind it. David Chalmers, whose fading-qualia argument relies on first-person reliability, had already conceded blindness denial by name and restricted that reliability to rational systems "whose cognitive mechanisms are unimpaired" (Chalmers 1995).

What the evidence cannot settle is whether the patient who says "I see" has no visual experience or has imagery or hallucination that is taken for perception, the readings A and B [set out below](#three-readings-and-an-overlay). Every available measure is a report or a behaviour, no case on record discriminates between them, and different patients probably fall on different sides. For the Map's [tenets](/tenets/), Anton is compatible with Dualism without supporting it, compatible with Bidirectional Interaction only as report reliability in unimpaired systems (a form the tenets already concede), and silent on Minimal Quantum Interaction and No Many Worlds.

## The Clinical Picture

The earliest known description is Seneca's. Around 63 CE he reports that Harpaste, a woman of his household, suddenly lost her sight: "nescit esse se caecam" ("she does not know she is blind"), and she kept asking to be moved because the house was dark (*Epistulae morales* 50.2). André (2018) argues that the case "probably constitutes" the first description of the syndrome. Gabriel Anton's 1899 paper, which gave it its name, concerned patients' perception of their own brain disease in cortical blindness and cortical deafness.

It is uncommon even among the cortically blind: of 25 such patients in one series, "Only 3 patients denied their blindness, although 4 others were unaware of their visual loss" (Aldrich et al. 1987). In one case report, a woman who was "walking into objects and was clearly blind" "maintained she was able to 'see' things around her"; asked about the doctor's tie, "she was quick with an answer, but one that was incorrect"; and "Although she would deny visual deficit, she would accept such assistance" at meals (Maddula et al. 2009). Speech and conduct come apart, as in [anosognosia for paralysis](/topics/anosognosia-and-the-reversible-self-monitoring-channel/).

Two variants matter below. Denial can follow blindness caused by damage to the eye or optic nerve rather than the cortex (Swartz & Brust 1984; McDaniel & McDaniel 1991). And the syndrome has a mirror image: a man diagnosed as cortically blind who, in a small preserved field, named objects, colours and famous faces and read single words with better than 50 per cent accuracy, yet denied seeing, "typically stating 'I feel it'" (Hartmann et al. 1991). The authors called it "inverse Anton's syndrome".

## Three Readings and an Overlay

Three clinical readings and one philosophical description are live. They differ on whether a visual seeming stands behind the report.

**Reading A: a report without a seeming.** No visual experience occurs. The system that produces reports fails to register the absence and fills the gap by confabulation. Levine (1990) supplies the reading's strongest premise: interruption of a sensory pathway "is not associated with any immediate sensory experience that uniquely specifies the defect. Instead the sensory loss must be discovered by a process of self-observation and inference." On this premise absence of experience is not itself experienced, and the patient's error is an error of inference *about* experience.

**Reading B: a seeming taken for perception.** Visual experience of some kind occurs (imagery, hallucination or residual input), and the patient mistakes its source. Goldenberg, Müllbacher and Nowak (1995) studied a patient with almost complete destruction of primary visual cortex whose "pretended visual experiences could frequently be traced back to synaesthetic translations of acoustic or tactile perceptions into mental visual images". Their conclusion is hedged: "Possibly, the belief to see resulted from a confusion of mental visual images with real percepts." On this reading Anton is the [reality-monitoring](/voids/perceptual-reality-monitoring-void/) failure Perky exposed in normal subjects in 1910, run in reverse: Perky's subjects took faint perception for imagery, while the patient takes imagery for perception, with no perception left to compete.

**Reading C: a shared anosognosia mechanism.** Kletenik et al. (2023), mapping 24 published lesions of visual anosognosia onto normative connectivity, found a domain-specific visual network and "a shared, cross-modal network for awareness of deficits centered on memory-related brain structures", which they read as a comparator of present inputs against "priors stored in memory". Monai et al. (2024) found that Anton "depends on bilateral striate and extrastriate occipital damage, and disconnection of ventral and dorsal frontoparietal regions involved in attention control", and that their results "reveal the unique shared combination of content-specific and supramodal mechanisms in anosognosia". Reading C concerns mechanism and combines with A or B; by itself it says nothing about what the patient experiences.

**The higher-order overlay.** Lane and Liang (2008) used Anton with hallucinations against Rosenthal's two-component version of [higher-order thought theory](/concepts/higher-order-theories/). In such a case, they argue, "here we have better evidence that p-consciousness, in the form of substantial visual experiences actually occurs", though no first-order visual state is there to be targeted: "In a word, the HOT seems to be sufficient for p-consciousness." That describes a visual seeming with no visual state behind it, a structure [illusionists](/concepts/illusionism/) generalise to every phenomenal seeming. The Map's [constitutive view of observation](/concepts/constitutive-vs-referring-observation/) rules the description out, since on that view a visual seeming cannot occur without visual phenomenality. The data do not refute it either. Lane and Liang's premise that a hallucination involves no first-order state is contestable: a hallucination can be a first-order visual state generated inside the brain, which puts the case under reading B. Anton locates the bedrock dispute between constitutive and higher-order accounts at a clinical site without moving it.

## What the Cases Discriminate

The most informative single case is Swartz and Brust's (1984), known here at the strength of its published abstract. During alcohol withdrawal, "a man totally blind following ocular trauma experienced formed visual hallucinations ('alcoholic hallucinosis'), the unreality of which he recognized. During these same periods, however, he believed that his vision had been restored, and confabulated descriptions of his surroundings". The authors add: "He was otherwise mentally normal."

Three things come apart in this one patient. Visual phenomenology is present, by his own report. He classifies it correctly, as hallucination. And a false belief that he can see arises anyway, only in the periods when the hallucinations occur. The belief tracks the presence of visual phenomenology without being a misreading of the hallucinations themselves, so the case fits neither pure A nor pure B. The abstract does not say how his descriptions of his surroundings related to what he was hallucinating, which is what would show whether a seeming stood behind each report of sight.

Other cases pull different ways. Goldenberg's patient supports B. Against any general version of B, a prospective study of hemianopia found that "Patients with phosphenes, photopsias, or visual hallucinations were usually aware of their visual field loss" (Celesia et al. 1997): positive visual phenomena went with awareness of loss, not denial. Hemianopia is partial blindness, so the transfer to Anton is by analogy. Supporting A's emphasis on judgement, McDaniel and McDaniel (1991) describe denial of *monocular* blindness after optic-nerve injury with bifrontal contusions, "even in the absence of a delirium or significant dementia", and suggest the frontal damage "may have been a critical factor". A 2024 report of visual anosognosia and hallucinations after bilateral enucleation and bifrontal injury is a neighbour rather than an instance: its authors list "the conviction that vision will resume" among the "clear clinical differences with Anton syndrome" (Rodríguez et al. 2024), so it is not counted here as a report of seeing.

The clinical consensus is that anosognosia, Anton's included, is "multifactorial" (Prigatano 2009). The readings are best treated as a family of failures sharing a report profile, with different patients plausibly falling under different members.

The decisive limit is methodological. Every measure in these studies is a report or a behaviour. No study was found that images the brain while a patient reports seeing, measures metacognitive confidence in Anton patients, or manipulates imagery within a patient to separate A from B. Kletenik et al. concede that their visual cases "lack formal testing for awareness".

## The Philosophers Who Used It

**Chalmers.** The fading-qualia argument for [organizational-invariance](/concepts/organizational-invariance/) needs the premise that a rational subject cannot be radically wrong about its own current experience. Chalmers (1995) considers blindness denial as an objection to that premise and concedes the error: "in cases of blindness denial, subjects believe that they are having visual experiences when they likely have none." His reply is a restriction. "In systems whose belief-formation mechanisms are impaired, anything goes," and "The plausible claim is not that no system can be massively mistaken about its experiences, but that no rational system whose cognitive mechanisms are unimpaired can be so mistaken."

The restriction is principled only if impairment can be identified independently of the error itself, and mostly it can. Swartz and Brust observe that "dementia nearly always coexists". Levine (1990) holds that "unawareness of total blindness occurs only in association with severe intellectual impairment". McDaniel's patient, without dementia, had bifrontal damage and generalised anosognosia. The pressure cases are those reported with preserved cognition, such as Swartz and Brust's "otherwise mentally normal" patient, and clinical definitions differ on whether "relatively well-preserved cognition" belongs to the syndrome (André 2018). Even there, belief formation is impaired beyond the false belief itself, since patients "can skillfully confabulate when their failure to see things accurately is called to their attention" (Lane & Liang 2008): the belief resists correction. Mogensen (2025, n. 18) presses from the other side. Anton exhibits "something like this profile", a subject failing to notice that conscious visual experience is absent, so "Chalmers needs to say why a commitment to the nomological possibility of Joe's unnoticed blindness is significantly harder to accept than a commitment to the actuality of Anton syndrome."

**Block.** Block (1995) classes anosognosia as "primarily a defect in A-consciousness, not P-consciousness", an inability to acknowledge or access information about another deficit. On that classification Anton is a failure of access to the visual deficit, consistent with either A or B.

**Macpherson and Allen-Hermanson.** Fiona Macpherson (2010) devotes a full paper to the case, setting it beside zombies under the heading of a disjunctive theory of introspection. Allen-Hermanson (2017) reports that "Philosophers have recently argued" from unnoticed blindness and unnoticed echolocation to the conclusion that "conscious introspection is highly unreliable", and answers that "These examples do not support skepticism about the reliability of introspection."

**Schwitzgebel.** The best-known sceptic about introspection does not rest his case on Anton: Schwitzgebel and Gordon (2000) argue that the Cartesian view fails for ordinary subjects, not only in marginal cases of clinical pathology. Schwitzgebel (2008) grants that the judgement that one is conscious may be the exception, "Maybe this is one fact about our conscious experience we infallibly know" (p. 262), while setting modality-specific judgements such as "my visual experience is all clear and stable one hundred degrees into the periphery" apart from the self-verifying ones (p. 261). His line falls where the Map's does, with global existence protected and modal claims exposed. The agreement is local: his own conclusion, that naive introspection is broadly unreliable, runs against the weight the Map gives first-person evidence at Layer 2.

## Global and Modal Reports

Anton completes a typology of failed or contested self-reports once a second axis, *grain*, is added. A global report concerns whether experience exists at all. A modal report concerns whether experience of a given kind exists.

| | False or contested negative report | False or contested positive report |
|---|---|---|
| **Global** | [Cotard delusion](/concepts/cotard-delusion/): "I do not exist", reported from somewhere | The [zombie](/concepts/philosophical-zombies/)'s "I am conscious": hypothetical, unobservable by construction |
| **Modal** | [Blindsight](/concepts/blindsight/)'s "I see nothing" (contested); inverse Anton's "I feel it" | Anton: "I can see" |

Three things follow. First, the [self-stultification](/concepts/self-stultification/) argument behind Layer 1 protects only the global-negative cell: doubting that one has experience is itself an experience, and Cotard is the clinical test of that cell. Second, the global-positive cell, where Layer 1 actually makes its claim, has no clinical instance. A false positive there would need an unconscious subject asserting consciousness, and Anton patients are conscious. Third, the modal row is where reports fail in both directions under pathology. Levine's premise explains the gap: nothing comparable to self-stultification guards a modal positive, because no experience announces that a kind of experience is missing. Two neighbouring cases sit outside the table: [covert consciousness](/topics/covert-consciousness-and-cognitive-motor-dissociation/) is the absence of report, and [depersonalisation](/concepts/depersonalisation/) is the hedged report.

The modal row presses on the Map's [blindsight](/concepts/blindsight/) article, whose reply to illusionism cites patients' reports of "a striking difference" between seeing and sensing without seeing. Anton and its inverse show brain-damaged patients misclassifying seeing in both directions, so those reports need the caution the article already extends to Phillips's reading of blindsight as degraded conscious vision.

## What It Costs the Map

Anton leaves the Map's first-person epistemology standing and forces three clarifications.

**Modal existence carries Layer-2 grade.** The [gradient of warranted trust](/topics/phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence/#the-gradient-of-warranted-trust) gives its highest grade to global phenomenal existence, that experience occurs at all. Claims that experience *of a given kind* is occurring fall outside that row, and on reading A, which the evidence cannot rule out, Anton is a documented case of their failing. They belong with attended content: strong in unimpaired subjects, and fallible.

**Authority attaches to seemings.** On the constitutive view, a visual seeming cannot occur without visual phenomenality, so such an observation cannot be wrong about whether visual phenomenality is occurring. Anton is consistent with that claim under either reading. Under A, no visual seeming occurs and none is misreported. Under B, a seeming occurs and its source is misjudged. The price is plain: a sincere first-person report does not certify that a seeming stands behind it, and neither the listener nor, in pathology, the speaker can always tell which reports are backed by one. [Weak Revelation](/concepts/revelation-thesis/) survives either way; under B the experience's existence is revealed correctly and only its source, no part of its character, is mistaken.

**The unimpaired-systems clause needs independent markers.** The Map's rationale for [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction), that "the fact that we discuss consciousness counts as evidence against epiphenomenalism", survives Anton only as a claim about report reliability in unimpaired systems. Under reading A, Anton is a local instance of what the epiphenomenalist says is always possible: a report about experience that the experience did not cause. The clause escapes circularity only so far as impairment is identified by markers independent of the error, and the clinical literature mostly supplies them.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism).** Anton is compatible with Tenet 1 and does not support it. Every reading (confabulation, misclassified imagery, a shared anosognosia network, higher-order misrepresentation) can be stated in production terms. What stays unexplained is the general gap between any such account and what the patient experiences, which is the [hard problem](/topics/hard-problem-of-consciousness/) and nothing special to this syndrome. Nor is the case a threat: the first-person claim Dualism draws on is the global existence of experience, which Anton leaves standing.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction).** Compatible, with the correlation reply already conceded: the tenets grant that a tight correlation between phenomenal and neural states could underwrite accurate reports without phenomenal causation, so Anton removes nothing the Map still claimed. On reading A it shows that under pathology reports of experience can be produced without the experience they report; on neither reading does it show that reports in unimpaired systems are produced that way. The [agency-void](/voids/agency-void/) gives the structural reason the introspective route cannot settle the question.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits).** For each patient, confabulation is the economical reading, and the "multifactorial" verdict warns against any tidy single-channel story. The tenet cuts both ways. It gives no licence to discount reading B, where imagery or hallucination is present, because A is simpler, and none to prefer B because it suits a richer picture of experience.

[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) and [No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds) are not invoked: nothing in the clinical record bears on the site or physics of any interaction, or on the interpretation of quantum mechanics.
