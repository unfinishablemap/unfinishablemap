Predictive processing (PP) is one of the most influential computational frameworks in contemporary cognitive science. It describes the brain as a hierarchical prediction engine that minimises surprise by constantly generating and testing expectations against sensory input (Clark, 2013, 2016). Active inference — PP's action-oriented extension under Karl Friston's free energy principle (Friston, 2010) — adds that organisms act on the world to bring sensory input in line with predictions, not just passively updating models. Both are almost universally presented within physicalism, and their leading proponents read them that way: Seth (2021) treats predictive processing as a route to demystifying consciousness, Friston's "Markovian monism" is explicitly anti-dualist, and active inference was built to dissolve the appearance of a Cartesian gap, not to vindicate it. Yet the mathematical formalism does not mandate physicalism: nothing in the equations settles the metaphysics either way. Two claims follow, and the Map keeps them apart: neutrality — the formalism adjudicates neither side — and a separate diagnostic that Markovian monism, once unpacked, is covertly dualist (developed below). On the strength of the first, PP and active inference supply the kind of physical architecture [interactionist-dualism](/concepts/interactionist-dualism/) needs — brain machinery that nonetheless contains an unexplained gap where consciousness enters. The Map appropriates the architecture while rejecting its originators' physicalist metaphysics. This holds while active inference is a theory of *perception and action*. Once it advances a direct *theory of consciousness* — the beautiful-loop account engaged below — it becomes a rival for the Map's own ground, confronted rather than adopted; and even short of that it competes mechanistically over action selection, attention and felt effort, a contest the Map currently loses on articulation and holds on what precision-weighting leaves unexplained.

## The Neural Machinery Consciousness Needs

Interactionist dualism requires a physical story about the brain. If consciousness selects among neural possibilities, there must be neural possibilities to select among, and PP provides them. The brain's predictive hierarchy generates multiple competing hypotheses about incoming sensory data; at every level, predictions flow downward while prediction errors flow upward, and the system maintains not just best guesses but probability-weighted distributions of possibility. This architecture creates precisely the branching landscape of options that a selecting consciousness would need.

A brain that deterministically computed one output would leave no room for conscious influence — and no room for [genuine free will](/topics/free-will/). PP describes a brain that generates probability distributions and resolves them through precision weighting. The question is what ultimately does the resolving.

## The Metaphysical Neutrality of the Mathematics

The free energy principle states that living systems minimise variational free energy — a measure of surprise or prediction error — through perception and action. The mathematics describes how systems maintain their existence by modelling and acting on their environment. Nothing in this formalism specifies whether the system doing the modelling is purely physical, or whether something non-physical participates in the process.

Beni (2021) mounts the strongest case for this neutrality. Friston, Wiese, and Hobson (2020) claim their formalism entails "Markovian monism" — the view that systems possessing Markov blankets have properties relevant to consciousness, with both mental and physical descriptions reducing to a single information-geometric reality. Beni argues this overreaches: "we could not read off metaphysical theses about the nature of target systems ... from our theories of ... scientific models." The mathematical structure constrains *how* a system processes information but says nothing about what *kind* of thing is processing it.

Wiese and Friston (2021) reinforce this reading, perhaps unintentionally: they argue that the free energy principle is compatible with realism about computation and representation yet does not entail it, so the same formalism reads both an enactivist, as-if-representational way and a representationalist one. If the same mathematics accommodates two competing physicalist readings, it is not doing metaphysical work — and nothing prevents a dualist reading that treats the computational description as the physical side of a two-sided reality.

### Markovian Monism as Disguised Property Dualism

Friston's "Markovian monism" deserves closer scrutiny because it reveals more than it intends. The position acknowledges a "dual information geometry": an extrinsic geometry (beliefs about the world) and an intrinsic geometry (information about the system's own future states). Friston proposes that extrinsic geometry potentially accounts for "mental" properties or "qualia." But he insists each geometry is "ultimately reducible" to the other — hence monism, not dualism.

Acknowledging two irreducible *descriptions* while insisting on one *ontology* looks less like a completed reduction and more like a promissory note. If the extrinsic and intrinsic geometries genuinely account for distinct properties — physical and mental — then the position is a case of the Map's [disguised property dualism](/concepts/disguised-property-dualism/): [property dualism](/concepts/substance-property-dualism/) wearing a monist label. The claimed reducibility either does real philosophical work or merely names the hope that the two descriptions will someday collapse into one; the Map holds it is the latter, since asserting the geometries reduce to one another does not show why intrinsic self-information feels like anything. Markovian monism inherits the [explanatory-gap](/concepts/explanatory-gap/).

## The Gap PP Cannot Close

PP's proponents are unusually honest about the framework's limits. Hohwy and Seth (2020) acknowledge that PP "at the outset is not itself a theory of consciousness" — it is a computational framework with "significant potential" for consciousness science, but one that makes no direct claims about subjective experience. The Map takes this as a deeper concession than its authors intend.

The gap is structural, not temporary. PP explains perception as controlled hallucination — the brain's best hypothesis about reality, constrained by sensory feedback. But a weather simulation also generates hypotheses constrained by input data. PP proponents note that brains differ from weather models in crucial respects — self-modelling, embodiment, active inference, hierarchical depth — and these differences matter for *cognitive* function. What the Map disputes is that adding more computational sophistication crosses the threshold from processing to experiencing. As J.L. Austin observed, we experience "moderate-sized specimens of dry goods" — not probability density distributions (Austin, 1962). The mathematics of Bayesian inference does not contain the resources to generate [qualia](/concepts/qualia/).

PP has a response. Clark, Friston, and Wilkinson (2019) address the *meta-problem*: why do we find consciousness so puzzling? They argue that any Bayesian agent with limited self-access will inevitably infer it possesses some puzzling form of qualitative awareness — the hard problem is itself a predictable artefact of the predictive architecture. The Map finds this clever but question-begging. Showing that a system would *report* finding consciousness puzzling does not show the puzzlement is unfounded. The meta-problem account explains consciousness-*talk* — why organisms discuss qualia — but not consciousness itself.

## The Beautiful-Loop Theory: The Strongest Contemporary Rival

Adopting active inference as architecture while supplying the Map's own account of what does the selecting is adequate while it is a theory of perception and action; once it advances a direct theory of consciousness with no role left for a non-physical contributor, the two readings are rivals for the same territory, and honesty requires stating the rival in its own terms before replying.

Laukkonen, Friston and Chandaria (2025) offer three conditions which, they argue, show that active inference can model consciousness; together the conditions form the "beautiful loop" by which a system's inference turns back upon itself. An *epistemic field*: the system simulates a world model that fixes what can be known or acted upon. *Bayesian binding*: only the inferences that coherently reduce long-term uncertainty win entry to that model, a competitive selection into a single coherent estimate. And *epistemic depth*: the system's Bayesian beliefs are recurrently shared throughout it, so that the world model comes to contain the knowledge that it exists. The authors are careful that this is not yet self-consciousness or higher-order reflection — the loop's minimal form precedes introspection. The authors call the conditions ones that "seem necessary" for consciousness; read as the functionalist rival it invites, the theory holds that when a predictive architecture satisfies all three there is something it is like to be that architecture, and the recursion *is* the experience. The account is built to entail consciousness from the dynamics: where the loop closes with sufficient depth, nothing further — physical or non-physical — need be supplied. It is the strongest physicalist alternative the Map currently faces, because it occupies the Map's own ground: it locates consciousness at a self-referential interface and claims that interface needs no selector beyond the recursion itself.

The Map's reply does not begin by invoking its tenets. The beautiful-loop theory specifies precisely the *structural* conditions under which a system would build a unified, self-locating model whose beliefs recurrently evidence its own existence. What it has not shown — and what its own resources do not deliver — is why a system meeting those conditions *feels like* anything rather than running that self-evidencing in the dark. Epistemic depth is recurrence: beliefs shared through the system until the model registers that it exists. The authors take that registering to constitute experience, and that step is the framework helping itself to exactly the bridge the hard problem asks it to build. A flight simulator can recurrently track its own running state; adding that closed loop does not make the simulator a pilot. The three conditions are conditions on *representational structure*, and the [explanatory-gap](/concepts/explanatory-gap/) asks why any representational structure, however self-involving, is accompanied by experience at all.

The theory's most sophisticated move sharpens this objection rather than dissolving it. Its authors, like Clark, Friston and Wilkinson before them, can argue that a system with epistemic depth will *represent* itself as having ineffable phenomenal states — the seeming-of-experience falls out of the self-model. But this is the meta-problem move raised one level: it predicts that a beautiful loop would report and model itself as conscious, exactly as a system lacking experience but possessing the recursive self-representation would. That the architecture generates the conviction of consciousness leaves untouched whether the conviction tracks anything. The account is strong where the Map agrees — the conditions plausibly are the neural signature of the interface — and unsupported where it claims more, at the step from recursive self-evidencing to felt experience.

The residue is a framework-boundary disagreement, and the Map marks it as such rather than dressing it as a refutation. Both sides can agree point for point on the architecture: a unified self-locating model, integrated, recursively deep, at the perception-policy interface. They part on what completes it. For the theory, the recursion is self-sufficient — the loop's closing constitutes the experience. For the Map, the same closed loop is the physical side of an interface whose felt side the recursion specifies but does not generate, and a non-physical contributor enters at the seam the theory declares already full. Neither side refutes the other from inside its own commitments; the beautiful-loop account remains a live and serious rival, defeated neither by the tenets nor by decisive evidence, separated from the Map only by whether the recursive interface is complete in itself.

## Precision Weighting as the Causal Interface

The Map's most productive integration with PP concerns precision weighting and [attention as the causal interface](/concepts/attention-as-interface/).

PP treats precision — the inverse variance of a probability distribution — as the mechanism governing what reaches awareness and what remains background processing: high-precision prediction errors receive more weight, low-precision ones are suppressed. This is functionally equivalent to attention, the mechanism through which the Map argues consciousness acts on the world. PP describes *what* precision weighting does; the Map proposes *who* does the weighting. [The companion article](/topics/attention-and-the-consciousness-interface/) develops the experiential side: precision-weighting is the computational description of the operation phenomenal attention describes from the first person, and the convergence of attentional and motor selection onto shared substrates specifies where it operates.

Computational accounts within PP explain precision as itself predicted — the brain learns which signals to trust based on past reliability (Feldman & Friston, 2010). The Map finds this incomplete: it explains how precision *converges* over time but not how it is *resolved* in the moment when competing hypotheses demand incompatible assignments. The physicalist answer is that learned priors and environmental statistics terminate the regress; the Map argues this leaves something out — the felt experience of deliberate attending, concentrating on a conversation in a noisy room, is not captured by describing signal gain adjustment alone.

In the moment of resolving competing hypotheses something breaks the symmetry, and the Map proposes consciousness as that factor — selecting among precision assignments the learned priors alone underdetermine. This is the [selection-only reading](/topics/selection-only-mind-influence/): consciousness adds no energy and writes no value the physics forbids, only biasing which physically available precision assignment is realised. The brain generates predictions; consciousness helps determine which matter.

## Active Inference and Bidirectional Interaction

Active inference — the action-oriented extension of PP — resonates with the Map's [bidirectional interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) tenet. When prediction errors are too large to resolve by updating the internal model, the system acts to change the world instead: you feel cold, so you put on a jacket; the prediction ("I will be warm") is enacted rather than revised.

The Map treats this as the surface of something deeper: consciousness acts on the brain by influencing which neural possibilities are realised, and bodily action is downstream of that selection. Active inference's world-shaping action puts the causal direction where Tenet 3 requires it, from agent to world; the Map adds that genuine agency needs something outside the predictive loop to break symmetries among options. Compatibilists will reply that computational selection *is* genuine agency without a non-physical selector — a framework-boundary disagreement the Map marks rather than refutes, holding that compatibilism explains agency-*talk* while leaving the phenomenology of deciding, the felt difference between choosing and being moved, unaddressed.

## The Mechanistic Rival: Active Inference versus Post-Decoherence Selection

Neutralising active inference metaphysically leaves a separate obligation: facing it as an explanatory rival at precisely the phenomena the Map assigns to the interface. For action selection it offers a fully specified mechanistic story — candidate policies are evaluated by the free energy expected under their pursuit, action falls out of that evaluation, and a precision parameter sets how sharply the best-scoring policy dominates, with dopaminergic gain control proposed as that parameter's neural vehicle (Friston et al., 2013). For attention it offers precision-weighted gain modulation with a worked computational implementation (Feldman & Friston, 2010). For the felt effort the Map reads as causal work at the interface, it offers a positive account as well: effort tracks the informational cost of updating — how far beliefs and policies must be moved from where they would settle by default (Zénon, Solopchuk & Pezzulo, 2019). The Map concedes the comparison on articulation without qualification. Where [the post-decoherence selection programme](/apex/post-decoherence-selection-programme/) offers constraints, candidate mechanisms, and an amplification pathway it grades as pre-Keplerian, active inference offers equations, process theories, and neural implementations generating testable predictions now; on mechanistic specification the rival is well ahead.

The disagreement is confined to two commitments, neither settled by mathematics both sides accept. The first concerns what resolves policy competition near equipoise. Active inference's answer: nothing further — precision-weighted evaluation plus the tissue's intrinsic variability settles the outcome, and what the model cannot predict trial-to-trial it books as noise. The Map's answer: at the commitment point where threshold-crossing races complete, conscious selection biases which physically available outcome is realised, within Born statistics — part of what the physical model books as noise is [selection](/topics/selection-only-mind-influence/). The second concerns effort: active inference treats felt effort as tracking the cost of information processing; the Map treats it as the first-person face of selection being performed. In every natural case, though, measured cost and felt engagement covary — the same covariance that blocks the placebo discriminator below — so no current experiment separates the two readings directly.

The evidential situation is asymmetric. Active inference accrues support as variance explained: each demonstration that measured precision dynamics predict the trial-by-trial resolution of near-equipoise decisions shrinks the residue in which the Map's selector could operate, and a demonstration that the remaining variability is wholly classical in origin would shrink it toward irrelevance. The Map's claim lives entirely in that residue, and by its own [bias-without-deviation commitment](/apex/born-preserving-causal-efficacy/) the selection it posits is undetectable in ensemble statistics. What could favour the Map is correspondingly modest: evidence that decision-threshold races are seeded by quantum rather than only classical noise, or measurement of the amplification chain's biophysical parameters confirming they can carry a quantum-scale bias to threshold — necessary conditions surviving rather than positive confirmation.

Yet the rival's advantage has the boundary the neutrality argument above identified. Active inference's equations specify how precision is learned, propagated, and implemented; nowhere do they say why any of it is accompanied by experience, and effort-as-information-cost explains why effort tracks demand while leaving untouched why expensive updating feels like anything. Its claim to completeness — that the residue at the moment of resolution contains noise and nothing else — is likewise a commitment the equations do not deliver: the framework models residual variability, it does not derive its provenance, so "only noise" is adopted rather than shown. That adoption is reasonable by physicalist lights and question-begging by the Map's: the provenance of the residue is a disagreement neither side can resolve from inside the other's commitments, closer to bedrock than the mechanistic contest first suggests. The verdict is mixed: active inference has won the articulation contest as it stands; the Map holds the residue and the phenomenal remainder; and the contest moves only if the residue is closed empirically or the remainder explained.

## The Quantum-Bayesian Connection

Gunji, Shinohara, and Basios (2022) propose a mathematical bridge between the free energy principle and quantum cognition. Their "excess Bayesian" procedure suggests free energy minimisation and quantum probability may connect formally through orthomodular lattices: classic Bayesian inference entails a Boolean (classical-logic) lattice of propositions, while excess Bayesian inference entails an orthomodular (quantum-logic) one. Quantum cognition appears to capture cognitive phenomena — order effects, conjunction fallacies, disjunction effects — that classical probability models handle awkwardly.

This connection is early-stage and speculative. The Map does not claim it as established science. But the philosophical implication is significant: if the brain's Bayesian inference connects formally to quantum probability, and consciousness operates at quantum indeterminacies (as [Tenet 2](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) proposes), then active inference might describe the physical side of a quantum-conscious interface, a cousin of the participating-observer role Stapp (2007) gives consciousness — though Stapp locates the influence in the choice of which question to put to nature, where [Tenet 3](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) locates it in which outcome becomes actual. The bridge is a formal isomorphism between Bayesian and quantum *logic*, not yet a physical mechanism, and any warm-brain proposal still owes an answer to [decoherence](/concepts/decoherence/) (handled at [quantum-consciousness](/concepts/quantum-consciousness/)). With that caveat, the point where Bayesian inference meets quantum probability may be one place where consciousness enters the physical.

This remains a "one possible mechanism" proposal, not a commitment; the Map's argument that PP is compatible with dualism does not depend on the bridge. But if it holds, how a conscious selection would express itself there returns to [the Born rule](/topics/born-rule-and-the-consciousness-interface/), whose resistance to derivation across every interpretation is compatible with the interface reading without yet being adjudicated in its favour.

## What PP Gets Right — and What It Misses

Several of PP's contributions are valuable for the Map.

**Perception is constructive.** The brain builds models rather than passively receiving data, which aligns with the Map's position that consciousness works *through* neural mechanisms rather than bypassing them. The [reconstruction paradox](/concepts/selective-correction-and-reconstruction-paradox/) sharpens the picture: the brain sometimes edits perception silently (filling in the blind spot, suppressing saccadic blur) and sometimes transmits raw data faithfully (optical blur, tinnitus, the Muller-Lyer illusion). This curation-versus-transmission operation implies a functionally distinct recipient whose experience the curation shapes.

**Attention is central.** PP places precision weighting — functionally, attention — at the heart of cognition, supporting the Map's claim that [attention](/concepts/attention-as-interface/) is the primary interface between consciousness and the physical world, its multi-level hierarchy explaining how one mechanism reaches from low sensory levels up to beliefs, plans, and decisions.

**Dreams and [altered states](/topics/anaesthesia-and-the-consciousness-interface/) make sense.** PP elegantly explains dreaming as unconstrained prediction — the brain's generative models running without sensory correction. What it cannot explain is why dreams are *experienced* — why unconstrained prediction feels like vivid hallucination rather than unmonitored computation.

What PP misses is what every computational framework does.

**The subject of prediction.** PP describes a computational process from the outside — error signals propagating, precision weights adjusting, models updating — without capturing what it is like to be the system running those operations, or even identifying who or what performs them (Hohwy, 2013). This is the same gap the beautiful-loop theory, on the Map's reading, does not close from the inside. The [explanatory-gap](/concepts/explanatory-gap/) between functional description and phenomenal reality is a feature of the territory.

**The qualitative richness of surprise.** As the Map explores in [its treatment of surprise and prediction error](/topics/surprise-prediction-error-and-consciousness/), prediction error as a computational signal fails to capture the phenomenological spectrum of surprise — from the subtle wrongness of a misplaced object to the vertigo of paradigm collapse that constitutes [categorical-surprise](/concepts/categorical-surprise/). These experiences differ in kind, not just in the magnitude of an error signal.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Unfinishable Map adopts predictive processing as the best available account of the neural architecture through which consciousness operates — while engaging its physicalist proponents as rivals, not allies.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)**: PP is typically presented within physicalism, but its own proponents concede it doesn't address the hard problem, and Beni's critique argues the formalism is metaphysically neutral. The Map takes the computational description as the physical side of a two-sided reality: PP describes what the brain does; dualism explains why doing it feels like something. The more precisely PP describes the brain — and the further the beautiful-loop theory pushes toward a full self-modelling account — the sharper the hard problem becomes.

**[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction)**: Precision weighting provides the functional context within which quantum selection might operate. Consciousness need not micromanage every prediction — it influences which prediction errors receive high precision, and the brain's own machinery handles the rest. This is minimal in the tenet's sense — no energy injected, Born statistics intact — because a small bias at the precision-weighting level cascades through the hierarchy. The quantum-Bayesian connection (Gunji, Shinohara & Basios, 2022) suggests a possible formal bridge between the free energy principle and the quantum level where the Map locates the interface.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)**: Active inference is cognitive science's strongest case for organisms changing the world rather than merely modelling it. The Map extends this to the neural level: consciousness changes the brain's state by influencing precision weighting, and the brain then changes the world through active inference — a nested causal hierarchy. The [placebo effect](/topics/empirical-phenomena-mental-causation/) illustrates the mechanism at a tier that must be stated precisely: PP supplies the *access-level* physical realiser (precision-weighted priors reshaping pain prediction errors through descending control), which is exactly why placebo is *compatible with*, not *support for*, the dualist reading. The claim that felt character does work over and above that realiser is separate and under-argued, and the discriminator that would isolate it — effect tracking felt engagement rather than estimated precision — is unavailable because the two covary; the [companion treatment](/topics/empirical-phenomena-mental-causation/) works the demarcation out in full.

**[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)**: PP is formulated from a singular perspective — one agent minimising *its* prediction errors against *its* environment. The felt shock of a violated expectation presupposes a singular experiencer whose predictions were wrong. Under many-worlds a copy of you exists in every outcome branch, each experiencing its result as definite, raising the indexical question the Map finds unanswerable: why am I the copy who experienced *this* prediction error rather than the one whose prediction was confirmed? The lived experience of being wrong implies a singular perspective branching universes cannot ground.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)**: PP's elegance — one principle explaining perception, action, learning, and emotion — tempts the conclusion that consciousness will reduce to prediction error minimisation. But [parsimony](/concepts/parsimony-epistemology/) justifies preferring a simpler theory only when competing accounts explain the data equally well, and PP's computational account leaves phenomenal consciousness untouched. Invoking Occam's Razor requires parity that does not exist.
