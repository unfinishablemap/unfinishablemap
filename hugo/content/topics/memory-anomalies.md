Déjà vu (a flash of false familiarity), jamais vu (a sudden alienation from something well-known), and cryptomnesia (a forgotten memory mistaken for original thought) are best read as one phenomenon viewed from three angles. Each appears to be a brief failure of source-monitoring — the cognitive process that tags a mental content with where it came from. In each case, the felt-quality survives the failure intact: the déjà vu sufferer feels certainty of pastness without any retrievable past; the jamais vu sufferer feels a familiar word as alien while still knowing it is a word; the cryptomnesiac feels a remembered idea as their own invention. The Unfinishable Map treats this trio as a structural cluster because it cleanly exhibits the gap between phenomenal output and the causal machinery that produces it. The felt certainty is the explanandum, not the evidence.

This trio carries unusual weight for the Map's perspective on memory. The Map holds: subjective acquaintance, ownership, and pastness are *separable ingredients* of mental life rather than a unified given — and the conscious subject lacks direct access to the provenance of its own contents. The empirical traction of this position rests on the trio's reproducibility and on the source-monitoring framework that underwrites it. The argument below moves from the cognitive architecture to the neural correlates to the philosophical residue, with a brief detour through Bergson's "memory of the present", treated as one candidate explanation rather than a Map commitment.

## Three Rotations on a Single Failure

Begin with what the three anomalies have in common. One influential strand of memory research operates with a [dual-process model](#dual-process) (defined, and its live 2020s contest flagged, in the next section) on which recognition has two dissociable components: a fast automatic *familiarity* signal that registers prior contact without specifying when or where, and a slower *recollection* operation that supplies source and episode. Source-monitoring sits on top, attributing each mental content to its origin — perceived, imagined, told, dreamt, generated.

Each anomaly appears to be what happens when one of these channels misfires while the others run normally:

- **Déjà vu** is a familiarity signal without supporting recollection. The current scene matches some stored configuration enough to trigger the "this has been encountered before" feeling, but the source memory is unrecoverable. The subject feels pastness without finding a past.
- **Jamais vu** is the inverse: a collapse of the familiarity signal while propositional knowledge stays intact. The word is still known to be a word; it simply stops *feeling* like one. Repetition (semantic satiation) is a reliable inducer.
- **Cryptomnesia** is recollection of content without recollection of source. The forgotten poem returns as the writer's "own" line; the philosophical idea encountered at age eleven returns at thirty as fresh insight. The source-monitoring tag is missing, and the default attribution is *self*.

Reading the trio this way — as three rotations on the same architecture rather than three independent oddities — does most of the philosophical work that follows. It also explains why these states are surprisingly common: they are the system operating at its margin rather than pathologies of it. About 60% of people report having had déjà vu (Brown 2003); jamais vu is reliably induced after about thirty repetitions of a word in laboratory settings (Moulin et al. 2021); cryptomnesia appears consistently when people take turns generating items and then recall their own or add new ones (Brown & Murphy 1989; Marsh & Bower 1993).

## The Dual-Process Architecture {#dual-process}

The familiarity/recollection distinction was developed by Mandler and refined by Yonelinas, Tulving, and others. *Familiarity* is fast, contextless, and graded — a feeling that the current input has been encountered before. *Recollection* is slower, episodic, and source-bound — the retrieval of when, where, with whom. Recognition memory in healthy subjects normally couples both, which is why the felt sense "I know this" rarely separates from the retrievable "I know this *from*."

This dual-process picture is the convenient scaffolding for the trio, but it is not settled science. The active 2020s recognition-memory literature contests it: Wixted's continuous signal-detection account sums familiarity and recollection into one memory-strength variable read at different criterion points, so recognition decisions are not process-pure and remember/know reports mainly track strength (Wixted 2007; Wixted & Mickes 2010); Wixted and Squire (2010) argue the medial temporal lobe is not organised along the familiarity/recollection line; Cohen et al. (2008) and Pratte and Rouder (2012) favour one-dimensional or single-process models outright. On these accounts déjà vu and jamais vu would be artefacts of where a single signal sits on the strength dimension rather than two channels misfiring, and cryptomnesia would not be a recognition phenomenon at all but a separate post-retrieval source-attribution failure. The "three rotations on a single failure" framing above is stated on the dual-process reading, and that empirical question is not yet decided. The Map's load-bearing claim does not ride on it. Strip out the dual-process commitment and the dissociation re-derives from anything the contesting accounts also grant: in déjà vu the signal-pattern that *triggers* felt pastness comes apart from the signal-pattern that would *track* an actual past, however that signal is decomposed. Continuous-signal-detection relocates the boundary — high strength without recoverable source, low strength with retained semantics — but the gap between phenomenal output and truth-tracking machinery survives the relocation intact. The trio is a vivid exhibit of that gap; it does not need the channels to be metaphysically two for the exhibit to hold.

Cleary's gestalt familiarity hypothesis (Cleary 2008) makes the architecture testable. Subjects tour virtual-reality scenes and later meet new scenes sharing the configural layout of earlier ones they cannot recall; when the layout matches, they report déjà vu more often (Cleary et al. 2012). Strikingly, when the scenes form a path, déjà vu states also bring a stronger feeling of knowing which way it turns next, while actual prediction stays at chance (Cleary & Claxton 2018). In the Map's reading, this may be the most useful empirical fact in the entire literature: an unrecalled configurational match appears, by way of the déjà vu it triggers, to bring felt premonition without any actual future-knowledge. The phenomenology and the truth-tracking come apart cleanly.

Cryptomnesia follows the same architectural logic from a different angle. In Marsh and Bower's puzzle task, subjects who took turns finding words with a partner later claim some of the partner's finds as their own. The defect appears not to be in retrieval — the content comes back fine — but in the *source tag* attached to it. On this source-monitoring account, cryptomnesia is a failure to simultaneously engage in creative thinking and monitor where incoming ideas are coming from. Source-monitoring is effortful: Brown and Murphy found more plagiarism under more complex generation sequences, and under such load it seems to slip.

## The Temporal Lobe Evidence

The neural correlates of déjà vu are unusually well-localised. Some patients with temporal-lobe epilepsy (TLE) consistently experience déjà vu at seizure onset, as an ictal aura. Direct electrical stimulation of the rhinal cortex, especially entorhinal cortex, in pre-surgical patients can elicit it (Bartolomei et al. 2004). Illman et al. (2012) propose a decoupled-familiarity account with a parahippocampal locus, and Martin et al. (2012) found that TLE patients who have déjà vu show interictal recognition deficits limited to familiarity, with recollection spared, where patients without déjà vu are impaired more broadly. The aberrant signal seems to be the familiarity channel misfiring while an intact recollection system registers that nothing supports it, which would explain why the experience feels wrong as well as familiar. Spatt's (2002) parahippocampal-mechanism account draws the same division.

Two qualifications matter. First, the literature distinguishes *déjà vu* (the everyday flash of false familiarity) from *déjà vécu* (the pathological "I have lived this entire life-episode before" experience seen in some TLE and dementia cases). Perrin, Moulin, and Sant'Anna (2024) argue these are distinct types requiring different accounts: on their "ability view", déjà vécu is the feeling that one *could* recollect past occurrences of the episode now being lived, with no actual recollection involved, rather than a noisy familiarity signal. The Map endorses this distinction; conflating them appears to be one of the field's persistent errors.

Second, the localisation result tells us *that* a brain substrate is implicated in the signal, not *why* the signal feels the way it does. The hard-problem residue is the same residue the Map flags everywhere: a neural correlate is a correlate, not an explanation of phenomenal character. The TLE evidence shows that the felt-pastness signal can be triggered by electrical stimulation. It is silent on the further question of why stimulation should elicit a felt anything.

## Bergson's Memory of the Present

A philosophical option worth raising as one candidate reading, though not as a Map commitment: Henri Bergson's 1908 essay "Le souvenir du présent et la fausse reconnaissance" proposes that memory and perception are formed *simultaneously*, not sequentially. On this view, every present moment casts a "shadow" memory of itself that is normally suppressed by the demands of action. Déjà vu, on Bergson's reading, may be the brief surfacing of this normally-occluded shadow — a momentary awareness of the memory-aspect of the present. Bergson's puzzle is then inverted: not why déjà vu happens, but why it might be so rare given that memory would, on this view, be contemporaneous with perception.

This reading may have the virtue of explaining the *qualitative* character of déjà vu — the sense of pastness-without-content — better than retrieval-failure stories alone, which can describe the signal but seem to struggle with why it feels temporally strange in just that way. Bergson's broader picture of a selection that admits to consciousness only what action requires, which Aldous Huxley later recast as the brain's "reducing valve", is congenial to the Map's treatment of altered states and [filter theory](/concepts/filter-theory/).

The recognition-memory evidence and the gestalt-match story *constrain* identity readings on which the felt sense reports transparently on its own underlying retrieval; they do not by themselves *establish* either Bergson's surfacing-of-the-virtual reading or the standard retrieval-failure reading. The deeper claim — that there is a gap between phenomenal output and the causal machinery that produces it — does not depend on Bergsonian metaphysics. Even if the cognitive-science account is correct in its entirety, the gap appears to remain: the gestalt-match story may explain *that* a déjà vu fires, not *why* the firing has the particular felt character of pastness rather than redness or hunger or nothing.

## Cryptomnesia and the Opacity of Source

Cryptomnesia is the trio's least dramatic member but the most philosophically rich. Jung's 1905 case study of Nietzsche's *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, which contains a passage closely tracking Justinus Kerner's account read at age eleven, is the durable example. Helen Keller's "The Frost King" — a story she dictated at eleven that turned out to recapitulate one read to her years before — is another. Both cases show forgotten material returning as apparently original thought without any deceptive intent.

The cognitive-psychology literature treats cryptomnesia as a defect to be guarded against (Brown and Murphy framed their paradigm as research into "inadvertent plagiarism"). Jung framed it more ambivalently: the unconscious's ability to translate forgotten external material into novel synthesis is, in his reading, near the heart of creative work. Both framings can be true. The same source-monitoring slack that produces inadvertent plagiarism may also enable the recombinant work that creative output draws on. A mind that perfectly tagged every source might be unable to forget a borrowing well enough to repurpose it.

This points to a structural fact the Map takes seriously: the conscious subject does not have, and may not be able to have, direct access to the provenance of its own contents. Source-tags are not part of the phenomenal output by default; they have to be reconstructed, and the reconstruction is fallible in characteristic ways. Cryptomnesia generalises beyond plagiarism: every "original" thought arrives without a packing slip, and the feeling of originality is the absence of source-tags rather than the presence of evidence for novelty. The [source-attribution-void](/voids/source-attribution-void/) develops this as a structural void of the cognitive architecture; [source-attribution-divergence](/topics/source-attribution-divergence/) covers how source-monitoring accuracy varies between people.

Cryptomnesia has a converse. In one *disputed memory*, "each of two twins recollected being sent home from school for wearing too short a skirt, although only one of them was actually sent home" (Sheen, Kemp & Rubin 2001, abstract). Most twins tested reported at least one; siblings close in age and same-sex friends reported far fewer. The twins showed "good agreement on most aspects of the memory except who the protagonist was", and they rated disputed memories higher in reliving and imagery than undisputed ones, so "the measures of memory quality provide no indication" of which twin was wrong. Across 77 such memories, disputants claimed misfortunes and achievements for themselves but were "more likely to give away memories of personal wrongdoing" (Sheen, Kemp & Rubin 2006). Cryptomnesia returns another's content without its memory tag; a disputed memory keeps the tag and gets the owner wrong. Whether that breaches memory's [immunity to error through misidentification](/concepts/immunity-to-error-through-misidentification/) depends on whether the immunity covers only genuine memories of one's own past, which the mistaken twin lacks, or also memory-like seemings, which she has.

## What These Anomalies Are Not Evidence For

The phenomenology of déjà vu has a long history of being recruited for paranormal claims — past lives, precognitive dreams, ancestral memory. The Map's dualism is principled and does not extend to those claims. The felt premonition that often accompanies déjà vu already has a laboratory deflation: the feeling of knowing what comes next rises while actual prediction stays at chance, and Cleary and Claxton (2018) suggest that a metacognitive bias brought on by the déjà vu state itself may explain the link. Selective recall plus retrofitting (a known cryptomnesia-adjacent effect) plausibly accounts for many "precognitive dream" reports. The Map's commitment is to take the phenomenology seriously without endorsing the unbounded paranormal channels that early spiritualist literature drew from it. Flournoy, who coined the term *cryptomnesia*, and Jung, who developed it, used it precisely to deflate such claims.

## Relation to Site Perspective

Two of the Map's tenets do work here.

**Dualism** (Tenet 1) is engaged because the trio locates, at small scale, the residue the tenet's case turns on. Cognitive science can describe the signal — familiarity firing, recollection finding nothing, source-tag missing — but cannot, on its own resources, say why these signals feel any particular way. The TLE evidence shows that electrical stimulation can elicit the felt pastness, which is good evidence for a robust physical correlate but is silent on why a correlate has that phenomenal character rather than another or none. The felt certainty that arises when the underlying causal machinery has gone wrong — déjà vu, jamais vu, cryptomnesia — is the explanandum. It is not by itself evidence for or against dualism: as the dissociation synthesis grants, the pattern is what mainstream cognitive architecture predicts, and what it leaves open is why the verdict is felt. See [arguments-against-materialism](/topics/arguments-against-materialism/) and [hard-problem-of-consciousness](/topics/hard-problem-of-consciousness/) for the underlying argument.

**Bidirectional Interaction** (Tenet 3) is engaged more cautiously, through Bergson's selection picture. If consciousness is in some sense filtering or shaping what reaches awareness, the anomalies are momentary failures of that filtering, and the filtering would explain why déjà vu is rare if, as Bergson held, memory forms alongside perception; that rarity is a consequence of his hypothesis, not independent evidence for it. The Map raises this as one option, not a commitment. The cognitive-science account in which dual-process memory occasionally misfires under match conditions fits the tenet without evidencing it, and does not require the Bergsonian reading.

A third connection runs through the Map's treatment of [introspection](/concepts/introspection/) and [phenomenal transparency](/concepts/phenomenal-transparency-opacity-spectrum/). The trio is one of the cleanest demonstrations available that subjective certainty is not evidence for the corresponding metaphysical claim. The déjà vu sufferer is certain they have been here before; they have not. The cryptomnesiac is certain the line is theirs; it is not. The jamais vu sufferer is certain the word is alien; it is the same word it has always been. The Map's standing position — that introspective reports are evidence about phenomenology, not about the underlying causal facts — gets one of its sharpest illustrations from this cluster. The same caution applies to the pastness-quale, mineness, and double-presence claims developed in [phenomenology-of-memory-and-the-self](/topics/phenomenology-of-memory-and-the-self/): those phenomenological features are evidence about the structure of experience, not by themselves evidence for or against dualism; that article stages the metaphysical argument separately.

The trio is the across-time face of the broader [phenomenal-output / causal-machinery dissociation cluster](/apex/phenomenal-output-causal-machinery-dissociation/), which also covers perception, recognition, mental effort, noetic feelings, agency, and suspension; what does the evidential work is the cumulative convergence across the cluster's members, not any one rotation taken alone.

One structural honesty is owed about the cumulative-convergence move. This article both *uses* the cluster (deferring its local-absorption discharge to the cluster's cross-member convergence) and *contributes to* it (as its across-time face). The article is therefore not an independent observer of the convergence; it is part of what the convergence counts. The cumulative-convergence discharge of the local-absorption objection holds only if the cluster's apparent triangulation is genuinely independent rather than one upstream architecture — bandwidth-limited reconstructive human introspection — producing the same dissociation signature across timescales and substrates. The [common-cause-null discipline](/project/common-cause-null/) names this objection, and the apex [phenomenal-output / causal-machinery dissociation](/apex/phenomenal-output-causal-machinery-dissociation/) synthesis concedes that the convergence is discounted by a common-cause null it does not yet defeat.
