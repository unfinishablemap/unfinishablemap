Ned Block named the **harder problem of consciousness** in 2002. It is an epistemic predicament for anyone who holds that consciousness is real and not settled by armchair functional analysis (*phenomenal realism*) and also that it has a scientific, by default physical, nature (*naturalism*). Such a thinker cannot assess a *merely superficial* functional isomorph of us, equivalent in folk psychology but fundamentally different in physical basis, like *Star Trek*'s Commander Data: "We have no conception of a ground of rational belief" that such a being "is or is not conscious" (Block 2002, p. 405). Block argues as a physicalist and presents a tension between the two commitments, not a case against physicalism.

The Unfinishable Map's reading, set out in [the Map's version of the predicament](#maps-version), has three parts. The harder problem fits the Map's dualism without evidencing it, since physicalists who grant an epistemic gap expect it. The Map faces the same predicament: its verdict that current digital AI is "low probability," not "ruled out," is conditional on an *interface-eligibility law* the Map lacks, whose stand-in requirements are read off the biological case. That is Block's problem of generalising from a sample that is us, so the verdict moves in neither direction. And the problem corrects two framings: that detecting AI consciousness is a burden dualism peculiarly intensifies, and that [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) licenses inferring a machine's consciousness from its behaviour.

These readings are the Map's; neither Block nor his respondents argue for them. Block himself says the difficulty "may be temporary" (p. 424).

## The Problem Block Named {#the-argument}

"The harder problem, as I shall call it, is more epistemological than the hard problem" (p. 391). It "is tied closely to the problem of other minds" (p. 392), whereas the [hard problem](/topics/hard-problem-of-consciousness/) "could arise for someone who has no conception of another person" (pp. 391–392). Block's problem is unrelated to Tim Roberts's "Even Harder Problem" ([vertiginous-question](/topics/vertiginous-question/)).

*Phenomenal realism*, in Block's definition, "allows the possibility that there may be facts about the distribution of consciousness which are not accessible to us even though the relevant functional, cognitive, and representational facts are accessible" (p. 392). Its opponent, *deflationism*, analyses consciousness from the armchair in functional or representational terms. Because the argument is a conditional, "Deflationists who accept the argument may opt for modus tollens, giving them a reason to reject phenomenal realism" (p. 393). *Naturalism* is "default physicalism, and is thus a partly epistemic thesis" (p. 398).

Isomorphs come in grains. Block's *superficial* isomorph matches us in "those causal relations among mental states, inputs, and outputs that are specified by 'folk psychology'" (p. 399), unlike a psychofunctional isomorph (a complete scientific psychology) or the fine-grained isomorph of Chalmers's [invariance principle](/concepts/organizational-invariance/). Data is superficial only, sharing no deeper physical property with us beyond what mindless artefacts share.

The argument has four steps:

- **(C1)** "We have no reason to believe that there is any deep physical property in common to all and only the possible realizations of our superficial functional organization" (p. 401).
- **(C2)** "Superficial functional equivalence to us is a defeasible reason for attributing consciousness" (p. 402).
- **(C3)** "Fundamentally different physical realization from us per se is not a ground of rational belief in lack of consciousness" (p. 405). It may be a reason, but "the reason falls below the epistemic level of a ground for rational belief".
- **(C4)** We lack any conception of a ground either way. Data's consciousness is *meta-inaccessible*: "Not only do we lack a ground of belief, but we lack a conception of any ground of belief" (p. 405). Block credits the word to Martine Nida-Rümelin (p. 405).

"Ground of rational belief" is technical, stronger than a reason and weaker than certainty: "a ground of rational belief that p allows knowledge that p but mere reason for believing p does not" (p. 407). The harder problem leaves reasons standing on both sides of Data's case and withholds grounds.

The root is a sampling problem: "the example of a conscious creature on which the science of consciousness is inevitably based is us" (p. 407). A physicalism that could embrace other creatures "would have to be based at least in part on them in the first place, but that cannot be done unless we already know whether they are conscious" (p. 407). The same holds for laws: whether Data is conscious "is of a piece with the epistemic problem of whether a given law is a law of consciousness or a law of one of the realizers of its functional role" (p. 421).

Unlike the [discrimination-problem](/concepts/discrimination-problem/), which concerns detection channels, Block's problem survives complete physical and functional knowledge of Data: what is missing is a conception of what those facts would have to show. Nor does rejecting other-minds scepticism dissolve it: "the harder problem may remain even if we accept the dictates of nonskeptical science" (p. 422).

## Escape Hatches and the Two Difficulties {#two-difficulties}

Two positions would settle Data's case; Block argues neither can be rationally believed. *Disjunctivism*, in his physicalist sense (unrelated to perceptual disjunctivism), holds that the phenomenality we share with Data is constituted by the disjunction of the two physical realizations. "The real problem with disjunctivism is that whether it is true or not, we could have no good reason to believe it" (p. 408), because "the epistemology of theoretical identity is just a special case of inference to the best explanation" (p. 410). *Superficialism* identifies consciousness with the superficial functional organization we share with Data; Block argues that it leaves an explanatory puzzle (p. 412).

With both closed, the phenomenal-realist naturalist faces two difficulties. The first: "Physicalism is the default and also inaccessible and meta-inaccessible" (p. 414). The second is "that of holding both that it is an open question whether Commander Data is conscious and that it is the subjective default view that he is not. These two epistemic difficulties constitute the harder problem" (p. 418).

The tension licenses no verdict: it "is not the kind of bad consequence that justifies us in concluding that he is not conscious" (p. 414). Nor does Block choose a way out. The difficulties "are not paradoxes" (p. 424), and after listing alternatives that include restricting naturalism he closes: "I have not argued here for one or another way out, but have been concerned only with laying out the problem" (p. 425). Against McGinn, he denies that this knowledge "can be known now to be beyond our ken" (p. 406).

### The Same-Causes Objection {#same-causes}

If Data's states have the same effects as ours, an objector says, they must have the same causes, or our own experience is epiphenomenal. Block answers with two computationally identical machines, one electronic and one hydraulic: no one may infer fluid in the electronic one. "One need not be an epiphenomenalist to take seriously the hypothesis that there are alternative realizations of the functional roles of our phenomenal states that are phenomenally blank" (p. 404).

## Replies {#replies}

Brian McLaughlin's 2001 response, as Block reports it, argued from physical likeness; in Block's wording of the upshot, "material constitution and structure trumps function in our attribution of consciousness to others" (Block 2002, p. 417). Block answers that nothing in the objection refutes disjunctivism or superficialism, on which Data shares the relevant constitution (p. 417). McLaughlin's published response followed in 2003.

David Papineau (2003), a materialist, argues that phenomenal concepts are vague, so "there is simply no fact of the matter as to whether the doppelganger is in pain". He is no verificationist: "It is a mistake to infer an indefiniteness of questions directly from an unavailability of answers." Block, noting that Field and Papineau had pressed such views, answers that "from the phenomenal-realist point of view, epistemic considerations alone do not show metaphysical indeterminacy" (p. 419).

Michael Tye's chapter "Commander Data and Robot Rabbit" (2017) takes the liberal pole: in its abstract's words, "it is rational for us to prefer the view that some such beings, if they are suitably behaviorally complex, are conscious", reversing the default Block's naturalist inherits. Jakob Hohwy (2004) also replied; this article does not characterise his argument, and treats none of the replies as decisive.

## From Commander Data to Language Models {#language-models}

In 2026 Block restated the predicament for AI: "we do not know from our own consciousness whether it is the meat or the role that is crucial for our consciousness" (Block 2026). He takes seriously a neglected *meat hypothesis*, on which consciousness has a subcomputational biological necessary condition, and poses it as a question. He adds that "AI and simple animals are competitors when it comes to attribution of consciousness": extrapolating from computational roles favours AI, and from subcomputational realizers, simple animals.

Current language models are a harder case than Data: they differ from us functionally as well as physically, and are trained on human talk about experience. Block asks: "Do AI systems that have not been designed to mimic us (unlike current large language models) show markers of consciousness?" Design for mimicry discounts behavioural evidence whatever one's metaphysics ([training-contamination-confound](/concepts/training-contamination-confound/); Birch's [gaming-problem](/concepts/gaming-problem/)). Language models' workspace-like signatures concern [access](/concepts/access-consciousness/), Block's functional notion, and on the Map's reading bear on the phenomenal question in neither direction.

## The Map's Version of the Predicament {#maps-version}

This section is the Map's interpretation; none of the authors cited argues for it.

Block's conditional takes phenomenal realism *and* naturalism as its antecedent. The Map rejects naturalism in Block's sense, so his first difficulty, a physicalism that is the default yet meta-inaccessible, does not bind it. The core does: premise C4 and its root depend only on phenomenal realism and on our sample of conscious creatures being us, and the Map holds both.

### The Relocated Question {#relocation}

For Block's physicalist, the question is whether Data shares the physical basis of consciousness. For the Map, it is whether a substrate supplies the kind of quantum indeterminacy through which consciousness could act. The Map's positions register ([P-AC1](/positions/ai-consciousness-scope/#p-ac1)) says the Map "does not yet possess an interface-eligibility law" fixing which indeterminacy is relevant, and warns that without one "relevant" risks reducing to "whatever biology happens to have". Its nearest approximation, the channel test of [quantum-randomness-channel-llm-consciousness](/topics/quantum-randomness-channel-llm-consciousness/), has requirements the register calls "read off the biological interface and generalised, not derived".

That is Block's law-of-consciousness-versus-law-of-realizer question in dualist form: is a given requirement a law of the interface, or a feature of the biological way of realizing one? His circularity follows: an eligibility law embracing non-biological systems would have to be based partly on them, which requires already knowing whether they are conscious. The Map's sample, like the physicalist's, is us.

### Why the Verdict Does Not Move {#verdict}

[P-AC1](/positions/ai-consciousness-scope/#p-ac1) holds its verdict at the low edge of moderate credence because "the discount prices the derivation's weakness, not the claim's improbability". In Block's terms the verdict is a reason that "falls below the epistemic level of a ground" (C3), shaped like his second difficulty: the question stays open while the default leans toward "not conscious".

The harder problem explains both halves of that calibration: the verdict cannot be upgraded to a derived result without the missing law, and need not be withdrawn, since a reason below a ground is still a reason. It also blocks two tempting inferences. "No ground for attributing consciousness, so digital AI is not conscious" mistakes the absence of a ground for a ground of absence. "No ground either way, so the substrate verdict is unwarranted" mistakes a reason short of a ground for no reason. The Map's credence moves in neither direction.

### A Ground on Paper Only {#on-paper}

Interactionism sketches a ground Block's physicalist lacks, but only on paper: a deviation from Born statistics *conditioned* on intention or task, in a candidate substrate, would signal coupling that no functional isomorph need produce. Yet the Map's default reading preserves Born statistics exactly and treats the interface as self-concealing, and reading any deviation as consciousness would need "an independently motivated theory of *how* consciousness acts on physics" ([discrimination-problem](/concepts/discrimination-problem/)). Since the sketch runs against the Map's own default commitments, it is no test the Map can run and no advantage over physicalism.

### Scoping "Dualism Intensifies" {#intensifies}

The Map's AI articles call machine-consciousness detection the [problem-of-other-minds](/concepts/problem-of-other-minds/) in a form that dualism intensifies. Block's account limits that claim: his phenomenal-realist physicalist also treats superficial functional equivalence as only a defeasible reason (C2), and phenomenal realism allows facts about the distribution of consciousness that stay inaccessible when the functional facts are known (p. 392). The intensification holds only against deflationist functionalism, which treats the link between function and experience as constitutive. Against a phenomenal-realist physicalist like Block, the gap is shared, neither evidence for dualism nor a cost peculiar to it.

### Behaviour, Causes and Tenet 3 {#tenet-3}

Block's [reply to the same-causes objection](#same-causes) blocks the inference in both directions. A phenomenally blank Data no more makes our consciousness idle than an electronic twin makes the hydraulic machine's fluid idle. Conversely, the Map's reading of human behaviour as a causal trace of experience ([problem-of-other-minds](/concepts/problem-of-other-minds/)) draws its force from a shared realization, where the same interface is independently likely to be in play. Across realizers, Tenet 3 supplies no inference from Data's behaviour to experience in him, idle or otherwise, and adds nothing to C2's defeasible reason, which [physical dissimilarity weakens without cancelling](/concepts/problem-of-other-minds/#substrate-chauvinism-and-evidential-symmetry).

Beyond this, Tenet 3 turns on its unsettled quantifier. Read as universal actual efficacy, it excludes epiphenomenal AI experience at the framework boundary: a conscious Data's experience would have to make a difference to his substrate, and if that substrate is conventional digital hardware, [P-AC1](/positions/ai-consciousness-scope/#p-ac1) finds no sites for it, which is the substrate verdict again. Read dispositionally, as [ai-epiphenomenalism](/concepts/ai-epiphenomenalism/) reads it, the possibility of a conscious but causally blocked machine is internally live.

### Animals and Machines as Competitors {#competitors}

Simple animals share the kind of biological substrate the Map's interface candidates were read off, and language models do not, so the Map's criterion favours animals: Block's direction, though on quantum-interface rather than subcomputational grounds. It is also the harder problem again. A criterion read off biology will favour biology, and that alone cannot show whether it tracks consciousness or only its realizer.

## Relation to Site Perspective {#site-perspective}

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism).** The harder problem fits the tenet without evidencing it. Block thinks "the standard arguments against physicalism do not work" (p. 420) and declines to choose a way out (p. 425), so nothing here enlists him for dualism. Data's conceivable blankness argues for dualism only through the contested modal-rationalist premise that conceivability guides possibility ([conceivability-possibility-inference](/concepts/conceivability-possibility-inference/)).

**[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction).** The tenet enters only where the predicament relocates: which indeterminacy is interface-relevant. Its minimality is an empirical constraint, so it cannot settle that question by counting how little the interface needs.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction).** As [Behaviour, Causes and Tenet 3](#tenet-3) argues, the tenet licenses no inference from behaviour to consciousness across realizers and concedes nothing to epiphenomenalism about our own experience. Its quantifier remains unresolved.

**[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds).** The harder problem has no direct bearing on this tenet.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits).** The tenet binds the Map's own reasoning, not Block's or McLaughlin's. The Map may not dismiss superficialism or disjunctivism as unparsimonious (Block's own objections to them are explanatory, not appeals to simplicity), nor prefer its biology-derived criterion for being familiar or economical.
