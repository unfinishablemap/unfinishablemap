On the Map's interface picture, Capgras delusion is read as a selective disconnection of the **affective-familiarity channel**: the felt familiarity or warmth that normally accompanies the sight of a loved one is severed while perceptual identification of the face stays intact. The patient looks at a spouse, correctly registers every feature, and concludes the person is an impostor — because the face is seen but no longer *felt*. This reading treats felt familiarity as a distinct contribution to recognition that can fail on its own, which is consistent with the Map's claim that experience has separable channels. It is not a demonstration that the channel is non-physical. A fully physicalist account — recognition as a bundle of information-processing streams, one of which happens to be damageable — explains the same clinical data, and that rival is left standing throughout this article. What Capgras supplies is a clean instance of felt familiarity coming apart from perceptual identification; what it does not supply is a verdict on the metaphysics of that separation. Whether the loss of familiarity is experienced before the delusion forms is disputed, as explained [below](#is-factor-1-experienced).

This article is the deep dive on the Capgras half of a pattern the Map develops elsewhere. The broader Capgras–prosopagnosia **double dissociation** — the mirror-image pairing that establishes recognition as a compound of at least two components — is argued in [the recognition void](/voids/recognition-void/); that argument is not repeated here. This piece goes instead into the affective-route *mechanism*, the skin-conductance evidence that confirmed it, the two-factor model that explains why the anomaly hardens into a fixed belief, and the live challenge to whether the lesioned channel is really "familiarity-specific" at all.

## The Affective-Route Mechanism

The foundational account is Ellis and Young's (1990) affective-route hypothesis. They applied functional models of face recognition to the delusional misidentification syndromes and proposed that Capgras is the mirror image of prosopagnosia. Where the prosopagnosic loses overt identification but retains a covert autonomic response to familiar faces, the Capgras patient retains overt identification but loses the covert affective response. Ellis and Young framed the delusion's seed as an anomalous experience: familiar people no longer *feel* familiar, and the impostor idea is the mind's attempt to explain that felt wrongness.

The mechanism they proposed is a **disconnection**. Face perception (in the temporal and fusiform regions) stays intact and delivers an accurate percept. What is severed is the pathway carrying that percept onward to the structures that tag a recognised face with personal significance. Hirstein and Ramachandran (1997) made the anatomy explicit: a failure of communication between ventral-stream face-processing areas in the temporal lobe and "the limbic complex, especially the amygdala" — damage to the brain's ability to "attach emotional significance to a familiar face". The result is a percept stripped of its affective annotation. The patient does not experience "my familiarity module is offline" — that machinery is itself invisible to introspection, a point [inventory blindness](/concepts/inventory-blindness/) develops. On Ellis and Young's reading, they experience a person who looks exactly right and feels categorically wrong, and the felt wrongness demands an explanation the intact perceptual system cannot supply.

Coltheart and Davies (2022) later generalised the trigger beyond the classic close-relative case. In their review, replacement beliefs can also target an acquaintance, a familiar voice, a pet, or a personal possession — five scenarios in all, sharing the structure of something familiar becoming, in affective terms, unfamiliar. Their restatement of the mechanism is precise: personally familiar stimuli normally activate the sympathetic nervous system far more strongly than unfamiliar ones, and in Capgras "this difference is absent." That the trigger generalises across sensory modalities matters for the Map's reading. It suggests the damaged contribution is familiarity *itself* — a channel indexed to personal significance — rather than anything specific to the visual system. Whether that channel is best described as one conscious contribution or as one physical sub-system is the open question the closing sections hold open.

The same architecture appears one level down, in the body. [Anarchic hand](/topics/anarchic-hand-and-action-ownership/) is distinguished from alien hand by what the patient disowns: in anarchic hand the limb is unquestionably the patient's own and the disowned thing is the *act*, which arrives without the felt signature of having been willed; in alien hand the *limb* itself feels foreign while its movements are not what is in question. Capgras is the third term in that series, and what it disowns is the *person*. Act, limb, person: one pattern at three scales. If felt authorship cannot be read off the bare fact that one's own body moved — if it is a signal the system supplies and can withhold — then the same holds for felt familiarity, which is not read off the face but delivered alongside it, and in Capgras simply not delivered. The parallel is architectural rather than anatomical: nothing connects the frontal and callosal lesions behind anarchic hand to the temporal-limbic disconnection behind Capgras, and no shared mechanism is claimed.

## The Skin-Conductance Evidence

Ellis and Young's 1990 hypothesis made a testable prediction *before* it was tested: Capgras patients should be hyporesponsive to familiar faces on autonomic measures. Two 1997 studies confirmed it.

Hirstein and Ramachandran (1997) reported a single patient, DS, whose skin-conductance responses to photographs of familiar people — including his own parents — "were not larger in magnitude than his responses to photographs of unfamiliar people." The normal autonomic boost that a familiar face produces was simply gone. The case carried a second, sharper finding: the delusion was **modality-specific**. DS called his parents impostors when looking at them but not when speaking to them on the telephone. A global belief disorder about who his parents *are* would not switch off over the phone; a disconnection specific to the visual-to-limbic route would. The modality-specificity is what makes the disconnection reading a finding rather than a story fitted after the fact.

The single case was corroborated by a controlled group study. Ellis, Young, Quayle and De Pauw (1997) tested five Capgras patients against two control groups: five middle-aged members of the general public and — critically — five psychiatric patients on matched antipsychotic medication. The Capgras patients were hyporesponsive to familiar faces, showing the predicted reduction in autonomic orienting response. The medication-matched control is what gives the study its force: it rules out the objection that the absent response is merely a side-effect of antipsychotic drugs rather than a feature of the delusion. Two independent methods — a single-case probe and a controlled group comparison — converged on the same missing signal.

This is the empirical core of the affective-channel reading. The affective-familiarity signal is not merely inferred from patients' reports; it leaves a measurable autonomic trace. The trace is absent in the Capgras patients, whose reports describe a felt absence, and, with no report on record, in four non-delusional ventromedial patients. Whether an absent trace is an absent feeling, and whether the reported strangeness preceded the belief, are taken up [below](#is-factor-1-experienced).

## Why the Anomaly Becomes a Delusion

An absent autonomic signal, even if felt as strangeness, is not yet a fixed false belief. Most people who feel that something is subtly wrong do not conclude their spouse has been replaced by a duplicate. This gap is what the **two-factor model** (Coltheart, Langdon, Davies, McKay; reviewed in Coltheart & Davies 2022) exists to close, and it marks the boundary of what the Map claims.

The model separates two deficits. **Factor 1** is the anomalous datum — here, the absent affective-familiarity signal. **Factor 2** is a separate impairment of belief evaluation, associated with right frontal (right dorsolateral prefrontal) damage, that lets the impostor hypothesis be adopted and then held against overwhelming counter-evidence. Factor 1 supplies the anomaly; Factor 2 is why an otherwise-rational person fails to reject the extraordinary explanation of it. The dissociation between the two is itself evidenced: four patients with ventromedial damage lack the autonomic response to familiar faces yet form no delusion (Tranel, Damasio & Damasio 1995), which the model credits to intact belief evaluation. The record describes them by skin conductance alone and does not show whether familiar faces seemed anomalous to them.

**Only Factor 1, and only insofar as it is experienced, is the Map's territory.** The channel-decomposition claim concerns the existence and separability of the felt-familiarity contribution — the anomalous datum as most accounts, though not all, describe it — and the autonomic evidence for it. Factor 2, the belief-evaluation deficit that converts anomaly into fixed delusion, is [a reasoning pathology the Map takes no position on](/topics/clinical-insight-presented-and-judged-unreality/#scope-rule); it is named here as the completion of the clinical explanation, not as evidence for interface architecture. Keeping the claim this narrow matters, and two distinct insufficiencies enforce the narrowness. Clinically, the two-factor model is precisely why a single strange feeling is insufficient *to produce the delusion*: the fixed impostor belief needs the separate belief-evaluation deficit. Metaphysically, even the isolated Factor-1 anomaly does not show the affective channel is conscious or non-physical — the physicalist functional decomposition explains it equally well, which is the underdetermination the closing section develops. The Map's reading rests on Factor 1 alone, and it should not be read as reaching further in either direction.

Setting Factor 2 aside is not the same as denying that anything nearby has been mapped. The closest adjacent material is [the left-hemisphere interpreter](/concepts/interpreter-module-narrative-construction-unity/), where a fluent false explanation is generated with full subjective conviction by a system that has no access to the true cause of what it is explaining — the same shape of failure as an extraordinary hypothesis being adopted and then held against the evidence. That article is offered here as a pointer to a neighbouring pathology of self-explanation, not as an account of Capgras: whether the interpreter's confabulation and the belief-evaluation deficit are one failure or two that merely resemble each other is a question about reasoning architecture, and the Map takes no position on it.

### Is Factor 1 Experienced? {#is-factor-1-experienced}

Restricting the Map's claim to Factor 1 *as experienced* answers a live dispute. Most accounts, one-factor and two-factor alike, take the anomaly to be conscious (Noordhof & Sullivan-Bissett 2021). Bayne and Pacherie (2004) named two versions of this experiential reading, which differ over how rich the experience is. On the **endorsement** model the experience already carries the delusion's content, and the belief endorses what the patient sees. On the **explanationist** model the experience is poorer, something like "this person looks a bit strange", and the impostor belief is adopted to explain it. Bayne and Pacherie also draw the distinction the question turns on: "one should not confuse the lack of experience with the experience of a lack."

Coltheart, Menzies and Sutton (2010) deny, for Capgras though not for every delusion, that any experience of a lack comes first. Because "people are not conscious of the activities of their autonomic nervous systems", the Capgras abnormality "is not an abnormality of which the patient is aware". The first delusion-relevant conscious event is the impostor belief, reached by unconscious abductive inference, so they speak of "abnormal data" rather than abnormal experience and treat the strangeness patients report as a consequence of the delusion. Garry Young (2014) calls theirs the revisionist model and argues that an anomalous experience co-occurs with the impostor thought before the belief forms.

The ventromedial cases cannot settle it. Skin conductance and felt familiarity can come apart: prosopagnosic patients typically show the familiar-face response without a conscious feeling of familiarity (Pacherie 2009). A record of skin conductance alone therefore cannot say what familiar faces were like for the ventromedial patients. Coltheart, Menzies and Sutton conjecture that those patients at first held the impostor belief too and then abandoned it, and say they "know of no studies" bearing on the conjecture.

Whether Factor 1 is experienced before the belief forms is an open empirical question, and the Map's claim is conditional on the answer. If the revisionist model is right, Capgras shows an unconscious signal coming apart from identification, with consciousness entering at the belief the patient then asserts and defends, and the felt-familiarity reading above loses its datum. Neither outcome bears on Dualism.

## The Challenge to Factor-1 Specificity

The affective-channel story is contested at exactly the point the Map most relies on, and honesty about that challenge is required rather than optional. Corlett (2019) mounts the sharpest version.

Corlett re-examines the four ventromedial-prefrontal cases marshalled to support a discrete Factor 1 — patients who lack the autonomic response to familiar faces but form no delusion — and finds the anatomy less clean than the model needs. All four had damage to anterior cingulate cortex, and two of them also had right dorsolateral prefrontal damage — the region assigned to Factor 2 — so the "two independent factors" may not be cleanly dissociable in the brain that is supposed to demonstrate them. More directly threatening to the channel reading is his second point about those same cases: their absent skin-conductance responses were not specific to familiar faces at all. They lacked a response, in his words, to "any psychologically salient visual stimuli", while responses to merely physical stimuli — a deep inhalation, a loud hand-clap — survived. Corlett does not leave that restriction to vision standing: his own statement of the challenge is that these cases lack responses to "salient psychological stimuli more generally". If that pattern holds for Capgras patients too, the deficit is a broad drop in psychological-salience processing rather than the selective loss of a dedicated familiarity channel, and the clean dissociation is an artefact of how a general salience deficit happens to present.

Corlett's critique does not refute the affective-route account. The group study's medication-matched design and the modality-specificity of DS's delusion remain on the other side of the ledger, and Ellis, Young, Quayle and De Pauw ran a specificity control of their own: their Capgras patients' "orienting responses to auditory tones were normal in magnitude and rate of initial habituation, showing that the hyporesponsiveness is circumscribed." Whatever is missing in these patients is therefore not a global collapse of arousal. But that control leaves Corlett's charge untouched. His contrast runs between psychological and merely physical salience rather than between vision and hearing — one of the two spared stimuli he cites, the loud hand-clap, is itself auditory. A pure tone sits on the physical side of that line, exactly where the responses of his cases survive, so the tone control leaves intact the possibility he presses: that psychological salience is broadly impaired, whatever modality it is probed in. The channel-decomposition inference remains **underdetermined**. This is the same caution the recognition void already flags: a clean selective dissociation may be what any bundle of separately-damageable functional sub-systems looks like, whether or not those sub-systems mark a boundary of anything conscious. The Map engages Corlett as a genuine live challenge, not a footnote to be dismissed.

## Relation to Site Perspective

This case connects to two of the Map's [tenets](/tenets/).

**[Dualism (Tenet 1)](/tenets/#dualism)** — consistent with, not proof of. On the [experiential readings](#is-factor-1-experienced) of Factor 1, the affective-route evidence shows that "recognising a loved one" is a compound in which felt familiarity is separable from perceptual identification: the felt-familiarity contribution can be selectively removed while perceptual identification runs untouched, and its removal leaves a measurable autonomic trace. That is exactly the kind of channel structure the Map's [interface architecture](/topics/neurological-dissociations-as-interface-architecture/) predicts — a specific conscious contribution disconnectable on its own. But the evidence is metaphysically neutral on its own terms. A physicalist functional decomposition — the affective route as one more information-processing stream, damageable like any other — accounts for every datum here just as well. Whether the separability of felt familiarity carries phenomenal weight, or is simply what a damageable functional sub-system looks like, is the reading the data underdetermine. The Map takes Capgras as *consistent with* interface architecture and explicitly not as a demonstration that the channel is non-physical.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits (Tenet 5)](/tenets/#occams-limits)** — the Corlett honesty. Tenet 5 warns that the simplest available story can over-reach when knowledge is incomplete, and Capgras is a case in point. The tidy "affective-familiarity channel selectively lesioned" narrative is attractive precisely because it is simple, and Corlett's 2019 challenge is the reason simplicity is not enough: the specificity of Factor 1 is itself contested, the anatomy may be less dissociable than the model requires, and the absent autonomic response may reflect a broad salience deficit rather than a dedicated familiarity channel. The disciplined position is to hold the channel reading open as consistent-with rather than to close it by fiat. The familiarity signal is real and separable in the classic data; whether it is a metaphysically discrete channel or one physical sub-system among many is not settled, and the Map does not pretend it is. The same caution covers whether the signal is felt before the belief forms: the experiential reading is usual rather than settled, and the revisionist model's economy is no reason to prefer it.
