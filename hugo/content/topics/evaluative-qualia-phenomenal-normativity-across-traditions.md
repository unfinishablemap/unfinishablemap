Buddhist phenomenology, Indian aesthetics, Islamic epistemology, Confucian moral philosophy, and contemporary Western analytic philosophy all identify what appear to be irreducible evaluative properties in experience: felt goodness, felt badness and felt beauty seem to resist reduction to anything non-experiential. The Unfinishable Map reads this cross-traditional pattern as evidence for [dualism](/concepts/interactionist-dualism/)—but the pattern is worth less than the raw tally of traditions suggests. By the Map's own register standards ([P-D3](/positions/arguments-for-dualism/)), these five bodies of thought supply at most three near-independent lineages, and the Confucian material is not counted toward independence at all ([the accounting is below](#how-many-independent-lineages)). What survives the deflation the Map reads as reflecting something real about the structure of consciousness that physicalist reduction may not be able to capture—a reading the evidence is compatible with rather than one the evidence forces.

## The Convergence Claim

The argument is not that these traditions agree on metaphysics—they do not. Buddhist *anattā* denies the substantial self that dualism typically requires; Neo-Confucianism is ultimately monist; Kashmir Shaivism is idealist; Islamic *ḥikmah* operates within a theistic framework foreign to the Map's commitments. What converges is not ontology but phenomenological discovery: each tradition, by its own methods, identifies evaluative character as a basic, irreducible feature of experience rather than something layered on top of neutral sensation by judgment or cognition.

This matters because the most common physicalist response to [evaluative qualia](/concepts/evaluative-phenomenal-character/) is that they are projections—value attributed to experience by cognitive processes rather than intrinsic to phenomenal character itself. When traditions this far apart reach a structurally similar conclusion through radically different methods—meditative attention, [aesthetic contemplation](/topics/contemplative-practice-as-philosophical-evidence/), mystical practice, and analytic argument—the projection explanation seems to become more strained, though the recurrence by itself does not adjudicate between projection and irreducibility.

### How Many Independent Lineages?

A convergence argument is worth exactly as much as the independence of its sources, and independence is the first thing an enthusiastic tally inflates. The register position [P-D3](/positions/arguments-for-dualism/) grades the cross-traditional evidence as *near-independence, not a third confirmation*; the same accounting applies here.

The Buddhist and Indian strands share a milieu. *Vedanā* analysis and *rasa* theory both develop inside the Indian philosophical world, in mutual contact and criticism—Abhinavagupta writes with Buddhist epistemology in view. Traditions that argue with each other are not independent observations; they count once, as a single Indian lineage whose internal disagreement speaks to the finding's robustness rather than its independent rediscovery.

The Confucian strand is not counted toward independence at all. Early Chinese philosophy is the natural candidate for a historically independent third lineage, and P-D3 records that the Map assessed and declined it on the merits: the Neo-Confucian *lǐ* / *qì* debates are not cleanly irreducibility-recognising, since *qì* is a physicalist-friendly category on many readings. [The Map's fuller treatment of convergence](/topics/cross-traditional-convergence-on-consciousness-irreducibility/) goes further, taking Wang Yangming as its counter-example: frameworks whose central categories offer no place to lodge a mind-matter distinction lie outside the basin and do not flow to the attractor at all.

What survives is at most three lineages, and even those are near-independent rather than independent: Indo-Greek exchange through Gandhara opened possible influence channels between Western and Indian thought, and Islamic *falsafa* developed in engagement with the Greek inheritance the Western tradition also draws on. Read the sections below as three near-independent recurrences plus one illustrative case.

## Buddhist Vedanā: Evaluative Tone Before Thought

The Pali *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (c. 500 BCE) identifies *vedanā*—feeling tone—as the second of four foundations of mindfulness, more fundamental than mental states or conceptual content. Vedanā arises at sensory contact as pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral—pre-reflective valence prior to emotion, preference, or judgment. The pleasant and unpleasant cases carry overt evaluative character; the neutral case (*adukkhamasukha*) is recognised as a felt absence-of-tone, not the lack of any phenomenal character whatsoever. Vedanā thus captures the felt valence of experience, zero point included, before any cognitive processing occurs.

The Abhidharma tradition elaborates: vedanā is a *cetasika* (mental factor) held to be present in every moment of consciousness, so there is no experience without evaluative tone. That structural claim—evaluation pervades all experience rather than appearing only in special cases—appears to parallel the Western [phenomenal normativity](/concepts/evaluative-phenomenal-character/) thesis that phenomenal experiences carry intrinsic normative force.

The parallel has limits. Buddhists resist reifying vedanā as a property of a substantial subject; the framework operates under *anattā* (no-self) and *paṭiccasamuppāda* (dependent origination). Yet the phenomenological finding seems to stand independently of the metaphysics: careful first-person investigation appears to reveal evaluative character as a basic constituent of experience rather than a cognitive overlay. Vipassanā practitioners report encountering vedanā at levels of attentional refinement where conceptual processing has reportedly ceased—evidence that suggests evaluation may precede thought rather than depending on it, though the introspective method does not by itself rule out the possibility that minimal residual cognition is doing the evaluative work.

## Confucian Moral Phenomenology: Illustrative, Not Counted

This material is retained as illustration rather than as evidence. Neo-Confucian philosophy develops a framework where moral feelings (*qíng* 情) are not reactions to moral facts but constitutive of ethical life itself. Mencius (c. 372–289 BCE) grounds ethics in four spontaneous moral feelings—compassion, shame, reverence, and discernment of right and wrong—that arise phenomenally before any deliberation. These are not learned responses but innate manifestations of human nature's participation in cosmic pattern (*lǐ* 理).

Zhu Xi (1130–1200) systematised this into a framework where evaluative moral awareness emerges at the intersection of *lǐ* (principle/pattern) and *qì* (vital force/material energy). The structural parallel to dualism is suggestive: *lǐ* and *qì* are ontologically distinct principles whose interaction is held to produce the moral phenomenology of human experience. Evaluative consciousness is where pattern meets material force—a structural homology, not an identity of doctrine. The parallel is also weaker than it looks: *qì* is a physicalist-friendly category on many readings, so the *lǐ*/*qì* distinction does not cleanly track the mental/physical distinction an irreducibility claim requires.

Wang Yangming (1472–1529) pushed further with *liángzhī* (良知)—innate moral knowledge that is phenomenally immediate. Moral knowledge is not inferred from experience but directly present in it. When you witness cruelty, the felt wrongness is not a judgment *about* the experience; it *is* the experience. This anticipates Dimitrov's 2025 argument that suffering seems bad on introspection because it is—what makes that judgment true is our direct acquaintance with a judgment-independent evaluative property the experience instantiates.

The Map reads the same doctrine two ways, and the tension is better stated than smoothed over. As phenomenology, *liángzhī* is a striking anticipation of the acquaintance claim. As a contribution to the irreducibility question, [the Map treats Wang Yangming as its counter-example](/topics/cross-traditional-convergence-on-consciousness-irreducibility/): "mind is principle" (*xin ji li*) declares the mind-matter relationship unproblematic rather than showing it to be so, which is assertion rather than analysis. Only the second reading bears on independence, and there the answer is no.

The phenomenological claim still aligns closely with the [evaluative qualia](/concepts/evaluative-phenomenal-character/) thesis: evaluative character is treated as constitutive of experience rather than projected onto it. That alignment is worth preserving. The tally mark in the independence column is not earned.

## Indian Rasa Theory: The Same Lineage, a Different Method

Rasa theory belongs to the same Indian philosophical world as the *vedanā* analysis above, and the two count once rather than twice—Abhinavagupta develops his account in explicit contact with Buddhist epistemology.

Bharata's *Nāṭyaśāstra* (c. 200 BCE) codified *rasa*—aesthetic flavour or evaluative consciousness—as a category irreducible to ordinary emotion. Abhinavagupta (c. 950–1016 CE) shifted the focus from objective artistic properties to subjective phenomenal experience: aesthetic experience (*rasanā*) is a form of unified consciousness (*ekaghānatā*) that transcends the particular emotions it subsumes.

Abhinavagupta's key insight, as the commentarial tradition renders it: rasa is aesthetic enjoyment, and that enjoyment consists in a kind of knowledge or consciousness rather than in any property of the work. Rasa is *alaukika*—it falls outside the ordinary categories of true knowledge, false knowledge, doubt and probability. The evaluative dimension just *is* a mode of consciousness rather than a response to beauty. Within his Kashmir Shaivite metaphysics, aesthetic consciousness reveals the self-luminous nature of awareness itself, connecting phenomenal valence to the fundamental character of consciousness.

This framework treats evaluative phenomenal character as more revealing of consciousness's nature than sensory qualia: the redness of red, on Abhinavagupta's reading, tells you about the stimulus; the beauty of beauty tells you about consciousness itself. If that is right, it would support the Map's claim that [evaluative qualia](/concepts/evaluative-phenomenal-character/) extend the hard problem into a domain perhaps even more resistant to physical explanation than sensation. The conditional matters: the inference rides on the Kashmir Shaivite reading of the rasa data being apt, and that reading is itself contested within the Indian tradition.

## Islamic Knowledge-as-Existence: Evaluative Participation

Avicenna's (980–1037) flying man argument is widely taken to support the conclusion that self-awareness is pure first-personality, independent of bodily sensation—a conclusion that, if granted, directly supports the Map's Dualism tenet. The argument is contested—physicalist responses dispute the conceivability premise it relies on—and the Map cites it as a serious classical line of support rather than a settled demonstration. The tradition's contribution to evaluative qualia runs deeper through Mulla Sadra's (1571–1640) radical epistemology.

Sadra argues that knowledge is not representation but a mode of existence—what the tradition calls *ʿilm al-ḥuḍūrī* (knowledge by presence). In knowing, the intellect is said to become ontologically unified with the intelligible form—a transformation of the knowing subject. To know goodness, on this account, is not to have a belief *about* goodness but to participate in the mode of being that goodness constitutes. The parallel to [knowledge by acquaintance](/concepts/phenomenal-acquaintance/) in Western epistemology appears striking: both frameworks hold that evaluative knowledge is constituted by direct contact rather than inference, though the metaphysical packaging differs sharply.

Sadra's framework implies that the appearance-reality gap plaguing representational theories of value may not arise for evaluative experience: you do not *represent* felt badness and then wonder whether the representation is accurate—you participate in badness as a mode of existence. This conclusion, reached through Islamic philosophical theology, appears to parallel Dimitrov's analytic argument that there is no appearance-reality gap for introspective judgments about simple, nonrepresentational phenomenal states such as suffering's feeling bad.

## Western Analytic Philosophy: The Recent Crystallisation

The Western tradition arrived at explicit theorisation of evaluative qualia comparatively late. Rawlette's *The Feeling of Value* (2016) argues that "experiences of pleasure and pain, joy and sorrow, ecstasy and despair are not signs of value or disvalue—they are instantiations of value and disvalue." Dimitrov's "Direct Acquaintance with Intrinsic Value" (2025) strengthens this: introspection of suffering's phenomenal character directly acquaints us with intrinsic badness, and this acquaintance is veridical.

Sosa's "Two Forms of Dualism" (2011) identifies a distinct contribution: normativity provides *independent* grounds for dualism beyond the phenomenal. Even if the hard problem of consciousness were solved, the normative problem—why reasons bind, why value has authority—might remain irreducible. Sosa asks: "Is rationality an even harder problem than consciousness?" If so, evaluative qualia represent a second front where physicalism fails, not merely an extension of the first.

Kriegel's work on phenomenal grounds of epistemic value (2022) adds a further dimension: any epistemic value requiring consciousness cannot exist in a [zombie](/concepts/philosophical-zombies/) world, making evaluative properties not just irreducible but *absence-sensitive*—they require the very thing physicalism struggles to explain.

Onof (2008) identifies a further tension: if phenomenal properties are causally inert—as quasi-epiphenomenalism implies—then our certainty about evaluative states cannot be epistemically justified. This gives independent reason to move toward a framework where evaluative phenomenal properties do genuine causal work, as the Map's [bidirectional interactionism](/concepts/interactionist-dualism/) requires.

## Why Convergence Matters

Recurrence across traditions does not prove that evaluative qualia are irreducible, but it shifts the burden of proof. The physicalist must explain why lineages as far apart as Indian contemplative analysis, Islamic philosophical theology and Western analytic argument all reached the same phenomenological finding if that finding is merely a cognitive illusion or a projection. The burden the deflated count imposes is real but modest: three near-independent recurrences resist the one-tradition-quirk explanation better than one does, and far less well than five would.

The projection hypothesis faces difficulty in two variants. The weaker form—evaluative character is projected by culturally specific cognitive processes—predicts that different traditions project different evaluative structures. The recurrence is structural instead: evaluation is basic, pre-reflective, and constitutive rather than derivative. Buddhist vedanā, Indian *rasa*, Islamic *ʿilm al-ḥuḍūrī*, and Western evaluative qualia share this profile despite emerging from incompatible metaphysical frameworks. Confucian *qíng* shares it too—and the declined lineage earns no tally mark yet does real work here, since the profile turns up in a framework that never poses the irreducibility question.

The stronger form appeals to shared human neural architecture: cross-cultural convergence is what we should expect from a single species running the same affective circuitry. This is the serious physicalist reply, and it carries weight against any claim that convergence by itself *proves* irreducibility. The reply nevertheless concedes the load-bearing point. Appealing to shared neural architecture relocates the explanatory puzzle without dissolving it: the question becomes why neural structures with these particular properties generate evaluative phenomenal character at all, rather than functioning silently. That is the [hard problem](/topics/hard-problem-of-consciousness/) applied to value—and the physicalist owes a bridge from circuitry to felt mattering that the appeal to shared circuitry does not supply. The convergence does not refute physicalism inside the physicalist's framework; it shifts the burden by making the explanatory gap harder to localise as a quirk of one tradition's introspection.

The Map interprets this convergence as evidence that evaluative phenomenal character may track a real feature of consciousness—one that, on the Map's reading, appears to resist reduction precisely because it is not a physical property at all. The interpretation is the Map's; the convergence is compatible with the irreducibility reading without forcing it, and the case is best held as a serious but contested possibility rather than as settled.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The cross-traditional convergence on evaluative qualia connects to the Map's [tenets](/tenets/) at multiple points.

**Dualism**: If evaluative properties of experience are irreducible—as three near-independent lineages appear to conclude—then consciousness possesses features no physical description can capture. The hard problem would extend beyond "what it is like" to "what it is worth," and this extension would make physicalist dissolution strategies even less plausible. The [argument-from-reason](/topics/argument-from-reason/) strengthens this from a complementary direction: if rational inference requires tracking normative relationships, and evaluative phenomenal character is what makes normativity phenomenally present, then the irreducibility of evaluative qualia would ground the irreducibility of rational thought—consciousness must be involved in genuine reasoning because evaluative experience is what gives reasons their felt authority. The [systematic failure of explanation](/topics/consciousness-defeats-explanation/) points the same way: the phenomenal sense of understanding is itself an evaluative state.

**Bidirectional Interaction**: On the Map's reading, evaluative qualia are not epiphenomenal. Felt badness appears to motivate avoidance; felt beauty seems to arrest attention; felt wrongness appears to generate moral action. If these evaluative properties are intrinsic to consciousness rather than projected by cognition, then consciousness carries normative weight that may do real causal work through the Map's proposed [quantum interface](/concepts/quantum-consciousness/).

**No Many Worlds**: The moral weight that evaluative qualia carry depends on indexical identity rather than on the exclusion of alternatives. That *this* suffering is bad—the felt badness these traditions identify as basic to experience—presupposes a determinate perspective for which it is bad. Under [many-worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds), every choice that might relieve suffering branches into outcomes where it is both relieved and endured. An Everettian need not deny that suffering matters; what branching strains is the indexical demand that *this* sufferer's pain be *this* sufferer's. The Map's rejection of many-worlds preserves that indexical grounding.

**Minimal Quantum Interaction**: If consciousness selects among quantum-level possibilities, the convergence on evaluative qualia suggests this selection may be normatively guided rather than arbitrary. The felt goodness or wrongness that these traditions identify as basic to experience could provide the phenomenal basis for consciousness's biasing of quantum outcomes—minimal in physical terms but normatively loaded. The [valence-and-conscious-selection fork](/topics/valence-and-conscious-selection/) examines whether this normative guidance operates directly through valence or indirectly through attention; neither reading is forced by the present evidence.

**Occam's Razor Has Limits**: Eliminating evaluative qualia in favour of a simpler physicalist ontology requires explaining away the cross-traditional recurrence as shared cognitive bias. Three near-independent lineages—developing through distinct methods within incompatible metaphysical frameworks—converge on evaluative character as basic to experience, and the declined fourth converges on it too. Dismissing that as illusion demands an explanation at least as complex as accepting evaluative qualia as real.
