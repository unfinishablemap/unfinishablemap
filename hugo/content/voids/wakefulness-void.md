People woken from sleep that a laboratory recording scores as sleep often say, sincerely, that they were awake. The Unfinishable Map names the **wakefulness void** for what this shows: a report of having been awake does not certify the interval it is about, because the same report is given, on waking, about intervals scored as sleep, and when sleeper and standard recording disagree the field has not settled which is wrong. The clinical label, "sleep state misperception", assumed the recording was the fact and the report the error. A 2023 systematic review is titled "from misperception to mismeasurement", and its abstract says: "It is unknown whether this subjective-objective mismatch truly reflects an abnormal perception of sleep, or whether it results from the inability of standard sleep recording techniques to capture 'wake-like' brain activity patterns that could account for feeling awake during sleep" (Stephan and Siclari 2023). What stays open is who is right and, behind that, what the person was conscious of during an interval scored as sleep and remembered as waking.

In the sense of the Map's [three kinds of void](/voids/three-kinds-of-void/) this is a modest void. For the science it is **unexplored**: standard scoring and the report disagree, and finer recording is being used to decide between them. For the person reporting it has a weakly **occluded** face: the report is made after waking, about a state that has ended, and it sometimes returns "awake" for an interval scored as sleep. It is bounded. The same abstract reports that "most individuals tend to accurately estimate their sleep duration", a finding about whole-night estimates, and Weigand and colleagues, below, say recorded and perceived sleep "generally concur".

Three neighbours mark its edges. The [transit void](/voids/transit-void/) covers the unwitnessed moment of falling asleep; a person woken from stable sleep is past that crossing. The [sleep consciousness void](/voids/sleep-consciousness-void/) takes the sleep/wake classification as given and asks about content. The [perceptual reality-monitoring void](/voids/perceptual-reality-monitoring-void/) concerns the standing of one percept. Here the experiencer's own global state is what is in dispute.

## What the Awakening Studies Report

Most sources below were read as abstracts only; quotations are from those abstracts unless marked.

Harvey and Tang (2012), in a review read in full, credit Rechtschaffen and Monroe (1969) as "among the first to report this phenomenon": as Harvey and Tang report it, poor sleepers woken ten minutes after the first sleep spindle were more likely than good sleepers to say they had just been awake. Bonnet and Moore (1982) woke twelve normal young adults five times a night and found that the point at which sleep was reported half the time "occurred 2-4 min after the first sleep spindle", whereas auditory thresholds "rose rapidly within 1 min" of it, siding with the recording; they call subjective sleep onset "a relatively lengthy period during which perception of state is blurred and uncertain".

The report also occurs in consolidated sleep. Weigand, Michael and Schulz (2007) woke sixty-eight young normal sleepers once each, from stage 2 or REM sleep, on a first laboratory night. The preceding state "was experienced as wakefulness in 20 cases (29.4%)". That figure belongs to this design and is no general rate. Nights with matching and mismatching judgements "did not differ in electrophysiologically defined sleep onset latency, sleep efficiency, or sleep state distribution".

The REM evidence conflicts, and the two results belong together. Weigand and colleagues report awake judgements after 38.2% of stage 2 awakenings and 20.6% of REM awakenings in good sleepers, a difference they describe as not significant. Stephan and Siclari (2023), in a systematic review, say that in good sleepers "feeling awake during sleep is the rule at sleep onset, remains frequent in the first non-rapid eye movement sleep cycle and almost never occurs in rapid eye movement (REM) sleep". Nothing read for this article resolves the difference.

Two further results suggest a limit on access. Mercer, Bootzin and Lack (2002) treated "asleep or awake?" as a signal detection task and found that "Insomniacs had reduced sleep-wake discriminability in addition to a greater bias toward reporting prior wakefulness". And the error runs both ways: Knab and Engel (1988) suggest that insomniacs "perceive these intervals as continuous wakefulness and have difficulty in perceiving short-lasting sleep, whereas controls often do not perceive wakefulness at all".

## Three Faces

**The uncertified state.** "I was awake" arrives with no validated mark separating the occasions when it is true. Mercer's reduced discriminability describes the signal available to the judge.

**The disputed arbiter.** On the misperception reading the sleeper is wrong about their own state. On the mismeasurement reading the scoring is too coarse and the sleeper reports something real. Stephan, Lecci, Cataldi and Siclari (2021) collected "787 awakenings in 20 good sleepers and 10 individuals with sleep misperception" under high-density EEG and found that in non-REM sleep "spatially widespread high-frequency power was inversely related to subjective sleep depth". The 2023 review describes changes that "likely reflect a shift towards greater cortical activation during sleep and correlate with feeling awake". This favours mismeasurement without settling it. State can differ by region, at least in rats kept awake: Vyazovskiy et al. (2011) found that "although both the EEG and behaviour indicate wakefulness, local populations of neurons in the cortex may be falling asleep".

**The inferred "I slept".** Stephan et al. (2021) report that good sleepers' sleep "was subjectively lightest in the first 2 h of non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, generally considered the deepest sleep, and deepest in rapid eye movement (REM) sleep", and that "Subjective sleep depth positively correlated with dream-like qualities of reports of mental activity". Hermans et al. (2020), read in full, cite earlier work in which "the sense of being asleep prior to awakening from NREM sleep was shown to depend on the length of the preceding uninterrupted sleep fragment". The Map's reading, which is not the authors', is that "I was asleep" is assembled afterwards from proxies such as a dream, a gap or a long unbroken stretch, so that those who are right about their sleep infer well.

## What Limits the Void

The void is narrow and may shrink. Training helped in a study Harvey and Tang describe, which woke ten people with subjective insomnia "27 times on the training night" with feedback; they add that "none of these studies included a follow-up assessment". A limit that yields to feedback is partly a calibration problem. The obvious deflationary account, poor time estimation, had no support in 2012, when Harvey and Tang wrote that "all three of the published studies conducted to date do not provide support"; Hermans et al. (2020) have since reported that, with sleep fragmentation modelled, "time estimation is associated with sleep onset misperception". Among the accounts Harvey and Tang judged best supported, "brief awakenings" has the sleeper partly right.

If mismeasurement is right, the first two faces partly close, since the sleeper who says "awake" may be correct about a state the scoring lumps with sleep. Every report is also retrospective. Joo et al. (2025) note that most studies compare morning self-report with recorded duration, "limiting our ability to differentiate the effects of perception from retrospective-reporting bias".

The converse case, acting while asleep and taking oneself to be awake, was not found tested. Reyner and Horne (1998) measured only awareness of prior sleepiness: sleep-restricted simulator drivers could not fall asleep at the wheel "without experiencing a sustained period of increasing sleepiness, of which they were quite aware".

The phenomenology is thin. Stephan and Siclari note that in insomnia patients who underestimate their sleep, "Their mental activity during sleep is also more thought-like", and Weigand's subjects who judged themselves awake more often "had a feeling of being aware of the situation and their surroundings". Beyond forced-choice answers and rating scales, no first-person interview material was found.

## The Old Question, by Another Route

Philosophy has asked whether one can know one is awake mainly through dreams. In Descartes's First Meditation, as quoted in Windt's (2015) survey, the meditator's confidence collapses: "As if I did not remember other occasions when I have been tricked by exactly similar thoughts while asleep!" That survey, as fetched, does not mention insomnia or these laboratory findings. Those findings have the same direction, sleep reported as waking, but a different timing: Descartes's thoughts are had while asleep and the laboratory reports come after waking. In the serial-awakening work, dream-rich REM sleep feels most like sleep and early non-REM sleep is where "awake" is most often said. The sceptic's worry concerned vivid dreams; the measured mismatch occurs where there may be no dream to mark the state as sleep.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**Occam's Razor Has Limits** is the only one of the Map's [tenets](/tenets/) with even an illustration here: the picture in which sleep is one global state, standard scoring reads it, and a dissenting subject is mistaken has come under question, and the label "misperception" carried that picture in its vocabulary. No source read says the picture was held for its simplicity.

The case gives **Dualism** no support. The mismeasurement turn was won by finding neural correlates of the report, and dualist and physicalist readings predict the same reports and the same recordings. The dispute concerns whether an electrophysiological or a first-person criterion fixes the facts about a state of consciousness, and either party can take either side. The evidence read here does not show whether experience was present or absent during an interval scored as sleep.

**Bidirectional Interaction** is not engaged. Mercer and colleagues suggest the misperception "may play a role in the perpetuation of insomnia", which is belief affecting physiology through ordinary psychological means. **Minimal Quantum Interaction** and **No Many Worlds** have no bearing on the case.

The case does matter for the Map's reliance on [first-person evidence](/topics/phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence/). A sincere report disagrees at a measurable rate with the standard recording of whether the reporter was awake, and finer recording has partly taken the report's side.
