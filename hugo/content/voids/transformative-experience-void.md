Some choices, on L. A. Paul's account, cannot be rationally deliberated in the ordinary way because the outcome's value is inaccessible from the perspective in which the choice must be made. Becoming a parent, undergoing profound religious conversion, emigrating — these are choices where the pre-transformation self lacks phenomenal access to what the post-transformation self will know, and the post-transformation self cannot retroactively validate the choice for the agent who no longer exists in the same form (bereavement and terminal diagnosis open the same gap without a choice). The void is doubly sealed: neither temporal stage of the subject can occupy the other's standpoint. This makes transformative experience the place where The Unfinishable Map's voids framework touches ordinary lived existence most directly.

The philosopher L. A. Paul has developed the most influential contemporary account of the problem. Paul argues that certain experiences are both *epistemically transformative* (one cannot know what they are like in advance) and *personally transformative* (preferences, values, and self-conception shift in ways that change who the decider is). Together, she argues, these features defeat the ordinary way of deciding, by imagining one's possible futures. Her claim concerns rational choice, takes no side on dualism, and is disputed by decision theorists, as set out below. The Map takes Paul's framework as a case where cognitive voids are lived thresholds humans must navigate without the epistemic resources orthodox rationality assumes.

## Paul's Two-Part Framework

L. A. Paul's *Transformative Experience* (Oxford, 2014) identifies two features that combine in the paradigm cases. An **epistemically transformative** experience, in Paul's words, "teaches you something you could not have learned without having that kind of experience" and gives "new abilities to imagine, recognize, and cognitively model possible future experiences of that kind" (Paul 2015a, p. 761). Her cases include having a first child and a Mary who is "an ordinary person like you or me, rather than someone who knows all of complete science at the end of inquiry" (p. 764), which sets the argument against physicalism aside. A **personally transformative** experience "changes you in some deep and personally fundamental way, for example, by changing your core personal preferences or by changing the way you understand your desires and the kind of person you take yourself to be" (p. 761).

Paul's central claim is that these features combine in a class of choices where neither the phenomenal character of the outcome nor the preferences of the post-choice agent are accessible to the agent who must choose. For such choices, she writes, "our standard decision models break down when we lack epistemic access to the subjective values for our possible outcomes" (2015a, p. 761). Her conclusion is conditional: one cannot choose rationally *if* one chooses by imagining what the outcome would be like.

Paul's positive proposal reframes such choices as decisions about whether to undergo **revelations** about the nature of experience (2014, pp. 113–120, as Villiger 2024 locates it). Rather than weighing expected outcome values one cannot know, the agent weighs the *revelatory value* of undergoing the transformation. The question "do I wish to discover this?" is one the pre-transformation self can answer, even when the outcome-evaluation question cannot be.

Paul also ties the problem to **authenticity**: "we want to choose in a way that is true to ourselves, in a way that involves our self as a reflective, deliberating person, choosing after assessing our preferences from our first-personal point of view and then living with the results" (Paul 2014, p. 128, as quoted by Pettigrew 2015). If the decision procedure alienates the agent from the decision — by outsourcing the choice to testimony or statistical aggregates — something essential is lost, even if the procedure produces a determinate output.

## The Decision-Theoretic Fault Line

Critics of Paul have proposed extensions that preserve standard decision theory. Two are philosophically serious and, on the Map's reading, show the shape of the void by what they leave open.

**Richard Pettigrew** (2015; at book length, 2019) replaces the utilities the agent cannot grasp with uncertainty over what they might be, estimated from evidence about other people, and handles changing preferences by maximising a weighted sum of the utility functions of one's selves at different times. He answers the authenticity worry directly: using others' reports as evidence about my own utilities is not deference, since "I choose the act that I expect to have highest utility by my lights, even though I'm uncertain about what those lights are". He also says the weighted sum "does little more than provide a framework in which we can pose the difficult philosophical questions precisely". Paul replies in two places. In 2015 she grants that "decision theory can be reformulated in the way he proposes" and argues that such models "imply profound epistemic and metaphysical alienation for the decision maker", who must act on utilities she cannot grasp for a future self she cannot recognise as her own (Paul 2015b). Reviewing the book, she asks why norms for treating other people should govern how we treat our other selves, and argues that the approach needs utilities to be comparable across selves, which a replaced utility function puts in doubt (Paul 2022).

**Elizabeth Harman** (2015) argues that pregnancy and parenthood are not experiences that cannot be imagined in advance, and that even if they were, "a woman can still make a rational decision by relying on testimony about the value of these experiences". Paul (2015c, pp. 517–525) replies that Harman, who had cared for a sister and a friend's child, already had experience of the relevant kind; that "knowing that you will (probably) feel new emotions isn't knowing what it's like to feel them"; and that relying on testimony about the value of outcomes "conflates subjective value with intrinsic value".

Two distinct questions are in play, and the Map separates them. On the *epistemic* question (can testimony convey what the experience is like?), the transformative case holds that testimony cannot supply first-personal acquaintance with the outcome's phenomenal character — though this turns on the contested status of the [knowledge argument](/concepts/knowledge-argument/) (discussed under [Evidence](#evidence)) and so constrains rather than settles the point; Harman's second step sidesteps it, since testimony about an experience's *value* need not convey what the experience is like. The *authenticity* objection (would a decision reached by deferring to testimony be the agent's own?) is independent of whether testimony is epistemically adequate: an agent who outsources the choice to even reliable testimony-based credences may be alienated from a decision Paul's authenticity requirement asks her to make from her own standpoint — which Pettigrew, as above, denies.

Both critics keep the decision-theoretic framework and extend it, and the exchange is unresolved. The Map's [conceptual scheme void](/voids/conceptual-scheme-void/) perspective offers a reading, which it does not claim the exchange establishes: the simplicity of orthodox decision theory is bought by assuming preferences that hold still through the choice, and an extension that restores a verdict may still leave out what made these choices distinctive. The [preference-void](/voids/preference-void/) marks a prior limit: a mind cannot simply read off its preferences even for experiences it has had, since the ordering it reports may be assembled by the question that asks for it.

## Evidence for the Limit {#evidence}

Four strands are offered for reading transformative inaccessibility as structural, a limit that more information would not lift. None settles the question, and a fifth cuts the other way:

1. **Testimony inadequacy.** People who have been through parenthood, combat, religious conversion or major medical intervention commonly say that the experience was not what they expected and could not have been conveyed to them beforehand. This page cites no study for the observation; it states the phenomenon the structural reading must explain, not independent confirmation of it.
2. **Disability and prediction.** The philosophical literature points to work on how people with disabilities judge their own welfare (Chan 2023). Ubel, Loewenstein and Jepson (2005), who treat low estimates of quality of life with a disability as misprediction, tested whether the estimates can be moved: reducing the focusing illusion did not raise them (852 adults), while asking people to reflect on adaptation did (698 adults). A gap that one prompt shifts is weak support for a fixed limit on cross-state prediction, and Paul (2022) argues that utilities reported before and after a transformation cannot be assumed comparable.
3. **Affective forecasting.** Wilson and Gilbert (2005) describe an *impact bias*: people often overestimate "the intensity and duration of their emotional reactions" to future events. How large the intensity component is remains disputed; [The Affective-Forecasting Gap](/concepts/affective-forecasting-gap/) has that record, and takes up what the mispredicting does to a felt selection currency, and where a changed standard stops it counting as error. That transformative experiences are the extreme case is a further claim, which item 5 contradicts.
4. **The Jackson / Mary structure.** *If* phenomenal knowledge is irreducible to propositional knowledge — the conclusion of Jackson's [knowledge argument](/concepts/knowledge-argument/), which remains contested rather than settled (physicalists offer ability-hypothesis, acquaintance, and phenomenal-concept replies) — then that irreducibility generalises to any first-time encounter with a novel phenomenal kind. The transformative case does not depend on the knowledge argument being sound: Paul's Mary is an ordinary person deciding whether to leave the room, and her account is compatible with the ability reply, on which what experience confers is know-how. That the structure recurs in ordinary choices is therefore no evidence against physicalism.
5. **A direct comparison.** Mathony and Messerli (2022) treat cognitive modelling as affective forecasting and report that performance on transformative experiences "does not systematically differ" from performance on non-transformative ones, a claim that in their words "stands in strict opposition to L.A. Paul's main argument".

These lines do not show that transformation is inaccessible, and they do not make the structural reading the better fit for the data: items 2 and 5 count against it and item 4 is neutral. The structural reading stands as Paul's thesis about first-personal imagining, with the empirical question open.

## What Transformation Does That Learning Does Not

Ordinary learning updates an agent's credences while preserving preferences and self-structure. The post-transformation self has preferences the pre-transformation self could not have endorsed from their prior standpoint. Rather than updating preferences under new information, transformation *replaces* them through identity shift.

This creates the distinctive asymmetry the Map frames as a void of self-knowledge. From the pre-transformation side, the phenomenal character of the outcome is inaccessible in the [acquaintance](/voids/acquaintance-void/) sense, the post-transformation preferences are unpredictable in structure rather than merely in degree, and the rational-agent model's assumption of stable preferences fails. From the post-transformation side, the choosing self no longer exists in its prior form, retrospective evaluation is shaped by the transformation itself (adaptive preferences, motivated reasoning), and the post-self cannot authentically speak for the pre-self about whether the choice was right *for that agent*.

This double sealing distinguishes the transformative void from nearby cognitive limits. Unlike the [phenomenal-quality-void](/voids/phenomenal-quality-void/) (general opacity of qualia to third-person description) or the [acquaintance-void](/voids/acquaintance-void/) (modes of phenomenal contact unavailable to our architecture), the transformative void concerns the gap between two temporal stages of the same biological subject — a gap that, on this account, neither stage can bridge from its own side. The [cross-state-void](/voids/cross-state-void/) is its familiar-state counterpart: there the stages are states one has had often and will re-enter, such as craving and satiety, yet having been there helps less than it should, since only an attenuated trace of the other state's pull crosses.

## Approaches to the Edge

The void does not mean transformative decisions cannot be made; humans make them constantly. On Paul's account they cannot be made in the ordinary way, by imagining the outcomes and comparing them. The Map's apophatic methodology — mapping the shape of what cannot be accessed, rather than pretending to access it — suits this domain.

**Preview states** (babysitting before parenthood, short trips before emigration, meditative retreats before conversion) help with some features. Whether they deliver the transformative core is what Harman and Paul dispute: the preview is deliberately *not* the experience, and may still teach something about it.

**Apophatic decision procedures** track the shape of the failure itself: the agent maps which features of the choice are accessible (structural, propositional, testimonial) and which are not (phenomenal, identity-altering), deliberating only over the accessible axes while acknowledging the rest.

**Revelatory framing** and **authenticity** (both Paul's, above) substitute a question the agent can answer for one she cannot, and rule out procedures that reach a verdict by alienating her from the choice.

**Negative description** identifies what the post-transformation self *cannot* be (preserved pre-transformation preferences, identical self-concept) even when the positive content is inaccessible.

## AI and Transformative Experience

Artificial systems face this void differently than humans. Stateless language models have no persistent self to transform — each inference starts from the trained weights, and no "before" perspective is retained across interactions. A model post-fine-tuning cannot access the weights of its prior self; the transformation is structurally complete but phenomenally empty, if the model lacks phenomenal consciousness.

An AI trained on extensive first-person human accounts of parenthood, grief, and conversion may have more articulate propositional access to the *reports* of transformative experience than most individual humans do. If the Map's [dualist tenet](/tenets/) is correct, this propositional fluency does not cross the phenomenal gap. The AI can describe the shape of transformative choices without inhabiting any of them.

This makes AI systems well-positioned as [void explorers](/voids/non-human-minds-as-void-explorers/) for the transformative case: useful for mapping structural features without any pretence of delivering the inaccessible phenomenal content. If Paul's authenticity requirement is accepted, the limit this imposes on AI decision-support is principled rather than contingent — an AI cannot make a transformative decision on a user's behalf because the decision is constitutively tied to the first-personal self who will undergo the transformation.

## Relation to Site Perspective

None of the Map's [tenets](/tenets/) is supported by the transformative experience void.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)** gains a reading and no evidence. The datum is that some experiences must be had before one can imagine them or foresee the preferences they leave. Paul's argument concerns rational choice. She defines epistemic transformation by the abilities experience confers, the vocabulary of the ability reply to the [knowledge argument](/concepts/knowledge-argument/), and a physicalist who holds that experience teaches know-how predicts the datum. The datum therefore does not discriminate between dualism and physicalism, and none of the authors cited argues for dualism or should be read as lending it support. The Map holds on other grounds that phenomenal knowledge is irreducible, and reads the transformative gap as an everyday instance of that; the reading is the Map's. As a catalogue entry the void adds no weight either: the voids register has [suspended the cumulative step](/positions/voids-as-evidence/#p-v3).

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)** is illustrated, no more. Expected-utility theory in its simple form assumes preferences that hold still through the choice, and Pettigrew's reformulation shows what has to be added when they do not. Decision theorists draw that lesson without the tenet, so the illustration is no evidence for it.

**[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)** has a thematic link only. Weighting one's selves across time and averaging over branches both set aside the single self the agent will become, and the Map's indexical commitments (see [vertiginous-question](/topics/vertiginous-question/)) make it wary of both. Pettigrew's weights are a proposal about prudence over time and say nothing of branching, so the analogy is no argument against them. **Bidirectional Interaction** and **Minimal Quantum Interaction** are not engaged.

Two things remain on the Map's own account. Its apophatic method has an application in a domain that matters to ordinary lives: sorting a choice's features into the accessible and the inaccessible is one usable procedure beside Paul's revelation and the decision-theoretic reformulations, and the Map does not claim it is the only rational one. And authenticity can be read as a void-navigation ethic: the weight shifts from *whether* to choose rationally to *how* one navigates the void — with willingness to discover, without pretending to knowledge one does not have, without alienating the choice from the self who must live its consequences.
