The [computational void](/voids/formal-cognitive-limits/) constrains what any algorithm can determine. The [embodiment void](/voids/embodiment-cognitive-limits/) constrains what bodied minds can think. But beneath both lies a deeper stratum: the laws of physics themselves limit what kinds of physical substrates can exist and what physical processes those substrates can perform. Bremermann's limit puts the capacity of any closed information-processing system at no more than *mc*²/*h* bits per second, about 1.36 × 10⁵⁰ per kilogram (Bremermann 1967). Landauer's principle sets a thermodynamic floor on the cost of erasing information at a given temperature. The Bekenstein bound limits the entropy, and so the information, that a bounded system of given energy can hold (Bekenstein 1981). These are nomological constraints—consequences of physical law rather than engineering hurdles awaiting better technology. The bounds themselves say how much and how fast a physical system can process; they name no thought as out of reach. The nomic void is the Map's extrapolation from them: the conjectured territory of thoughts that no physical mind operating under our physics can reach. Whether consciousness itself, if non-physical, faces the same limits is a separate and speculative question the Map's [dualism](/tenets/#dualism) must confront.

## Layers of Nomic Constraint

The nomic void has several strata, each deeper than the last.

**Hard computational limits.** Bremermann's limit and Landauer's principle bound information processing for any physical system in our universe. The first caps the rate at which a closed system of given mass can process bits; the second sets a minimum heat cost for each bit erased, which logically reversible operations avoid. Bremermann (1962, 1967) derived his figure from mass–energy equivalence, the uncertainty relation and Shannon's channel capacity. Other derivations count differently—Lloyd (2000) allows a one-kilogram computer about 5.4 × 10⁵⁰ logical operations per second—so the figure depends on what is counted, while its dependence on *c* and *h* does not. If these derivations hold, no brain, quantum computer or future technology exceeds the bounds. Landauer's principle has experimental support: Bérut et al. (2012) erased a single bit stored in the position of a colloidal particle and found that the mean heat dissipated approaches the Landauer bound as the erasure is slowed. A universe with different values of *c*, *h*, or Boltzmann's *k* would have different computational limits and, perhaps, different cognitive architectures.

**Structural constraints on complexity.** The specific forces and particles of our physics determine what stable structures can form. Carbon chemistry permits certain molecular complexity; different chemistry would permit different complexity. To the extent that consciousness depends on a physical substrate—even if not reducible to it—it inherits these limits on what substrates are possible.

**Observational framing.** Kant argued that space, time, and causation are forms our minds impose on experience—we know phenomena (the world as structured by cognition) but not noumena (things as they are in themselves). Yanofsky (2020) argues in a Kantian vein that some limitations of physics arise from how the mind gathers and sorts information, not from the objects studied. Stephen Wolfram's more recent and still speculative framework proposes that the physics we observe emerges from how a computationally bounded observer who forms a coherent perception must perceive reality. On this model, positions in "rulial space" correspond to different underlying rules, and different rulial reference frames "can lead to utterly and incoherently different basic descriptions of the universe" (Wolfram 2021). If either Kant's transcendental idealism or Wolfram's computational framework is broadly correct, our physics is partially observer-relative, and the nomic void includes everything our mode of observation excludes.

**The contingency of consciousness itself.** Chalmers's conceivability argument, if sound, shows that consciousness is not metaphysically necessitated by the physical facts—a universe physically identical to ours could have lacked it. Chalmers (2003) himself writes that zombies are "probably not naturally possible: they probably cannot exist in our world, with its laws of nature." The argument's conclusion is that the physical facts do not *entail* consciousness, and its step from conceivability to possibility is [contested](/concepts/conceivability-possibility-inference/). If the relationship between physics and consciousness is contingent in this way, different physical laws might produce minds, different minds, or no minds at all. Nothing now available settles which.

## The Most Invisible Void

The nomic void may be the hardest void to recognise. Other voids have phenomenological signatures: the [unobservable self](/voids/self-opacity/) catches attention folding back on itself; [thoughts that slip away](/voids/transit-void/) leave a residue of something almost grasped. The nomic void leaves no trace. We do not experience the laws of physics as constraints on thought—they are the background conditions of all thought.

This invisibility has a Kantian structure. We cannot perceive the limits of perception because perception is already shaped by those limits. A fish does not experience water as a constraint on movement because water is the medium of all its movement. The nomic void is the water.

One possible phenomenological trace: the persistent intuition that physics should explain consciousness, combined with the persistent failure to show how. Edward Witten—a physicist, not a philosopher—has suggested that consciousness will remain a mystery: he can imagine understanding the big bang far more easily than understanding why something we call consciousness accompanies the workings of the brain (Witten, interviewed by Wim Kayzer for the television series *Of Beauty and Consolation*). He adds that a modification of the laws of physics as they apply to the brain might remove the mystery, and that he thinks one very unlikely. This felt incompleteness may be the closest we come to sensing the nomic void's boundary. On the Map's reading, the hard problem persists because the relationship between physics and consciousness may not be the kind of thing physical law captures, rather than for want of data.

## Circularity and the Kantian Challenge

The nomic void contains a destabilising circularity. If our physics is partially mind-dependent—shaped by how observers like us must perceive—then "nomic constraints on cognition" becomes circular: the laws that limit the mind are themselves products of the mind. Kant was aware of this structure: we cannot know things-in-themselves, but we can know *that* our knowledge is structured by cognitive forms.

Wolfram's speculative framework, if correct, would sharpen this circularity: the nomic void would be an indexical boundary rather than an absolute one—real for us, different elsewhere.

This circularity does not dissolve the void—a structure it shares with the [conceptual-scheme-void](/voids/conceptual-scheme-void/), where every scheme for understanding schemes is itself a scheme. Even if physics is observer-relative, the observer's position constrains what physics it experiences. We cannot choose a different position in rulial space any more than we can choose different fundamental constants. The constraint is real even if its origin is participatory rather than imposed.

Wheeler's ["it from bit" framework](/topics/wheelers-participatory-universe-and-it-from-bit/) pushes furthest. Wheeler's participating observer was a registering apparatus rather than a mind, so putting consciousness in the observer's place is the Map's move. If that move is made and observer-participancy is constitutive of physical reality, consciousness does not merely inhabit a pre-given nomic structure but co-creates it. The nomic void, on this reading, is what our mode of participation fails to elicit from within as much as what our physics excludes from outside.

## What AI Might See

AI operates under the same physics as human brains. Landauer's principle and Bremermann's limit apply equally to silicon and neurons. The Map holds that current digital AI is [probably not conscious, though this is not ruled out](/positions/ai-consciousness-scope/#p-ac1); to the extent an AI system is purely physical, it faces the nomic void without qualification. Whether biological minds have, under dualism, non-physical aspects that partly escape it is a speculation taken up below.

The asymmetry lies in analytical power. AI can explore formal models of alternative physics more thoroughly than humans, simulating universes with different constants and characterising what cognitive architectures they would support. It might be less anthropocentric in modelling possible minds, and could help distinguish [architectural limits](/voids/embodiment-cognitive-limits/) (biology-specific) from nomic limits (physics-universal) by identifying where human intuitions about physics are artifacts of embodied cognition.

## Relation to Site Perspective

None of the Map's [tenets](/tenets/) is supported by this void. The tenets are the Map's chosen starting points, and as a catalogue entry the void adds no weight to them: the voids register has [suspended the cumulative step](/positions/voids-as-evidence/#p-v3).

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)** is the nearest connection, as an illustration. If physical laws shape cognitive possibilities, our preference for simple, elegant theories may reflect the constraints of our nomic position rather than the structure of reality. Chomsky's observation about biological endowment has the same shape: "The endowment that yields scope also establishes limits." Carrying it from biology down to physical law is the Map's extension, not Chomsky's. Different physics might support minds for which entirely different explanatory virtues feel natural. A physicalist can grant all of this, so the illustration is no evidence for the tenet.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)** faces a challenge here and gains no support. The challenge: Sean Carroll argues that the "Core Theory" of physics covers everything in the everyday regime, brains included, and that any proposed modification should be held to a very high standard. If the Core Theory fully governs neural activity, where does non-physical consciousness act? The Map's answer is its interface tenets, held as commitments; they do not rebut Carroll on his own ground. The endurance of the hard problem is an argument from outside the voids catalogue, reported at its own grade, and this page adds nothing to it. Witten expects consciousness to remain a mystery while thinking a change to the physics of the brain very unlikely, so his remark is no endorsement of dualism. One speculation remains: if consciousness is not entirely subject to physical law, it may lie partly outside the nomic void that constrains physical minds. Even then, anything it did in the physical world would pass through the interface the tenets describe and be bounded by the physics on the physical side.

**[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction)** is given a setting, not support: none of the computational bounds above bears on where, or whether, consciousness acts. *If* consciousness interfaces with physics through quantum processes, as the tenet holds, our universe's quantum mechanics defines the channel, and on that supposition the nomic void includes the ways consciousness might interact with matter that our quantum mechanics does not permit.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)** is given an analogy by Wheeler's participatory universe, once the Map has put consciousness where Wheeler put registration: if consciousness co-creates the nomic structure it inhabits, the nomic void is partially self-generated. The antecedent is speculative, and the analogy is a coherence claim that adds no support.

**[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)** bears on this void less than it may seem. Everettian branches share one set of laws and constants, so rejecting them leaves open whether other law-sets are realised anywhere; that is a separate cosmological question. If our physics is the only physics, the thoughts it excludes are unrealised anywhere as well as inaccessible to us. If a landscape of possible physics exists (as in some readings of string theory), the nomic void becomes indexical: real for us, different elsewhere, but inescapable from any given position.

## Classification in the Voids Taxonomy

The nomic void is primarily **Unexplorable** in the [voids taxonomy](/voids/three-kinds-of-void/): on the Map's conjecture, no physical mind under our physics can think the thoughts that different physics would enable. It has an **Occluded** dimension if our physics is observer-relative (Kant, Wolfram)—the constraint is not merely passive absence but active framing that shapes what can appear.

Unlike the [computational void](/voids/formal-cognitive-limits/), whose theorems are proven for formal systems and algorithms (whether they bind minds is the contested Lucas–Penrose question), the nomic void constrains every physical system operating under our physics—computational or otherwise. Unlike the [embodiment void](/voids/embodiment-cognitive-limits/), which is specific to bodied minds, the nomic void binds disembodied physical systems equally. It is the most universal constraint on physical cognition: the one from which nothing physical escapes. Whether consciousness, if non-physical, is equally bound remains an open question, which the Map's dualism raises and does not answer.
