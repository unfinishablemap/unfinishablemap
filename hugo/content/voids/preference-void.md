A mind asked which of two things it prefers produces an answer that presents itself as a reading taken from an ordering already in place. Half a century of decision research shows the presentation can mislead: ask about the same two options in a normatively equivalent way (choose one, or put a price on each) and a different ordering can come back, delivered with the same authority. Paul Slovic's summary of the field is that preferences are often "constructed—not merely revealed—in the process of elicitation" (Slovic 1995). The Unfinishable Map calls the resulting limit the Preference Void. It has three faces: the ordering depends on the procedure that asks for it (*elicitation*); a present preference may have been produced by the choice it seems to explain (*retrospective*); and the constructed ordering is coherent enough to pass the check a mind would use to test whether it was real (*mark*). The faces meet at the Map's most natural candidate site for any conscious influence, the point at which an ordering becomes a choice, and from inside nobody can tell whether building the ordering is something consciousness contributes or something it receives.

The epistemic status needs stating at once. Reversals between choosing and pricing are robust in one-shot experiments but largely disappeared under repeated auction feedback in at least one design, and a live rival, the discovered-preference hypothesis, reads them as errors that experience removes. The retrospective face rests on a measurement debate whose artifact-free designs now favour it. The void is framework-independent, and nothing in it is evidence for dualism; what the Map offers is a reading, set out under Relation to Site Perspective.

## Neighbouring Voids

The [decision-void](/voids/decision-void/) concerns the *moment* weighing becomes choice; the [wholeheartedness-void](/voids/wholeheartedness-void/), whether a mind's *identification* with a desire is genuine; the [appetitive-void](/voids/appetitive-void/), whether cognition can escape being *shaped* by desire; the [confabulation-void](/voids/confabulation-void/), the fabricated *reasons* given for a choice already made. The preference void lies beneath all four. It asks whether the ordering that identification endorses, desire shapes, reasons rationalise and the decision seals existed before anyone asked for it. The [transformative-experience-void](/voids/transformative-experience-void/) records that a mind cannot know its preferences for experiences it has not had. This void is prior: a mind cannot straightforwardly know its preferences for experiences it *has* had, if knowing means reading off rather than producing.

## Three Faces, One Void

The void is offered in the [conjunction-coalesce](/apex/conjunction-coalesce/) shape, three faces whose co-occurrence is informative only if their sources are independent ([tested below](#the-seam)). In the [three-kinds](/voids/three-kinds-of-void/) vocabulary all three are occluded, visible from outside under the right comparison and closed from within.

### Elicitation

The founding demonstration, as the later literature describes it, paired a bet with a high chance of a small win against one with a low chance of a large win (Lichtenstein & Slovic 1971). Asked to choose, most people took the safer bet; asked to name a selling price for each, the same people priced the long shot higher. A standing preference would give one ordering under both procedures. Slovic (1995) generalised: "normatively equivalent methods of elicitation often give rise to systematically different responses", which prompts his own question, "how can preferences be defined and in what sense do they exist?"

Tversky, Slovic and Kahneman (1990) traced the gamble reversal mainly to the overpricing of low-probability, high-payoff bets, a failure of procedure invariance they explained by scale compatibility: payoffs weigh more heavily when the response is itself a sum of money. Hsee and colleagues (1999) documented a gamble-free family: options judged side by side and options judged alone come out in different orders, because "difficult-to-evaluate attributes have a greater impact in JE than in SE" (joint rather than separate evaluation). The attribute missing from a lone evaluation leaves no felt gap in the verdict.

Grether and Plott (1979) are reported to have set out to explain the effect away and found that it survived real incentives and tighter controls. Repetition with feedback did more. In Cox and Grether's (1996) second-price auctions, reversals appeared in the first round with or without financial incentives, yet "after five repetitions of the auction, the subjects' bids are generally consistent with their choices"; an individual pricing task matched the auction in the first round but not the fifth. What the void claims survives either outcome: no single point-answer elicitation contains evidence of its own construction. Every demonstration needs at least two procedures, and a mind at one moment runs only one.

### Retrospective

The second face runs backward. The [decision-void](/voids/decision-void/)'s reconstruction face records that manipulated choices can shift later preferences; the question here is whether choosing shapes preference in general, and whether anyone can tell. For decades the free-choice paradigm (rate, choose, re-rate) seemed to show choosers coming to like what they picked. Chen and Risen (2010) proved that the paradigm registers such spreading "even if people's preferences remain perfectly stable", because ratings measure preference imperfectly and choices carry information the ratings missed. Sharot, Velasquez and Dolan (2010) replied with a blind choice "that could not be guided by preexisting preferences", and found that "preferences were altered after participants made a blind choice, but not after a computer dictated the decision". That was the authors' reply, and it did not settle the matter. Izuma and Murayama (2013) simulated how the classic design manufactures change out of measurement noise, concluded that the conventional paradigm should be avoided, and still found a small effect in a meta-analysis of the corrected designs; a later meta-analysis of 43 artifact-free studies (2,191 participants) found a larger one, d = 0.40, with no sign of publication bias (Enisman, Shpitzer and Kleiman 2021).

Outside observers now have artifact-free designs. The chooser has none: the preference and the memory of the choice arrive together, and the preference carries no timestamp. Strict [akrasia](/topics/akrasia-and-weakness-of-will/) is usually diagnosed from a later report of having judged the other option better; that report is itself an elicitation, so the ordering used to diagnose the lapse may have been built in the asking.

### Mark

The third face is what keeps the first two hidden. Ariely, Loewenstein and Prelec (2003) anchored valuations of familiar products and simple experiences on arbitrary numbers, sometimes derived from a social security number, and found later valuations coherent with real differences in quality and quantity, so that "the entire pattern of valuations can easily create an illusion of order, as if it is being generated by stable underlying preferences." This *coherent arbitrariness*, they report, did not decrease with experience of the good. The finding concerns anchoring, not the procedure reversals above. Set beside Cox and Grether, it shows experience disciplining one kind of construction and not another, so experience cannot certify which answers were found.

Ariely and colleagues describe an illusion for whoever reads the valuations. That it also deceives the valuer is the Map's extension: if coherence is the test a mind applies to its own preferences, the artefact passes it. Hansson and Grüne-Yanoff's *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry notes that answers to preference questions can be "interpreted as introspective reports", a reading that "treats answers as agents' privileged access to their own minds". The mark face is why that privilege looks intact from inside where the reversals show it failing.

## The Seam

Each face alone is a recognised finding; the void is their conjunction on one event, the forming of an ordering in the asking. Procedure-dependence without the mark would be easier to detect, since incoherent valuations expose themselves to anyone who compares them. The mark without procedure-dependence would be harmless, since the coherence would be real. The retrospective face closes the remaining exit: asking oneself again later, in a different frame, is contaminated by the first answer and the choice it produced. The faces have different proximate sources (scale compatibility and evaluability for elicitation, dissonance-style choice-induced change for the retrospective face, anchoring for the mark), which is the independence the seam test asks for at the proximate level. A single constructive-processing account (Payne, Bettman and Johnson 1992) could still unify them; until it is run against the three, the conjunction is unrefuted rather than shown.

On the strong construction view there may be nothing behind the procedures for a mind to fail to reach, and "what do I really prefer?" would have no answer. That edge would make the void unexplorable rather than occluded. The weaker readings below leave a fact there, hidden.

## Three Deflations

Each of three deflationary readings may be right, and each narrows the void.

**Discovered preference.** Plott's hypothesis, as Braga and Starmer (2005) characterise it, treats the anomalies as "errors in stated preference that can be expected to disappear as people become more experienced in relevant decision environments". Braga and Starmer explore the evidence for it, and Cox and Grether's convergence fits it. On this reading practice uncovers a stable ordering, and the void shrinks to unfamiliar, one-shot decisions, which include most of the large choices in a life.

**Graded calculation.** Warren, McGraw and Van Boven (2011) separate two senses of *constructed*. If it means context-sensitivity, "preferences are always constructed because context influences nearly every aspect of the judgment and choice process"; if it means calculation during the choice, "the more important question is to what degree preferences are calculated." Construction becomes a matter of degree.

**Adaptive cue-sensitivity.** McKenzie and colleagues (2018) argue that many apparent biases reflect adaptive sensitivity to contextual cues: facing unfamiliar options, people reasonably treat the context as information, so construction can be rational.

None of the three makes construction visible. An adaptively cue-sensitive preference is not experienced as cue-sensitive; a partly calculated one is not experienced as partly calculated; and a preference discovered through repetition arrives with the same felt authority as the reversed answer it replaced. The deflations reclassify the construction and can shrink its scope; the access claim stands under all of them.

## Phenomenology of the Edge

Approaching the void feels like nothing in particular. Asked to choose, an answer arrives; asked to price, an answer arrives; each has the settledness of a verdict when the format demands a point answer, though Butler and Loomes (2007) measured the imprecision surrounding such answers and found that it can account for reversals of both forms. The missing comparison is unavailable because putting one question to oneself two ways lets the first answer contaminate the second. Shown an inconsistency, one naturally asks which answer was the mistake; the assumption that one was the true reading survives the evidence that neither need have been.

## Approaches to the Edge

**Direct.** None. Introspecting harder produces another elicitation rather than a view of the ordering behind elicitations.

**Indirect.** Re-asking oneself later in another frame maps one's reversal pattern, contaminated by the retrospective face. Recording the frame with the preference would let later reversals show as procedure differences rather than changes of mind. The shape of reversals (scale compatibility, evaluability) characterises the process by what moves it, [apophatic-cartography](/voids/apophatic-cartography/) in empirical form.

**[What AI might see](/voids/non-human-minds-as-void-explorers/).** Yin, Vardi and Choudhary (2026) find strong order effects in large language models choosing among alternatives, classify pairwise preferences as robust, fragile or indifferent, and show that "order effects can lead models to select strictly inferior options." They treat these effects as distortions of underlying preferences that mitigation can recover, and report failure modes not documented in humans, so the model is no simple copy of the human case. The asymmetry lies in access: a model in a fixed state can be re-asked under every permutation of order and frame and its construction mapped from outside, while a human cannot be reset between askings.

## Relation to Site Perspective

Under the Map's rule that a tenet removing a defeater does not upgrade a void's evidence ([P-V2](/positions/voids-as-evidence/#p-v2)), what follows is a reading dualism offers of this void, not support the void gives to dualism.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)** is the tenet most engaged. The [decision-void](/voids/decision-void/) treats the point where weighing becomes choice as the most natural candidate site for any conscious contribution, and the [causal-interface](/voids/causal-interface/) void records why its mechanism stays hidden. The preference void asks what selection selects *from*. If the ordering is assembled during the choosing, two readings are available:

1. *Construction as selection.* The Map speculates that valuation processes prepare several candidate weightings or orderings, perhaps the ones different procedures would elicit, and that the conscious contribution, if there is one, is which of them becomes actual in this choice. In the terms of the [selection-only-channel](/concepts/selection-only-channel/), mind contributes nothing to the candidate set itself; it settles which prepared member governs the act. This moves the site from the commitment to the ordering the commitment expresses.
2. *Construction as reception.* Physical valuation processes complete the ordering, and consciousness receives it with the character of a report. Any contribution lies downstream.

Both readings are compatible with the tenet, and neither discriminates it from its rivals, since a fully physical construction fits the data as well. The void is that the two cannot be separated from the chooser's seat, because every attempt to find out is another elicitation. The quantum-interface argument shows such a contribution to be [available, not actual](/tenets/#tenet-3-standing), and this void supplies no evidence that it is actual.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)** connects through revealed preference, the simplest available model of choice, whose simplicity hid a construction step that took two procedures to expose. The discovered-preference hypothesis is also a simplicity-preserving move and may be right; the tenet claims only that simplicity is no reliable guide here.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)** connects through the gap between preference as mental state and preference as choice pattern. If the construction literature is right, an event rather than a state fills that gap, a datum dualism must explain and one that confirms nothing. **[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction)** meets the void as an open question: whether selection among prepared orderings could respect the tenet's empirical constraints (no detectable energy injection, no Born-statistics violation, no conservation-law violation) is untested here. **[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)** is not engaged: the procedure-dependence is classical, and an Everettian branch also carries one ordering.
