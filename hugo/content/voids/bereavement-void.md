When someone close dies, the survivor usually believes it soon. What comes slowly is what the belief should bring with it: an end to expecting the person, to turning to tell them something, to sensing them in the house. Later the grief that registered the loss fades, while the death and the person's importance stay as they were. The bereavement void is the pair of limits this leaves, one at each end of mourning. Early, Thomas Fuchs's phenomenology describes "a cognitive-affective conflict between two experienced realities" (2018, p. 43), in which the dead person is known to be gone and still expected. Late, Berislav Marušić's 2018 essay argues that the reason for grief has not changed, so that reason cannot justify the fading, and Dan Moller (2007) argues that recovery leaves the survivor less able to register in full what was lost. The full weight of another person's death is hard to hold at either end.

Both faces are bounded. On the first, acceptance was the most endorsed of five interview items from the first month in a cohort of 233 bereaved people (Maciejewski et al. 2007), so what lags is expectation and habit while explicit belief is largely in place. On the second, how many bereaved people count as resilient depends on how the data are modelled (Infurna & Luthar 2016), and Fuchs holds that grief resolves by re-establishing the relation to the dead person, which would carry the loss forward in another form ([What Limits Each Face](#what-limits-each-face)). Whether mourning contains a stretch in which the fact has landed and the feeling is still at strength is a question this article raises and does not answer ([An Interval?](#an-interval)). In the terms of [the three kinds of void](/voids/three-kinds-of-void/), the first face is unexplorable only in a weak, time-bound sense and the second is occluded on the adaptive reading. Attachment learning predicts the first and hedonic adaptation the second, so nothing here is evidence for dualism, and sensed presence of the dead is not offered as evidence that anyone survives.

## The Lag

Fuchs describes the first shock in these words: "The event cannot be grasped; it has already seized the person before she can realize what it actually means" (p. 45). He adds that "That is not true!" is a frequent first reaction, which he reads as anticipating a later struggle between wish and reality. The passage concerns what the death means and is compatible with knowing that it happened. He locates the core of grief in "a conflict between a presentifying and a 'de-presentifying' intention" (p. 43). In grief "the other is lost as a real person, while he is still present in my inmost self-experience" (p. 49), and "the mourner oscillates between the intruding impression of the deceased's presence and the reality of his irrevocable absence" (p. 54). Fuchs compares bereavement to amputation and sensed presence to a [phantom limb](/topics/phantom-limb-phenomena/), with one stated difference: the phantom is sharply located and the dead person's presence is vague. He adds that grief varies by person and culture and that his analysis is not normative.

A survivor who begins to dial the dead person's number, or sets out a second cup, acts on an expectation that runs against a sincerely held belief, the structure the Map discusses under [alief-and-acting-against-belief](/topics/alief-and-acting-against-belief/).

O'Connor and Seeley (2022) offer a neuroscientific account of the same gap, worded as a hypothesis: "We hypothesize that the neural architecture of the bond supports the belief (or semantic knowledge) that the other persists, despite any sensory evidence to the contrary." After a death, "prediction error is experienced until the updated semantic knowledge can be learned", so that "grieving can be considered a form of learning, requiring time and feedback". The vocabulary is that of [predictive-processing](/concepts/predictive-processing/). The proposal rests on prairie-vole pair-bond neuroscience, human neuroimaging of self-other overlap and attachment theory, and the step from voles to a human expectation that the other persists is the authors' inference.

## Sensed Presence

A much-studied sign of the lag is the sensed or perceived presence of the dead person. W. D. Rees (1971) interviewed 293 widowed people in mid-Wales (227 widows and 66 widowers); his abstract mentions no comparison group and reports: "Almost half the people interviewed had hallucinations or illusions of the dead spouse." As tabulated by Kamp et al. (2020), his figures by modality were a sense of presence in 39.2%, visual experiences in 14.0%, auditory in 13.3% and tactile in 2.7%. Olson et al. (1985) interviewed widowed residents of two nursing homes, where 28 of 46 widows (61%) reported such experiences. Grimby (1993) assessed 50 people in their early seventies during the first year after a spouse's death; half felt the presence of the deceased and about one third reported seeing, hearing and talking to them.

Two reviews give ranges, and no single figure is warranted. Castelnovo et al. (2015) report prevalence among widowed subjects "ranging from 30% to 60%" and caution that "all studies in the bereaved population had a very small number of subjects". Kamp et al. (2020), an integrative review with no pooled estimate, write that estimates of having at least one such experience "range from 47% to 82% across several studies." The ranges are not directly comparable, since the reviews count different experiences and different samples, and Kamp et al. note that "there is currently no validated measure of SED", their abbreviation for sensory and quasi-sensory experiences of the deceased.

Three features bear on the void. The older word "hallucination" misdescribes the usual case: Matthew Ratcliffe (2021) observes that "the most common form of experience is a non-specific sense or feeling of presence", with no sensory content. The experience coexists with knowledge of the death, since the research Kamp et al. survey "suggests preserved reality testing in SED, in the sense that the experiencer knows the deceased has died". And it went largely unreported. Rees reports that the experiences had rarely been disclosed, even to close friends or relatives, and Olson et al. report that 54% of the widows had never discussed them before the study.

None of these authors claims that the dead are present, and several do not treat the experience as a failure of comprehension. Rees's abstract calls them "normal and helpful accompaniments of widowhood." Ratcliffe proposes that at least some such experiences consist in "a dynamic and non-localized experience of significant and salient possibilities", a sense of still relating to a particular person. On that reading the survivor understands the situation and relates to the dead person in a new way.

## The Lapse

Marušić (2018) opens with his own case: "My grief has passed, almost entirely. But my mother's death has not been undone." His puzzle concerns reasons. Grief responds to a reason, the reason does not change, and yet grieving less does not seem mistaken. Anticipation, he says, illuminates the puzzle: "we anticipate the end of grief as a failure to adequately respond to our reasons". The usual explanations, such as the need to get on with life, explain the fading without justifying it, because "the diminution of grief does not seem to be primarily a response to a change in the significance or value of the loss but is, rather, occasioned by the needs of the griever". He examines four responses and accepts none: "I will argue that the puzzle eludes a solution, but that there are principled reasons for why that is so." He does not say that grief ought to last. That is the 2018 conclusion; his later writing on the subject was not consulted for this article and may qualify it.

Moller (2007) reaches a neighbouring worry from the empirical literature on resilience after a spouse's death, including Bonanno et al. (2002), and from the immune-neglect literature the Map discusses under [miswanting](/concepts/miswanting/). He distinguishes "the superficial discrimination of some fact" from what is meant "by saying that someone has really 'absorbed' or 'taken in'" what has happened (p. 311), and argues that resilience costs the second: "Our 'emotional immune system,' while promoting our interests by allowing us to continue functioning in the face of trauma, also renders us unable to take in and register fully the significance of our losses" (p. 310). "In a sense, resilience is a form of benign—or at least adaptive—blindness" (p. 312). Moller does not conclude that resilience is bad on balance or that people should grieve longer. He rejects the reply that our degree of resilience is a sensible mean between feeling nothing and never recovering, and states a limited aim: "Our embrace of the Adaptive Theory should be an uneasy one at best" (p. 313).

Moller's argument assumes that grief registers the size of a loss, so that losing the grief means losing sight of something real. The [emotional-epistemology-void](/voids/emotional-epistemology-void/) holds that assumption open: the conviction that grief reveals something true has great force, and that force cannot be validated from inside. If grief only feels like a measure, the lapse costs no knowledge. If it is one, the lapse resembles the [sour-grapes-void](/voids/sour-grapes-void/), an adaptation that leaves no mark on its output, and the recovered survivor stands to earlier grief as the [cross-state-void](/voids/cross-state-void/) describes for any state represented from outside it.

## What Limits Each Face

**The lag is a lag in expectation, with belief largely intact.** In the Yale Bereavement Study, a longitudinal cohort of "233 bereaved individuals living in Connecticut" rated from 1 to 24 months after a death from natural causes, the abstract reports: "Counter to stage theory, disbelief was not the initial, dominant grief indicator. Acceptance was the most frequently endorsed item and yearning was the dominant negative grief indicator from 1 to 24 months postloss" (Maciejewski et al. 2007). Together with the preserved reality testing above, this counts against describing the bereaved as unable to believe the death. The study's reach is limited: nobody was interviewed within a month of the death, people meeting criteria for complicated grief were excluded, and once rescaled, disbelief still fell from a high at one month. Attachment-based "searching" accounts also cover only part of sensed presence: Kamp et al. note that "the theory does not account for SED occurring later", sometimes years on. The three primary studies of sensed presence cited here sampled widowed people, one in nursing homes and one in their early seventies.

**The lapse rests on a contested premise.** Bonanno et al. (2002) followed 205 people from before a spouse's death to 18 months after it and reported in their abstract: "Common grief was relatively infrequent, and the resilient pattern most frequent." Infurna and Luthar (2016) re-analysed 28 annual waves of a German panel survey and showed how far such results depend on modelling. Under earlier studies' specifications the resilient class after spousal loss was largest, "with 75% of the population likely to belong to this sub-group". With slope variance estimated, "only 27% of the sample was likely to belong to a resilient trajectory", and a further model put 36% in a resilient class and 64% in a recovery class. In both of these analyses the authors found that a single class fitted the spousal-loss data best, and they examined the multi-class solutions for comparison with earlier work. Their outcome was yearly life satisfaction, and grief was not measured. The re-analysis leaves the fading itself standing: its recovery class dips and returns. What it disputes is how many people never dip.

**Fading may be accurate.** Fuchs criticises Freud's detachment model and holds that grief resolves through identification, recollection and symbolisation, so that the relation to the dead person is re-established in a new form. If that is right, the weight of the loss is carried forward and the recovered survivor is not blind to it.

## An Interval?

Together the two faces raise a question. Grief is at full strength early, when by Fuchs's account what the death means has not yet been realised. The meaning has been absorbed later, when grief has typically diminished. Is there a period in which both are held together? The pairing is this article's synthesis. No source read for it states the conjunction, six of the thirteen were read only in abstract, and Fuchs's account of re-integration suggests the answer may be yes. The article does not claim that the full weight of a loss can never be held.

A diary or experience-sampling design could follow three things separately across the first two years: assent to the death, residual expectation, and felt grief. The research behind this article did not search for such studies, so nothing is claimed here about whether one exists. Two philosophical probes are available now: Marušić asks how the end of grief looks from inside grief, and Moller imagines people who feel nothing at a spouse's death and asks what they lack.

## What AI Might See

A system whose model of the world updated in one step on being told of a death would show no lag. Whether it would register the loss is the question Moller's argument raises, since on his view holding the belief is not enough. This is speculation, and none of the sources addresses artificial minds.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The bereavement void is framework-independent, and none of the Map's [tenets](/tenets/) is supported by it.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism).** The datum has two parts. Assent to a death and the expectations that should follow from it come apart for a time, and grief diminishes while its object stays fixed. A physicalist account predicts both. Slow retraining of a learned prediction that the attached person persists, which is what O'Connor and Seeley propose, predicts the lag. Hedonic adaptation, which Moller calls an "emotional immune system" and treats as adaptive, predicts the lapse. The datum therefore does not discriminate between dualism and physicalism, and none of the authors cited here argues for dualism or should be read as lending it support. Sensed presence is a report about the survivor's experience, and the Map draws no inference from it about whether anyone is there. The Map does hold, in [emotion-and-dualism](/topics/emotion-and-dualism/), that grief's qualitative depth resists functional analysis. That is a commitment the Map brings to this material, and nothing in the material backs it. As a catalogue entry the void adds no independent weight either: the voids register has [suspended the cumulative step](/positions/voids-as-evidence/#p-v3) by which the catalogue's findings would carry any.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)** is the nearest tenet, in a modest sense. "She knows he is dead" reads as one fact and covers at least three things that come apart: assent to the fact, the expectations that should follow from it, and a valuation of what was lost. This illustrates the tenet and is not evidence for it, since a physicalist psychology draws the same distinctions.

**[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)** has a thematic link only: the loss is felt as the loss of this particular person, and [the beloved void](/voids/beloved-void/) examines whether that felt irreplaceability survives scrutiny. **Bidirectional Interaction** and **Minimal Quantum Interaction** are not engaged. Whether grief's felt character does causal work is a question the Map answers elsewhere and on other grounds; the findings above are silent on it. Listed in the [voids](/voids/) index.
