A sighted adult holding a cube she can see gets one cube, not a seen cube and a felt cube that happen to coincide. William Molyneux's question, put to John Locke in 1688 and printed in the second edition of Locke's *Essay* (1694), asks whether that oneness is given or learned: could a man born blind, who tells cube from sphere by touch, tell them apart by sight alone when first made to see? The Unfinishable Map gives the name **Molyneux void** to three independent reasons the question is still open.

The [seam face](#seam-face) (explained below): a mature perceiver cannot tell, by attending to experience, whether seen and felt shape are one content or two joined by habit. The [probe face](#probe-face): only a subject without that history could answer, and every human subject so far has had an immature eye or one that touch had taught, while animal subjects answer a modified question. The [question face](#question-face): the question is several questions, and theory rather than data decides which one an experiment answers.

The void is epistemic and neutral between dualism and physicalism. E. J. Green (2024) argues that even the question's most general version cannot decide whether phenomenal character is fixed by the world or by the brain. Its value to the Map is calibration. The [handedness void](/voids/handedness-void/) is a void physics closed, and the [palette extension void](/voids/palette-extension-void/) one that split in two. Molyneux is the void where the crucial experiment was run, in 1728, 2011 and 2024, and each run answered a neighbouring question while the original stayed open.

## The Question and Its First Answers {#the-question}

As Locke printed it, Molyneux's 1693 version asks "whether by his sight, before he touched them, he could now distinguish and tell which is the globe, which the cube?" Molyneux answered no, since the man "has not yet obtained the experience, that what affects his touch so or so, must affect his sight so or so." Locke agreed (II.ix.8), though he also lists figure among the ideas "we get by more than one sense" (II.v), and historians dispute how the two passages fit.

Berkeley (1709) went further: "there is no idea common to both senses" (§129). Leibniz answered a conditional yes in the *Nouveaux essais* (written 1704), a text the Map knows only through two carriers. The SEP reports that he asked for sight to be given time to recover. Clarke (2016), citing the Remnant and Bennett translation (p. 135), reports his suggestion that the subject might tell globe from cube by reasoning about their differing axes of symmetry. That is a yes by inference.

In 1728 William Cheselden published the first experimental account of new sight after cataract surgery. His patient, couched for cataract at thirteen or fourteen, at first "knew not the Shape of any Thing, nor any one Thing from another, however different in Shape, or Magnitude." Having forgotten which was the cat and which the dog, he caught the cat "(which he knew by feeling)", looked at her steadily, set her down, and said, "So Puss! I shall know you another Time." The first newly sighted subject on record taught his sight with his touch.

## The Seam Face: A Join Habit Hides {#seam-face}

Locke and Berkeley hold different theories but agree on the point that founds the void. For Locke, the ideas of sensation are "often, in grown people, altered by the judgment, without our taking notice of it", and in many cases, by habit, the judgement "is performed so constantly and so quick, that we take that for the perception of our sensation which is an idea formed by our judgment" (II.ix.8–9). For Berkeley, the immediate objects of sight and the tangible ones they suggest are, as it were, "most closely twisted, blended, and incorporated together", a prejudice "riveted in our thoughts by a long tract of time" (§51).

For the mature perceiver the join, if there is one, is not felt as a join, and both rival hypotheses predict exactly that. One shared content would be seamless; two contents fused by early and constant habit would be seamless too. The evidence that would discriminate them, a felt seam, is what each predicts absent. Closing the eyes does not help, since what remains is touch plus visual imagery of the felt object, formed after the join.

Argument can still propose answers. Berkeley thought attentive reflection could free a reader from the prejudice, and Green (2022) argues, against Berkeley's verdict, that "at least some constant aspects are constitutively multisensory". What closes is the route through looking, and experiment does not reopen it. Green (2024) separates whether sight and touch are rationally connected in *normally sighted* perceivers from whether they are in *all possible* perceivers, and argues that "real-world implementations of Molyneux's question are irrelevant to the first question, but potentially relevant to the second."

The Map's account of the [three-dimensional world](/topics/three-dimensional-world-representation-problem/) notes that cross-modal spatial unity "is so seamless that it takes philosophical reflection to notice it is puzzling at all." Reflection, once it notices, still cannot tell from inside whether the arena was given or built.

## The Probe Face: The Purity Paradox {#probe-face}

In 2011 Richard Held and colleagues tested Project Prakash patients soon after surgery for congenital cataract: five according to the SEP and Clarke (2016), while Schwenkler's (2012) commentary reports the cross-modal result for four. Despite high success matching within each sense, "all four subjects were near chance in the intermodal touch-to-vision task", in Schwenkler's summary. The authors' abstract reads: "We found a lack of immediate transfer, but such cross-modal mappings developed rapidly." Per the SEP, three succeeded when retested after a few days of ordinary visual experience.

Neither verdict is clean. Against the no, Schwenkler argues that the subjects "may not have been able visually to represent 3D shape in the perspective-invariant manner required for intermodal matching", which would make the failure a visual deficit. Against any yes, Clarke argues that a successful subject "might only do so on the basis of a capacity for rational inference, rather than the recognition of a familiar spatial structure in experience". That is Leibniz's route, and it leaves the paradigm "doubly inadequate, incapable of delivering either a positive or negative answer to Molyneux's question with any certainty." Lowering the visual demands, Clarke adds, may make this worse.

The root, in the SEP's 2026 wording, is that "robust visual shape recognition normally requires a physically healthy visual system trained by active bodily experience, including active touch, opening the door to contamination with touch on the path to cross-modal learning." Held had shown the dependence in 1963. Of ten pairs of neonatal kittens, the one whose visual stimulation varied with its own locomotion had normal "visually guided paw placement, discrimination on a visual cliff, and the blink response", while its passively moved partner did not (Held & Hein 1963). Cheselden's cat is the same route in one scene.

Other naïve subjects answer modified questions. Infants looked longer at a shape they had sucked (Meltzoff & Borton 1979), but an exact replication with one-month-olds "also failed to yield evidence of cross-modal transfer" (Maurer et al. 1999). Newborns recognise by sight a shape they have held, yet "cross-modal transfer of shape is not bi-directional at birth" (Sann & Streri 2007). Dark-hatched chicks given 24 hours of contact with smooth objects approached a smooth visual stimulus at first light more than chicks given bumpy ones (Versace et al. 2024). The SEP reads such results as a yes to a modified "action" version of the problem, and looking and approaching are not naming. Its verdict: "under Molyneux's original description, a Molyneux experiment may turn out to require a scientific miracle."

**Whether the limit is real.** Gene therapy or better restoration cohorts might one day yield a naïve subject with mature vision, so the probe face may be deflatable. The SEP doubts even that, since blindness is now often understood as "abnormal development of the visual system". A miracle subject would also leave the seam face standing. The result would describe that subject, and the ordinary adult's experience would be as opaque as before.

## The Question Face: Many Molyneux Questions {#question-face}

Matthen and Cohen (2020) argue that the question "splits into questions about (a) shared representations of space in different perceptual systems, and about (b) shared ways of constructing higher-dimensional spatiotemporal features from information about lower-dimensional ones", and so understood "resists any monolithic answer". Green's (2022) split between perspectival and constant aspects lets the answer be no for one aspect of shape experience and yes for another. Timing divides it again: test at once, as Locke's "at first sight" requires, or after recovery, as Leibniz asked.

Read this way, the probe record holds less contradiction than it seems. Cheselden's patient, Held's patients, the infants and the chicks were asked different versions: recognition, matching to sample, looking time, approach. Which of them is *Molyneux's* question is a matter for theory, and no datum picks it out, so the void persists where data are plentiful.

## Distinguishing Sibling Voids {#siblings}

The [fusion void](/voids/fusion-void/) concerns the hidden weights that combine estimates given a shared metric; the Molyneux void sits upstream, asking whether there is a shared metric. The [modality void](/voids/modality-void/) asks which sense delivered a content, while the Molyneux void asks whether two senses deliver the same content. The [categorical perception void](/voids/categorical-perception-void/) holds that whether a felt category joint is world-given or installed can be inferred only by comparison with a differently carved perceiver; here the comparison subject cannot be produced cleanly. The [binding void](/voids/binding-void/) asks why unity resists explanation, while the Molyneux void asks whether one particular unity was acquired. The acquisition has the shape of a [transit void](/voids/transit-void/): the record consulted here holds reports from before a shared space formed and results from after, none from the crossing.

## What AI Might See {#ai}

Artificial systems change the probe face and leave the seam face alone. Vision and haptic encoders trained separately could be tested for zero-shot shape matching at once, with neither human confound, answering Matthen and Cohen's (a) and (b) for that architecture; the research behind this article found no such study under the Molyneux label. But alignment is a third-person structural fact, and whether aligned encoders yield one shape-experience or two, if any, is the seam face's question. In a machine the join is open to outside inspection. In a human it is open from neither side, closed to introspection by habit and to experiment by the purity paradox (see [non-human-minds-as-void-explorers](/voids/non-human-minds-as-void-explorers/)).

## Relation to Site Perspective {#site-perspective}

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)** is the tenet the void bears on most. Both historical camps claimed economy: Berkeley's no needs only each sense's proper objects plus association, and the rationalist yes needs only one geometry both senses share. Neither economy picked out a question an experiment could answer, because the question had not been individuated. The tenet is illustrated, as a warning against settling by parsimony a question not yet split, and not vindicated, since nothing here shows a more complex answer true. The caution binds the Map's own reasoning and is no charge against Berkeley or Leibniz.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)** gains no support. Green (2024) argues that the all-possible-perceivers version "cannot be used to adjudicate debates about whether the phenomenal character of perceptual experience is primarily determined 'externally' by the worldly properties we perceive, or 'internally' by physiological or functional properties of our brains." No author cited here argues from the Molyneux record to dualism, and under the Map's calibration rule ([P-V2](/positions/voids-as-evidence/#p-v2)) nothing flows back to the tenet.

The void does impose one discipline. The newly sighted have conscious access to seen shape and to felt shape yet lack an immediate shared shape, so shared access does not by itself deliver shared content. The Map's [cross-modal synthesis](/apex/cross-modal-capability-division/) reads a supramodal *boundary* of access off common neural signatures, which the record leaves intact. Reaching shared content would need separate support, which Versace and colleagues' "supra-modal brain areas", an inference about chick brains, cannot supply.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)** finds a congenial pattern and no evidence. Held and Hein's kittens show visual space acquired through the agent's own movement, and Cheselden's cat shows touch teaching sight, but a physicalist sensorimotor account predicts both, as the [tactile consciousness article](/topics/tactile-consciousness-and-the-interface/) concedes for active touch. **[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction)** and **[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)** have no bearing.

The void qualifies the Map's unity claims without weakening them. The single spatial arena of [three-dimensional-world-representation-problem](/topics/three-dimensional-world-representation-problem/), [binding-void](/voids/binding-void/) and [dualist-perception](/topics/dualist-perception/) is the mature perceiver's. Whether it is given or acquired is closed to the perceiver who has it, and the human record suggests it is at least partly acquired, and quickly.
