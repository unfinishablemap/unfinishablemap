The **rollback argument** holds that if a choice is undetermined, then replaying the universe many times from exactly the same prior state would yield different choices on different replays, in proportions that settle towards a ratio, so that which choice occurs on any one occasion is a matter of chance. Peter van Inwagen (2000) ran the argument with agent causation explicitly granted and concluded that "whether or not Alice's acts are the results of agent-causation—it is a mere matter of chance whether she will tell the truth or lie" (p. 17). "Rollback" is a later label; van Inwagen's own word is "replays". It is the [luck objection](/concepts/agent-causation/#the-luck-objection) in the form aimed at agent-causal libertarianism, the view that an agent, rather than any event, settles which open option becomes actual.

The Unfinishable Map's verdict comes first, and it holds only inside the Map's framework. Every agent-causalist reply the Map has found ends in the same primitive, the agent's exercise of her power, with nothing more basic explaining why it went this way rather than that. Where the literature adds structure, it adds a probability profile over the agent's exercises, which is the replay's own premise. On the Map's own reading, which no cited author makes, the replay lands on the probabilities the Map's Born-preserving default leaves unfixed, and both branches of the resulting [probability fork](#probability-fork) (explained below) end at a companion law the Map has not written. Under that law the token-level settling stays primitive: the Map can say *who* settles an outcome, and not why the settling went one way.

## The Argument {#the-argument}

Van Inwagen grants agent causation "for the sake of argument" and presents an argument "that free will and indeterminism are incompatible even if our acts or their causal antecedents are products of agent causation" (2000: 1), so that "the concept of agent causation is entirely irrelevant to the problem of free will" (p. 11).

Alice tells the truth in an undetermined choice. God causes the universe "to revert to precisely its state" a minute earlier (p. 14) and lets it run again, many times. If she tells the truth in about half the replays, what could we believe about the next one except that each outcome "has an objective, 'ground-floor' probability of 0.5—and there's nothing more to be said" (p. 15)? Rerun the case with Alice agent-causing the brain events behind whichever act she performs, and the conclusion quoted above follows either way (pp. 16–17). Van Inwagen believes "there is something wrong with this argument", but holds that "its flaw is not that it overlooks the possibility that my actions have their root in agent-causation" (p. 18).

The argument needs a probability as well as openness, and van Inwagen infers it from the frequencies the replays converge on.

**Names.** Van Inwagen calls the replay "the plausible, intuitive version of the Mind Argument" (p. 16), his own label for an argument "which I have called the Mind Argument because it has appeared so frequently in the pages of *Mind*" (p. 10). John Martin Fischer's review of van Inwagen's *Essay on Free Will* (1983) calls the argument "associated with Hobart" (Fischer 1988: 402), after R. E. Hobart's 1934 *Mind* paper. "Rollback" came later: Lara Buchak writes of "the 'rollback argument,' pioneered by Peter van Inwagen" (2013), and the Stanford Encyclopedia groups "the 'Mind', 'Rollback', or 'Luck' argument" as one family (O'Connor and Franklin 2022, §3.1).

**The cross-world form.** The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on nondeterministic theories states the objection across worlds. Leo agent-causes a decision to tell the truth; in a world exactly like ours until then, he agent-causes a decision to lie. "Nothing about the world prior to the moment of the agent-causing accounts for the difference between Leo's causing one decision and his causing the other. This difference, then, is just a matter of luck" (Clarke, Capes and Swenson 2021, §3.2). Alfred Mele argues that present luck, "luck at the time of action", "poses an as yet unresolved problem for libertarianism" (2006, ch. 3). The akratic case is treated in [the divided-will article](/topics/the-divided-will/#luck-objection).

## Three Families of Reply {#reply-families}

László Bernáth and János Tőzsér (2020) sort the replies into three families, and this article follows their taxonomy.

**Accept the chance and call it harmless.** Event-causal libertarians such as Robert Kane, Mark Balaguer and Christopher Evan Franklin deny that the chance undermines control; Franklin (2011) considers "four formulations of the luck argument and find[s] them all wanting". The contrastive explanation these replies use, in which probability-raising reasons account for a choice, is an event-causal resource that cannot distinguish the agent-causal view, and the Map's own luck reply does not rest on it ([acting for reasons](/topics/agent-causation-and-acting-for-reasons/)).

**Deny that free choices have robust probabilities.** Carl Ginet, E. J. Lowe and Thomas Pink take this line. Buchak argues that the replay's premise about chances "is totally unjustified" (2013), and Leigh Vicens (2016) finds no reason to posit "particular objective probabilities of our free choices". With no fact about what proportion of replays would go each way, the replay has nothing to converge on.

**Keep robust probabilities and deny that they make the act chancy.** Timothy O'Connor (2000), Randolph Clarke (2003) and Meghan Griffith (2005) belong here. The probability "simply measures relative likelihood" (O'Connor 2011: 326–27, as quoted in Bernáth and Tőzsér 2020), and control "is not exercised prior to, but at the time of the choice—in the very act of bringing it about" (O'Connor and Franklin 2022, §3.1). Applied to Leo, the difference between the worlds "is a matter of how Leo exercises his free will", a reply the entry states only conditionally (Clarke, Capes and Swenson 2021, §3.2). Griffith argues that "the problem is not fundamentally a problem of explanation" (2010: 43), and Babakhanian (2025) reads her 2005 reply (p. 268) as "adopting the view that free acts could be inexplicable".

The Map enlists none of these defenders for dualism. O'Connor argues that agent-causal theories "do not necessarily require substance dualism, but may require causal powers and properties that are ontologically emergent" (2011, abstract). Clarke answers van Inwagen in a chapter that finds his integrated agent-causal view adequate only "Assuming that all it requires is possible" (2003, ch. 9), and ends his 2011 chapter "on a skeptical note about the viability of libertarian accounts of free will generally". Van Inwagen holds that "free will undeniably exists" (2000: 1) and argues against the agent-causalist's reply.

## Where Every Reply Ends {#where-replies-end}

At the **token** level, the question is why this exercise of the power went to A rather than B, given everything before it. Every agent-causal reply above ends there in a primitive: an exercise "at the time of the choice", a matter of "how Leo exercises his free will", acts that "could be inexplicable". At the **type** level, the question is what constrains the power's exercises across like circumstances, and here the literature does answer: O'Connor's objective probabilities, or Clarke's integrated view, in which the agent's reasons co-cause the choice with the agent.

That answer cannot dissolve the objection, because a theory that supplies objective probabilities over the agent's exercises supplies the replay's own premise. The second family blocks the replay precisely by refusing it.

Joel Archer's (2022) grounding reply and the bodies of Clarke (2005) and Franklin (2011) were not reached, so "every reply" means every reply the Map has found.

## The Probability Fork {#probability-fork}

The literature's fork is to deny robust probabilities and block the replay, or to keep them and reconcile them with the agent's control. Bernáth and Tőzsér argue that reconciliation fails: "metaphysically robust probabilities are incompatible with a kind of control which can ensure that free actions are not a matter of chance" (2020, abstract). If the agent's control settles each token, it settles the pattern, and the probabilities have nothing left to explain. On their argument O'Connor's division of labour between agent and probabilities is unavailable, and a libertarian who wants that control should deny robust probabilities.

Their target is one combination; they leave the probability-denying alternative open and do not refute agent causation. The field treats the question as open: why such probabilities "might present special problems beyond those posed by the absence of determinism has been insufficiently explored to date" (O'Connor and Franklin 2022, §3.1).

## The Map's Reading: One Debt {#one-debt}

This section is the Map's own analysis, which no source above makes, and it takes the Map's tenets as premises.

**The grain.** On the Map's default reading of [Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction), conscious selection leaves the Born statistics intact, and the register's wording adds that "only conditionals on the conscious state itself are free" ([P-Q2](/positions/quantum-interface/#p-q2)). The Map has not settled the ensemble over which Born-preservation is asserted, and nothing here depends on settling it: on each reading in play, physics leaves unfixed the probability of an outcome given the conscious state. Van Inwagen's God restores "precisely" the prior state, which for a Map-style selection includes the conscious state, so the replays converge, if at all, on that conditional probability. Physics fixes only the public statistics, the average of those conditionals over the conscious states that actually occur.

**The fork at that grain**, stated symmetrically:

- **If the conditionals are objective propensities**, the Map grants van Inwagen's premise at that grain and meets Bernáth and Tőzsér's objection. This is the reading the Map needs if selection is ever to depart from the public statistics under some conditioning, the first horn of [the Born-preservation trilemma](/apex/born-preserving-causal-efficacy/).
- **If there are no such propensities**, the Map blocks the replay as Buchak and Vicens would. The Born-exact public statistics must then hold without being a mixture of conditional chances: across many agents and occasions, propensity-free settlings would match the Born weights with nothing explaining the match. That is Derk Pereboom's wild-coincidences objection (Pereboom 2001, ch. 3; 2014) applied to the Born weights, a cost the Map records as undischarged ([hard incompatibilism](/topics/the-manipulation-argument-and-hard-incompatibilism/)).

**Both branches end at one law.** The Map's own analysis concludes that exact Born-preservation "needs a companion law over the distribution of conscious reasons, and the Map has never written one" ([Born-preserving causal efficacy](/apex/born-preserving-causal-efficacy/)). On the first branch that law would be a propensity profile over conscious states, constrained to keep the public statistics Born-exact; on the second, a law of conformity making the match lawful rather than coincidental. On the Map's reading, then, the modal structure its luck reply owes, the wild-coincidence cost and the companion-law debt are one unwritten law.

Writing that law would meet the demand for type-level structure and leave the token untouched. If Bernáth and Tőzsér are right, it would sharpen the tension, since the more the law explains the distribution, the less remains for the settling to explain. [The integrated reading of the Map's selector](/topics/agent-causation-and-acting-for-reasons/#costs-integrated) meets the same trade from the side of reasons.

None of this concedes epiphenomenalism. On both branches the agent settles each token outcome; what is owed is a law over the propensities or the conformity of those settlings. Whether one law can be Born-preserving and an O'Connor-style propensity profile without falling to Bernáth and Tőzsér's objection is the open question this analysis poses and does not settle.

## Bedrock at the Token {#bedrock}

On the Map's analysis, the first branch leaves the agent's settling and a primitive chancy transition supporting the same counterfactuals about replays: the same frequencies, and the same unpredictable next replay. On the second, the settling has no frequency profile at all, which supports fewer counterfactuals. What agent causation claims beyond either is categorical; the Map's register calls the difference "metaphysical (which thing is the causal source)" ([P-A2](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a2)).

No reply the Map has found turns that categorical difference into a counterfactual one. Explanation stops there in the Map's account, and the Map says so plainly: it answers who settles the outcome, and has no further answer to why the settling went one way. [The Map's luck reply](/concepts/quantum-indeterminacy-free-will/) defends that stopping point as a legitimate terminus within its framework, without claiming that the quantum interface removes it.

Three developments would change the verdict: a reply grounding the token without restoring determinism (Archer 2022 is the unread candidate, so this is unresolved rather than tested); a demonstration that the agent's control and robust probabilities coexist (O'Connor's 2016 reply to Vicens is unread, and Bernáth and Tőzsér's argument stands against it); and a Born-preserving companion law motivated independently of luck, which the Map has not attempted.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction).** The argument grants that the agent causes her choice and asks whether the causing is control or chance. The tenet commits the Map to conscious causal influence; reading that influence as agent-causal origination is the Map's further step, which the argument presses. Whether some choices or all are agent-caused stays open, as the tenet leaves it.

**[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction).** The Born-preserving default makes the fork bite at the grain of the conscious state, fixing the public statistics and leaving open the conditionals the replay asks about. The interface says where a settling would touch physics; it does not answer the luck objection.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism).** Dualism gains nothing here. The argument runs with agent causation granted, and O'Connor argues that agent causation does not itself require substance dualism.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits).** The replay's premise is an inference from frequencies, not a parsimony judgement, so the tenet cannot answer it. Since the tenet constrains only the Map's own reasoning, it brings no charge against event-causal or compatibilist rivals either.
