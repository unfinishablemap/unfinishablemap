The Process 1 specification problem is the demand that any account on which the mind chooses *which question* is put to nature must also say what fixes the projector: in which basis it acts, at what grain, and by what map from a neural representation of the alternatives to a projection operator. Henry Stapp's von Neumann framework gives the mind Process 1, the choice of observable, and leaves the answer to nature's Born-rule choice. Three critics press the demand from different directions. Matthew Donald (2003) argues that Stapp never says who builds the projections or how, and that they would need a precision no biology can supply. Danko Georgiev (2015) turns the decoherence-timing objection into a **basis dilemma**: a mind projecting in a basis other than the decoherence pointer basis can only raise entropy and loses the Zeno effect within the decoherence time, while a mind projecting in that same basis is doing work decoherence already does. José Acacio de Barros (2014) adds a circularity charge: choosing a basis needs physical apparatus, so the mind would have to act on matter in order to act on matter.

Stated up front: none of this refutes Process 1, and this page claims no refutation. Georgiev's theorem is conditional on Born-consistent, brain-local projections; Stapp's 2012 reply concedes the decoherence basis and relocates the mind's role to picking "some particular tiny portion" of it, and whether that relocation survives when statistics are judged unconditionally is unresolved in print. What the three critiques establish together is a *specification debt*. Stapp's own later wording, and a fix de Barros credits to Kathryn Laskey, both pay it the same way: the brain supplies the basis, and the mind chooses only whether and when the question is put. The Map registers a Born-intact question-choice reading without adopting it, and this debt is the price of that reading's minimality.

## What Process 1 Leaves Unsaid

In von Neumann's scheme as Stapp reads it, Process 1 is the choice of which yes/no question to ask, Process 2 is Schrödinger evolution, and nature's answer follows a statistical rule. Stapp's quantum Zeno mechanism (see [quantum-zeno-effect](/concepts/quantum-zeno-effect/) and [stapp-quantum-mind](/concepts/stapp-quantum-mind/)) has the mind ask the same question rapidly enough to hold a chosen neural pattern against the brain's own spreading. In his 2012 reply to Georgiev he calls the Process 1 postulates "neither novel nor mine", identifying them with the Copenhagen experimenter's free choice.

The point every camp can accept is that this leaves three things unspecified. A projection operator has a *basis* (which observable), a *grain* (how fine a partition of that observable's spectrum), and, if it is the mind's rather than the laboratory's, a *map* from whatever the brain represents as the alternatives to the operator applied. Laboratory Zeno experiments supply all three through apparatus. Process 1 as stated supplies none. The three critiques below are three ways of arguing that the answer to "who does?" cannot be "the mind alone".

## Donald: Precision, Representation, Boundary

Donald's 2003 review starts from a decoherence premise Stapp shares. A difference of 10⁻⁵ s in when a neuron fires is "macroscopic on the picosecond timescale of molecular vibrations", so the branches decohere at once into a mixture, and "no conventional physical process within the brain will be able to cause a (generalized) 'collapse' from ρ to either ρ₁ or ρ₂". The Zeno effect can in principle drive such a state to a chosen branch with probability one, but only under a carefully built sequence of projections. That dynamics "is not the well-understood biologically-evolved dynamics … It is a purely theoretical dynamics which depends on working towards the desired outcome right from the start."

Three objections follow. The Map has so far credited only a compressed version of the first.

**Precision.** "How and when the choice is supposed to result in the construction and action of the projection operators still has to be explained. The projections need to be defined to the precision of an individual wavefunction. Above atomic scales, no biological mechanism can control or even repeat states at this level of precision." Since a laboratory Zeno effect needs apparatus (Itano et al. 1990), "Stapp needs to tell us what apparatus biology has built into our brains which allows us to use the quantum Zeno effect to make choices." Otherwise Stapp has "invented a homunculus with access to a space of projection operators defined on an atomic scale".

**Representation.** "My basic objection to Stapp's work is that he does not appear to have made any connection between the representation of choices by physical neural processing – or indeed any other cellular process – and their representation as projection operators to be measured." This is the map, and the sharpest form of the problem: even granting a chooser, what does it choose *among*, and what fixes that set?

**Boundary.** "Why should they be restricted to changes within a brain? If mental forces can effectively decide the trajectories of atoms or molecules inside a brain, why can they not decide the trajectories of electrons in a laboratory or of prey in the ocean? What determined the point in evolutionary history when brains are supposed to have started to be able to make choices?" This is the question [brain-specialness-boundary](/topics/brain-specialness-boundary/) answers, and it has not until now been attributed to Donald there.

Donald's own framework is a many-minds theory he describes as "epiphenomenalistic in the sense that a mind does not direct a pattern of observed physical events", which runs counter to the Map's third and fourth tenets. His criticisms of Stapp do not depend on it, and a dualist has to answer them on their own terms.

## Georgiev: The Basis Dilemma Under Born-Consistent Collapse

Georgiev's 2015 *IJMPB* paper is usually cited on the Map as a timing result, and it is one: Monte Carlo simulation of a triple-well electron in an ion-channel voltage sensor shows that with decoherence in a different (energy) pointer basis the state behaves like a random telegraph, and "the quantum Zeno effect breaks down for timescales greater than the brain decoherence time". The deeper result is Theorem 4: local projective measurements "cannot decrease the von Neumann entropy of the unconditional density matrix".

Two consequences matter here. First, collapse does not help. "Both collapse and no collapse approaches are restrained by the quantum informational theorem 4 because, if the probabilities for all collapse outcomes are consistent with the Born rule, one could statistically average over all possible outcomes … to get the very same unconditional density matrix." His analogy is a lottery: winning the jackpot does not make the scheme efficacious. Second, the dilemma. "If the decoherence pointer basis happens to be the one in which the mind action is intended, then the decoherence alone can achieve the quantum Zeno effect and the mind action will be irrelevant or redundant." On the other horn, "there could be no free will if the mind cannot choose the basis in which the projective measurement is performed", and if the mind's basis is mutually unbiased with the pointer basis, the probability of the intended state falls to 1/n after the first projection.

Georgiev leaves one door open, a decoherence-free subspace in which unitaries "hide" the initial state and later restore it, which he calls "admittedly speculative"; the question then becomes why a subspace of the brain escapes decoherence.

The theorem's scope is narrower than its title, and the narrowing matters for the Map. It assumes projections that are *brain-local* and *Born-consistent*. It says nothing against a model that bends Born weights, and nothing against a model that does not rely on repeated projection at all.

## Stapp's Concession and the Open Crux

Stapp's 2012 reply accepts that decoherence turns superpositions into mixtures, that his model "does not use interference effects between superposed states", and that the brain density matrix is "reduced to near-diagonal form in the coordinate-space basis". Then he concedes the basis: "Yes, the decoherence basis is the coordinate basis, and the projection operator P might be a projection onto a certain particular interval in coordinate space. But this does not make the latter redundant with the former … The action of the environment reduces the nonzero part of the brain density matrix essentially to the long strip along the diagonal, whereas the action of the mind picks out some particular tiny portion of that strip." On this account the mind does not slow decoherence but "rolls back the brain-based process of evolution", the Schrödinger spreading away from the interval the last Process 1 choice picked out. Georgiev's claim that Zeno cannot slow decoherence "is certainly correct, but in no way contradicts my model". (Quoted from the pre-publication draft on Stapp's Berkeley site; body wording not checked against print.)

The concession settles the basis horn in Georgiev's favour and moves the dispute to the grain. What the reply does not say is how the "tiny portion" is specified, which is Donald's representation question asked about an interval rather than a basis. Nor does it address whether projecting onto a sub-interval of a density matrix already near-diagonal in that basis changes the *unconditional* statistics. Stapp's 2015 rejoinder promises "a simple proof that environmental decoherence does not nullify the quantum Zeno effect"; only its abstract could be located. No worked model on either side compares the interval grain with the decoherence strip width. That comparison is the quantitative crux, and this page states it rather than settling it.

## de Barros: Circularity and the Laskey Narrowing

de Barros (2014) makes the apparatus point directly. A measurement in a given basis needs physical apparatus: "the Stern-Gerlach setup needs to be there; it cannot be produced by the mind." A mind that "does not have a preferred pointer basis" cannot supply one, so "we get into a circular argument: to solve the problem of how the mind affects matter, we need to postulate that the mind affects matter." de Barros prefers non-quantum accounts of mind and is cited here as a rival.

His constructive contribution is a fix he attributes to a private communication from Kathryn Laskey. Let the *brain* run a cyclic apparatus whose measurement basis rotates in time. "The mind, in this case, can choose to observe or not the system, and can also choose when to observe the system." He notes this "could in principle be observable". The mind's choice has narrowed from *which question* to *whether and when a physically supplied question is put*.

That narrowing is already in Stapp's own words. Donald quotes Stapp (2000) as locating the one element "not governed by any known law of physics" in "the choices to consent or not consent at time t to putting to nature the question associated with the possible experience E(t)" (reported through Donald; not checked against the 2000 text). Consent over a supplied question and timing over a cycling basis are the same move. Read at source, the three critiques converge on a Process 1 in which basis and grain come from the brain and the mind contributes timing and assent, which is the [timing-control mode](/concepts/coupling-modes/) rather than basis control.

## How the Debt Might Be Paid

Four responses are live. **Primitive choice**: Stapp's position that the free choice of question is no more owed an explanation than the Copenhagen experimenter's, and that demanding a physical mechanism for it begs the question against interactionism. This answers the homunculus charge for a dualist, but not the representation point: a primitive chooser still needs a set to choose from. **Brain-supplied basis, mind-supplied timing**: the Laskey fix is non-circular, matches Stapp's consent wording, and has an in-principle signature, at the cost that "question-choice" no longer names what the mind does. **Decoherence-free subspace**: if some neural degrees of freedom are protected by a symmetry of the system-environment coupling, Georgiev's theorem does not apply within them; Georgiev flags this as speculative, and no protected neural subspace has been identified. **Post-decoherence selection**: the Map's default, [post-decoherence-selection](/concepts/post-decoherence-selection/), chooses no basis and repeats no projection. Decoherence fixes the pointer basis; the mind's contribution is to which pointer outcome becomes actual, at its Born-rule rate. Georgiev's theorem does not reach it, because there is no sequence of mind-chosen projections to average over. The escape is real and has its own price: the mind on this reading inherits Donald's representation question in a different form, what fixes the set of pointer alternatives it selects among and how a criterion is expressed in it, which is the [selection-criterion-problem](/concepts/selection-criterion-problem/).

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Map's [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) tenet commits to outcome-selection and registers a Process 1, context-selection alternative as a path it has not adopted. The [Born-rule article](/topics/born-rule-and-the-consciousness-interface/) carries that alternative as its Born-intact question-choice row, and [agency-budget](/concepts/agency-budget/) as the question-choice reading of the budget. This page changes what those entries can honestly say, in four ways.

First, Georgiev's theorem targets that row exactly. Its premise is that collapses are consistent with the Born rule, so the Born-intact reading is the model the no-go was built against, not a way around it. The row's claim that it "faces no Born dilemma" is true; it faces the basis dilemma in its place.

Second, Born-intact does not mean statistically inert, and the row should keep two readings apart. On Stapp's favourable reading, choosing which question is asked and when changes the distribution of brain-state histories while every individual answer obeys the Born rule, so question-choice does bear on what happens. On Georgiev's reading, that lever vanishes under decoherence once statistics are judged unconditionally. The row's "fixes what is asked rather than what happens" is too strong on the first reading and exactly right on the second. That is why "Stapp's quantum Zeno" appears both in the Born-intact row and among the Born-bending mechanisms on the same page: whether conditional-history work counts as shifting probabilities depends on the register in which it is judged.

Third, under pressure "which question" narrows to consent and timing over a brain-supplied basis. Stapp's 2012 concession, his quoted 2000 consent wording, and the Laskey fix all make the same move. The most accurate gloss of the row is *when and whether a physically supplied question is put*, with basis choice an open debt. The [coupling-modes](/concepts/coupling-modes/) verdict that basis control is the most minimal mode therefore needs a qualifier: its minimality is bought by leaving the basis unspecified, and once the basis is handed to decoherence the mode is timing control under another name.

Fourth, [Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) cuts both ways. Post-decoherence selection escapes the dilemma because it makes no basis choice, which is a genuine reason to prefer it over a Zeno-holding mechanism. But the Map's answer to Donald's boundary question, that the mind can only select among alternatives the brain represents in its control loop, is Donald's representation gap run in reverse: he says Stapp gives no map from neural representations to projectors, and the Map's boundary criterion is only as good as that map. The specification problem is a debt on every Process 1 reading, including the one the Map has registered without adopting, and the honest statement is that the dispute is open inside Stapp's framework rather than closed by it.
