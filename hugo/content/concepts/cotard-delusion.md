Cotard delusion is the clinical condition in which a patient asserts, and holds against correction, that they are dead, have no body or organs, or do not exist. On the neutral clinical description every camp accepts, it is a nihilistic delusion most often arising in severe depression, occasionally after brain injury or encephalitis, and rare: a prospective single-centre series of 1,321 neuropsychiatric consultations found it in 0.11 per cent of neurological and 0.62 per cent of psychiatric patients (Ramirez-Bermudez et al. 2010). The Map's interest is narrower than the syndrome. It concerns the subset of cases whose report content is the denial of the reporter's own existence, because that content is the hardest available test of a commitment the Map already holds — that [every reported case is reported from somewhere](/concepts/self-and-self-consciousness/#constitutive-as-kind-not-as-degree). Cotard is the case where the somewhere is what the report denies.

The verdict, stated first so that it survives truncation: Cotard passes the "reported from somewhere" test — the patient who says "I do not exist" argues, resists, worries and, on the case record, feels — but passing it does not discriminate between the Map's reading (a subject receiving a corrupted self-report) and the physicalist self-model rival (a self-model misrepresenting itself, with no one further). Both sides own the datum. What Cotard does tighten is the architecture-tier claim that the self-model's existence report is a separable component, dissociable from ongoing emotional and cognitive life; it leaves the significance tier — whether that separability marks anything non-physical — exactly where the [Capgras article](/topics/capgras-delusion-and-the-affective-recognition-channel/) left it. The reading is compatible with Tenet 1 and at most suggestive for it, says nothing about Tenet 2's mechanism, and is offered provisionally: the higher-order (Gennaro 2020) and book-length active-inference (Gerrans 2024) treatments of how a model represents its own non-existence have not yet been read against it. The [mirror](#the-capgras-mirror), the [two-factor debate](#two-factors-one-factor-and-the-depersonalisation-continuum) and [Gerrans's dissent](#gerranss-interoceptive-dissent) precede the [verdict](#reported-from-somewhere) below.

## What the Syndrome Is, and Which Part This Page Selects

Jules Cotard's 1880 *délire des négations* was, on Berrios and Luque's conceptual history, "a subtype of depressive illness" — an anxious melancholia with nihilistic content — rather than a belief in one's own death; the Map has not consulted Cotard's text and relies on their reading of it (Berrios & Luque 1995b). Their retrospective analysis of 100 published cases supplies the base rates: depression in 89 per cent, nihilistic delusions concerning the body in 86 per cent and concerning existence in 69 per cent, with anxiety (65 per cent), guilt (63), hypochondriacal delusions (58) and delusions of immortality (55) also common (Berrios & Luque 1995a). Existence-denial is therefore present in about two-thirds of the historical corpus, not all of it, and a factor analysis in the same paper separated a "pure" Cotard type whose nosology "may be closer to the delusional than the affective disorders" from cases that were depressive first. The syndrome has no DSM entry and is "typically viewed as a part of other underlying disorders" (Debruyne et al. 2009, a narrative review).

Two consequences follow. This page selects: everything below concerns the existence-denial subset and does not transfer automatically to the body-nihilism or immortality contents. And the report is internally inconsistent in part of the corpus — with immortality delusions in over half of the cases and existence-denial in two-thirds, the two must co-occur in at least a quarter, so a patient can claim both to be dead and to be unable to die — a datum about the kind of output the report is, taken up in the [analytic section below](#reported-from-somewhere).

## The Capgras Mirror

The modern neuropsychology of Cotard begins as an extension of the Capgras story, in a three-paper sequence often compressed to "Young and Leafhead". Young, Robertson, Hellawell, de Pauw and Pentland (1992) reported a single case: a young man with right temporo-parietal contusions and bilateral frontal damage who became convinced he was dead and who also showed difficulties recognising familiar faces, buildings and places, together with feelings of derealisation. They suggested these impairments fed the delusion "by heightening feelings of unreality" — derealisation, rather than face-affect specifically, already named as the felt seed. Wright, Young and Hellawell (1993) then described one patient, KH, who held the Cotard delusion while depressed and the Capgras delusion later, in a persecutory context, and proposed that the two "reflect different interpretations of similar anomalous experiences": the suspicious patient mistakes "a change in themselves for a change in others ('they are impostors')", whereas the depressed patient exaggerates the same change "whilst correctly attributing it to themselves ('I am dead')". Young, Leafhead and Szulecka (1994), a theoretical paper, generalised this into a two-level account — anomalous perceptual experience plus an attribution-theoretic interpretation of it — on which the two delusions, though "phenomenally distinct", "may therefore represent attempts to make sense of fundamentally similar experiences".

The one direct test of the attribution prediction is McKay and Cipolotti's (2007) single case, LU, who "showed a significantly greater proportion of internalising attributions than the control group" of ten; the extended case series in Young and Leafhead (1996) is known to the Map only through McKay and Cipolotti's summary of it and is cited here for the internalising-style claim alone. Two features of the mirror matter for what follows. On Wright and colleagues' phrasing the Cotard patient's *attribution* is right and the *content* is wrong — the change really is in them. And the pairing brings with it the Capgras article's [scope rule](/topics/capgras-delusion-and-the-affective-recognition-channel/#why-the-anomaly-becomes-a-delusion), which this page inherits without change: only the anomalous experience is the Map's territory; the belief-evaluation side is a reasoning pathology the Map takes no position on.

## Two Factors, One Factor, and the Depersonalisation Continuum

Davies, Coltheart, Langdon and Breen (2001) formalised the two-factor account for a battery of monothematic delusions including Cotard. Against Maher's view that delusions are normal responses to anomalous experience, they argued that "a second factor is required to account for the transition from unusual experience to delusional belief" — superficially, a loss of the ability to reject an implausible candidate belief — while conceding "problems that confront any attempt to say more about the nature of this second factor". It carries a clean prediction. A patient with the first factor alone should report the *as-if* form — "it is as if I were dead" — with insight preserved, which is the clinical picture of depersonalisation; a patient with both factors drops the "as if". And remission should track the second factor more than the first.

What the first factor *is* has moved. Gerrans (2000) suggested the affective deficit in Cotard "may be more global than in Capgras cases", so that persons whose "global affective responses were suppressed would explain their lack of response by saying that they had no bodily existence"; the popular version, in Ramachandran and Blakeslee (1998), is total emotional flatness. Davies and Coltheart (2022) tested that seed against the published case record and rejected it: "In numerous cases, patients with Cotard delusion are described as experiencing emotions including anxiety, fear, guilt, distress, euphoria and worry", so "complete absence of emotional responsivity cannot be what prompts the delusional idea that one is dead". They propose instead that "in at least some cases" the idea arises from symptoms of depersonalisation or derealisation — the seed Young and colleagues had named in 1992. The imaging is consistent with a two-factor decomposition without establishing it. Restrepo-Martínez and colleagues (2019) report a single case, a nineteen-year-old with autoimmune encephalitis, scanned with FDG-PET before and after successful electroconvulsive treatment, showing "marked insular and prefrontal metabolic abnormalities" of which all but the insular hypometabolism improved after treatment; the authors map insula to the first factor and prefrontal cortex to the second, and the asymmetric recovery is the pattern the account predicts if remission runs through the second factor. It is one inflamed brain, and the datum belongs to the reversibility literature rather than to this page's argument; the earlier imaging record, including the first PET case report, has not been read at the primary and is not summarised here.

Two-factor is not uncontested on this pair. Gerrans (2002) argued that "once we distinguish the phenomenology (and the neuroetiology), a one-stage model is adequate" for the Capgras–Cotard difference. And Billon (2016), the philosophical account the Map sits closest to, reads Cotard through the better-studied and non-delusional condition of depersonalisation: depersonalised patients "seem to have experiences that are quite similar to those of Cotard patients", experiences "essentially characterised by a (more or less important) lack of subjective character" and of two further structural features he calls "the present character" and "the actual character". On his account "Cotard's nihilistic delusions simply consist in taking these anomalous experiences at face value" — a reading on which the existence report is accurate to the experience and the pathology lies in the experience rather than in belief-fixing. The three missing characters map the three Cotard contents: I do not exist; nothing is happening; the world is unreal. This depersonalisation continuum — depersonalisation as the sub-delusional form of the same experience, Cotard as the form in which the *as-if* is dropped — is the majority reading in the 2016–2022 literature, shared by the face-value account and the revised two-factor account, which differ over whether a second factor is needed to lose the "as if". The Map does not re-argue depersonalisation here: [mine-ness](/concepts/mine-ness/) carries it as the central evidence for separable for-me-ness, with the Knappik caveat that the feature may be plural, and [necessary-opacity](/voids/necessary-opacity/) and [phenomenal-quality-void](/voids/phenomenal-quality-void/) treat it as transparency cracking with insight retained. Cotard is the insight-lost complement.

## Gerrans's Interoceptive Dissent

The standing objection to the continuum is Gerrans (2023, online 2022). On his interoceptive active-inference account, "The initial cause of Cotard delusion is pervasive dyshomeostasis (dysregulation of basic bodily function)"; the self is "an hierarchical predictive model made by the brain to facilitate homeostatic regulation", and the existence report is a top-level inference from bottom-level failures of bodily prediction. He offers this explicitly as "an alternative to two factor accounts of the Cotard delusion that treat depersonalisation experience as the first factor", arguing that "depersonalisation experience and the Cotard delusion are produced by different breakdowns in the process of self-modelling". So the continuum is contested, and this page does not build on its being true; but the dissent leaves the framing intact, since on every account in play the failure is one of self-modelling, and Gerrans's is the strongest self-model-only account on offer — the one that would most naturally treat the existence report as nothing over and above a model's output. A book-length version (Gerrans 2024) exists and has not been read; the neutrality verdict below is held provisionally against it.

## Reported From Somewhere

The performative datum is real and both sides own it. The patient who denies existing produces the denial, defends it, and — on Davies and Coltheart's reading of the case record — feels anxiety, fear and guilt while doing so. [Self and Self-Consciousness](/concepts/self-and-self-consciousness/) states the Map's constitutive claim about for-me-ness as a claim about kind rather than degree, with a specifiable falsifier: "phenomenal life continuing without any residue of for-me-ness, no position from which the lack is registered". Cotard is the hardest instance, because the report's content denies the position from which it is made, and it passes: what fails is the report, not the position. The syndrome's internal inconsistency points the same way: co-occurring existence-denial and immortality delusions are what a *constructed* status report looks like rather than a monitored fact — the point [neurological-dissociations-as-interface-architecture](/topics/neurological-dissociations-as-interface-architecture/) makes about anosognosia, where consciousness "receives a coherent but false report about the body's capabilities", carried here from the body's capacities to the system's existence. [anosognosia-and-the-reversible-self-monitoring-channel](/topics/anosognosia-and-the-reversible-self-monitoring-channel/) owns the capacity-report failure and its reversal literature; Cotard is the same ascending self-model channel reporting the wrong value of a different variable.

Passing the test settles less than it appears to. The physicalist self-model theorist grants every step: a system can misrepresent its own existence exactly as it can misrepresent its own arm, and a running model that reports non-existence is still a running model — "residual position" is exactly how such a model presents from inside. A pure self-model account, whether Metzinger's transparent [phenomenal self-model](/concepts/self-model-theory-of-subjectivity/) or Gerrans's homeostatic hierarchy, predicts that whatever the model's existence component says, there is nothing else whose status could differ from it. The Map's interface reading predicts that the model's existence report can fail while for-me-ness is attenuated but never eliminated. The clinical record fits the second description; it is also fully compatible with the first. Cotard therefore does not discriminate between the two readings at the significance tier. The [two-tier discount](/project/architecture-vs-significance-two-tier-discount/) applies verbatim: the architecture-tier claim — that the existence or aliveness dimension of the self-model is a separable component, dissociable from ongoing emotional and cognitive life — is tightened by a case in which the report fails while emotion, argument and worry continue; the significance-tier claim is untouched.

One modest asymmetry is worth recording as a prediction rather than evidence. On the interface reading the existence-report failure should be content-general — one under-served receiving position losing felt existence for self, present, world and body alike — whereas a strictly modular self-model account has no particular reason to expect those contents to co-vary within patients. Berrios and Luque's rates are consistent with co-variation but cannot test it, and no source found answers the within-patient question. Nothing on this page rests on the prediction.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Dualism (Tenet 1)](/tenets/#dualism)** — compatible with, at most suggestive for. Cotard shows that the self-model's report of the subject's own existence can be falsified from inside while the subject continues to argue and feel — the kind of separable channel the Map's [interface architecture](/topics/neurological-dissociations-as-interface-architecture/) predicts, and the fourth term of the Capgras article's disowning series: the *act* in [anarchic hand](/topics/anarchic-hand-and-action-ownership/), the *limb* in alien hand, the *person* in Capgras, the *self* here. But the physicalist self-model account explains every datum on this page equally well, and the [compatibility-vs-support](/project/evidential-status-discipline/) discipline forbids counting compatible evidence as support. Nothing in Cotard discriminates; what it earns is framework-internal weight, and even the neutrality verdict is provisional until the higher-order and active-inference treatments named above have been read against it.

**[Minimal Quantum Interaction (Tenet 2)](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) and [Bidirectional Interaction (Tenet 3)](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)** — nothing. Cotard says nothing about the mechanism by which any non-physical contribution would enter the brain, and the patient's report, true or false, is ordinary physical output; the case neither supports nor strains either tenet.

**[No Many Worlds (Tenet 4)](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)** — consistent with, not evidence for, the determinate subject the indexical objection presupposes ([individuation and subjecthood](/positions/individuation-and-subjecthood/), [P-I1](/positions/individuation-and-subjecthood/#p-i1)) — the same posit the [quantum factorisation problem](/concepts/quantum-factorisation-problem/) shows the indexical argument also owes a factorisation for, since a determinate "I" has first to be a determinate subsystem of the wave function. A subject persisting under a corrupted self-report is what Tenet 4 needs; a self-model persisting while its existence component misfires is what the rival needs; the case is the same case.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits (Tenet 5)](/tenets/#occams-limits)** — the tenet's discipline binds this page's own tidiness. The temptation a page titled after the "existence report" invites is to read a localised existence channel off the content of a delusion. Berrios and Luque's conceptual history is the standing warning against exactly that move: "it is unclear why a delusion should merit, simply because of its 'nihilistic' content, a special brain location or presage chronicity" (1995b). Theirs is a documented reading of the historical corpus rather than a settled one — the two-factor imaging programme is a bet that content does track a lesion pattern — but the Map takes the caution as binding on itself. The self-model channel vocabulary is a framework-internal organising device, not an anatomical discovery, and the simplicity of *one channel, one failure* is no guide to its truth while the nosology remains as unsettled as the 100-case corpus shows it to be. Nor does the page raise any void's independence score: on [voids-as-evidence](/positions/voids-as-evidence/) this is coherence with the [self-opacity](/voids/self-opacity/) and [ownership](/concepts/mine-ness/#the-ownership-void) voids, not a further confirmation of them.
