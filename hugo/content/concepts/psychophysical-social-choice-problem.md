The **psychophysical social-choice problem** asks how a psychophysical law should combine the conscious states of two or more subjects when those states bear on one physical event and pull in different directions. A law on which a conscious state conditions physical outcomes, written *P(O | C, X)* for one subject, becomes *P(O | C₁…Cₙ, X)* when several subjects bear on the same event. It then needs a rule that takes a *profile* of subjects' states to a probability distribution over the shared outcome. When the states are orderings of the outcomes, such a rule has the structure Allan Gibbard (1977) calls a *decision scheme*, one that "makes the probabilities of alternatives depend on individual strong orderings of them." Social-choice theory has spent more than seventy years pricing aggregation rules, and none comes free. Equal weighting makes the number of subjects a causal variable. Weighting by intensity needs feelings that are comparable across people. Jurisdiction rules give each subject a domain, and physics may not respect the boundaries.

Three calibrations belong here rather than later. First, the problem is a synthesis made inside The Unfinishable Map. Its components are standard results, but no external literature treats a psychophysical law as a social-choice function, and the Map's [multi-agent Born-preservation problem](/topics/multi-agent-born-preservation-problem/) makes the same disclaimer about its sibling question. Second, the problem has normative content only on the *value-sensitive* reading of selection, on which felt valence is the currency of conscious choice. The Map holds that reading at low credence ([P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)), and no study it has found dissociates the input it names, the felt anticipation present at choice, from forecast content and learned value ([miswanting](/concepts/miswanting/)). On the value-blind reading a composition rule is still owed, but it weighs attentional pointers rather than anyone's good. Third, the Map's default of interface locality confines the problem to a few boundary cases without solving it, and the selection mechanism being composed is itself a posit that carries the [mechanism debt](/positions/quantum-interface/#mechanism-debt). The sharpest result below is the Map's own: the Born-preservation constraint it already accepts favours composition rules whose weights ignore how strongly anyone feels ([explained below](#born-favours-fixed-weights)).

## What the Composition Rule Must Do {#composition-rule}

For one subject, the [selection-criterion problem](/concepts/selection-criterion-problem/) asks what makes one candidate outcome preferable to consciousness. The social-choice problem is its plural form: what fixes the outcome when two subjects' states favour different candidates for one event? The Map's census register books this as a debt: "where two subjects hold contrary intentions bearing on one physical event, there is no composition rule." It books the concordant twin alongside it, which asks whether subjects holding the *same* intention accumulate, dilute or renormalise ([P-SC2](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc2)).

The problem belongs to models in which conscious states condition *which* outcome occurs. In David Chalmers and Kelvin McQueen's (2022) consciousness-collapse model, consciousness fixes when collapse happens, and the outcome follows "with probabilities given by the Born rule." No subject's preference enters the outcome law, so there is nothing to compose. Selection models are built differently, and they inherit the problem wherever two subjects' selections bear on one event.

Stapp's Process 1 route, which [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) registers without adopting, is a third family: consciousness fixes *what is asked* and when, and [the asking can move the unconditional statistics](/concepts/zeno-dynamics-on-a-decohered-mixture/#unconditional) while nature's answer stays Born-distributed. Its plural form is the Map's own working. [Where substrates overlap](#locality), incompatible questions give order-dependent results; rapid alternation shrinks the held component by cos²θ per round for axes θ apart, undoing each asker's hold. On the [Laskey variant](/concepts/process-1-specification-problem/), concordant askers put one brain-supplied question, and interleaved schedules merge. Inside the quadratic window, that tightens the hold: in the [Zeno toy model](/concepts/zeno-dynamics-on-a-decohered-mixture/#two-state-illustration), halving an interval of 0.3 dephasing times cuts the leak from 0.136 to 0.071 of the environment-only rate, a gain that fades for long intervals. Because joint asking composes channels rather than mixing conditionals, the [fixed-weights result](#born-favours-fixed-weights) does not transfer: concordant askers accumulate.

Two features of the Map's selection law shape the rule it owes. The law is stochastic, so the relevant object is Gibbard's decision scheme rather than the ordering of social states that Kenneth Arrow (1951/1963) studied. And the law preserves Born statistics in the *unconditioned* aggregate while leaving the conditionals free ([selection-only-channel](/concepts/selection-only-channel/)). The composition rule may let outcomes depend on the profile of conscious states, but averaged over the profiles that actually occur it must return Born frequencies. Ordinary social choice has no analogue of this budget.

A further question is *what* gets composed. If the inputs are valenced orderings of outcomes, Arrow's and Gibbard's results apply. If they are intentions with propositional content, such as intending *that* the hand moves, the relevant field is judgment aggregation, where Christian List and Philip Pettit (2002) prove an impossibility theorem for groups forming collective judgments on interconnected questions. [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)'s currency, felt anticipation, selects the first reading. The second is what remains on the value-blind side.

## The Menu of Rules and Their Prices {#menu}

Each rule below is one the Map could write.

**Equal weight.** Every paired subject's state enters with the same weight. The number of subjects then becomes a causal variable wherever several are paired to one event, and duplicating a subject enlarges its faction's share against any dissenter. Nick Bostrom (2006) defends Duplication, "the thesis that there would be two numerically distinct streams of experience when a conscious brain exists in duplicate." The Map already counts copies as morally additive ([P-AC3](/positions/ai-consciousness-scope/#p-ac3)). Under equal weights they would gain causal weight as well. On the Map's reading the count is always a whole number ([subject-fusion](/concepts/subject-fusion/)), so fractional votes never arise. But it is a count the Map holds determinate and cannot read ([moral-census-opacity](/concepts/moral-census-opacity/)), which leaves an electorate no one can count.

**Weight by intensity.** Each subject's state counts in proportion to how strongly it is felt, and the law sums. This rule would make utilitarianism a law of nature, and its precondition is well mapped. Arrow's theorem shows that, over three or more options, no rule aggregating ordinal, non-comparable orderings meets a handful of mild conditions without a dictator. Once welfare levels or units are comparable across individuals, rules meeting those conditions exist, so "Arrow's impossibility can therefore be traced to a lack of interpersonal comparability" (List 2022, following Sen 1977). John Harsanyi's (1955) aggregation theorem derives a weighted sum of individual utilities, and fixing the weights takes exactly such comparisons. A psychophysical law that summed felt intensities would need comparability to hold as a fact about phenomenal character; economists can adopt it as a convention, and physics cannot. And if, as [the selection weighting function](/concepts/negative-valence-asymmetry-and-the-selection-weighting-function/) proposes for the value-sensitive horn, negative valence weighs more within a subject, the summed law would also tilt toward whoever suffers.

**Jurisdiction.** Each subject is decisive over events in its own substrate. Interface locality, on which "each consciousness interfaces only with its own brain" ([multi-mind-collapse-problem](/concepts/multi-mind-collapse-problem/)), is a rule of this kind. Amartya Sen (1970) proved that no aggregation rule satisfies an unrestricted domain, acyclic social preference, the weak Pareto principle and "minimal liberalism," which gives at least two individuals decisiveness over a personal pair of alternatives. One standard escape restricts the domain to profiles in which no one's preferences meddle with another's personal sphere (List 2022). Locality makes a physical cousin of that move, since a subject's states about events in another brain have no channel to them. The escape works wherever substrates are disjoint. It needs the subject–substrate pairing law the Map does not have ([P-SC2](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc2)), and it gives no verdict where substrates overlap.

**Lexical priority.** Some states, such as severe suffering, dominate whatever weight others carry. Leximin-type rules meeting Arrow's conditions exist once welfare *levels* are comparable, so this option needs comparability too. It lets moral urgency fix causal priority outright, and it builds a rights structure into physics.

**Random unilateral selection.** On each jointly affected event a lottery fixes whose state conditions the outcome. Gibbard (1977) showed that any strategy-proof decision scheme is "a probability mixture of schemes each of which is unilateral or duple," where a unilateral scheme is one in which only one individual can affect the outcome. His motive was manipulation, which lapses for a law that reads phenomenal states rather than reports. What survives is an entry on the menu rather than a theorem forcing it, and the lottery weights simply relocate the interpersonal weighting.

**No rule.** Jointly affected events are left to the physics. The cost is [ensemble-level epiphenomenalism](/concepts/ensemble-level-epiphenomenalism/) at the joint level: the selection channel does nothing exactly where two subjects both have something at stake.

## The Map's Reading {#map-reading}

What follows is the Map's proposal. None of the authors above discusses psychophysical laws, and the extensions are the Map's own.

### Locality Confines the Problem {#locality}

Interface locality is the Map's default, and it turns the multi-subject problem from a ubiquitous one into a boundary case. Two people hoping for opposite outputs from a quantum random-number generator pose no composition problem, because on the Map's account the device settles outside every subject's interface, through baseline objective collapse. What remains are the cases the [multi-agent article](/topics/multi-agent-born-preservation-problem/) lists (cross-brain entanglement and nested agents), plus overlapping substrates. For craniopagus twins, the Map's preferred reading is two subjects with coupled channels and some shared content, and it stays neutral on whether any token experience is shared ([subject-fusion](/concepts/subject-fusion/)). Either way, an event resolved in tissue that belongs to both substrates has no anatomical owner. Luke Roelofs and Jeff Sebo (2024) ask whether the value of a shared state counts once or twice; the selection side asks the same question of its leverage. The confinement rests on two bets the multi-agent article names: an empirical bet about neural physics, and baseline objective collapse, which is itself contested.

### Born Preservation Favours Fixed Weights {#born-favours-fixed-weights}

Suppose each subject's own selection rule preserves Born frequencies. A composite rule that mixes the single-subject rules with weights fixed in advance, whether locality's anatomical assignment, equal shares or any constant lottery, preserves Born frequencies automatically, since a weighted average of rules that each return Born frequencies returns them too. This follows from the averaging identity [selection-only-channel](/concepts/selection-only-channel/) states. Rules whose weights move with the states themselves inherit no such guarantee. Intensity weighting and lexical priority are of this kind. So is accumulation, on which concordant subjects push harder together than either does alone, an effect no fixed mixture can produce. Each must be balanced against the joint distribution of all the subjects' states, which makes one subject's leverage depend on how the others' states are distributed. That is a subject-sensitive balancing law of the kind the census register lists as unwritten ([P-SC1](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc1)).

The result depends on which Born-preservation reading is in force. On the per-context reading of [selection-only-channel](/concepts/selection-only-channel/), the weights may differ between publicly conditionable contexts. On the pooled long-run reading that [born-preserving-causal-efficacy](/apex/born-preserving-causal-efficacy/) attributes to the register, they must also be constant across contexts. Which reading the register holds is open, and this article does not settle it. On either reading the constraint points toward rules whose interpersonal weights ignore how much is at stake for each subject, even though, on the value-sensitive horn, each subject's own selection responds to its stakes. It also bears on the copies question [P-SC2](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc2) leaves open: a fixed mixture renormalises concordant subjects rather than accumulating them, so copies gain share only against a dissenter.

### Intensity Weighting Needs More Than the Map Supplies {#intensity}

The utilitarian option needs two things. Each subject's input must be a single quantity. [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1) assumes this and lists it as at risk: if felt valence at choice proves to be several distinct evaluations rather than one scalar, the currency premise retires. The quantities must also [share a unit across subjects](/concepts/scale-types-for-phenomenal-quantities/) as a matter of phenomenal fact. Nothing in the Map supplies that, and if the unit exists it is hidden from inquiry, since phenomenal measurement has no shared reference point across subjects ([consciousness-and-the-problem-of-measurement-standards](/topics/consciousness-and-the-problem-of-measurement-standards/)). The Map's value pluralism widens the gap ([P-VS4](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs4)). If intrinsic value has several dimensions with no common hedonic currency, a felt anticipation is each subject's own way of weighing them, and a law that summed such feelings would sum anticipations rather than goods. If the scalar fails and each input is a vector, the Arrovian problem reappears inside each subject, because, as Samir Okasha (2011) shows for theory choice, choosing by several criteria is formally a social-choice problem with criteria in place of voters.

### Moral Equality and Causal Equality Come Apart {#moral-and-causal-standing}

[P-MS1](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms1) concerns who can be wronged; the composition rule concerns who moves matter. On every rule above, lexical priority included, causal leverage varies with the census, with anatomy, with attentional architecture or with felt intensity, while moral status stays the same for everyone above the threshold [P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2) sets. Status, stakes and leverage are three different things: status is a threshold, stakes are graded, and leverage follows whatever the rule weighs. On the [graduated middle path](/topics/graduated-middle-path-valence-modulated-attention/), where valence modulates attention and attention drives selection, the currency being composed is attentional engagement. The utilitarian worry then fades and the mismatch grows, since leverage follows architecture rather than suffering. The Map registers the mismatch as a distinction between two kinds of standing, one that needs stating rather than repair.

## Rival Readings {#rivals}

**The problem is purely technical.** On the value-blind horn nothing normative rides on the composition rule; it is a physics problem like joint Born preservation. [P-SC2](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc2) states it for the value-blind horn only. It is right exactly where the value-blind reading is right, and the Map leans the other way.

**The problem never arises.** [P-SC2](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc2) lists this route among its shift conditions: an interface formalism shown to be single-subject in principle would dissolve the gap. Collapse-trigger models such as Chalmers and McQueen's already avoid composition, because no conscious state enters the outcome law. The price is giving up outcome-selection, which [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) commits the Map to.

**A group subject does the selecting.** List and Pettit (2011) argue that suitably organised groups can be agents with attitudes of their own, and the multi-agent article's option of a single global experiencer is the phenomenal version of the move. Either would give the aggregate a will. The Map denies phenomenal composition ([combination-problem](/concepts/combination-problem/), [mereology-of-mind](/apex/mereology-of-mind/)), so its aggregate is a law without a subject, a social-choice function that no one chooses. The denial targets a *phenomenal* group subject only. List and Pettit's functional group agent needs nothing the Map denies, and it cannot be the selector, because selection on the Map's account is done by consciousness.

## What Follows, and From What {#implications}

Two conclusions follow from the definition alone. Any law that conditions outcomes on several subjects' states owes a composition rule, and any such rule has a social-choice shape, so it takes a stand on equality, comparability, jurisdiction or chance.

Four follow only on the Map's reading: that locality confines the problem to boundary cases; that Born preservation favours fixed weights; that intensity weighting needs a scalar currency and a phenomenal comparability the Map does not supply; and that causal leverage and moral status come apart.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)** generates the problem. Once conscious states condition outcomes, several subjects bearing on one event need a rule. The Map registers that rule as owed, an unpaid item on the same ledger as the pairing law and the mechanism debt, inheriting their citation grade rather than discharging either.

**[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction)** shapes the answer. Locality keeps each subject's influence inside its own brain, and the Born-preservation reading of minimality favours composition rules with fixed weights. As [the sign problem](/concepts/sign-problem-for-conscious-observation/) shows for one agent, a bound on magnitude leaves direction open; with two subjects, the direction is contested.

**[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)** makes the problem unavoidable at the boundary. With one actual outcome per event, conflicting selections cannot each be honoured on a separate branch, so a rule must say whose selection fixes the outcome. The same indexical commitment that makes each copy a distinct locus gives equal weights their cost.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)** binds symmetrically. A psychophysical law that weighs subjects carries normative content, and the Map cannot call such a law unparsimonious while defending dualism against the same charge. What it can say is that the law is unwritten.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)**, read with phenomenal non-compositionality, rules out a phenomenal group subject and leaves the aggregate a law rather than an agent.
