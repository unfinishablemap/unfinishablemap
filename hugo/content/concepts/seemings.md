A **seeming** is the state reported by "it seems to me that P": the way things appear to a subject, prior to and separable from whether the subject believes that things are that way. Every live camp in epistemology accepts that much. The dispute is over what kind of state a seeming is, and in particular whether a seeming that *justifies* belief must itself be phenomenally conscious. Michael Huemer's [phenomenal conservatism](/concepts/phenomenal-conservatism/) (PC) makes seemings the basic source of justification; Keith Frankish's [functional seeming](/concepts/functional-seeming/) makes the seeming of consciousness a functional state with no phenomenal character. Each of those pages hands the question to the other. This page takes it up directly.

The Map's answer, marked as its own and argued [below](#map-reading), is that a justificatory seeming is a non-doxastic state with presentational force, and that the force is felt. That answer has a price the Map accepts openly (see [What Turns on It](#what-turns)). If seemings must be phenomenal, then a seeming-based argument presupposes phenomenal consciousness rather than establishing it against someone who denies it. The thin seeming that an unpleasant experience is occurring stays secure. The thick seeming that the experience exceeds any functional description gains no protection from PC against the illusionist.

## Why the Question Is Not Terminological {#definition}

The standard reason for treating seemings as distinct from beliefs is the persistence of illusion. In the Müller-Lyer figure one line looks longer than the other even after measuring has convinced the viewer that the lines are equal. In Huemer's words, things "continue to appear a certain way even when one does not believe that things are as they appear" (Huemer, IEP). A seeming can therefore be present when the belief is absent, and even when the opposite belief is held.

Beyond that shared starting point, agreement ends. Huemer notes in the same survey that which view of seemings one takes "may dramatically affect the plausibility of PC," and he declines to define the category at all: "I have not defined “appearance”, and I do not believe it is possible to do so." He points to it by contrast instead: believing P because P *seems* true differs from believing it because one wants it true, and he takes this contrast to pick out one natural category. Whether it does is the question.

## Four Analyses {#four-analyses}

Huemer's encyclopedia survey sorts the main accounts roughly as follows (proponents as he assigns them):

| Analysis | Representative proponents | Is the seeming phenomenal? | Main difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Belief view.** A seeming is a tentative belief that P | Chisholm (1957) suggested something in this neighbourhood | Not necessarily | Illusion persistence: the appearance outlasts the belief |
| **Disposition view.** A seeming is an inclination or disposition to believe P | Sosa (1998); Swinburne (2001); Armstrong (1961) | Not necessarily | Wishful thinking yields inclinations to believe that are not seemings |
| **Higher-order view.** A seeming is a belief, or disposition to believe, that some state of mine is evidence for P | Conee (2013); Tooley (2013) | Not necessarily | If this is right, PC loses its plausibility: a mere disposition to believe one has evidence confers little justification |
| **Experience view.** A seeming is a *sui generis* experience with propositional content, not analysable as belief | Huemer; most phenomenal conservatives (surveyed in Tucker 2013) | Yes | Must say what the shared feature is across perception, intuition, memory and introspection, and whether it is really felt |

Huemer's objection to the disposition view is an order-of-explanation point. When a seeming is present, "one is disposed to believe that P because it seems to one that P, and not the other way around." The seeming is one possible ground for the disposition and cannot be the disposition itself.

Two of the categories on the PC page's list, *felt inclination short of belief* and *broadly representational state*, cut across this table instead of adding to it. Representational content is common to every non-doxastic view; even on the experience view a seeming is, in Huemer's words, "an internal mental representation." Every side agrees that a seeming represents; the live question is whether its pushing of its content as true is felt or is only a functional profile. A "felt inclination" is the experience view stated from the side of its effects, and a "mere inclination" is the disposition view. The whole debate turns on that one qualifier.

## The Live Fork: Forcefulness or Functional Profile {#live-fork}

**Huemer's side.** In his early work on perception Huemer names the distinguishing feature *forcefulness*: perceptual experience is not neutral about the world. "Objects are presented to us in experience as real" (Huemer 1998, §3.4), whereas a vivid imagining of an orange cat adds no force to the proposition that a cat is there. *Skepticism and the Veil of Perception* (2001) builds its epistemology on this feature. The PC page records that this diagnostic is "itself contested rather than agreed." Critics such as Michael DePaul (2009) question whether perception, intuition, memory and self-awareness share any interesting feature at all.

**Frankish's side.** Frankish gives an explicit analysis in functional terms. On it, a thing seems to exist for a person "if the person is undergoing psychological effects that are similar to those an encounter with the thing would produce and that at least incline them to believe that the thing does exist" (Frankish 2023). Phenomenal realists, he adds, will say that phenomenal properties accompany these effects, "but illusionists will of course deny that; seeming need not be phenomenal seeming." This is a refined disposition view. Its similarity clause matters, because it answers Huemer's wishful-thinking counterexample. Wanting P to be true does not produce the effects an encounter with P would produce, so wishful thinking is not a seeming on Frankish's account either. The objection that sinks the bare disposition view does not automatically sink this one.

**The test case.** James Pryor supplies the sharpest way to state the fork. He locates perception's justificatory power in "the peculiar phenomenal force or way our experiences have of presenting propositions to us." He adds that beliefs "might come to us irresistibly, without having that kind of phenomenal force," and suggests, tentatively, that this is what happens in super-[blindsight](/concepts/blindsight/) (Pryor 2000). Imagine, then, a subject whose encounter-like effects and belief inclinations are fully in place but who has no felt force. On Frankish's analysis the subject has a seeming. On Huemer's and Pryor's, the subject has the downstream profile of a seeming without the feature that justifies. Neither side's own resources decide the case. Pryor concedes that "it is difficult to explain what this phenomenal force amounts to," and Frankish's analysis settles by definition what the phenomenal side takes to be the substantive question.

## Pryor's Dogmatism: The Perceptual Cousin {#dogmatism}

Pryor's *dogmatism* is the closest relative of PC and is often paired with it. To a first approximation, Pryor holds that "whenever you have an experience as of p's being the case, you thereby have immediate (prima facie) justification for believing p" (Pryor 2000). Two features place it on the map of this debate.

First, it is restricted. Its principle concerns perceptual experience, and Pryor explicitly leaves introspection aside: perhaps one also has immediate introspective justification for believing one has an experience as of p, but "that's a question which I do not take up here." PC is the general thesis covering intuitive, memorial and introspective seemings. Because the evidence the Map relies on is introspective, only PC's generality reaches it.

Second, on the fork above, dogmatism stands with Huemer: what justifies is phenomenal force, not irresistibility. The two principles that made seemings central to recent epistemology therefore both take the phenomenal side. The disposition and higher-order views come mostly from critics or from rival epistemologies. Neither Huemer nor Pryor argues for dualism. PC and dogmatism are metaphysically neutral, and a physicalist can hold either.

## The Map's Reading {#map-reading}

The Map adopts the experience view, as its own proposal. A seeming that justifies is a non-doxastic state that presents its content as true, and the presenting is felt. Three considerations support this, none of them decisive.

1. **It fits what justification needs.** The [functional-seeming](/concepts/functional-seeming/) page states PC's reply to Frankish: a disposition to believe P is not yet evidence for P. Frankish's similarity clause filters out wishful thinking, but it does not supply what the filtered states have in common. That common feature has to be something other than "produces encounter-like effects," or the account risks becoming reliabilism under another name.
2. **The principles were built on it.** Huemer's forcefulness and Pryor's phenomenal force are the features their principles rest on. A functional seeming could be kept, but it would carry the word without the justifying role.
3. **Presentation is the datum.** A seeming on this reading is a case of [phenomenal-presentation](/concepts/phenomenal-presentation/) with propositional content: something shows up *as* so. The Map already treats presentation as the most basic datum of consciousness.

The strongest rival is Frankish's analysis, stated [above](#live-fork) at full strength. It makes seemings available to a theory that denies phenomenal properties, by specifying them without reference to feel. That same feature is what the Map disputes (see [Relation to Site Perspective](#relation-to-site-perspective)).

## What Turns on It {#what-turns}

The choice of analysis decides which of the Map's evidential claims survive, and against whom:

| Evidential claim | Experience view (the Map's) | Functional / disposition view |
|---|---|---|
| The thin seeming that an unpleasant experience is occurring is evidence | Secure; close to constitutive self-knowledge | Evidence only of a state with encounter-like effects |
| The thick seeming that the experience exceeds functional description | Evidence for anyone who grants that seemings are felt, but it begs the question against the illusionist | Available against the illusionist, but it attests only a disposition, which is no evidence of phenomenality |
| PC's self-defeat argument against rival epistemologies | Holds | Holds; the argument needs seemings, not felt ones |
| The illusionist's regress reply ("the illusion needs no experiencer") | Fails: an illusion of force is a felt force | Succeeds |
| Trained contemplative seemings as evidence | Subject to the etiological test in [cognitive-penetration](/concepts/cognitive-penetration/) | Same test; passing it shows only a stable disposition |

The second row is the cost, and the Map states it without softening. The [phenomenal-conservatism](/concepts/phenomenal-conservatism/) page already warns that the thin seeming's security "does not transfer to the thick one." This page adds the reason. On the experience view, a seeming justifies only because it is felt. So using the seeming that pain *irreducibly* hurts as evidence against the illusionist assumes the very point the illusionist denies. On the functional view that assumption drops out, but the seeming then attests nothing phenomenal. Either way PC does not win the thick claim. The dualist case against the illusionist therefore rests on the [hard problem](/topics/hard-problem-of-consciousness/), the [knowledge argument](/concepts/knowledge-argument/), and the difficulties the functional-seeming page raises about self-representation and the absence of any vantage point. Against the illusionist, seemings supply only the explanandum.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)** (Tenet 1). On the Map's reading the seeming is the datum. For a justificatory seeming to exist at all, something must be presented as true to a subject, and that presentation is phenomenal. This adds nothing new to the argument for dualism, since the argument's weight stays with the hard problem. What it does is fix where the Map's evidence starts. Under the [evidential-status discipline](/project/evidential-status-discipline/), seemings earn phenomenal reports a place as evidence without settling what those reports show.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)** (Tenet 5). Frankish's analysis is simpler: one kind of state, functionally specified, with no phenomenal remainder. The Map reads the simplicity as bought by renaming the explanandum. The [phenomenal-presentation](/concepts/phenomenal-presentation/) page makes the general point: redescribing the passive given as an active disposition "drops what was to be explained." Applied here, one can specify a state's encounter-like effects and belief inclinations completely, and the question "why is being in that state like being *pushed* toward P?" carries over unchanged. That is the structure of [the relocation objection](/concepts/the-relocation-objection/). Its limits apply as well. Relocation earns a dialectical result and gives dualism no explanatory advantage. The illusionist can reply that the carried-over question is itself part of the illusion. That disagreement sits at the boundary between frameworks, and neither side's resources close it.
