Depersonalisation — in the clinical manuals *depersonalization/derealization disorder* (DPDR; DSM-5 300.6, ICD-11 6B66), in much of the philosophical literature *depersonalisation disorder* (DPD) — is a persistent or recurrent alteration in which one's own thoughts, feelings, body and actions, or the world around one, are experienced as unreal, detached, or observed from outside. Three structural facts make it the clinical datum the Map cites more often than any other. Insight is retained: DSM-5 Criterion B requires that "reality testing remains intact" (as transcribed from the manual by secondary sources), so the patient who feels unreal knows she is not. Reports take an "as if" form — the Cambridge Depersonalisation Scale item "Parts of my body feel as if they didn't belong to me" (Sierra & Berrios 2000, as quoted in Billon 2016) is representative — and that preface belongs to the phenomenological tradition and the scale, not to the diagnostic text. And perception and cognition are otherwise intact: the patient sees, remembers, reasons and acts normally while the feeling that any of it is hers, or real, has drained away.

The Map's verdict on this datum runs on two tiers, as it does for [Cotard delusion](/concepts/cotard-delusion/). At the *architecture* tier, depersonalisation establishes that something normally bound to experience can come apart from experiential content without damage to the substrate that delivers the content; whether that something is a single phenomenal feature of for-me-ness (the Map's reading, shared with Billon), emotional colouring, a form of awareness, the transparency of self-consciousness, or a bundle of local self-relations is the [contested question below](#four-readings-of-what-comes-apart). At the *significance* tier the result is null: no reading on the table discriminates dualism from physicalism, two of the readings are fully specified physicalist mechanisms, and the philosopher whose work the Map leans on hardest suggests that what is missing "can only be explained in purely neurophysiological" terms. This page states the datum once so that the Map's six other treatments of it can point here rather than restate it.

## The Clinical Datum

The documented result is diagnostic and psychometric before it is philosophical. DSM-5 Criterion A describes experiences of "unreality, detachment, or being an outside observer" with respect to one's thoughts, feelings, sensations, body or actions (depersonalisation) or surroundings (derealisation); Criterion B requires intact reality testing; ICD-11 6B66 carries the same two-part description. Transient depersonalisation is common — a systematic review gives a lifetime prevalence of between 26 and 74 per cent in the general population, with clinically significant DPDR "in the region of 1-2%" (Hunter, Sierra & David 2004, abstract) — so the disorder is the chronic form of a common experience.

The Cambridge Depersonalisation Scale (Sierra & Berrios 2000) is the standard instrument, and its exploratory factor analysis found four factors — Anomalous Body Experience, Emotional Numbing, Anomalous Subjective Recall and Alienation from Surroundings — concluding that the symptoms "belong to distinct but related psychopathological domains" (Sierra, Baker, Medford & David 2005, abstract). That structure is the psychometric fact any one-feature reading has to accommodate, and it is where the localist reading starts. Two further facts travel with the datum: the patient's limbic activation to aversive emotional stimuli is reduced (Sierra & David 2011, abstract), and, as Gerrans (2019) puts it in a formulation every reading accepts, "It is important to this account that cognition and perception are otherwise intact".

The relation to Cotard delusion is a continuum on the majority reading, contested by Gerrans; the [Cotard page](/concepts/cotard-delusion/#two-factors-one-factor-and-the-depersonalisation-continuum) carries that debate. The one-line version is Billon's: depersonalisation is "the 'as if' form of the Cotard syndrome" (Billon 2016), and Davies and Coltheart (2022, abstract) propose that "in at least some cases" the delusional idea "I am dead" arises in response to depersonalisation symptoms.

## Four Readings of What Comes Apart

That something comes apart is not in dispute. What it is divides the literature into four families. Their disagreement is about the architecture of experience; on the metaphysics none takes a side the Map could use.

### The Affective Reading

The neurobiological account that framed the modern literature treats the subtraction as emotional. Sierra and Berrios (1998, abstract) proposed a corticolimbic model on which a prefrontal mechanism inhibits the amygdala, producing "dampened autonomic output, hypoemotionality, and lack of emotional coloring that would in turn, be reported as feelings of 'unreality or detachment.'" Sierra and David (2011, abstract) updated it to a fronto-limbic suppressive mechanism that "disables the process by which perception and cognition normally become emotionally coloured, giving rise to a subjective feeling of 'unreality'"; Medford (2012, abstract) names the reduced emotional responsivity "de-affectualization". No for-me-ness component is posited: "unreality" is how a system reports uncoloured perception.

Gerrans (2019, abstract) is the predictive-coding descendant. On his account "The 'self' to which experience is attributed is a predictive model made by the mind to explain the modulation of affect", and when the world stops producing predicted affect the system must explain the "unpredicted absence of feeling". The subtraction is still affective; the sense of absence is the model's inference from it. The reading grants separability — of affect from content — under a different description, and closes the significance question by construction. Its pressure points are Knappik's observation (below) that "a breakdown of affective feelings can leave the other factors intact" in post-traumatic numbing, and the factor analysis itself, in which emotional numbing is one domain of four rather than the root of the other three.

### Billon: The Lack of Subjective Character

Alexandre Billon's work across four papers is the reading closest to the Map's and the strongest form of the architecture claim. Depersonalised patients, on his account, have experiences "essentially characterised by a (more or less important) lack of subjective character" together with two further structural features he calls "the present character" and "the actual character" (Billon 2016); derealisation is the loss of the actual character, and Cotard's delusions "simply consist in taking these anomalous experiences at face value". The 2023 chapter restates the claim in the Map's vocabulary: depersonalisation consists in "the lack of a phenomenal feature that marks my experiences as mine", usually called mineness, and the disorder is "a neglected yet incomparable probe" of that feature's scope, role and nature (Billon 2023). Two qualifiers in his text do real work. The "as if" is, on the usual interpretation Billon adopts, a "mark of mere appearance" (Billon 2023): the patient reports that it seems as if her body is not hers while knowing that it is. And the subject is not subtracted — the most intense lack of subjective character would "make the subject feel as if he is not an 'I'" (Billon 2016); the subject persists and feels, and what the experience lacks is its subjective *character*. Mineness so understood "is a feature of experience", distinct from the sense of ownership, which "is a form of awareness" (Billon 2023).

This reading is the one most hospitable to separable for-me-ness, and its author's conclusions stop well short of anything metaphysical. On scope Billon is a generalist, not a universalist: mineness is "general, if not universal", present by default and able to lapse partially or globally. On nature his verdict is neurophysiological: the probe "allows us to reject many psychological theories" of mineness, and the chapter suggests that "mineness can only be explained in purely neurophysiological (as opposed to 'psychological or neuropsychological') terms" (Billon 2023). The 2024 paper on derealisation runs the same way: having described DPD as occurring "without delusional ideas" and "accompanied by insight", he draws on the patients "to reject, and indeed diagnose, the intuition of unreality", concluding that "The intuition of reality is correct, then, and it is an intuition of substantiality" (Billon 2024). So Billon supports the Map's reading at the first two levels of the dissociation chain, separability and phenomenological reality, and points away from the fourth; citing him for anything stronger recruits him to a conclusion he denies. One patient report he collects stands for the whole datum: "[It is like] seeing life as if it were played like a film in a movie. But in that case where am I? Who is watching the film?" (Simeon & Abugel 2006, as quoted in Billon 2017). The question is still being asked by someone.

### The Active-Inference Self-Model

The strongest physicalist rival to the Map's reading does not deny a separable self-component; it supplies one, as a computational variable. Seth, Suzuki and Critchley (2012) model conscious presence as the "successful suppression by top-down predictions of informative interoceptive signals" and treat depersonalisation and derealisation as the chronic loss of presence when that suppression fails. Ciaunica, Seth, Limanowski, Hesp and Friston (2022, abstract) extend the model to the disorder: depersonalisation reflects "failures to attenuate or modulate sensory precision", which the system explains with the hypothesis "I am an embodied perceiver, but I am not in control of my perception" — while "maintaining full insight that the 'other agent' is 'me'". That last clause is Criterion B restated in active-inference terms. Deane, Miller and Wilkinson (2020) give the framework its comparative form: selfhood emerges from a "temporally deep generative model", depersonalisation "may result from an inferred loss of allostatic control", and the structurally similar "'selfless' experiences" of contemplatives are "highly sought after" — the valence of the absence is independent of the absence, a point [comparative-phenomenology-of-meditative-traditions](/topics/comparative-phenomenology-of-meditative-traditions/) develops.

The reading matters for a reason [clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence](/topics/clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence/) has already conceded: a precision disturbance in interoceptive prediction is *expected* to produce a globally felt loss of reality and mineness, because on this model presence just is the successful weighting of those predictions. The breadth of the change, once an objection to any narrow functional story, is predicted by a single parameter. Ciaunica, Charlton and Farmer (2021) add a phenomenological hybrid: depersonalisation alters the transparency of pre-reflective self-consciousness, revealing it "in the same way that a crack in a transparent glass reveals the presence of an unnoticed window", with patients "aware that these are subjective phenomena rather than the objective reality". The Map's cracked-window image comes from here, and what cracks for its authors is [transparency](/concepts/revelation-thesis/#two-senses-of-phenomenal-transparency), not a feature of mode. Seth, Friston and Ciaunica build physicalist models of the self and are engaged here as rivals, not allies; the Map adopts the mechanics without the metaphysics.

### Universalism and Localism

The two remaining readings contest the architecture claim from opposite sides, and neither says anything about physicalism. Zahavi and Kriegel (2016) hold that "What-it-is-like-ness is properly speaking what-it-is-like-for-me-ness", that for-me-ness is "a universal feature of experience", and that "once anything occurs consciously, it must be given to the subject and thus exhibit for-me-ness". Decisively, for-me-ness on their view "does not require there to be a detachable self quale that one could introspect in isolation from any other content of consciousness". If so it cannot be what depersonalisation subtracts, because it cannot be subtracted while experience persists. Their reply to the pathology objection is made for [thought insertion](/concepts/thought-insertion/): inserted thoughts "cannot lack minimal ownership altogether, since the afflicted subject is aware that it is she herself rather than somebody else who is experiencing them". Gallagher and Zahavi's encyclopaedia entry applies the point to depersonalisation by name — an experience "that the subject claims not to be his is nonetheless experienced by him as being part of his stream of consciousness" — and Henriksen and Parnas (2019), answering Billon's 2013 thought-insertion argument, hold that some "ownership or for-me-ness is retained even in these psychopathological experiences". Neither Zahavi and Kriegel (2016) nor Henriksen and Parnas (2019) mentions depersonalisation disorder; their relevance is by extension from thought insertion, and no depersonalisation claim is attributed to either. The universalist thus reads the coherence that [mine-ness](/concepts/mine-ness/) reads as evidence for separability — the patient can say *as if not mine* — as evidence that for-me-ness survives: the alienation can be reported only because the report is hers. On the introspective evidence this is a standoff. The Map's own [kind-claim](/concepts/self-and-self-consciousness/#constitutive-as-kind-not-as-degree) sides with the universalist about the reporting position and with Billon (2016, 2023) about the felt feature, without settling the standoff.

Knappik (2022) attacks from the other side. The global-feature accounts — Billon's three characters, Gerrans's flow of affect, Ciaunica's transparency — all owe an explanation of why depersonalisation and derealisation occur independently and why predicted symptoms are often absent; Billon's three posited characters answer it, "But this just adds to the substantive theoretical assumptions". Knappik's alternative starts from the observation that "many mental states relate to the subject because of the kind of state they belong to", collects such cases as "type-specific self-relation" — the autonoetic relation of episodic memory, the bodily relation of sensation — and argues that most symptoms of the disorder are impairments of these local relations, which break independently. On this reading there is no mode-feature laid over all experience to be lost; what separates is built into particular state types. [mine-ness](/concepts/mine-ness/)'s standing reply, that a plural for-me-ness is still a family of separable phenomenal features, survives Knappik only if each type-specific relation is itself phenomenal rather than functional, which his paper does not grant.

## What the Datum Shows: Two Tiers

The dissociation chain the Map applies to clinical evidence has four levels — separability, phenomenological reality, mechanistic independence, metaphysical irreducibility — and depersonalisation must be located on it. Separability is the documented clinical result: perception, cognition and insight are intact while a felt dimension of the same experiences has altered. The phenomenological reality of the separated feature *as a feature* is a contested interpretation: Billon and the Map affirm it, the affective reading redescribes it as missing emotional colour, the universalist denies it varies at all, and the localist denies it is one thing. Mechanistic independence is supplied by every rival — corticolimbic inhibition, interoceptive precision failure, impaired transparency, broken type-specific relations — so the datum offers mechanisms in abundance and no way to choose. Irreducibility is reached by nothing on the table.

The architecture-tier claim is therefore defensible at the first level and contested at the second. What the Map may add, as its own reconstruction rather than a finding, is that depersonalisation is the best within-subject exhibit of the [discrimination-problem](/concepts/discrimination-problem/) at the level of a phenomenal feature: the same subject, with the same first-order content available, reports the for-me character degraded while the content stays fixed. The reconstruction carries its own caveats — the universalist holds there is nothing here to discriminate, the localist that there are several things — and the "self-model ascending channel" of [neurological-dissociations-as-interface-architecture](/topics/neurological-dissociations-as-interface-architecture/) is the Map's organising vocabulary, not a description the data impose.

The significance-tier claim is null. The only route from "separable" to "non-physical" runs through a bridging premise: that a feature which varies independently of content and of any single functional alteration must be contributed by something outside the functional organisation. That premise is the dualist thesis itself, so the inference would assume what it set out to establish; the honest form is the conditional — *if* no functional organisation could ground a feature of mode, the separability result is where its absence would show — and the antecedent is exactly what the active-inference reading denies with a worked mechanism. The result is compatible with the Map's interface reading and with each of its rivals, and the discipline that governs the corpus forbids counting compatible evidence as support.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Dualism (Tenet 1)](/tenets/#dualism)** — compatible with, not evidence for; [mine-ness](/concepts/mine-ness/) and [cotard-delusion](/concepts/cotard-delusion/) both hold "compatible". A phenomenal feature varying while content is held fixed is the shape of result the Map's interface architecture predicts. But the physicalist self-model account predicts the same result from a single precision parameter, the affective account from inhibited emotional colouring, and the author of the phenomenological reading the Map adopts reads the datum as grounds for neurophysiological reduction. Nothing in depersonalisation discriminates; the gain is framework-internal coherence, which raises no void's independence score.

**[Minimal Quantum Interaction (Tenet 2)](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) and [Bidirectional Interaction (Tenet 3)](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)** — nothing. The datum says nothing about the mechanism by which any non-physical contribution would enter the brain, and the patient's "as if" report is ordinary physical output on every reading.

**[No Many Worlds (Tenet 4)](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)** — consistent with, not evidence for, the persisting subject the indexical objection presupposes: the patient who asks who is watching the film is the one asking. The rival's persisting self-model does the asking equally well.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits (Tenet 5)](/tenets/#occams-limits)** — binding on this page's own tidiness. The simplest story, one feature with one failure, is the one the four-factor structure and the localist reading tell against; the tenet's warning cuts against the Map's single-channel phrasing as readily as against the rival's single parameter.
