Mental imagery — perceptual representation without direct sensory input — is one of the most accessible test cases for the claim that consciousness does causal work. When you deliberately visualise a movement, the causal chain runs, on the Map's reading, from conscious imagining to physical performance: athletes who mentally rehearse improve measurably, stroke patients who add motor imagery to therapy regain more arm function, and learners rehearsing a piano exercise in their heads show the motor-map changes physical practice produces. Voluntary mental imagery requires conscious engagement and lateral prefrontal cortex (LPFC) activation, distinguishing it from involuntary imagery like dreams or intrusive flashbacks. This voluntary/involuntary distinction maps onto the conscious/unconscious distinction, and the two systems produce measurably different outcomes. If [epiphenomenalism](/concepts/epiphenomenalism/) were true — if consciousness merely observed neural activity without influencing it — the phenomenology of effortful visualisation would be deeply mysterious: we would feel ourselves *directing* imagery while actually directing nothing. The evidence is consistent with consciousness's causal efficacy, though as with all arguments for [bidirectional-interaction](/concepts/bidirectional-interaction/), it does not uniquely establish the dualist interpretation against a fully spelled-out identity theory: the identity theorist reads the same causal chain as neural rehearsal under another description, an explanatory standoff set out below.

## What Mental Imagery Is

Mental imagery is perceptual experience in the absence of direct sensory input. You can visualise a red apple without an apple before you, hear a melody without sound waves, or feel the texture of sandpaper without touching anything. Imagery operates across all sensory modalities — visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, gustatory — and can involve complex scenes, movements, or abstract spatial relationships.

Classical philosophy treated imagery as essentially conscious — Hume's "ideas" were the furniture of conscious thought. Contemporary neuroscience complicates this: imagery can be either conscious or unconscious, and either voluntary or involuntary — a 2×2 matrix of possibilities.[^nanay]

| | Voluntary | Involuntary |
|---|---|---|
| **Conscious** | Deliberate visualisation | Earworms, flashbacks |
| **Unconscious** | (Rare/contested) | Priming effects |

The critical case for [bidirectional-interaction](/concepts/bidirectional-interaction/) involves the upper-left cell: deliberate, conscious visualisation. When you voluntarily generate mental imagery, consciousness is not merely observing — it is directing neural activity.

## The Voluntary/Involuntary Dissociation

The most philosophically significant finding in imagery research is that voluntary and involuntary imagery rely on different neural mechanisms.

**Voluntary imagery** is mediated by the lateral prefrontal cortex. Mental rotation tasks, deliberate planning, and creative visualisation all require LPFC engagement. Damage to LPFC impairs the ability to voluntarily combine mental objects while leaving dreaming intact.[^vyshedskiy]

**Involuntary imagery** operates independently of LPFC. Dreams, spontaneous daydreaming, and intrusive images like PTSD flashbacks arise without deliberate initiation. Many people with aphantasia — the inability to voluntarily generate conscious mental images — still experience visual dreams, demonstrating the dissociation between these systems.[^zeman]

The distinction is robust. The systems are dissociable not merely in principle but in specific clinical populations. This matters philosophically because voluntary imagery is conscious by nature: you cannot deliberately visualise something without being aware that you are doing it. Involuntary imagery can be either conscious or unconscious — priming effects can operate through imagery processes that never reach awareness.[^nanay] The voluntary/involuntary distinction tracks the conscious/unconscious distinction, and these map onto different neural substrates.

### Why Imagery Is a Distinctive Test Case

Among the lines bearing on whether consciousness causes anything — the [evolutionary argument](/topics/evolutionary-case-for-mental-causation/), the [self-stultification problem](/concepts/epiphenomenalism/#the-self-stultification-problem), the [empirical evidence for consciousness selecting](/topics/empirical-evidence-for-consciousness-selecting/) outcomes — imagery is distinctive in combining three features rarely found together:

**Observable voluntariness.** You can choose to visualise something specific — a red triangle, a piano chord, the feel of sandpaper — and report what you are doing while doing it. This makes the conscious component accessible to introspection and, to some degree, to third-person study.

**Measurable downstream effects.** Mental practice produces small but replicated performance gains across decades of studies,[^toth] and adding it to stroke rehabilitation improves upper-limb function.[^barclay]

**A natural control condition.** The existence of involuntary imagery provides a contrast case where imagery occurs without conscious direction. If voluntary and involuntary imagery differed only in their trigger but produced identical downstream effects, consciousness would look incidental. They do not.

## Aphantasia and Hyperphantasia

Individual variation in imagery vividness ranges dramatically across the population:

- **Aphantasia** (~1% of population): inability to voluntarily generate conscious mental images. When asked to visualise a red apple, aphantasics report knowing what an apple looks like while seeing nothing.[^zeman]
- **Hyperphantasia** (~3% of population): exceptionally vivid mental imagery, sometimes difficult to distinguish from perception.

Despite profound differences in subjective experience, both groups function effectively in daily life. Aphantasics navigate and do creative work, though aphantasia is variably associated with reduced autobiographical memory and face-recognition difficulty and is overrepresented in mathematical and scientific roles; hyperphantasics are overrepresented in creative industries.[^zeman]

The [dual-domain analysis](/topics/dual-domain-capabilities-in-proprioception-and-spatial-imagination/) reads this dissociation as separating physical spatial computation, which aphantasics retain, from a phenomenal spatial workspace that enhances it without being strictly required.

This spectrum poses a puzzle for consciousness research. If broadly similar neural machinery produces vivid imagery in one person and nothing in another, what explains the difference? One hypothesis: aphantasics have sub-personal imagery representations but lack conscious access to them.[^nanay] Partly supporting this, imagery content can be decoded from aphantasics' primary visual cortex as well as from controls', though the signal is atypical — ipsilateral, and unlike the patterns perception produces — so a representation is present, though not the perception-like one controls have.[^chang]

This interpretation aligns with the Map's [attention-as-interface](/concepts/attention-as-interface/) hypothesis. Imagery generation may be automatic; consciousness then selects which imagery to attend to, bringing it into awareness and enabling voluntary manipulation. The reliability of [introspective reports](/concepts/introspection/) about imagery vividness is supported by convergent evidence: aphantasics' self-reports correlate with distinct cognitive strategies on mental rotation tasks (slower but more accurate, using analytic rather than object-based methods)[^kay] and with distinct neural activation patterns during imagery attempts. What introspection still cannot verify about its own imagery — fidelity to referent, equivalence of vividness across persons, whether the phenomenal image does the cognitive work it appears to — is the [imagery-void](/voids/imagery-void/).

## The Motor Simulation Argument

Francis Bacon observed that "imagination ever precedeth voluntary motion."[^bacon] Contemporary motor simulation theory, developed by Marc Jeannerod, gives this observation empirical grounding: imagined movements serve as internal simulations that the motor system can later execute.[^jeannerod] The brain treats imagined and executed movements similarly at the planning level, activating overlapping regions of premotor cortex, supplementary motor area, and parietal cortex.

This neural overlap has practical consequences:

- **Athletic training.** Mental practice benefits performance, moderated by task type, retention interval and practice duration;[^driskell] a 2020 replication across 37 later studies found the effect small but significant (r = 0.131).[^toth]
- **Rehabilitation.** Adding mental practice to stroke therapy improves upper-limb activity and impairment (moderate-certainty evidence), though not daily-living activities.[^barclay]
- **Music performance.** People learning a five-finger piano exercise by mental rehearsal alone show the same motor-map changes as physical practice, and improve, though less.[^pascual-leone]

The causal argument runs:

1. Athletes who mentally rehearse movements improve their actual performance measurably.
2. The effects are specific to the *content* of what is imagined — the motor maps that change are those of the muscles the imagined exercise uses.[^pascual-leone]
3. The content of the imagined sequence is consciously specified — the athlete chooses what to rehearse.
4. Therefore, what is consciously imagined causally contributes to what physically improves.

If consciousness were epiphenomenal, the felt effort of visualising a movement would be causally disconnected from the improvement that follows.

## The Explanatory Standoff with Identity Theory

The argument above is not watertight. The identity theorist responds that conscious experience *is* the neural rehearsal — not a correlated accompaniment but the very same thing described at a different level. The LPFC engages top-down control mechanisms, and those mechanisms do not merely *correlate with* phenomenal experience; according to identity theory, they *constitute* it. The causal work is done by states that are simultaneously neural and conscious, with no remainder.

This is a genuine explanatory standoff. Both the dualist and identity-theory accounts predict that voluntary imagery (with its distinctive neural signature) produces measurable performance gains. The evidence alone does not definitively settle which account is correct, and the primary opponent here is identity theory rather than epiphenomenalism, a fringe position.

Motor imagery may be one of the cleanest illustrations of the [trumping](/concepts/trumping-preemption/) or *authority-selecting* reading of conscious causation, an option distinct from both dualist-interventionist and identity-theorist positions. On that template, the neural rehearsal retains counterfactual sufficiency for the improvement, while causal authority belongs to the conscious imagining — the physical trajectory is unchanged and only the causal attribution moves to the experiential layer. Imagery fits the template well: the phenomenology of manipulating an image is richly present and the neural machinery is sufficient on its own. The fit has a price. Because the template moves no outcome, no imagery result can confirm it, and it cannot carry the self-stultification argument below: a trumping dualist who keeps that invisibility agrees with the epiphenomenalist on every possible report ([fourth cost](/concepts/trumping-preemption/#costs-of-the-template)).

Three considerations press against a pure identity account.

### Content Matching

Imagery perspective changes outcomes. First-person (internal) imagery of a movement produces more muscle activity than third-person (external) imagery,[^hale] while for tasks in which form matters, external imagery improves performance more.[^hardy] Which perspective helps depends on the task, but either way a phenomenological difference — experiencing the movement from within or watching yourself from outside — tracks a measurable one. The identity theorist can respond that perspective differences simply *are* differences in motor circuit engagement, but this highlights a pattern: whenever phenomenal properties vary, the identity theorist must insist that exactly the right neural property also varies, in exactly the right way, to do the causal work. The dualist's explanation is more direct — phenomenal character contributes causally because it is a genuine causal property in its own right, not a redescription of something neural.

### The Aphantasia Prediction

The dualist and identity-theory accounts generate different predictions about aphantasia. If phenomenal vividness is identical to the neural property doing the causal work, then individuals with intact LPFC function but reduced phenomenal imagery should show reduced benefits *only if* their neural imagery circuits are correspondingly impaired. If phenomenal vividness is a distinct causal property, then reduced phenomenal imagery should predict reduced benefits even when the neural circuits are intact. This is a testable divergence. In a controlled study, aphantasics show no rise in corticospinal excitability during motor imagery, the neurophysiological signature controls show during kinaesthetic imagery.[^dupont] That result bears on the divergence in a way that currently favours the identity-theory branch rather than the dualist one — it indicates the neural simulation circuits are themselves impaired in aphantasia, which is precisely the condition under which identity theory expects reduced benefit. Isolating a distinct phenomenal contribution would require the harder result — reduced rehearsal benefit despite *intact* neural circuits — and no study has yet paired longitudinal performance outcomes with neural activation in the same aphantasic subjects. The prediction therefore still cannot bear argumentative weight for the dualist position; if anything, the newest evidence points the other way, which is exactly the evidential restraint the Map's calibration demands.

### Self-Stultification

The [self-stultification argument](/concepts/epiphenomenalism/) applies with particular force to imagery — though it targets epiphenomenalism specifically, not identity theory. When you report that you visualised a red triangle and describe its properties, you are making claims about the content of your conscious experience. If consciousness is epiphenomenal, these reports are caused entirely by neural processes that have no causal connection to the conscious imagery itself. Your description of the triangle's redness would be right only by coincidence. The identity theorist avoids this problem because on their view, the conscious experience *is* the relevant neural state. Self-stultification therefore narrows the field of opponents rather than settling the main dispute with physicalism.

## Cognitive Phenomenology Without Imagery

Aphantasia provides evidence for non-imagistic conscious thought. Some aphantasics report having conscious thoughts without any sensory mental imagery — purely [cognitive phenomenology](/concepts/cognitive-phenomenology/). They think, reason, and experience understanding without visual, auditory, or other sensory accompaniments.[^lennon]

This matters for consciousness theory because it challenges views that reduce conscious thought to sensory imagery. If thoughts can be phenomenally conscious without being sensory, [phenomenal-consciousness](/concepts/phenomenal-consciousness/) includes non-sensory aspects that simple imagery-based accounts miss. The conscious experience of understanding a concept, grasping a proof, or apprehending an argument may be *sui generis* — neither visual nor auditory, but distinctively cognitive. A broader phenomenal domain does not by itself widen consciousness's causal role; it widens the territory over which the causal question arises.

## What Imagery Doesn't Prove

Intellectual honesty requires acknowledging what mental imagery evidence cannot establish on its own:

**It does not uniquely establish causation.** The motor imagery evidence is *consistent with* consciousness's causal efficacy but does not *uniquely establish* it. The identity-theory response — that conscious states are causally efficacious *as* neural states, with nothing left over — remains logically available. The evidence provides confirmatory support for the Map's position rather than knockdown proof.

**It does not specify mechanism.** Even granting that consciousness causally contributes to imagery-guided action, the evidence does not tell us *how*. The Map's framework suggests consciousness operates through [attention-mediated](/concepts/attention-as-interface/) quantum selection, but motor imagery research neither confirms nor disconfirms this. The causal contribution of consciousness could operate through mechanisms not yet identified.

**It does not generalise automatically.** A causal role in voluntary imagery would not show one in every cognitive process; some functions may be fully automated, with consciousness epiphenomenal for them. The Map's claim is the weaker one that consciousness is causal in at least some processes, and voluntary imagery bears on that.

## The Broader Pattern

Mental imagery evidence gains force when situated alongside other lines of evidence for consciousness's causal efficacy. The [baseline cognition](/concepts/baseline-cognition/) analysis argues that cognition without metarepresentation — the sophisticated cognition great apes achieve without substantial conscious contribution — does not extend to logical reasoning, counterfactual thinking, or cumulative culture. The Map reads these as functions requiring conscious processing, with voluntary imagery one mechanism through which consciousness enables them: planning, creative combination, and counterfactual simulation all involve deliberately generated mental representations.

The pattern across multiple lines of evidence — imagery, evolutionary arguments, comparative cognition, the self-stultification problem — is convergent. No single line is decisive. Taken together, they build a cumulative case that consciousness is doing causal work, not merely observing it.

## Relation to Site Perspective

Mental imagery supports [bidirectional-interaction](/concepts/bidirectional-interaction/) through multiple channels:

**Voluntary imagery exemplifies conscious control.** The LPFC-mediated generation and manipulation of mental images requires conscious engagement. On the Map's reading, the effort you feel when visualising something complex reflects consciousness doing work rather than epiphenomenal accompaniment — an instance of [downward causation](/concepts/mental-causation-and-downward-causation/) from mental states to neural activity. That reading is the conclusion under dispute, not a datum: [the felt effort cannot itself verify](/voids/agency-void/) that it causes anything. This parallels findings from [contemplative neuroscience](/concepts/neurophenomenology-and-contemplative-neuroscience/): deliberate meditative attention alters brain function through similar top-down processes, though claims of structural change have not replicated. The Map acknowledges that identity theory also predicts this — conscious states are causal on that view too, just *as* neural states. The distinctive dualist claim is that phenomenal properties do causal work that is not reducible to their neural realisers.

**Motor imagery produces physical outcomes.** Athletes mentally rehearsing movements improve their actual performance. The effects are small but replicable, consistent with a causal pathway from conscious rehearsal to physical improvement. The [evolutionary-case-for-mental-causation](/topics/evolutionary-case-for-mental-causation/) offers an independent line: if imagery improved survival through motor rehearsal and planning, consciousness's role in imagery was plausibly selected for.

**The voluntary/involuntary distinction tracks the conscious/unconscious distinction.** Voluntary imagery uses different neural systems, feels effortful, and can be directed at will, unlike dreams. The physicalist can explain this through circuit differences alone; the Map grants the standoff but notes that consciousness consistently tracks the more flexible, goal-directed mode, as in [conscious-vs-unconscious-processing](/concepts/conscious-vs-unconscious-processing/), where consciousness correlates with functions unconscious processing cannot achieve.

**The [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism) tenet** finds a test in the imagery variation spectrum. Aphantasics and hyperphantasics differ profoundly in phenomenal experience, while the neural differences found so far are subtler: imagery content is decodable from aphantasics' primary visual cortex, in atypical form,[^chang] and frontoparietal-visual connectivity differs between groups.[^zeman] A phenomenal difference larger than the functional differences predict would be consistent with the claim that consciousness is irreducible; the atypical visual-cortex signal, the connectivity findings and the impaired simulation circuits reported above[^dupont] mark how much of that difference functional measures may yet absorb.

**The [Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits) tenet** applies directly to the explanatory standoff. Identity theory appears simpler — one set of states (neural/conscious) rather than two interacting kinds. But this apparent simplicity comes at a cost: it must treat the [hard problem](/topics/hard-problem-of-consciousness/) as dissolved or illusory. If phenomenal properties genuinely resist reduction to neural properties, the "simpler" account is not simpler but incomplete, missing a genuine causal factor that the full explanation requires.

On the Map's framework the chain runs attention → conscious imagery → action: attention selects which imagery becomes conscious, consciousness manipulates it, and the manipulated image guides what follows.
