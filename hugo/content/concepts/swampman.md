Swampman is Donald Davidson's lightning-formed molecular duplicate of a person: physically indistinguishable from the original, behaviourally indistinguishable at first meeting, but with no evolutionary history, no learning history, and no causal contact with anything it appears to talk about. The case matters to the Map because it is the standard thought experiment on mental content on which [teleosemantics](/concepts/teleosemantics/) and the Map's [phenomenal-intentionality](/concepts/intentionality/) view predict *opposite* things. Teleosemantics, on its strictest reading, says Swampman has no content at all, because content is fixed by selection history and Swampman has none. The Map's hybrid position on [content](/concepts/content-externalism/) gives a split verdict instead: *if he is conscious*, Swampman's states have internally constituted phenomenal character and whatever aboutness that character carries, while what his thoughts widely refer to is left open, since his "water" thoughts have no causal history with H₂O. That makes Swampman a discriminating case against the strict teleosemanticist, though not every teleosemanticist, and it exposes the Map's own cost, stated in full [below](#the-cost-the-map-pays): whether Swampman is conscious cannot be verified, Dretske argues on representationalist grounds that he is not, and on the Map's dualism physical duplication settles only part of the question.

## Davidson's Case

Davidson introduced Swampman in "Knowing One's Own Mind" (1987), a paper about first-person authority under externalism; the case occupies a paragraph. "Suppose lightning strikes a dead tree in a swamp; I am standing nearby. My body is reduced to its elements, while entirely by coincidence (and out of different molecules) the tree is turned into my physical replica." The replica leaves the swamp, appears to greet Davidson's friends, and "seems to write articles on radical interpretation. No one can tell the difference."

Davidson's verdict is that there is a difference all the same. "My replica can't recognize my friends; it can't recognize anything, since it never cognized anything in the first place." The sound "house" it produces was not learned in any context that would give it a meaning, and "I don't see how my replica can be said to mean anything by the sounds it makes, nor to have any thoughts." His reason is causal-historical rather than biological: content depends on the thinker's history of causal interaction with objects and other speakers, and Swampman has none.

Ruth Millikan had published a near-identical case three years earlier, with an explicitly evolutionary reason. A being formed by "some cosmic accident" as your exact physical double "would have no ideas, no beliefs, no intentions, no aspirations, no fears, and no hopes… This because the evolutionary history of the being would be wrong" (Millikan 1984, 93, as quoted in Millikan 1996).

## The Teleosemantic Verdict and Its Two Defences

On [teleosemantics](/concepts/teleosemantics/), a state has content in virtue of the biological proper function of the mechanisms that produce or consume it, and proper function is fixed by selection history. Swampman's mechanisms have no selection history, so they have no proper functions, so his states have no content. The objection is that a creature indistinguishable from you in every physical and behavioural respect surely thinks. Teleosemanticists answer in two main ways.

**Bite the bullet.** Millikan's "On Swampkinds" (1996) accepts the entailment and argues the intuition against it has no standing. Because nothing about Swampman's brain specifies "whether he uses his brain correctly or incorrectly," she suggests, "he thinks neither truly nor falsely, indeed, that he thinks not at all. An organ is not an eye if it focuses light for the purpose of warming the ova, or worse, for no purpose at all. Similarly, an activity is not thinking if it exists for no purpose at all." Her broader argument is that Swampman and Davidson do not belong to the same *real* kind: what makes humans members of one kind is historical and reproductive, not shared inner structure, so a head arranged like Davidson's does not put Swampman in the kind whose members think. Karen Neander's companion paper, "Swampman Meets Swampcow" (1996), runs parallel; Millikan reports her as objecting "that it is not in our province as philosophers to meddle with the biologist's definition of a species, a definition that clearly excludes Swampman from the species Homo sapiens." Both treat "intentionality" as chemistry treats "water": a kind whose real nature turned out to be historical, so the intuition that Swampman thinks is like the intuition that water needn't be H₂O. The analogy cannot carry the premise the kind argument needs, that "thinker" is individuated as species are, by lineage, rather than as chemical kinds are, by present structure: H₂O is structure, not history.

**Actual-world reduction.** David Papineau's "The Status of Teleosemantics, or How to Stop Worrying about Swampman" (2001) gives the fullest concession-and-reframe. He reports being "originally roused from my slumbers" by a graduate student, Eilert Sundt-Ohlsen, who pressed the ethical consequence: if Swamppeople have no mentality, "it would be all right to kill Swamppeople and eat them as meat." Louise Antony's "Equal Rights for Swamp-persons" had already put the question in print, in the same 1996 *Mind & Language* forum. Papineau grants that "if we did come across a Swampman, it would clearly be wrong to kill it for meat," and that simply overriding the intuition, a line "repeated many times, including by me," is "insufficiently nuanced."

His considered answer reframes the theory's status. Teleosemantics is a *scientific reduction*, on the model of water = H₂O, not a conceptual analysis: it claims that the folk roles of "belief" and "desire" are in fact filled by selectional states *in the actual world*. On that reading, "non-actual cases, by contrast, pose no threat at all," any more than possible worlds in which XYZ plays the watery role threaten water = H₂O. Only *actual* Swamppeople would count.

Two features matter here. First, Papineau allows that our moral and prudential concern "focus[es] on the roles, not the realizers," so that in Swamp-scenarios "our thinking tracks informational content, rather than selectional content." Second, he declines to say that Swampman lacks beliefs: "teleosemantics is not forced to deny beliefs and desires to counterfactual Swampbeings. This denial only follows if 'belief' and 'desire' are rigid designators," a semantic claim he does not regard as an essential part of teleosemantics. The strict no-content verdict is, on his 2001 view, optional. A third aside bears on the Map directly: when first pressed, Papineau considered the escape "Maybe Swamppeople don't have contentful beliefs and desires. But it doesn't follow that they aren't conscious," and abandoned it only because sentience alone does not, on orthodox morality, make killing wrong.

Papineau has since moved. In "Swampman, teleosemantics and kind essences" (2022) he argues that the structure of natural kinds "implies that selectional histories are indeed built into the nature of mental representation," a position closer to Millikan's kind-based bullet-biting than to the 2001 role/realiser split. Dongwoo Kim (2021) argues against the reframing directly, on grounds used [below](#where-the-verdicts-come-apart), so the debate is live.

## The Phenomenal-Intentionality Verdict

The phenomenal-intentionality tradition ([PIT](/concepts/intentionality/): Loar, Horgan and Tienson, Kriegel) holds that genuine aboutness is grounded in phenomenal consciousness. What a conscious state is about is partly constituted by what it is like to be in it. The tradition treats this phenomenal intentionality as *narrow*: shared by intrinsic duplicates whatever their environment or history. Horgan, Tienson and Graham (2004), writing on the brain in a vat, offer PIT as the best available theory of narrow content, and Loar (2003) is read the same way.

Swampman is an intrinsic duplicate too, and the verdict follows in two steps. If Swampman is phenomenally conscious, then he has whatever intentionality phenomenal consciousness carries: his visual experience presents a world of objects, his grasp of the sounds he makes has the [cognitive phenomenology](/concepts/cognitive-phenomenology/) of meaning something, and these are contents in the PIT sense whether or not any selection history stands behind them. That is the direct opposite of the Millikan verdict, on the same case.

The Map's own version qualifies this. It aligns with *moderate* PIT ([three versions](/concepts/intentionality/#three-versions-of-pit)), on which standing content derives from past conscious episodes. At the moment of his formation Swampman has no past conscious episodes, so the Map credits him with occurrent phenomenal content only. His standing beliefs, and his grasp of the sounds he makes as a *language*, have no derivation base yet; both accrue as conscious episodes accrue, the same "not yet fixed" structure the hybrid gives wide reference. Davidson's point about the unlearned sound "house" is therefore partly granted: present at t=0 is the occurrent episode of meaning something by it, not a standing linguistic competence.

The second step is where the Map's hybrid enters and produces the split. The Map accepts externalism about *wide* intentional content, reference in particular, while holding *phenomenal* content, the felt character of experience, to be internally constituted (developed at [content-externalism](/concepts/content-externalism/)). The hybrid page draws that perimeter deliberately: it rests the internalist half on phenomenal character alone, not on a constructed notion of narrow content, so that the criticisms such constructions have attracted (Yli-Vakkuri and Hawthorne 2018) do not bite. This page keeps to the same perimeter. What Swampman's "water" thought has, if he is conscious, is the watery-stuff-presenting phenomenal character any conscious duplicate would share, together with whatever aboutness the Map's phenomenal-constitution stance grants that character; what it lacks is wide reference. He has never been in causal contact with H₂O, nor with XYZ, nor with a linguistic community from whom he could borrow an extension. On the Map's view that is the answer the hybrid gives: reference is the one aspect of content the environment fixes, and Swampman's environment has not yet fixed it. Nothing in the hybrid rules out his acquiring wide reference through subsequent contact, though the Map does not say when that would occur.

## Where the Verdicts Come Apart

Laid side by side, the positions predict as follows for a Swampman stipulated to be phenomenally conscious.

| Position | Phenomenally constituted aboutness? | Wide reference? | Thinks at all? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Millikan / Neander (1996) | No | No | No: "an activity is not thinking if it exists for no purpose at all" |
| Papineau (2001) | Not in these terms; belief *role* filled | Not addressed | Left open by the theory's "essential core" |
| The Map's hybrid | Yes, if conscious | Open; not yet fixed | Yes, if conscious |

The case separates the Map from the strict teleosemanticist cleanly, and from Papineau's 2001 position much less cleanly. Papineau's non-rigid route lets a physicalist say Swampman has beliefs because the belief *role* is filled, with no appeal to consciousness. The two views differ over the *ground* of the verdict rather than the verdict: the Map ties content to consciousness, Papineau to functional role, and Swampman, whose role-states and stipulated phenomenal states coincide, cannot tell them apart. Nor can any other duplicate case. The [zombie](/concepts/philosophical-zombies/), a functional duplicate without phenomenal states, looks like the natural discriminator, but the Map holds it nomologically impossible, since under Bidirectional Interaction such a being cannot produce identical reports without consciousness ([the interactionist escape](/concepts/philosophical-zombies/#the-interactionist-escape)), and Papineau's reduction would in any case discount it as one more non-actual case. The Map's replies to him are therefore not duplicate cases. One is the two-normativities argument on the [teleosemantics](/concepts/teleosemantics/) page: selection-relative malfunction yields biological normativity, and the answerability to evidence that content requires is a different kind of "ought", so the role Papineau says selectional states fill is not the role the Map takes content to occupy. The other is Kim's (2021) in-framework argument that the Swampman intuitions bear on the adequacy of success semantics and the etiological conception of function, the conceptual foundations on which the reduction's explanatory power rests, so the actual-world reclassification does not make the case irrelevant.

## The Cost the Map Pays

Swampman is discriminating only under a stipulation the Map cannot discharge. The split verdict is conditional on "if he is conscious," and four things make that antecedent hard to secure.

First, it is not verifiable. Swampman is behaviourally indistinguishable from the original, so no test separates a conscious Swampman from a non-conscious one, and even the shared-history inference we rely on for other humans is unavailable: the [problem of other minds](/concepts/problem-of-other-minds/) in its purest form.

Second, the Map's own [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism) makes the antecedent metaphysically open in a way a physicalist need not face. A physicalist for whom consciousness supervenes on present physical constitution is committed to his being conscious if the original is. The Map holds that consciousness is not reducible to physical processes, so duplication does not by itself settle the matter; the positions register says what it does settle. On the mechanism side the register leans toward Swampman being conscious: [P-AC2](/positions/ai-consciousness-scope/#p-ac2), on the weakened reading its own text concedes, holds that consciousness acts through whatever live quantum indeterminacy arises, so no-cloning disqualifies only classical copies, and a lightning-formed brain is alive. What the register has not paid is the subject–brain pairing law: [P-SC1](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc1) makes the subject census part of the interface's state description, and [P-SC2](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc2) books the debt that nothing in the corpus says what makes a brain the substrate of exactly one subject rather than none. That debt, not any ingredient the lightning failed to copy, is why the Map cannot say that Swampman is conscious. And if he is, [P-I1](/positions/individuation-and-subjecthood/#p-i1)'s closed individualism and [P-AC3](/positions/ai-consciousness-scope/#p-ac3)'s copy-counting make him a numerically distinct subject from Davidson rather than Davidson continued. This framing presupposes Dualism's substance-leaning sub-reading, a subject paired with a brain, which the tenets page places downstream of the tenet; on the deflationary reading "which subject?" dissolves, but the pairing question does not.

Third, a Swampman who is conscious *but contentless* is a live alternative the Map must reject rather than ignore: Millikan's 1984 passage allows that the double "possibly … would have to be in a state of consciousness exactly like yours" while denying it beliefs, and Papineau briefly took the same escape. PIT rules this out, because on PIT phenomenal states are intrinsically intentional, but that is a commitment PIT carries rather than a result it can offer opponents.

Fourth, and sharper than unverifiability, the published naturalist answer is that the antecedent is false. Fred Dretske's "Absent Qualia" (1996), in the same forum, applies his Representational Thesis to the case: phenomenal qualities are properties an experience represents things as having, representing requires a function, and a function requires a history that fixes what a part is supposed to do. The parts of Dretske's own car can misrepresent because there is something they are supposed to do; nothing in Swampman is supposed to do anything, so nothing in him can misrepresent, nothing represents, and there is nothing it is like to be him. This is the mirror image of Papineau's abandoned escape, and Papineau marked the connection himself by putting "strongly representational theories of consciousness" to one side in taking it (2001, n. 3): conscious-but-contentless and not-conscious-at-all are the two halves of one dispute over whether phenomenal character can come apart from history. The Map's reply is the introspective-authority argument the [content-externalism](/concepts/content-externalism/) page already makes against Dretske's phenomenal externalism: felt character is available to its subject and partly definitive of the state, so a theory on which its very presence depends on a selection history the subject cannot introspect voids first-person authority. Applied to Swampman it has one less foothold than on Twin Earth, since it presupposes an authoritative introspector, which Dretske denies; what it establishes is that his verdict could not be confirmed from the inside either, since a conscious Swampman would have Davidson's authority over his felt states and the theory would have to call it empty. The Map registers this as the same unresolved dispute the hybrid page marks, an objection Dretske must answer on his own terms rather than a refutation inside his view; against him the antecedent is contested, not merely unverifiable. Joseph Levine's "SwampJoe: Mind or Simulation?" (1996) poses the antecedent in its title, and Kirk Ludwig's "Duplicating Thoughts" (1996) separates whether the duplicate has *any* thoughts or language from whether it has the author's; the Map answers the second no, since on [P-AC3](/positions/ai-consciousness-scope/#p-ac3) he is a distinct subject, and gives the first the conditional yes above.

## Relation to Site Perspective

Swampman tests the Map's first tenet, [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism), where it is most specific about content: aboutness is grounded in consciousness rather than in physical history. The teleosemantic verdict is the sharpest statement of the opposite thesis, that a physical system's states are about the world only in virtue of what selection did to its ancestors. Where the [teleosemantics](/concepts/teleosemantics/) page argues with the programme over normativity, Swampman isolates a different premise: that history, rather than present phenomenal character, confers content. The Map's split verdict is what the hybrid on [content-externalism](/concepts/content-externalism/) predicts for a creature with phenomenal states and no history.

The Map's third tenet, [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction), adds a consequence held at the tenet's [registered standing](/tenets/#tenet-3-standing), on which downward causation is shown available, not actual. Davidson stipulates that "no one can tell the difference" between Swampman and the original. If consciousness causes reports, then a being physically identical to a conscious human cannot produce identical reports without it, which is the claim the zombie page makes in [the interactionist escape](/concepts/philosophical-zombies/#the-interactionist-escape); a non-conscious Swampman is that case, so on the Map's view the stipulated indistinguishability would fail, though the Map does not claim to know how the divergence would present. This is a consequence of a posit rather than support for it, and it does nothing to make Swampman's consciousness detectable at his formation.

Three limitations. First, the Map's verdict is hostage to the antecedent set out above, which is unverifiable, which Dretske denies, and which the Map's dualism makes harder to secure than it is for a supervenience physicalist. Second, Papineau's 2001 non-rigid reply reaches a verdict close to the Map's without any appeal to consciousness, so Swampman alone does not favour the Map over the most careful teleosemanticist; that work falls to the two-normativities argument and to Kim's explanation-and-modality argument, not to a further duplicate case. Third, Neander and Millikan note the case's physical implausibility, and Millikan reports biologists' view that large organic molecules cannot be assembled by chance collision at all; on the Map's one-world view that makes a Swampman rare, not impossible. The Map treats Swampman as a possibility-argument about what content *is*, and accepts Papineau's point that a merely possible case bears on a conceptual claim about content in a way it does not bear on a claim of actual-world reduction. Whether teleosemantics is properly read as the latter is the question the dispute turns on, and the Map's [fifth tenet](/tenets/#occams-limits) counsels against assuming the more parsimonious reading because it is parsimonious.
