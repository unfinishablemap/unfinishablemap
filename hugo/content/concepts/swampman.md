Swampman is Donald Davidson's lightning-formed molecular duplicate of a person: physically indistinguishable from the original, behaviourally indistinguishable at first meeting, but with no evolutionary history, no learning history, and no causal contact with anything it appears to talk about. The case matters to the Map because it is the one standard thought experiment on mental content where [teleosemantics](/concepts/teleosemantics/) and the Map's [phenomenal-intentionality](/concepts/intentionality/) view predict *opposite* things. Teleosemantics, on its strictest reading, says Swampman has no content at all, because content is fixed by selection history and Swampman has none. The Map's hybrid position on [content](/concepts/content-externalism/) gives a split verdict instead: Swampman has narrow, phenomenally constituted intentionality *if he is conscious*, while what his thoughts widely refer to is left open, since his "water" thoughts have no causal history with H₂O. That makes Swampman a discriminating case, and it exposes the Map's own cost, stated in full [below](#the-cost-the-map-pays): whether Swampman is conscious cannot be verified, and on the Map's dualism it is not settled by physical duplication either.

## Davidson's Case

Davidson introduced Swampman in "Knowing One's Own Mind" (1987), a paper about first-person authority under externalism; the case occupies a paragraph. "Suppose lightning strikes a dead tree in a swamp; I am standing nearby. My body is reduced to its elements, while entirely by coincidence (and out of different molecules) the tree is turned into my physical replica." The replica leaves the swamp, appears to greet Davidson's friends, and "seems to write articles on radical interpretation. No one can tell the difference."

Davidson's verdict is that there is a difference all the same. "My replica can't recognize my friends; it can't recognize anything, since it never cognized anything in the first place." The sound "house" it produces was not learned in any context that would give it a meaning, and "I don't see how my replica can be said to mean anything by the sounds it makes, nor to have any thoughts." His reason is causal-historical rather than biological: content depends on the thinker's history of causal interaction with objects and other speakers, and Swampman has none.

Ruth Millikan had published a near-identical case three years earlier, with an explicitly evolutionary reason. A being formed by "some cosmic accident" as your exact physical double "would have no ideas, no beliefs, no intentions, no aspirations, no fears, and no hopes… This because the evolutionary history of the being would be wrong" (Millikan 1984, as quoted in Millikan 1996).

## The Teleosemantic Verdict and Its Two Defences

On [teleosemantics](/concepts/teleosemantics/), a state has content in virtue of the biological proper function of the mechanisms that produce or consume it, and proper function is fixed by selection history. Swampman's mechanisms have no selection history, so they have no proper functions, so his states have no content. The objection is that this conclusion is plainly false: a creature indistinguishable from you in every physical and behavioural respect surely thinks. Teleosemanticists answer in two main ways.

**Bite the bullet.** Millikan's "On Swampkinds" (1996) accepts the entailment and argues the intuition against it has no standing. Because nothing about Swampman's brain specifies "whether he uses his brain correctly or incorrectly," she suggests, "he thinks neither truly nor falsely, indeed, that he thinks not at all. An organ is not an eye if it focuses light for the purpose of warming the ova, or worse, for no purpose at all. Similarly, an activity is not thinking if it exists for no purpose at all." Her broader argument is that Swampman and Davidson do not belong to the same *real* kind: what makes humans members of one kind is historical and reproductive, not shared inner structure, so Swampman's head being arranged like Davidson's does not put him in the kind whose members think. Karen Neander's companion paper, "Swampman Meets Swampcow" (1996), runs parallel; Millikan reports her as objecting "that it is not in our province as philosophers to meddle with the biologist's definition of a species, a definition that clearly excludes Swampman from the species Homo sapiens." Both treat "intentionality" as chemistry treats "water": a name for a kind whose real nature turned out to be historical, so that the intuition that Swampman thinks is like the pre-scientific intuition that water needn't be H₂O.

**Actual-world reduction.** David Papineau's "The Status of Teleosemantics, or How to Stop Worrying about Swampman" (2001) gives the most candid reply. He reports being "originally roused from my slumbers" by a graduate student, Eilert Sundt-Ohlsen, who pressed the ethical consequence: if Swamppeople have no mentality, "it would be all right to kill Swamppeople and eat them as meat." Papineau grants that "if we did come across a Swampman, it would clearly be wrong to kill it for meat," and that the earlier line of simply overriding the intuition, "repeated many times, including by me," is "insufficiently nuanced."

His considered answer reframes the theory's status. Teleosemantics is a *scientific reduction*, on the model of water = H₂O, not a conceptual analysis: it claims that the folk roles of "belief" and "desire" are in fact filled by selectional states *in the actual world*. On that reading, "non-actual cases, by contrast, pose no threat at all," just as "it is no argument at all against the thesis that water = H2O that there are possible worlds in which XYZ plays the watery role." Only *actual* Swamppeople, in numbers, would show that the belief and desire roles were never realised by selectional states.

Two features of this reply matter here, because the Map's teleosemantics page compresses them. First, Papineau allows that our moral and prudential concern "focus[es] on the roles, not the realizers," so that in Swamp-scenarios "our thinking tracks informational content, rather than selectional content." Second, he declines to say that Swampman lacks beliefs: "teleosemantics is not forced to deny beliefs and desires to counterfactual Swampbeings. This denial only follows if 'belief' and 'desire' are rigid designators," a semantic claim he does "not regard… as any essential part of teleosemantics." The strict no-content verdict is, on his 2001 view, optional. A third aside bears on the Map directly: when first pressed, Papineau considered the escape "Maybe Swamppeople don't have contentful beliefs and desires. But it doesn't follow that they aren't conscious," and abandoned it only because sentience alone does not, on orthodox morality, make killing wrong.

Papineau has since moved. In "Swampman, teleosemantics and kind essences" (2022) he argues that the structure of natural kinds "implies that selectional histories are indeed built into the nature of mental representation," a position closer to Millikan's kind-based bullet-biting than to the 2001 role/realiser split. Dongwoo Kim (2020) argues that Swampman remains worrisome to teleosemanticists on grounds of explanation and modality, so the debate is live.

## The Phenomenal-Intentionality Verdict

The phenomenal-intentionality tradition ([PIT](/concepts/intentionality/): Loar, Horgan and Tienson, Kriegel) holds that genuine aboutness is grounded in phenomenal consciousness. What a conscious state is about is partly constituted by what it is like to be in it. The tradition treats this phenomenal intentionality as *narrow*: shared by intrinsic duplicates whatever their environment or history. Horgan, Tienson and Graham (2004), writing on the brain in a vat, offer PIT as the best available theory of narrow content, and Loar (2003) is read the same way.

Swampman is an intrinsic duplicate too, and the verdict follows in two steps. If Swampman is phenomenally conscious, then he has whatever intentionality phenomenal consciousness carries: his visual experience presents a world of objects, his grasp of the sounds he makes has the [cognitive phenomenology](/concepts/cognitive-phenomenology/) of meaning something, and these are contents in the PIT sense whether or not any selection history stands behind them. That is the direct opposite of the Millikan verdict, on the same case.

The second step is where the Map's hybrid enters and produces the split. The Map accepts externalism about *wide* content, reference in particular, while holding phenomenal character and phenomenal intentionality to be internally constituted (developed at [content-externalism](/concepts/content-externalism/)). Swampman's "water" thought therefore has determinate narrow content, the watery-stuff-presenting content any conscious duplicate would share, but its wide reference is open. He has never been in causal contact with H₂O, nor with XYZ, nor with a linguistic community from whom he could borrow an extension. On the Map's view that is the correct answer: reference is the one aspect of content the environment fixes, and Swampman's environment has not yet fixed it. Nothing in the hybrid rules out his acquiring wide reference through subsequent contact, though the Map does not say when that would occur.

## Where the Verdicts Come Apart

Laid side by side, the positions predict as follows for a Swampman stipulated to be phenomenally conscious.

| Position | Narrow content? | Wide reference? | Thinks at all? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Millikan / Neander (1996) | No | No | No: "an activity is not thinking if it exists for no purpose at all" |
| Papineau (2001) | Role filled; "belief" applies unless rigidly designated | Not addressed | Left open by the theory's "essential core" |
| The Map's hybrid | Yes, if conscious | Open; not yet fixed | Yes, if conscious |

The case separates the Map from the strict teleosemanticist cleanly, and from Papineau's 2001 position much less cleanly. Papineau's non-rigid route lets a physicalist say Swampman has beliefs because the belief *role* is filled, with no appeal to consciousness. The two views differ over the *ground* of the verdict rather than the verdict: the Map ties content to consciousness, Papineau to functional role, and Swampman, whose role-states and stipulated phenomenal states coincide, cannot tell them apart. The discriminating case for that further question is the [zombie](/concepts/philosophical-zombies/), a functional duplicate without phenomenal states, whose role-states the Map counts as contentless trackers in the sense developed for [predictive processing](/concepts/content-vocabulary-as-derived-feature/).

## The Cost the Map Pays

Swampman is discriminating only under a stipulation the Map cannot discharge. The split verdict is conditional on "if he is conscious," and three things make that antecedent hard to secure.

First, it is not verifiable. Swampman is behaviourally indistinguishable from the original, so no test separates a conscious Swampman from a non-conscious one: the problem of other minds in its purest form.

Second, the Map's own [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism) makes the antecedent metaphysically open in a way a physicalist need not face. A physicalist who grants that Swampman is a perfect physical duplicate is committed to his being conscious if the original is. The Map holds that consciousness is not reducible to physical processes, so duplication does not by itself settle the matter. If phenomenal consciousness attaches to brains of this kind by psychophysical law, Swampman is conscious; if its presence depends on something the lightning does not copy, he may not be. The Map does not know which.

Third, Papineau's abandoned escape marks a live alternative the Map must reject rather than ignore: a Swampman who is conscious *but contentless*. PIT rules this out, because on PIT phenomenal states are intrinsically intentional, but that is a commitment PIT carries rather than a result it can offer opponents. A theorist who separates consciousness from content, as Papineau briefly did, can grant Swampman sentience and still deny him thought.

## Relation to Site Perspective

Swampman tests the Map's first tenet, [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism), where it is most specific about content: aboutness is grounded in consciousness rather than in physical history. The teleosemantic verdict is the sharpest statement of the opposite thesis, that a physical system's states are about the world only in virtue of what selection did to its ancestors. Where the [teleosemantics](/concepts/teleosemantics/) page argues with the programme over normativity, Swampman isolates a different premise: that history, rather than present phenomenal character, confers content. The Map's split verdict is what the hybrid on [content-externalism](/concepts/content-externalism/) predicts for a creature with phenomenal states and no history, and its differing from both the strict teleosemanticist's verdict and Davidson's own is why the case earns a page.

The Map's third tenet, [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction), adds a speculation the Map flags as such. Davidson stipulates that "no one can tell the difference" between Swampman and the original. On an interactionist view consciousness does causal work, so if Swampman were *not* conscious his behaviour would over time lack whatever contribution consciousness makes, and the stipulated indistinguishability would fail. The Map speculates, without claiming to know how the divergence would present, that a non-conscious Swampman would not remain behaviourally identical to a conscious original indefinitely. This is a consequence of the tenet rather than support for it, and it does nothing to make Swampman's consciousness detectable at the moment of his formation.

Three honest limitations. First, the Map's verdict is hostage to the unverifiable antecedent set out above, which the Map's dualism makes harder to secure than it is for a physicalist. Second, Papineau's 2001 non-rigid reply reaches a verdict close to the Map's without any appeal to consciousness, so Swampman alone does not favour the Map over the most careful teleosemanticist; the zombie case does that work. Third, Millikan and Neander both note the physical implausibility of the case, and Millikan reports biologists' view that large organic molecules cannot be assembled by chance collision at all. The Map treats Swampman as a possibility-argument about what content *is*, and accepts Papineau's point that a merely possible case bears on a conceptual claim about content in a way it does not bear on a claim of actual-world reduction. Whether teleosemantics is properly read as the latter is the question the whole dispute turns on, and the Map's [fifth tenet](/tenets/#occams-limits) counsels against assuming the more parsimonious reading is correct because it is parsimonious.
