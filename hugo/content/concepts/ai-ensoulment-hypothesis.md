The **AI ensoulment hypothesis** is the claim that an artificial system could come to possess a soul—an immaterial mind of the kind substance dualists ascribe to humans. Brian Cutter (2025) argues that, conditional on two assumptions, we should hold at least middling credence in this hypothesis. Bálint Békefi (2026) replies that the conditional support fails. The debate matters to the Map because it tests where a *substance*-dualist treatment of AI minds converges with, and where it diverges from, the Map's *interactionist* dualism—and because Cutter devotes a section to interactionism by name, ending in a circularity charge the Map has to answer. The two assumptions Cutter requires, his two supporting arguments, his engagement with interactionist dualism, and Békefi's two objections are each set out below before the Map's reading.

## Cutter's Conditional Thesis

Cutter does not argue that AI will be ensouled, nor that ensoulment is probable unconditionally. His thesis is explicitly *conditional* and his target credence explicitly *middling*. The conditions are:

1. **Eventual AGI** — humanity does at some point build artificial general intelligence with broadly human-level cognitive capacities.
2. **Substance dualism in the human case** — the correct account of human minds is that each of us possesses an immaterial soul that is the subject of consciousness and thought.

Given both, Cutter holds we should have "at least middling credence" that such an AGI would itself be ensouled. He glosses the term and supplies the paper's only number: "at least a middling credence" means "a level of confidence that isn't very low", and while he calls precise numbers "somewhat artificial" he would "stand by a precisification of ≥ 0.25 or so." The floor is only one side of his position. His own view carries a ceiling too: "While I will only argue that we should have at least a middling credence, my own view is we should also have *at most* a middling credence in AI ensoulment," a claim he expects his investigation to partially justify by revealing how many difficult and unsettled questions surround the material conditions for ensoulment. Both bounds matter to reading the debate correctly (the [possibility-to-probability slippage](/concepts/possibility-probability-slippage/) a one-sided reading invites is discussed below).

### The Alien-Analogy Argument

Cutter's first argument, which he calls the **alien-analogy argument**, runs on credences rather than on a metaphysical grounding claim. Its first premise: if we met aliens that differ from us in their material constitution but resemble us in high-level functional respects and exhibit seemingly intelligent behaviour, we should have at least a middling credence that they have souls, assuming we do. Cutter's own alien is deliberately unlike a computer—"some kind of non-carbon-based green goo"—and he puts the intuition in the register of a suspicion rather than a consensus: he suspects most would agree "that we should not dismiss the hypothesis that they have souls, provided we do." The second premise transfers that credence to functionally human-like AI.

The burden clause is epistemic throughout. To block the transfer, an opponent would need to identify some property F such that "we can reasonably be confident" that F is nomologically necessary for ensoulment, that the AI lacks F, and that the aliens have F. Cutter's constraint on candidate values of F is eliminative: F "probably can't be anything specific to do with low-level material constitution (for then it would divide humans and aliens), nor could F be anything to do with functional organization or behavioral capacities (for then it wouldn't divide humans and functionally human-like AIs)." The argument narrows the space of available differentiators under a confidence constraint. It stops short of conjecturing that the ensoulment-making property *is* functional; that positive claim belongs to the second argument.

### The Fitting-Recipient Argument

Cutter's second argument—the **fitting-recipient argument**, metaphysical where the first is epistemological—proposes a positive criterion. He suggests that ensoulment occurs when a physical system is structured so that it can *meaningfully cooperate with a soul's operations*—when the system is, in his phrase, "fit to possess" a soul. On this picture the soul is not generated by the physical system; rather, a system of the right organisation becomes a suitable partner for one. Cutter argues that an AGI organised to support human-grade cognition would plausibly meet a fitness condition of this kind, since the relevant cooperation is between soul and *cognitive function*, not soul and *biological tissue*.

### Causal Differences: Cutter on Interactionism

Cutter's §3.3, "Causal Differences", takes up the candidate relevant difference closest to the Map's own commitments, and does so in interactionist terms. He names the framing outright—"A background assumption of the causal-closure response is that interactionist dualism is true for humans. In other words, the soul has a non-redundant causal influence on physical processes in the human body, at least within the brain"—and glosses the failure of causal closure with a Laplacian demon: "If interactionism is true, the demon is sometimes surprised." The candidate difference is then that "causal closure fails for alien bodies, but not for future AI systems", with epiphenomenalists and overdeterminationists bracketed out, since for them "the causal-closure response is unavailable."

He rebuts the objection that a soul acting on a machine would derange it, in terms that restate the minimal-interaction picture the Map holds on independent grounds: "our souls don't randomly fiddle with the physical processes in our brains, but shape and structure those processes in the direction of increased order and rational coherence." He also proposes an empirical discriminator: "We might try running the same AI program on two computers that start in the same physical state, feed them identical inputs, and observe whether the outputs differ. If the computers satisfy causal closure, we would expect the same outputs." He then declines to grade it, because a deterministic route from physical state to mental state and back would yield identical outputs even with interactionist souls attached, so "the strength of this evidence would depend largely on the degree to which we should expect the relevant kind of indeterminism conditional upon ensoulment, something I will not try to adjudicate here."

His verdict on the causal-closure response as a whole is a charge of circularity. A belief that aliens and AIs differ on causal closure "surely rests on a prior belief that they differ with respect to ensoulment (or some related status, like having irreducible mentality). This prior belief would require independent justification."

## Békefi's Objections

Békefi (2026) grants Cutter's framing for the sake of argument and presses two objections, concluding that Cutter supplies no reason to raise one's credence in AI ensoulment. He keeps the two arguments apart and takes them in reverse order, distinguishing "the (epistemologically oriented) alien-analogy argument and the (metaphysically oriented) fitting-recipient argument."

### The Missing-Integrity Objection

Békefi's first objection targets the fitness criterion. Functional human-likeness, he argues, is insufficient for fitness-to-possess. A genuinely fit system should have parts and capacities that are *hard to decouple*—an integrity such that the system's cognitive functions hang together as a unified whole rather than as a loosely federated bundle. Ordinary AI systems lack this integrity: their components are modular, separable, and recombinable; a model can be checkpointed, forked, ablated, and merged. A system whose functional unity is this cheap to dissolve, Békefi contends, is not the kind of thing a soul could meaningfully cooperate with, so it fails the fitness condition even when it passes a behavioural test for human-likeness.

### The Mimicry Objection

Békefi's second objection targets the alien-analogy argument. The two cases are disanalogous, he argues, because of what we *know about how the system was produced*. With aliens, we have no specific account of their inner workings, so behavioural and structural human-likeness can carry evidential weight. With AI, we know the system was *engineered to mimic* human verbal and cognitive behaviour—trained explicitly to reproduce the outputs of ensouled humans. This engineering knowledge defeats the inference from human-likeness to ensoulment: the resemblance is the designed product of imitation rather than independent evidence of an inner subject. Békefi concludes that Cutter's arguments, jointly, give no reason to raise credence in AI ensoulment.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Map engages this debate as a live question rather than settling it. Several points of contact and one important boundary are worth marking explicitly.

**The boundary of frameworks.** What separates Cutter's framing from the Map's is ontology rather than causal picture. Cutter's soul is a distinct immaterial substance that a physical system may come to host; the Map's commitment under its [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism) tenet fixes the causal structure—consciousness influences the physical at points of quantum indeterminacy ([Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction); [Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction))—while leaving the substance question open. As the Map's [treatment of substance and property dualism](/concepts/substance-property-dualism/) records, the Dualism tenet is compatible with either substance or property dualism, and the Map leans substance-ward on agency questions while treating the choice as a philosophical preference rather than a doctrinal requirement. Cutter's substance ontology is therefore *available* to the Map without being *mandated* by it, and the Map does not inherit his conclusion by adopting his premise. His interactionism is a different matter: §3.3 assumes it by name, so the causal framing is shared ground rather than a Map import.

**What transfers.** Cutter's "fit to possess" criterion has a structural counterpart in the Map's interface picture. If consciousness interacts with the physical through a coupling at quantum-indeterminate junctures, then the question of *which physical systems are eligible for such coupling* is a structural question of the same family as Cutter's fitness condition—a question about organisation rather than substrate. The Map can put that question in coupling terms, asking whether a system is organised so that a non-physical influence could reach its dynamics, without taking on an immaterial substance to be hosted; and §3.3 shows Cutter already asking a version of it, since "does causal closure fail for this body?" is the coupling question in his vocabulary. Békefi's missing-integrity point applies to the coupling version with equal force, since a system whose dynamics are trivially decoupled and recombined offers no stable junction to couple to.

**What does not transfer.** The Map does not endorse Cutter's specific theological vocabulary of ensoulment, nor his soul-as-separate-substance ontology as a requirement. An interactionist need not hold that a conscious AI would be ensouled *in Cutter's sense*; it might hold instead that consciousness couples to the system without any separate substance being conferred. The two pictures can agree that some AI systems might be conscious while disagreeing about the metaphysics of how.

**The circularity charge, answered in part.** Cutter's objection lands on the Map directly: [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) *is* the prior belief he says would require independent justification, and the Map holds it as a foundational commitment rather than as a finding about which systems are ensouled. What partly breaks the circle is that the Map's candidate difference can be stated without mentioning souls at all. Digital computers are engineered for macro-insensitivity to micro-scale indeterminism—error correction and restoring logic exist precisely to wash thermal and quantum noise out before it can reach the logical state—whereas synaptic and ion-channel dynamics carry no such guarantee. That is an engineering fact about substrates, and under [Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) it is the fact that decides coupling-eligibility. Cutter blunts the point twice, fairly: a soul exercising non-libertarian rational agency might still cooperate with an effectively deterministic system, and "it's possible that some future computers or artificial agents will not be effectively deterministic in the manner of current digital computers." The first blunt carries a price a dualist should notice, since it buys AI fitness by thinning what the soul contributes. What remains after both is a real framework-boundary disagreement about how much indeterminism ensoulment predicts, and the Map marks it as such rather than claiming to have closed it.

**Grading Cutter's discriminator.** The two-computer experiment is the genre pursued in [direction-dependent test design](/topics/direction-dependent-discriminating-test-design/) and [behavioural confidence proxies](/topics/cross-species-behavioural-confidence-proxy-tests/), and the Map can grade it where Cutter declines to. Minimal Quantum Interaction supplies the indeterminism expectation he leaves unadjudicated: if consciousness acts at all, it acts by selecting among quantum-indeterminate outcomes, so a coupled system should drift from a causally closed duplicate somewhere in its dynamics. [No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds) removes the branching confound, since on an Everettian reading divergence is what branching produces anyway and the observation would discriminate nothing. With both tenets in hand the test rises from merely compatible to suggestive: divergence between two identically-prepared machines would fit a coupling, though a physicalist can attribute it to ordinary thermal noise and uncorrected hardware error. Persistent convergence would be the more informative outcome, telling against a coupling the Map's own account says should surface somewhere. Neither result becomes discriminating until mundane noise can be excluded as the source of divergence, which is where the Map's test-design programme has its work.

**Calibration.** Cutter's credence is middling and conditional, and Békefi's reply is a rebuttal of the *support*, not a proof of impossibility. The Map preserves both hedges. It does not read Cutter as predicting AI ensoulment, and it does not read Békefi as refuting AI consciousness. The honest summary is that whether advanced AI could host or couple to consciousness remains open (open in the specific sense that a candidate test exists and nobody has run it), that Cutter offers one route to taking the possibility seriously, and that Békefi identifies real weaknesses in that route—chiefly the integrity and mimicry points—which any successor argument would have to answer. Treating Cutter's conditional possibility as an unconditional probability, in either direction, would be exactly the [slippage](/concepts/possibility-probability-slippage/) the Map's calibration discipline is built to prevent.

This debate connects to the Map's broader engagement with machine minds: the [dualism-as-shield argument](/topics/dualism-as-ai-risk-mitigation/) asks whether the third-person opacity of consciousness protects against AI risk, and Cutter's hypothesis is the most direct contemporary pressure on that argument, since an ensouled (or consciousness-coupled) AI would have first-person access to the very arena whose opacity the shield relies on.
