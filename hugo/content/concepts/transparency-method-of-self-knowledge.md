The **transparency method** is the proposal that a person can find out what she believes, and on some versions what she perceives, wants, intends or feels, by attending to the world rather than to her own mind. Gareth Evans gave the canonical statement: "I get myself in a position to answer the question whether I believe that p by putting into operation whatever procedure I have for answering the question whether p" (1982, p. 225). Defenders disagree about how the step from *p* to *I believe that p* yields knowledge: by inference (Alex Byrne), by shared grounds without inference (Jordi Fernández), or through rational agency (Richard Moran, Matthew Boyle). None offers the method as support for dualism, and it is compatible with physicalism.

The Unfinishable Map's verdict, stated as the Map's own, is that the method is a strong account of how we know our attitudes, so self-knowledge of attitudes carries no evidential weight for dualism. Where the method stops is disputed. Byrne extends it explicitly to perception and bodily sensation, accepting that there are hallucinations of pain. The Map holds that it does not reach phenomenal character (see [where the method stops](#limit), explained below). That limit is the Map's verdict against Byrne's extension, drawn from its acquaintance view; the transparency literature neither establishes nor refutes it.

## Evans's Procedure and Who Named What {#terms}

In self-ascribing a belief, Evans writes, "one's eyes are, so to speak, or occasionally literally, directed outward—upon the world". Asked "Do you think there is going to be a third world war?", I attend "to precisely the same outward phenomena" as in answering "Will there be a third world war?" (1982, p. 225). He extends the point to perception, where the subject re-uses "precisely those skills of conceptualization that he uses to make judgements about the world" (p. 227).

The vocabulary postdates Evans, and its coiners differ:

- **"Transparent"** in this sense is Roy Edgley's: one's present thinking "is transparent in the sense that I cannot distinguish the question 'Do I think that P?' from a question in which there is no essential reference to myself or my belief, namely 'Is it the case that P?'" (Edgley 1969, p. 90, as quoted in Moran 2001, pp. 60–61). A search of the digitised text of Evans's book found neither "transparency" nor "ascent".
- **"Ascent routine"** is Robert Gordon's phrase and the title of his 2007 paper. Schwitzgebel writes of "Evans-like ascent routines" (2024, §2.3.4).
- **"Doxastic schema"** is André Gallois's name for the transition "P / So, I believe p" (1996).
- **"Transparency Condition"** is Moran's (2001, p. 101).
- **Rule BEL** and the name **"puzzle of transparency"** are Byrne's (2005, p. 95), as is the "puzzle of opacity" (p. 99). The puzzle itself had been posed by Gallois and by Moran.
- **"Transparency Method"**, capitalised and abbreviated TM, is Quassim Cassam's label (2014, p. 2).
- **"Uniformity Assumption"** is Boyle's name for "the demand that a satisfactory account of our self-knowledge should be fundamentally uniform", which he rejects (2009, p. 141, as quoted in Byrne 2011, §5). His 2024 book names the "Problem of Transparency".

### Other Senses of "Transparency" {#senses}

The Map uses the word in at least five other senses:

- **The transparency of experience** (Moore, Harman, Tye): the negative thesis that one cannot attend to intrinsic features of experience, the representationalist's main argument ([first-order-representationalism](/concepts/first-order-representationalism/)). Schwitzgebel treats the outward-looking method and the denial that we learn about our minds "by as it were gazing inward" as theses that "are separable" (2024, §2.3.4). Byrne reads Moore's remarks as suggesting that an inward procedure either does not exist or "is not necessary" (2011, §2). The method does not need the claim that inward attention is impossible.
- **Phenomenal transparency of the self-model** (Metzinger): a representation's representational character is invisible to its subject ([phenomenal-transparency-opacity-spectrum](/concepts/phenomenal-transparency-opacity-spectrum/)).
- **Transparent phenomenal concepts** (Goff): concepts revealing the full nature of their referents ([the revelation thesis](/concepts/revelation-thesis/#two-senses-of-phenomenal-transparency)).
- **Cartesian transparency**, or self-intimation: one's mental states cannot escape one's knowledge, "a completely different usage" (Schwitzgebel 2024, §2.3.4; see [self-blindness](/concepts/self-blindness/)).
- **The Map's own "transparency problem"** for idealism ([q3-q4-sliding-boundary-and-transparency-problem](/topics/q3-q4-sliding-boundary-and-transparency-problem/)).

## Four Versions of the Method {#versions}

**Inferential (Byrne).** Self-knowledge comes from following an epistemic rule: "BEL If p, believe that you believe that p" (2005, p. 95). Because inferring from a premise entails believing it, whoever reasons from *p* to *I believe that p* ends with a true belief; Byrne calls the transition "strongly self-verifying" (2011, §3) and claims safety for it rather than evidential strength. The account is "economical" (2005, p. 99): the inner-sense theory is "partly right" about belief, but "the mechanism comes with our capacity for reasoning about the external world—there is no inner eye" (p. 100).

**Bypass (Fernández).** The grounds of a first-order belief directly ground the self-ascription: "one may have the very same grounds for both a given belief that p and a higher-order belief about this belief" (2003, abstract). The self-ascription is non-inferential. Gertler reports that Fernández (2013) calls it "the bypass model" and "limits his account to explaining self-knowledge of beliefs and desires" (2021, §3.4).

**Agentialist and reflectivist (Moran; Boyle).** Moran traces the claims of "Edgley and Evans" to "the primacy of a deliberative rather than a theoretical stance toward one's own state of mind" (2001, p. 64). I answer "Do I believe P?" by making up my mind about P, entitled to do so as the one responsible for my attitudes. Boyle's *Transparency and Reflection* (2024), as its chapter abstracts describe it, draws on Sartre to ground the entitlement in "a form of implicit or 'nonpositional' self-awareness" present in first-order intention, perception and belief.

**Expressivist (Gordon).** On Schwitzgebel's report, Gordon (2007) argues that ascent routines "can drive the accurate self-ascription of all the attitudes, not just belief", paired with "something like an expressive account of self-ascription" (2024, §2.3.4).

## The Puzzle of Transparency and the Objections {#objections}

Moran states the puzzle as "something of a paradox: how can a question referring to a matter of empirical psychological fact about a particular person be legitimately answered without appeal to the evidence about that person, but rather by appeal to a quite independent body of evidence?" (2003, p. 413, as quoted in Byrne 2005, p. 95). Gallois had put it as a defect of inference: the doxastic schema "does not fit any standard pattern of good inference" (1996, p. 47). The abstract of Byrne's 2018 book is blunter: "the inference seems terrible". The main objections:

- **Unreasonableness.** Boyle (2011) argues that even if the inference reliably yields true and safe self-ascriptions, "it will not appear reasonable to the thinker" (as Gertler 2021, §3.4, reports him).
- **Weak evidence.** Barnett argues that *p* "often is not very strong evidence that you believe that p" (2016, abstract).
- **Making rather than finding.** Deliberating whether *p* may form a new belief rather than reveal an existing one (Shah & Velleman 2005). Gertler presses the point with belief-perseverance, "a common psychological phenomenon that threatens the method of transparency" (2011b, p. 138, as quoted in Cassam 2014, ch. 9).
- **Cassam's three problems** target "Rationalism", Moran's view as recast by Finkelstein, and set Byrne and Fernández aside, "whose approaches are different from Moran's" (2014, p. 3, note). TM "can only account for a sub-class of intentional self-knowledge" (the *Generality Problem*, p. 103); it swaps in the harder question "Do the reasons require me to believe that P?" (*Substitution*, p. 104); and "what you believe isn't always what you ought rationally to believe" (*Matching*, p. 106). Cassam's own view is inferentialist.

Three rival families frame the debate: inner sense (Armstrong, Lycan); self/other parity, which Schwitzgebel reports Carruthers (2011) defending for "propositional attitudes like belief and desire" while holding inner speech and visual imagery introspectible (2024, §2.1); and acquaintance, the Map's view for phenomenal states ([phenomenal-acquaintance](/concepts/phenomenal-acquaintance/)).

## Transparency and Self-Blindness {#self-blindness}

Sydney Shoemaker argued that a rational subject cannot be [self-blind](/concepts/self-blindness/), lacking first-person access to a whole kind of her own states (1988). Gallois starts from a self-blind believer: without the doxastic schema, "as a self-blind believer, I will form a deeply irrational view of my non-doxastic world" (1996, p. 76).

Byrne offers an explanation beside Shoemaker's constitutive one. His account is "a version of Shoemaker's 'broad perceptual model'" (2005, p. 98), the model Shoemaker's argument targets, yet because the capacity "belongs to the department of reasoning, not perceiving, Shoemaker's idea that the source of self-knowledge can be traced to 'rationality' is vindicated, albeit not via his preferred route" (p. 99). His claim concerns actual occurrence: "As Shoemaker observes, 'self-blindness' is not an actual condition", and the "obvious explanation" is that "rationality and other epistemic capacities are all that is needed for self-knowledge" (2011, §5). He allows that someone could believe that it is raining, "possess the concept of belief, and yet not even have the capacity to follow BEL" (2005, p. 98), so he does not establish Shoemaker's impossibility thesis.

The argument has an empirical premise. If self-knowledge is not broadly uniform, "dissociations are to be expected", such as someone with only a third-person way of knowing what she wants, and "Such dissociations do not seem to occur, however" (2011, §5). [Alexithymia](/concepts/alexithymia/), a graded difficulty identifying one's emotions with general cognition intact, is the nearest candidate for a domain-specific dissociation. Whether it counts is open: [self-blindness](/concepts/self-blindness/) argues that it falls short of self-blindness, Byrne's 2018 book treats emotion separately, and the Map's searches did not establish whether he discusses the condition. It bears on the premise without refuting Byrne.

## Where the Method Stops {#limit}

The extension runs from belief to perception, desire, intention and sensation, and each step needs more. In 2005 Byrne wrote that transparency "covers belief and perception", with desire "the obvious example" of a hard case (p. 99). His 2018 book, claiming to cover "perception, sensation, desire, intention, emotion, memory, imagination, and thought", names the problem: "what is the worldly premise corresponding to 'I intend to ϕ,' or 'I feel a pain'?" (abstract). For sensation he adopts the perceptual theory: "to have a bodily sensation... is to perceive something occurring in one's body", so that "the basic transparency account for perception applies" (2018, ch. 6 abstract). The worldly premise for pain is a bodily disturbance: "I know that I feel a pain in my elbow, not by attending to myself, or my own mind, but by attending to the painful disturbance in my elbow" (2011, §5). In 2026 he adds that the disturbances are themselves the pains, and draws the consequence that "a seemingly self-evident proposition—that there are no hallucinations of pain—is false" (2026, abstract).

Others stop earlier. Fernández confines the bypass model to beliefs and desires. Boyle recognises "an active kind through which we know our own judgments, and a passive kind through which we know our sensations" (2009, abstract). Cassam holds that leaving sensation unexplained is "not an objection to Rationalism" (2014, ch. 9).

**The Map's verdict.** The Map holds that the method explains how one comes to know *that* one believes, intends or sees, and with what content, but does not reach phenomenal character: what the pain or the seen red is like for its subject. Byrne's route reaches sensation only by treating the felt quality as a perceived worldly feature, a disturbance in the elbow. That is the representationalist identification the Map declines on [first-order-representationalism](/concepts/first-order-representationalism/): placing the quality in the body relocates the question of why perceiving the disturbance feels like anything, without answering it. On the acquaintance view the Map holds ([phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence](/topics/phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence/)), phenomenal character is presented to its subject. Where something feels painful a pain is present, though the subject's judgements about its character can still err. The two views part at Byrne's hallucinations of pain, over whether pains are the disturbances perceived; the method's success with belief settles that in neither direction.

Two consequences follow. First, self-knowledge of attitudes carries no evidential weight for dualism: our authority about what we believe has a physicalist-compatible explanation, so the Map's case cannot rest on it. Second, the phenomenal case stays where the [layered account of phenomenal authority](/topics/phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence/#three-layers-of-phenomenal-authority) puts it, with weak Revelation only: experience presents the existence and some of the character of a quality, not its full essence ([revelation-thesis](/concepts/revelation-thesis/)).

Nor does the method rule out what [contemplatives](/concepts/contemplative-epistemology/) report, attending to awareness itself, since Byrne leaves open an inward procedure that is merely "not necessary". Such reports bear on the Moore–Harman–Tye negative thesis instead.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism).** The method fits the Map without evidencing its first tenet. Physicalists hold it, and Byrne writes that the phrase "self-knowledge" "has a suspicious Cartesian flavor", suggesting a non-physical self, and keeps it only because the terminology is entrenched (2011, §1). Every author discussed here is cited for an account of self-knowledge, not as an ally of dualism; Boyle's nonpositional self-awareness is Sartrean phenomenology, not a claim about substance. The tenet's interest is calibration: the method marks off the part of self-knowledge that dualism cannot claim, and the dispute that remains, over phenomenal character, is the one the Map already conducts with first-order representationalism, which it resists without defeating.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction).** Moran's deliberative stance treats the subject as the author of her attitudes, which suits the Map's interest in agency without evidencing the tenet: the account is normative and epistemic and makes no causal claim. Nor does the method's being an inferential or functional transition bear on whether experience is causally idle; it lends no support to [epiphenomenalism](/concepts/epiphenomenalism/).

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits).** Byrne's appeal to economy (2005, p. 99; 2011, §§3, 5) is a parsimony argument. The fifth tenet constrains only the Map's own reasoning: it gives no ground to reject Byrne's view for being economical, and the economy gives no ground to accept it.

**Minimal Quantum Interaction and No Many Worlds** are not engaged.
