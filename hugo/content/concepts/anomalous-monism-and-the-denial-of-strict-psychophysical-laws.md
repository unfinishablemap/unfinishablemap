Anomalous monism is Donald Davidson's proposal that every mental event *is* a physical event, yet no strict law connects the mental to the physical. It is a subtle physicalist rival to the Map's law program, not a refutation of it and not a vindication of it. Davidson and the Map converge on one premise — strict *reductive* psychophysical bridge laws fail — and split on the verdict. Davidson concludes the mental is anomalous yet physical: its links to the physical are at best rough or hedged generalizations, and the strict laws that back every causal relation are physical. The Map posits instead a *non-strict, probabilistic selection law* operating at the quantum measurement interface, a psychophysical law meant to carry the mental's causal work itself ([Tenets 2 and 3](/tenets/)). The pressure point that makes this contrast matter is the charge, pressed early by Honderich in 1982, that Davidson's view leaves mental *properties* causally idle — an epiphenomenalism of the mental qua mental. That objection is exactly why the Map wants a genuine law rather than mere token-identity, and it is developed at length below.

## Davidson's Three Reconciled Principles

Davidson introduces anomalous monism in "Mental Events" (1970) as the resolution of an apparent inconsistency among three principles he finds independently plausible.

1. **The Principle of Causal Interaction**: at least some mental events causally interact with physical events. Perceptions are caused by the world; intentions cause bodily movements.

2. **The Principle of the Nomological Character of Causality**: "where there is causality, there must be a law: events related as cause and effect fall under strict deterministic laws" (Davidson 1970). Singular causal relations are backed by strict laws.

3. **The Anomalism of the Mental**: "there are no strict deterministic laws on the basis of which mental events can be predicted and explained" (Davidson 1970). No strict law — psychophysical or purely psychological — governs mental events *as* mental.

Taken at face value the three seem to clash: if mental events cause physical events (1), and causes fall under strict laws (2), how can the mental escape strict law (3)? Davidson's move is to notice that principle 2 concerns *events*, not descriptions. A singular causal relation holds between two events however they are described, but the covering law that backs it need only be framed in *some* vocabulary — the physical one. So a mental event can enter causal relations, fall under a strict law under its *physical* description, and still fall under no strict law under its *mental* description. The only way to satisfy all three is for each mental event that causally interacts to be *token-identical* to a physical event falling under physical law.

Token identity is therefore *derived*, not assumed. This is what distinguishes anomalous monism from a bald identity theory: Davidson does not begin by asserting mind-brain identity; he concludes to it as the sole consistent reconciliation of premises he already holds.

## Anomalism Grounded in the Constitutive Role of Rationality

Why accept the third principle — that the mental falls under no strict law? Davidson's argument goes further than noting that psychophysical laws have not yet been found: inferring someone's beliefs and desires from the evidence forces us to impose *constitutive* "conditions of coherence, rationality, and consistency" — conditions that, as Davidson puts it in the companion essay "Psychology as Philosophy," "have no echo in physical theory, which is why we can look for no more than rough correlations between psychological and physical phenomena" (Davidson 1974). To interpret someone as believing something, we must fit that belief into a broadly rational total pattern of attitudes. "Mental Events" puts the upshot in one line: "There are no strict psychophysical laws because of the disparate commitments of the mental and physical schemes" (Davidson 1970). The two schemes cross-classify the same events, so no strict bridge law can lock them together.

The result is a distinctive package: **ontological monism** (every mental event token is a physical event token) combined with **conceptual and nomological dualism** (mental predicates are not law-linked to physical predicates). The mental *supervenes* on the physical — no two events can differ mentally without differing physically — without being reducible to it. For how supervenience delivers dependence without reduction, see [supervenience](/concepts/supervenience/); Davidson is often credited with introducing the term to philosophy of mind in this very paper.

## The Epiphenomenalism Objection

The most persistent objection, and the one most useful to the Map, is that anomalous monism secures the causal efficacy of mental *events* while abandoning the causal relevance of mental *properties*. Ted Honderich gave it one of its earliest statements in "The Argument for Anomalous Monism" (1982). His point: not all of an event's properties are causally relevant to a given effect. Pears put on a scale can truly be described as green and as French, but it is not their greenness or their Frenchness that moves the pointer to the two-pound mark. Causal relevance, Honderich argues, tracks the *nomic* properties — those that figure in the backing law. On Davidson's view the backing laws are all physical, so only an event's physical properties are nomically efficacious. Its mental properties, being anomic, do no causal work. Honderich proposes reframing the cause-law principle as the "Principle of the Nomological Character of Causally Relevant Properties," which explicitly denies causal relevance to anomic mental properties. He draws the dilemma himself: grant that the mental *as mental* causes the physical, and the principle forces psychophysical lawlike connections, contrary to anomalism; keep anomalism, and the mental causes nothing as mental.

Ernest Sosa pressed the same charge in "Mind-Body Interaction and Supervenient Causation" (1984). The shape of the worry shows up in ordinary cases: a gunshot that is both loud and lethal kills by virtue of the bullet's speed and mass, not by virtue of its loudness, even though one event has both properties. If the mental stands to the physical as loudness stands to mass, then the mental looks like the causally idle property — present at the event, but not what does the work.

Jaegwon Kim generalized the worry into a structural argument against nonreductive physicalism as such. In "The Myth of Nonreductive Materialism" (1989) he presses the fork: either reduce the mental to the physical or accept that it is epiphenomenal. In *Mind in a Physical World* (1998) he develops the supervenience (causal exclusion) argument: a mental cause M has a physical supervenience base P that already has full credentials to produce the effect, so M is *excluded* unless one accepts systematic overdetermination or reduction. Brian McLaughlin, in "Type Epiphenomenalism, Type Dualism, and the Causal Priority of the Physical" (1989), maps the varieties of epiphenomenalism at stake and isolates the one that matters here: *type* epiphenomenalism, on which mental *types* never cause anything qua mental. McLaughlin is not finally hostile to Davidson — the same paper appeals to hedged, *ceteris paribus* psychophysical laws to argue that mental properties can be causally sufficient for their effects — but his taxonomy is what gives the charge its sharp form. The exclusion machinery itself, the engine behind the property-idleness charge, is treated in [type-token-causation](/concepts/type-token-causation/).

## Davidson's Reply: Deny Property-Causation

Davidson answers in "Thinking Causes" (1993). He rejects the shared premise of every version of the objection — that events cause "in virtue of" their properties. For Davidson, causation is a relation between events, extensional and description-independent: an event causes another *simpliciter*, and there is no further fact about *which of its properties* did the causing. Properties enter at the level of *explanation*, not causation. Since the mental event genuinely is a cause (principle 1 asserts as much, a priori), and since there is no property-relative causation for it to be excluded from, the epiphenomenalism charge misfires: it presupposes a framework of property-level causal relevance that Davidson denies. He adds two more conciliatory points. Anomalism rules out only *strict* laws, so hedged, *ceteris paribus* psychological and psychophysical laws remain available; and supervenience lets mental properties make a difference to what an event causes, since two events that differ mentally must differ physically. Given his extensionalism, Yalowitz argues, some of these remarks have to be read as concerning explanatory rather than causal relevance (Yalowitz 2025).

The reply is powerful but widely regarded as controversial. Many critics (including Kim and Sosa in the same 1993 volume) hold that the extensional reply changes the subject: the whole point of the mental-causation debate is whether an event's *mentality* makes a difference to what it causes, and answering that events cause *simpliciter* concedes rather than rebuts the worry. Whether [causal relevance](/concepts/causal-relevance-of-content/#standards) requires nomic properties, and whether extensional event-causation is the right framework at all, remain open.

## The Double-Edged Relation to the Map

The Map's stance toward anomalous monism is genuinely double-edged, and honesty requires stating both edges.

**Where they agree.** The Map does not defend strict *reductive* psychophysical bridge laws. It accepts, with Davidson, that the mental and physical vocabularies cross-classify, and it holds for experience what he argued for the attitudes: no clean type-type reduction to neural kinds is on offer. The Map's [acceptance of phenomenal-physical covariance](/concepts/supervenience/) without reductive collapse is close in letter to Davidson's supervenience-without-reduction.

**Where the scopes differ.** Davidson's mental is marked by intentionality — "the distinguishing feature of the mental is not that it is private, subjective, or immaterial, but that it exhibits what Brentano called intentionality" (Davidson 1970) — and his anomalism rests on the rationality norms that govern attributing beliefs and desires. Anomalous monism therefore "does not address the status of mental events such as pains, tickles and the like" (Yalowitz 2025). The Map's law concerns phenomenal consciousness, which those norms do not obviously reach, though experience with propositional content brings them back. [The articulability analysis](/concepts/articulability-of-q1/#the-anomalism-objection) develops this narrower-ground reply and the pressure that survives it.

**Where they split.** Davidson leaves the psychophysical link at the rough correlations quoted above. The hedged laws he later allows do not change where causes get their backing, which on his official view is always a strict law and so a physical one, and whether hedged laws fit anomalism at all is disputed (Kim 1993). The Map takes the first horn of Honderich's dilemma. If Honderich, Sosa, and Kim are even roughly right that causal relevance needs a nomic anchor, then a view that keeps consciousness genuinely efficacious — which [Tenet 3](/tenets/) demands — needs a psychophysical law that itself carries causal work, not a correlation riding on physical law. The Map's proposal ([Tenet 2](/tenets/)) is that this link is a minimal, non-strict, probabilistic *selection law* at the quantum measurement interface: consciousness settles which otherwise-indeterminate quantum outcome becomes actual, within a corridor that leaves the unconditioned Born statistics intact ([P-Q2](/positions/quantum-interface/#p-q2)), doing the least work compatible with having real effects. That is neither a strict reductive bridge law (so it survives Davidson's argument against strict psychophysical laws) nor a correlation riding on physical law (so it can escape the property-epiphenomenalism that dogs Davidson, but only if the law answers to [what consciousness presents](/topics/acquaintance-without-causation/#content-efficacy), the open question [ensemble-level-epiphenomenalism](/concepts/ensemble-level-epiphenomenalism/) records).

So the Map treats Davidson as the *sophisticated physicalist alternative it must out-argue*, and the epiphenomenalism objection as the lever. Davidson's considered reply buys mental efficacy by denying property-causation across the board — an exit the Map declines, because it wants genuine *property-level* (indeed quantum-level) mental influence, not bare event-causation. Note also the deeper disagreement: Davidson is a physicalist who rejects dualism, whereas the Map is dualist ([Tenet 1](/tenets/)). Anomalous monism is thus a rival ontology, not a friend wearing different clothes. In the terms of Davidson's own triad, the Map keeps the first principle and, its law being non-strict, the letter of the third; having rejected token identity, it must give up the second for interface causation, where the cause has no physical description and the only law linking it to its effect is non-strict.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The primary tenet in play is **Tenet 2 (Minimal Quantum Interaction)**. Anomalous monism is the clearest named foil for the Map's selection-law program: it agrees the reductive road is closed but leaves the psychophysical link rough, where the Map takes the minimal-law exit. Reading the two side by side clarifies what the Map is committing to — a psychophysical connection that is real and property-relevant but as small and non-strict as efficacy allows.

**Tenet 3 (Bidirectional Interaction)** supplies the motivation. The epiphenomenalism objection is, in effect, an argument that token-physicalism cannot honor bidirectional causal relevance of the mental *as* mental. The Map takes that objection seriously enough to shape its interface law around it, though whether the law meets it turns on the content-efficacy question left open above; the [argument from reason](/topics/argument-from-reason/) presses a parallel worry from the side of rational justification, and Davidson's action theory — the causal theory of intentional action — is the backdrop against which the Map develops its own [dualist account of action](/topics/philosophy-of-action-under-dualism/).

**Tenet 5 (Occam's Razor Has Limits)** cuts against a too-quick preference for Davidson's view. Anomalous monism can look simpler — one ontology, no fundamental psychophysical law. But the Map holds that simplicity is an unreliable guide when knowledge is incomplete: if the price of that simplicity is mental-property epiphenomenalism, parsimony has been bought at the cost of the phenomenon it was meant to explain. The Map speculates that the extra commitment of an interface law is warranted precisely where the simpler theory leaves the mental doing nothing *as* mental.
