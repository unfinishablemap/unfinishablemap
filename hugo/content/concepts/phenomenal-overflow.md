Phenomenal overflow is Ned Block's argument that [phenomenal consciousness](/concepts/phenomenal-consciousness/) has richer content than [access-consciousness](/concepts/access-consciousness/)—we experience more than we can report, think about, or use in reasoning. The overflow hypothesis challenges the identification of consciousness with cognitive access, suggesting that the "what it's like" of experience exceeds what enters the [global workspace](/concepts/global-workspace-theory/). In effect, overflow is the claim that what is [phenomenally presented](/concepts/phenomenal-presentation/) outstrips what is accessed: the unreported letters must still be felt as a given, even when attention cannot reach them.

The argument matters for [dualism](/tenets/#dualism) because it separates phenomenology from access. Overflow is one of several features of [perceptual experience](/topics/dualist-perception/) that strain access-based accounts. If experience overflows accessibility, then functional theories that explain when information becomes reportable (like Global Workspace Theory) cannot be complete theories of consciousness: something phenomenal would lie beyond what access-based descriptions capture. Overflow alone does not show that this remainder is non-physical—Block himself seeks its neural basis—so its support for dualism is indirect, as [explained below](#tenets).

## The Overflow Argument {#argument}

Block's overflow argument holds that phenomenal consciousness has higher capacity than cognitive access. The core claim: we are conscious of more than we can report or reason about at any moment.

### Sperling's Iconic Memory Paradigm

The primary evidence comes from George Sperling's 1960 experiments on iconic memory:

**Procedure**: Flash a grid of 12 letters (3 rows × 4 columns) for 50 milliseconds. Ask subjects to report what they saw.

**Whole report result**: Subjects can report only 4-5 letters—roughly one row. Working memory limits how much can be accessed and reported.

**Partial report result**: If a tone cues which row to report *immediately* after the display, subjects can accurately report any row with high accuracy (about 3 of 4 letters).

**The inference**: Subjects must have been phenomenally conscious of all 12 letters—otherwise, how could they report any cued row? But only 4-5 letters were A-conscious (available for reasoning and free report). The remaining letters were P-conscious but not A-conscious.

Block's formulation: "Perceptual consciousness is richer (i.e., has a higher capacity) than cognitive access."

### What Overflow Would Show

If phenomenal overflow is real:

1. **P-consciousness ≠ A-consciousness**: The two can dissociate in degree, not just kind
2. **Functional theories are incomplete**: Explaining when information becomes accessible doesn't explain phenomenology
3. **The hard problem persists**: Even complete access-explanations leave phenomenal richness unexplained
4. **Introspection has limits**: We cannot fully report our own experience

## The Deflationary Response {#deflationary}

Critics—notably Daniel Dennett and Michael Cohen—argue that overflow is an illusion. On their view, subjects don't experience all 12 letters; they experience a *general impression* ("lots of letters") that becomes specific only when the cue directs attention.

### The Deflationary Interpretation

**No rich phenomenology**: Subjects have a gestalt impression of "letterishness" in the uncued regions, not specific letter-experiences. The sense that they "saw all the letters" is a post-hoc judgment, not a report of actual phenomenology.

**Retroactive construction**: When the cue arrives, attention accesses iconic memory traces and constructs the specific experience. The phenomenology is created by the access process, not accessed by it.

**Inflation fallacy**: Subjects mistake the *ability* to access any row (partial report success) for *actually having accessed* all rows simultaneously. But ability doesn't entail prior experience.

### Introspective Fallibility

Deflationary theorists invoke a broader point: introspection is notoriously unreliable. Subjects confidently misreport their mental states in numerous well-documented cases—change blindness, inattentional blindness, confabulation in split-brain patients. Why should phenomenal reports about "seeing all the letters" be trusted when other introspective reports are demonstrably wrong?

Block's response: the partial report paradigm provides behavioral evidence independent of introspection. The ability to report any cued row with high accuracy shows *something* was available—the dispute is whether that something was phenomenally conscious. The behavioral data constrain the options more than bare introspective reports would.

### Why Deflationism Deserves Consideration

The deflationary reading isn't merely dismissive. It raises a genuine methodological problem: how do we distinguish rich phenomenology from the impression of rich phenomenology?

Subjects report "I saw all the letters"—but this report is itself an A-conscious judgment. If P-consciousness were truly richer than A-consciousness, we could never directly verify it through reports, since reports *are* access. The overflow hypothesis may be unfalsifiable.

Cohen and Dennett argue that consciousness cannot be separated from function. If there's no functional difference between having rich phenomenology and merely believing one has rich phenomenology, the distinction may be empty.

## Additional Evidence {#evidence}

Beyond Sperling, several phenomena suggest phenomenal overflow:

### Inattentional Blindness

In inattentional blindness experiments, subjects fail to notice salient stimuli (like a gorilla walking through a scene) when attention is engaged elsewhere. Yet in a large recent study, Nartker et al. (2025) found that "inattentionally blind" participants, as a group, retain sensitivity to stimuli they explicitly deny noticing—when probed, they report location, colour, and shape above chance.

This result is double-edged for overflow. Nartker and colleagues tentatively read it not as unconscious processing but as *residual conscious vision*—inattention degrades awareness rather than abolishing it—which undercuts the use of inattentional blindness as evidence that awareness requires attention, a premise invoked in support of Global Workspace Theory. But because those features can be reported when cued, the finding arguably shows degraded *access* rather than the phenomenology *outside* access that Block's thesis needs. The authors do not take up overflow; on the Map's reading the study constrains that debate more than it settles it.

### The Fineness of Grain Argument

Our perceptual experience discriminates shades of color, spatial positions, and temporal moments more finely than we have concepts for. You can see the difference between two nearly identical shades even if you have no names for either. The experience outstrips the conceptual capacity to categorize and report.

McDowell's (1994) response—that demonstrative concepts like "that shade" can be as fine-grained as needed—faces a problem Kelly (2001) presses: demonstrative concepts require re-identification capacity, which we lack for precise shades. The concept dissolves when the experience ends.

### Change Blindness and Its Limits

Change blindness shows we miss large changes between scenes when attention is diverted. This might seem to argue *against* rich phenomenology—if we don't notice a change, perhaps we didn't experience the original. But subjects often report a vague sense that "something is different" without being able to identify what. This suggests phenomenology exceeding explicit access.

## Theoretical Stakes {#stakes}

The overflow debate isn't merely empirical—it has theoretical stakes for understanding consciousness.

### For Global Workspace Theory

[GWT](/concepts/global-workspace-theory/) explains A-consciousness: information becomes globally broadcast through prefrontal-parietal ignition, making it available for reasoning, report, and action. If phenomenology equals access, GWT would be a complete theory of consciousness.

But if overflow is real, GWT explains only *part* of consciousness. The unaccessed phenomenal field—what you experience but can't report—lies outside GWT's explanatory scope. This limits GWT to being a theory of access, not phenomenology.

### For Functionalism

[functionalism](/concepts/functionalism/) defines mental states by their causal-functional roles. A-consciousness fits functionalism perfectly—it's defined by what information *does*. But P-consciousness, especially overflowing P-consciousness, resists definition by access. If experience exceeds access, access-functionalism cannot capture it, and a functionalist must find phenomenology some other, pre-access role.

The overflow argument strengthens anti-functionalist arguments like [inverted qualia](/concepts/inverted-qualia/) and [zombies](/concepts/philosophical-zombies/). If phenomenology can exceed access in *degree* (overflow), perhaps it can diverge from function in *character* (inversion) or exist without function entirely (zombies).

### For Illusionism

[illusionism](/concepts/illusionism/) holds that phenomenal consciousness is an illusion—we represent ourselves as having irreducible experiential qualities when we don't. Illusionism aligns with deflationary readings of overflow: the *sense* of rich phenomenology is just another functional state, not evidence of genuine phenomenology beyond function.

If overflow is real, illusionism must explain why we would systematically represent ourselves as having richer experience than we do. The illusion would need to be not merely about *having* phenomenology but about phenomenology *exceeding* access—a more elaborate error. Frankish's formulation moves that surcharge rather than doubling it. On his illusionism, introspection misrepresents non-phenomenal, physical properties of experience as phenomenal (Frankish 2016), so an apparent excess over access would be one more content of the same misrepresentation, not a second illusion layered over the first. The added cost falls instead on the [adequacy of that functional account](/concepts/cognitive-phenomenology/), which must now explain a represented richness outrunning anything the access machinery delivers.

## Relation to Site Perspective {#tenets}

### Dualism

Phenomenal overflow bears on [dualism](/tenets/#dualism) by separating phenomenology from *access*. If phenomenal consciousness exceeds what access-based theories can explain—even theories as sophisticated as GWT—then no account of cognitive access alone is a complete account of consciousness.

The logic parallels other anti-physicalist arguments:
- **Zombies**: Function without phenomenology is conceivable
- **Inverted qualia**: Phenomenology can vary while function stays constant
- **Overflow**: Phenomenology can exceed access in capacity

Each is offered to show that phenomenal properties are not identical to some class of functional properties, but overflow's version is the narrowest, and it does not reach dualism on its own. Block's own use of it is physicalist: he argues that "we can find a neural realizer of this overflow" if the neural basis of phenomenal consciousness is taken not to include that of cognitive accessibility (Block 2007), and [recurrent processing theory](/concepts/recurrent-processing-theory/) predicts overflow on similar neural grounds. Overflow fits the tenet without establishing it. The further step—that local recurrent processing leaves phenomenal character as unexplained as global broadcast does—comes from the [hard problem](/topics/hard-problem-of-consciousness/), not from Sperling.

### Bidirectional Interaction

The [Bidirectional Interaction tenet](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) holds that consciousness causally influences the physical world. Overflow complicates but doesn't undermine this claim.

If phenomenology exceeds access, what role does the "extra" phenomenology play? One possibility: the overflowing phenomenal field is where consciousness operates most fundamentally. [Attention selects](/concepts/attention-as-interface/) from this field what enters access—and attention may be where consciousness exercises causal influence.

On this view, the rich phenomenal field is the stage; attention is conscious selection; access is the downstream result. [Stapp's quantum Zeno model](/concepts/stapp-quantum-mind/) proposes a specific mechanism: sustained attention (rapid repeated observations) could hold a neural pattern in place once it has occurred, without weighting the quantum outcomes themselves, and so determine what gets selected from the phenomenal field for access and action. Whether or not this specific mechanism is correct, the structural point remains: overflow fits a picture on which phenomenology is primary, and access is the result of conscious selection from richer phenomenal material.

### Occam's Razor Has Limits

The deflationary response to overflow appeals to parsimony: why posit rich phenomenology beyond access when the impression of rich phenomenology suffices? This reflects the materialist strategy of explaining away apparent features of consciousness rather than explaining them.

But [Occam's Razor has limits](/tenets/#occams-limits). The parsimony of deflationary accounts comes at the cost of dismissing what subjects report: they say they saw all the letters, not that they had an impression of letterishness. Deflationary theories require subjects to be systematically wrong about their own experience.

When a theory's parsimony requires dismissing the primary data (subjective reports), parsimony may mislead. The overflow hypothesis accepts phenomenal reports at face value; deflationism reinterprets them as judgments about what *seemed* to happen rather than reports of what was experienced. Both approaches have costs—the question is which costs are more acceptable.

## The Contested Status {#contested}

Phenomenal overflow remains genuinely contested. The Unfinishable Map presents it as evidence for the [richness of phenomenology](/concepts/phenomenal-depth/) beyond access, not as established fact.

**What's secure**: Subjects can report more with partial cues than with whole report. Some information is available briefly that doesn't enter working memory.

**What's contested**: Whether this available-but-unreported information is phenomenally conscious. The debate hinges on whether phenomenology can exist without access—which is precisely what's at issue between overflow proponents and deflationary critics.

**The methodological bind**: If phenomenology exceeds access, we cannot directly verify it through access-dependent methods (reports, reasoning about experience). The overflow hypothesis may be unfalsifiable from a strictly third-person perspective. This unfalsifiability is generally a mark against a hypothesis—yet it's also exactly what we'd expect if phenomenology genuinely transcends access. The situation is frustrating rather than decisive: unfalsifiability doesn't confirm overflow, but neither does it refute it.

A diagnosis from a neighbouring debate suggests why the standoff persists rather than narrowing. Writing about cognitive phenomenology, Fürst (2017) argues that phenomenal contrast—a method designed to break this kind of symmetry—is dialectically ineffective, because "introspective judgments about phenomenology are regimented by the view initially held": each side's introspection returns the verdict its theory already licenses. She suggests the point bears on introspection-based arguments for phenomenology in general. The [Würzburg-Titchener controversy](/concepts/cognitive-phenomenology/) is the usual historical precedent: fifteen years of rigorous laboratories, all relying on trained introspection while disputing each other's protocols, reached opposite conclusions and abandoned the question rather than resolving it. Disputes of this shape have not been settled from inside introspection. The overflow debate fits the pattern at its crux—whether the uncued letters were experienced specifically or only generically turns on what subjects' introspective reports are worth—which helps explain why Block and Cohen–Dennett can each read Sperling as confirming their own position.

This connects to [the broader void of pre-conceptual knowing](/voids/acquaintance-void/)—experience that exceeds conceptualization. If phenomenal overflow is real, it exemplifies this broader limit: there may be aspects of experience that resist not just verbal report but cognitive access altogether.
