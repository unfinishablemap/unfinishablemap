The circularity objection holds that memory cannot be what personal identity consists in, because remembering an experience already requires being the person who had it. Joseph Butler stated it in 1736: "consciousness of personal identity presupposes, and therefore cannot constitute, personal identity". *Quasi-memory* is Sydney Shoemaker's term (1970) for the standard reply. A quasi-memory meets the conditions of remembering except one: the rememberer need not have had the experience. It presents a past experience from the inside and derives, by the right kind of causal chain, from *someone's* having had it. Ordinary memory is then the special case in which that someone is oneself, so a memory criterion of identity need not presuppose the identity it explains. Terence Penelhum reached the same idea in the same year as "retrocognition", and Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) made it central to the reductionist case. Whether quasi-memory is intelligible apart from memory is disputed, and the [coherence debate](#coherence) (explained below) remains open.

For The Unfinishable Map the verdict is compatibility without support. The Map *posits* a subject that persists through time rather than inferring one from memory, so the reply removes no premise the Map uses. The same reply shows that memory, however accurate and however vividly it feels like one's own, cannot certify which subject had a remembered experience, because a quasi-memory could present itself in the same way. On the Map's own reconstruction it is also committed to quasi-memory's coherence, since its verdict that a copy is a new subject needs the copy's accurate apparent memories to be something other than memories or confabulations. The critics of coherence give a dualist no ally: the persisting subject they defend is an embodied person. What makes one subject the same subject over time is left open.

## Butler's Objection and Reid's Distinction {#circularity}

Against Locke's memory account of personal identity, Butler's dissertation "Of Personal Identity", appended to *The Analogy of Religion*, grants that consciousness of the past does "ascertain our personal identity to ourselves", but denies that it makes identity. If it did, "a person has not existed a single moment, nor done one action, but what he can remember". Consciousness of identity presupposes identity and so cannot constitute it, "any more than knowledge, in any other case, can constitute truth, which it presupposes."

Butler's own view ties identity to substance: "The identity of these, therefore, cannot subsist with diversity of substance." In his "Thirdly" he argues that consciousness of being the same person proves sameness of substance even if a person is only a property of one, "since the same property cannot be transferred from one substance to another". Locke had left that premise open, and Kant's elastic-ball footnote treats the transfer it rules out as conceivable.

Thomas Reid (1785) sharpened the line between evidence and constitution. Memory "testifies not only that this was done, but that it was done by me who now remember it" (p. 203), but Locke's doctrine is one in which "personal identity is confounded with the evidence which we have of our personal identity" (p. 214). Reid's subject is simple: "A person is something indivisible, and is what Leibniz calls a monad" (p. 203).

The objection admits three readings:

- **Conceptual.** In Parfit's statement of it, "It is part of our concept of memory that we can remember only our own experiences" (1984, p. 220), so any memory criterion is circular.
- **Evidential.** Butler was warning against treating evidence of identity as its ground. Marc Slors judges it "likely that Butler's point was merely to argue against the conflation of epistemic and metaphysical criteria of identity" (2001, p. 187).
- **Direct awareness.** Parfit raises the possibility that Butler meant "In memory we are directly aware of our own identity through time, and aware that this is a separate, further fact" (1984, p. 222). The words are Parfit's statement of a reading, not Butler's.

Quasi-memory answers only the first. The second is common ground. The third is where the Map's interest lies.

## Quasi-Memory: Shoemaker's Reply {#quasi-memory}

Shoemaker introduced the term in "Persons and Their Pasts": "Quasi-remembering, as I shall use the term, includes remembering as a special case" (1970, p. 24; reprint pagination throughout). It "is subject to a weaker previous awareness condition" than remembering (p. 24); in its developed form, the state must "stand in an appropriate causal relationship to, a past cognitive and sensory state of someone or other" (p. 34). Butler-style objections, Shoemaker argues, "assume that remembering involves the satisfaction of the strong previous awareness condition" (p. 42), and a weaker condition avoids them.

Two consequences follow. Shoemaker holds it "logically possible that one should remember an action or experience from the inside (i.e., quasi-remember it)" (p. 45) when the experience was not one's own. And memory judgements keep only "de facto" [immunity to error through misidentification](/concepts/immunity-to-error-through-misidentification/) (p. 46): they cannot go wrong about *whose* past is remembered only because, as things are, our memories come from our own pasts.

Penelhum's *Survival and Disembodied Existence* (1970) reached the same idea under another name. On Locke's tale of a prince's soul, with its consciousness of the prince's past, entering a cobbler's body, he argues that bodily transfer is not the only reading (p. 88): "We could say that the cobbler and prince retrocognize one another's pasts" (p. 85). He keeps "the priority of the bodily criterion" (p. 67). Which of the two came first within 1970 is unresolved. Parfit credits the term to Shoemaker: "I follow SHOEMAKER (2)" (1984, p. 516 n. 13), that is, "Persons and Their Pasts".

The idea is older than either name. Locke asked why one intellectual substance might not "have represented to it, as done by itself, what *it* never did, and was perhaps done by some other agent" (*Essay* II.xxvii.13). Unable to exclude this "from the nature of things", he conceded that if consciousness can be transferred "it will be possible that two thinking substances may make but one person." Kant's footnote to the third paralogism imagines, by analogy with elastic balls, a series of substances "of which the first would communicate its state, together with its consciousness, to the second" (A363–364n), until the last is conscious of every earlier state as its own.

## Parfit's Development {#parfit}

Parfit's definition has three clauses (1984, p. 220): I seem to remember having an experience; someone did have it; and "my apparent memory is causally dependent, in the right kind of way, on that past experience". So "ordinary memories are a sub-class of quasi-memories" (p. 220).

His case is Jane, who receives copies of some of Paul's memory-traces and seems to remember lightning over Venice that Paul saw. Knowing the cause, she can conclude that she accurately quasi-remembers Paul's experience. Her apparent memories "may come to her in what Peacocke calls the first-person mode of presentation" (p. 221). What Parfit separates is the further belief. Our apparent memories "come with a belief that, unless they are delusions, they are about our own experiences. But, in the case of experience-memories, this is a separable belief" (p. 222).

The reply to the conceptual reading follows: "Since the continuity of quasi-memory does not presuppose personal identity, it may be part of what constitutes personal identity" (p. 222). Quasi-memory shows that a memory criterion *need not* presuppose identity. It does not by itself show that reductionism is true.

Against the direct-awareness reading Parfit offers testimony and an argument. The testimony: "This is not a point that can be argued" (p. 223); he reports no awareness of himself as a separately existing subject. The argument is the relay, an example Parfit credits to Wachsberg (n. 17): "As both Locke and Kant argued, there might be a series of such entities that were psychologically continuous. Memories might be passed from one to the next like a baton in a relay race" (p. 223). A Replica who seems to remember the original's last thought would believe, falsely, that one thinker had both that thought and his own next one (§81). Such awareness, Parfit concludes, "cannot in fact be distinguished from our awareness of mere psychological continuity", and "Unless we have other reasons to believe in their existence, we should reject this belief" (§81).

Gareth Evans objected, as Parfit reports him (n. 15), that a subject who unknowingly receives another's memory-traces cannot gain knowledge of the past by retreating to judgements that *someone* had the experience. Parfit accepts this for Evans's subject and replies that Jane, who knows the cause, does gain knowledge.

## Is Quasi-Memory Coherent? {#coherence}

The critics deny that quasi-memory can be understood except through memory.

- **John McDowell** (1997) asks us to "pause before we accept Butler's assumption that circularity would be a problem for what Locke intends" (p. 367). His main charge is derivativeness: quasi-memory is reached by dropping a requirement from memory, and "it is disastrous to conclude, from the fact that we can arrive at it by dropping a requirement, that what it is for the second notion to be applicable is intelligible otherwise than in terms of an appearance" (p. 374). For McDowell the persisting subject is "not a peculiar Cartesian item, but a person, of whose continued life that continuity is, precisely, an aspect" (p. 361).
- **Arthur Collins** (1997, abstract) argues that apparent memories meeting the weak definition "turn out not to involve remembering anything at all".
- **Slors** (2001) holds that "Q-memory is impossible to square with a holism of the mental" (p. 190), and proposes that memory is "only indicative rather than constitutive" (p. 187) of psychological continuity.
- **Stanley Klein and Shaun Nichols** (2012) took patient R.B., whose accurate memories, by their account, did not feel like his own, to show, in Rachel Roache's summary, "that the conception of quasi-memory that underpins many memory-based accounts is inadequate" (Roache 2016, abstract). The case is treated in [phenomenology-of-memory-and-the-self](/topics/phenomenology-of-memory-and-the-self/).

The defenders hold that the concept stands on its own.

- **Roache** (2006, abstract): "From my quasi-memory of doing x, I cannot infer that I did x; but I can infer that somebody did x." In 2016 she argued that Klein and Nichols's "characterization of R.B.'s experience is implausible".
- **Sven Bernecker** (2009, chapter abstract) holds that quasi-memory "is indeed coherent and is not a derivative from the concept of memory", the direct denial of McDowell's charge.
- **Christopher Buford** (2009, abstract) answers McDowell with Annalisa Coliva's two types of immunity to error through misidentification.
- **Georg Northoff** (2000, abstract) rejects two of Marya Schechtman's arguments against quasi-memory and finds quasi-memories "compatible with current empirical knowledge".
- **Vilius Dranseika** (2020, abstract), an experimental study of ordinary usage, reports that "neither the factivity constraint nor the strong previous awareness condition is a necessary part of ordinary use of 'remembering'". That bears on the folk concept, not on what memory metaphysically requires.

Neither side has a decisive argument. No actual quasi-memory across persons has been documented, so the dispute concerns a conceptual possibility.

## What the Map Takes from the Debate {#map-reading}

From here the analysis is the Map's own reconstruction. None of the authors above discusses the Map.

**What the neutral result shows.** If quasi-memory is coherent, a memory criterion of identity is not circular. That helps the reductionist only against the conceptual reading. It leaves Reid's indivisible subject untouched and qualifies only his evidential claim: memory's testimony that an act "was done by me who now remember it" holds because, in fact, memories come from their owners' pasts. In the actual world memory stays defeasible evidence of identity, and nothing more.

**The posit loses no premise.** The Map's [first background posit](/tenets/background-commitments/) holds that a subject's sameness over time is "a real further fact rather than a convention over successive states", and the Map's [reading of Kant's Paralogisms](/topics/kants-paralogisms-and-the-maps-subject/) holds that the Map "*posits* its subject rather than *inferring* it". Quasi-memory closes a route the Map does not take, from memory to a persisting subject. It does not show the subject absent, and it settles neither Parfit's constitution claim nor the Map's further fact.

**Memory cannot certify the subject.** Parfit grants quasi-memories the first-person mode and makes the belief in ownership separable. Whatever a memory presents, whether its pastness, its from-the-inside character or its felt [mine-ness](/concepts/mine-ness/), a quasi-memory with another source could present in the same way. The felt pastness and ownership of a memory can *present* it as one's own; they cannot *certify* that it is. This is the Kant article's first exposure, felt mine-ness used as evidence, in diachronic form: felt mine-ness is compatible with the Map's subject and gives it no support over rivals that predict the same experience. The [personal-identity](/topics/personal-identity/) article's past-self argument survives in its non-recurrence form, which turns on the undergoing that memory cannot restage rather than on who underwent it.

**Butler's premise is where the case turns.** Locke left the non-transfer premise open, Kant treats the transfer as conceivable, and Parfit's relay builds on both. The Map's [haecceity](/concepts/haecceity/) posit holds that the later subject *is* the earlier one, and the Kant article grants that this is "stated rather than argued". If quasi-memory is coherent, as the Map accepts, no memory, however accurate, could tell a persisting subject from a relay of subjects. Memory therefore does not separate the Map's posit from a reductionist chain or an embodied person, as [P-I4](/positions/individuation-and-subjecthood/#p-i4)'s calibration already records: "no independent evidence discriminates it from Parfitian reductionism".

**The Map is committed to coherence.** The [individuation register](/positions/individuation-and-subjecthood/)'s [P-I4](/positions/individuation-and-subjecthood/#p-i4) holds that a copy is "a numerically distinct subject however complete the psychological continuity". A copy's accurate, rightly caused apparent memories of the original's life are, in Parfit's phrase, "full quasi-memories" (p. 295). If quasi-memory were incoherent, those states would be either genuine memories, which would make the copy the original against [P-I4](/positions/individuation-and-subjecthood/#p-i4), or confabulations, which ignores how they were caused. The Map therefore sides with Shoemaker and Parfit on coherence and against Parfit on what continuity constitutes. [P-I4](/positions/individuation-and-subjecthood/#p-i4) needs the concept only to apply; whether it can be understood apart from memory, which McDowell denies, it leaves open.

**The critics are no allies.** McDowell rejects quasi-memory on behalf of "a person". Nichols (2017, abstract) holds that memory as *evidence* of identity "fits naturally with animalist views". Defeating quasi-memory would favour an embodied or animal subject, not a non-physical one, so the Map cannot borrow the critics' conclusion without their premises.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism).** Tenet 1 is untouched in its bare form, because the Map does not argue for the irreducibility of consciousness from memory. Quasi-memory gives the Map's subject no support, and its critics' alternative is the embodied person.

**[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds).** The indexical objection needs a determinate "I", and its diachronic form, which asks which *future* subject is genuinely me, needs that "I" to persist. Memory cannot supply the persistence. The individuation register's [P-I2](/positions/individuation-and-subjecthood/#p-i2) already records that Tenet 4 "is stated unconditionally but argued conditionally", so this adds no new concession. Parfit's own verdict is conditional on there being no "other reasons". The Map's other reasons, agent causation's need for a persisting bearer and the indexical objection itself, are framework-internal: reasons for a posit, not evidence.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction).** The [personal-identity](/topics/personal-identity/) article's reply to the replica case appeals to the causal history of "*this* consciousness" making "*these* quantum selections". As the Kant article argues, that history individuates a subject only if one persisting selector made the selections, and a later selector's memory of them cannot show that one did. Nothing here bears on whether conscious states make a physical difference, or in which systems.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits).** The reductionist view does without the further fact. Tenet 5 stops that economy counting against the posit, without letting it count for the posit.

**What stays open.** The Map's [aspect ontology](/concepts/bi-aspectual-ontology/) is silent on what makes a subject one subject over time, while agent causation and the indexical objection draw on a persisting subject ([where-the-substance-commitment-enters](/concepts/where-the-substance-commitment-enters/)). Whether an aspect can supply that subject is open. Quasi-memory sharpens the question by showing that memory cannot answer it, and settles it in neither direction.
