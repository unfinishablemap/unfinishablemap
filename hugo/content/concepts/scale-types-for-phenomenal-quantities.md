The mathematics of measurement gives the question "can experience be measured?" a layered answer, not a yes or no. The representational theory of measurement (RTM) — the axiomatic framework of Krantz, Luce, Suppes, and Tversky — makes measurement claims checkable: an attribute is measurable on a given scale type exactly when its qualitative structure satisfies the axioms of a representation theorem, and its numbers mean only what a uniqueness theorem says survives the scale's admissible transformations. Applied to phenomenal quantities — pain intensity, the vividness of an afterimage, the felt contrast between understanding and incomprehension — the framework delivers a graded verdict. Within a single subject, ordinal and even interval-grade structure is an open empirical question that has, in restricted perceptual cases, been tested and passed. Across subjects, the verdict is sharper but conditional on one premise: if phenomenal states are accessible only from the first person, as the Map holds, then no observable relation connects two subjects' experiences, no cross-subject empirical structure exists for a representation theorem to be stated about, and interpersonal phenomenal comparison remains a free parameter that no axiom constrains. The conditional matters: a physicalist who identifies phenomenal magnitudes with neural magnitudes denies the premise, and whether consciousness has degree structure at all is itself theory-relative — both rivals are taken up below.

This article presents RTM compactly, runs the scale-type ladder for phenomenal quantities, states the cross-subject impossibility as a uniqueness-theorem fact, and closes with the [meaningfulness discipline](#the-meaningfulness-discipline) (explained below) — the testable methodological constraint that [Judging the Map as Science](/apex/judging-the-map-as-science/) says the interface programme owes. Along the way it refines the unit argument of [consciousness-and-the-problem-of-measurement-standards](/topics/consciousness-and-the-problem-of-measurement-standards/) against the strongest rejoinder measurement theory offers.

## What the Representational Theory Says

RTM is the neutral formal core every live camp accepts as mathematics, whatever they dispute about its application. In Eran Tal's summary for the Stanford Encyclopedia, "RTM defines measurement as the construction of mappings from empirical relational structures into numerical relational structures" (Tal 2020, citing Krantz et al. 1971: 9). An empirical relational structure is a set of objects together with qualitative relations among them — orderings, comparisons of differences, concatenation operations, trade-offs. A **representation theorem** proves that if the structure satisfies certain axioms, a structure-preserving map into the numbers exists. A **uniqueness theorem** says which transformations of that map preserve all the empirical information — and Stevens's famous scale types (Stevens 1946) just *are* the resulting invariance classes: "scale types are individuated by the families of transformations they can undergo without loss of empirical information" (Tal 2020). Ratio scales tolerate multiplication by a positive constant, interval scales any positive linear transformation, ordinal scales any increasing monotone map, nominal scales any one-to-one substitution.

The companion notion is **meaningfulness** (Narens 1981; Luce et al. 1990): a statement involving scale values is meaningful only if its truth value is invariant under the admissible transformations. "Today is twice as hot as yesterday" is meaningless on the Celsius scale — its truth flips under conversion to Fahrenheit — but meaningful in kelvin, because temperature ratios are invariant on a ratio scale. Meaningfulness is the discipline that stops numbers from claiming more structure than the axioms earned.

RTM is also silent on realism about the measured attribute: its theorems serve quantity realists like Michell, operationalists, and the Map's dualism alike.

## From the Ferguson Committee to Conjoint Measurement

The framework's history explains why the Map cannot rest its measurement arguments where they currently rest. In the 1930s the British Association's Ferguson committee examined whether sensation intensity is measurable; the physicists' position, as Tal summarises Norman Campbell: "Since intensities of sensation cannot be concatenated to each other in the manner afforded by lengths and weights, there could be no fundamental measurement of sensation intensity" (Tal 2020; Ferguson et al. 1940). Stevens's countermove was definitional: he redefined measurement as the "assignment of numerals to objects or events according to rules" (Stevens 1951: 1, quoted in Tal 2020), under which his sone scale of loudness counts as measurement because the assignment rule is consistent.

Sociologically Stevens won; formally, both sides were superseded. Against Stevens, the axiomatic school showed that rule-governed assignment is not sufficient — representation must be earned by axioms, which is why RTM abandoned his permissive definition. Against Campbell, Luce and Tukey (1964) proved that **conjoint measurement** delivers interval-scale representations of two attributes from a qualitative ordering of their joint effects alone, given cancellation axioms — no concatenation operation required. Additivity-via-concatenation is sufficient for quantitative structure, not necessary.

That second result bears directly on the Map's own corpus. [consciousness-and-the-problem-of-measurement-standards](/topics/consciousness-and-the-problem-of-measurement-standards/) argues the unit failure from non-additivity — "Units require that the measured quantity be decomposable into identical, additive portions" — and pains interact and mask in ways that violate additivity. Conjoint measurement is the standard rejoinder to exactly this argument form: quantitative structure does not require that instances of the attribute be additively decomposable, only that trade-off orderings satisfy the cancellation conditions.

Whether the conjoint route works for psychological attributes is contested from both directions. Ho, Landy, and Maloney (2008) ran the conjoint axiom tests inside perception, on observers' judgments of gloss and surface texture, and the additive representation survived — an existence proof that the axioms are checkable in practice, though for structures of *perceptual comparison judgments about stimuli*, whose promotion to phenomenal quantities is the bridge the [ladder below](#the-scale-type-ladder-for-phenomenal-quantities) polices. Trendler (2009; 2019) argues the opposite: testing the cancellation axioms requires experimental control — isolation, manipulation, an error theory — that psychological attributes do not admit, and the maximum-likelihood conjoint method fits a model rather than testing cancellation directly, so the conjoint programme is unrealisable for psychology at large. The Map leans on neither side uncritically. What the debate settles is narrower: a unit argument for phenomenal quantities cannot rest on non-concatenability alone. Its honest forms are either per-attribute — the difference and conjoint axioms fail, or remain untested, for *this* phenomenal attribute — or holistic, resting on the unity of the experiential field that the measurement-standards article already invokes. That refinement strengthens the argument by moving it off the ground Luce and Tukey took away.

## The Scale-Type Ladder for Phenomenal Quantities

What follows is the Map's application of the formal apparatus; the rival verdicts in the next two sections dispute parts of it.

**Nominal.** The taxonomy of phenomenal character — *which* experience — is scale-type zero. The instrument failure the measurement-standards article identifies (a neural correlate registers *that* an experience occurs, not *which*) is, in RTM terms, the observation that public data support at most a nominal classification of phenomenal states, and only via correlation.

**Ordinal, within-subject.** Pairwise introspective comparisons — this pain is worse than that one — plausibly satisfy weak-order axioms over restricted domains, though the axiom tests actually run are psychophysical rather than introspective. Two cautions apply. Threshold effects generate intransitive indifference (A indistinguishable from B, B from C, yet A distinguishable from C), so the honest model is a semiorder rather than a weak order — a structure RTM handles. And ordinality must be earned per dimension, not assumed globally: Bayne, Hohwy, and Owen (2016) argue that the levels-based framework for global states of consciousness is untenable, replacing it with a multidimensional account on which sedation and psychedelic states need not be comparable on any single dimension. The global "level of consciousness" is argued to fail even the ordinal rung.

**Interval, within-subject.** Difference structures — orderings of *differences* between experiences — yield interval scales if their axioms hold, and the axioms are statistically testable. Maximum likelihood difference scaling (Maloney and Yang 2003) estimates within-subject interval-scale representations of perceptual differences with the underlying model open to test, and the conjoint results above sit at the same grade. Whether the stark contrasts the [phenomenal-contrast-method](/concepts/phenomenal-contrast-method/) extracts — understanding versus incomprehension, agency versus passivity — themselves satisfy difference-structure axioms is a question this article has not found posed anywhere in the literature; the Map poses it as open and testable.

**Ratio, within-subject.** Stevens claimed ratio scales of sensation via magnitude estimation. Narens (1996) made the implicit assumptions axiomatic and testable, separating the claim that subjects have a ratio-scale representation from the claim that their numerals report its values veridically. Direct laboratory tests of those axioms in loudness production (Ellermeier and Faulhammer 2000; Birkenbusch and Ellermeier 2016) did not vindicate taking subjects' numerals at face value; the cautious surviving position is at most ratio-like structure without veridical numerals. This is among the strongest empirical vindications of the measurement-standards article's proxy discipline — "The language of measurement is borrowed; the reality of measurement is absent" — delivered by the measurement theorists themselves. The asymmetric trust — order-judgments credited, numerals discounted — is principled: monotone distortion of the mapping from experience to response preserves order but destroys ratios; interval claims sit between, standing or falling with the difference-structure tests above.

## The Cross-Subject Rung: A Uniqueness-Theorem Fact

The fifth rung is where the formalism localises the Map's calibration problem precisely. Each subject's representation, where one exists, is fixed only up to that subject's own admissible transformations — monotone, affine, or similarity, according to the structure their comparisons support. Nothing in any axiom on a single subject's structure constrains the alignment *between* subjects. Interpersonal comparison would require choosing one representative mapping per subject and pairing them — a degree of freedom the within-subject data leave open. The point is general to uniqueness theorems rather than special to consciousness — formally the same undetermined parameter as interpersonal utility comparison in decision theory.

What would close the gap is an *empirical relation spanning subjects* — some observable qualitative relation between one subject's experience and another's, from which a joint representation theorem could be proved. Here the neutral mathematics ends and frameworks diverge. From RTM alone it follows only that within-subject axioms never deliver interpersonal comparability; whether a spanning relation exists is a further empirical and metaphysical question. On the Map's premise that phenomenal states are accessible only first-personally, no spanning relation is observable even in principle: the empirical relational structure whose objects are drawn from two subjects' experiences has no observable relations, so the homomorphism construction cannot begin. The missing "shared water bath" of the measurement-standards article is exactly this absent structure, restated formally. A physicalist who identifies phenomenal magnitudes with neural magnitudes gets a spanning relation for free — cross-subject calibration becomes neural measurement — at the price of assuming the identity the explanatory gap contests.

The stronger naturalist route runs through proxies rather than identity. Measurement never requires direct observation of its attribute — temperature is read off mercury columns — and behavioral relations span subjects trivially: matching tasks, discrimination-based scaling, counts of just-noticeable differences in Fechner's tradition. These are genuine spanning structures, and representation theorems can be proved about them; but, as at the nominal rung, they represent *discrimination behavior*, and promoting that to phenomenal magnitude is the contested bridge, not a theorem — the access problem Browning and Veit (2020) call the measurement problem of consciousness. Within-subject comparison is not wholly innocent either — across occasions it routes through memory, itself a proxy — but it is at least a performed act generating data; cross-subject phenomenal comparison corresponds to no act at all. One presupposition deserves naming: the within/across partition assumes subjects are determinately individuated, and the split-brain case puts that in question rather than merely complicating it ([unity-of-consciousness](/concepts/unity-of-consciousness/)). The scale-type analysis therefore formalises the calibration argument rather than independently proving it: the impossibility is permanent exactly if the first-person-access premise holds.

## Rival Verdicts

Three rivals read the ladder differently.

**Operationalism.** Stevens's heirs can grant every theorem and insist that measurement never needed more than consistent numeral-assignment. The reply is the one the representational school already gave: assignment rules earn no structure. And the question operational substitutes cannot reach — whether your pain-at-label-five feels like mine — is the one a phenomenal measurement would have to answer.

**The quantity objection.** Michell (1997; 1999; 2000) argues that whether a psychological attribute has quantitative structure at all is an empirical hypothesis that psychology, under what he calls the quantitative imperative, assumed rather than tested — in Tal's summary of the later papers, psychometricians "have not made serious attempts to test whether the attributes they purport to measure have quantitative structure" (Tal 2020). Michell must be represented accurately: he is an accuser of untested assumption, not a prover of non-quantitativity — he holds the question open and testable, and psychometricians have replied that latent-variable models test it by other means (Borsboom and Mellenbergh 2004; Michell 2004). If the objection lands for phenomenal-intensity attributes, within-subject scaling caps at ordinal, which would threaten from below any hope of a mathematically rich psychophysical coupling law. Trendler's harder line — that the test itself is unrunnable — converges with the Map's structural framing of the measurement limit, but by a methodological route the Map should count as distinct rather than as independent confirmation.

**Theory-relativity about degrees.** Lee (2023) argues that "whether consciousness comes in degrees ultimately depends on which theory of consciousness turns out to be correct," while most theories entail that it does. The consequence for the Map cuts inward: the interface framework cannot inherit degree structure from common sense; it must declare the scale structure it attributes to each phenomenal variable and defend it as part of the theory.

## The Meaningfulness Discipline

[Judging the Map as Science](/apex/judging-the-map-as-science/) identifies an unreconciled seam in the Map's philosophy-of-science cluster: one progress condition for the interface programme demands phenomenal states "measured on a scale that admits mathematical relationships," while the measurement-standards argument holds no such scale constructible across subjects. Its proposed resolution — phenomenal contrasts "scaled within subjects, never calibrated across them" — is offered there as a hypothesis about method, with the explicit caveat that it carries no independent weight until stated as a testable methodological constraint. The scale-type apparatus supplies that statement. A proposed phenomenal metric must:

1. **Declare its scale type** — nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio, per attribute and per subject;
2. **Exhibit the axioms** it claims the attribute satisfies, together with the within-subject tests that would check them — difference-scaling designs, conjoint designs, semiorder models where thresholds intrude;
3. **Show its lawlike uses are invariant** under the transformations its scale type leaves free — including, decisively, that no use smuggles in the cross-subject alignment that no axiom delivers.

The constraint has teeth because scale types constrain what a law can look like. Luce (1959) argued that the possible forms of a psychophysical law are limited by the scale types of its variables, since admissible transformations of the independent variable must be absorbable by admissible transformations of the dependent one — between ratio scales this essentially forces power functions. The principle was contested and later re-scoped by Luce himself (Luce 1990), but its core survives: what a [psychophysical control law](/topics/the-psychophysical-control-law/) can look like depends on the scale type of its phenomenal variable. An ordinal-only phenomenal side fixes no functional form at all, only order relations. Interval-grade within-subject structure is the minimum for a coupling law with non-trivial mathematical content; ratio-grade would license genuinely metric claims. The seam hypothesis "scaled within subjects" therefore comes in three strengths, and the interface programme's progress conditions should say which strength they need.

The discipline also sorts existing proposals. "This subject's pain doubled" is meaningful only under ratio structure; "this contrast exceeds that contrast" only under interval; "A suffers more than B," across subjects, is decidable by no representation the axioms license. The phrasing is deliberate: on the Map's realism there may well be a fact of the matter that no admissible pair of representations preserves — empirically undecidable, not contentless — which is why cross-subject welfare aggregation inherits the opacity [moral-census-opacity](/concepts/moral-census-opacity/) describes. Birch's five-tier sentience scale, read through this lens, is deliberately ordinal-grade precaution machinery that claims no more structure than it can earn ([birch-edge-of-sentience-and-the-five-tier-scale](/topics/birch-edge-of-sentience-and-the-five-tier-scale/)). Integrated information theory sits at the opposite pole: Φ is presented as a ratio-grade quantity of consciousness, yet this article knows of no exhibited representation axioms for it — precisely the demand the discipline makes of any such metric ([consciousness-and-integrated-information](/topics/consciousness-and-integrated-information/)).

## Relation to Site Perspective

The scale-type ladder is the Map's [Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits) tenet formalised as method: claim only the structure your axioms have earned, and treat every unearned rung — interval where only order is tested, ratio where numerals are labels, cross-subject where no structure exists — as a simplicity assumption masquerading as a datum. Because RTM is ontologically silent, adopting it commits the Map to a discipline rather than smuggling in support for any tenet.

The [Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) and [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) tenets inherit the Luce constraint: whatever coupling law the interface programme eventually writes down, its possible mathematical forms depend on the scale type its phenomenal variable supports, and the candidate [psychophysical-laws](/concepts/psychophysical-laws/) should be audited against that dependency. The mechanism debt may be payable only up to the invariance class the evidence supports — a weaker but more honest specification target. The [No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds) tenet claims no exemption from the same machinery: the Deutsch–Wallace Born-rule derivation is itself a representation-and-uniqueness argument, and the Map's objection to it runs through indexical identity, not through doubt about representation theorems.

The cross-subject rung connects to the [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism) tenet in the register the Map's methodology requires: the absence of a spanning empirical structure follows from first-person access, and the formal impossibility is consistent with irreducibility without upgrading it — the same consistency-only claim the measurement-standards article makes, arrived at through uniqueness theorems rather than philosophy of science. The [observation-and-measurement-void](/voids/observation-and-measurement-void/) and [the-quantitative-comprehension-void](/voids/the-quantitative-comprehension-void/) mark the void-side of the same territory: the first traces the impossibility through five independent routes, the second asks whether introspective access itself caps the quantitative structure available to be scaled.
