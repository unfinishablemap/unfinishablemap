Revelation is the thesis that to have an experience is to be in a position to know the nature of what it presents. In its *colour* form it says that seeing canary yellow reveals what canary yellow is; in its *phenomenal* form it says that feeling pain reveals what pain is. Both forms come in degrees. A **weak** Revelation holds that experience reveals the existence of a quality and part of its character; a **strong** Revelation holds that experience reveals the quality's full essence, so that nothing about what it is could be hidden from the person having it. Every camp accepts that much as a statement of the options; the disputes are over which degree is true and what follows.

The Map's position, stated here as the Map's, is that it holds weak Revelation and denies strong Revelation — and that this choice costs it an argument. The argument from revelation runs: if pain were a brain state and feeling pain revealed pain's essence, feeling pain would reveal a brain state; it does not; so pain is not a brain state. That argument, in its standard form, needs the strong thesis, which the Map cannot hold, for three reasons set out in the [site-perspective section](#relation-to-site-perspective) (explained below): it contradicts the Map's own claims that consciousness is partly opaque to itself; it would make the causal power the Map assigns to consciousness inessential to it, the epiphenomenalist's conclusion by another route; and it is the premise of the Russellian rival the Map argues against. Weak Revelation is accepted by physicalists and settles nothing. The argument from revelation is therefore not available to the Map as support for its first tenet. Note also that "phenomenal transparency" carries [two opposite senses](#two-senses-of-phenomenal-transparency) (explained below), and this corpus uses the one that does *not* mean Revelation.

## Two theses with one name

The term entered the literature through colour. Johnston's "How to speak of the colors" (1992) lists Revelation among the core beliefs any account of colour must respect; Byrne and Hilbert (2007) call it "one Johnston has dubbed" Revelation, and Liu (2019) records the term as "introduced by Mark Johnston (1992)". Johnston *named* the thesis; whether he presents the name as his own or as inherited from Strawson's (1989) claim that colour words name qualities "fully revealed in sensory experience" is reported by the secondary literature and not verified here, since Johnston's text was not retrieved. The constraint itself, as reproduced identically by Maund's *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry (2024, §1.4) and by Byrne and Hilbert (2007, citing the 1997 reprint at p. 138), reads: "The intrinsic nature of canary yellow is fully revealed by a standard visual experience as of a canary yellow thing." Johnston's own dispositionalism cannot meet it, and he gives it up; [colour-ontology-and-the-secondary-quality-residue](/topics/colour-ontology-and-the-secondary-quality-residue/) reads that concession as the point at which the quality is relocated from the object into the "appear" clause of a disposition.

Byrne and Hilbert are physicalists about colour — reflectance realists who reject primitivism — and their 2007 paper is the sharpest analysis of what the colour thesis commits one to. "Revelation admits of two readings", they note: is what is revealed a similarity fact, or that the similarity is *in the nature* of the colours? On the second reading they treat Revelation as the conjunction of two theses. Self-Intimation says that "If it is in the nature of the colors that p, then after careful reflection on color experience it seems to be in the nature of the colors that p"; Infallibility is its converse. "Self-Intimation, in particular, is a very strong thesis", entailing that colours are not reflectance types, which is why they conclude that Revelation "is tantamount to Minimal Primitivism". Their verdict on the package is that realist primitivism "is unacceptable". The confusion they warn against matters for everything that follows: "Revelation will appear to be obviously true if one confuses it with the claim that ordinary visual experience is sufficient to know what colors are." One can know *what* salt is while ignorant of its nature. Allen (2011, abstract) later pulls the equation apart from the primitivist side: "if naïve realism is true, then Revelation is false".

Byrne and Hilbert also name, in their own text, the second thesis. In their potted history of eliminativism there is "the Cartesian idea that careful introspection reveals the essence of the mental—in other words, Revelation-about-the-mind is true." Put that together with the Cartesian idea that colour is in the mind and colour-Revelation follows for the eliminativist. Liu (2019) opens from the other side: her paper concerns not the colour thesis but "a parallel idea that also goes by the name 'revelation' in the philosophy of mind." So there are two formally parallel theses, one about object colour and one about experience, and the bridge between them is the relocation the colour page traces. On the Map's reconstruction, when a colour ontology moves the quality into experience, colour-Revelation about the relocated quality becomes phenomenal Revelation about an experience — which is why a corpus that cites Johnston's constraint against object-primitivism must say separately what it holds about the mind.

## Lewis's Identification Thesis

The phenomenal version was best stated by a philosopher who rejected it. David Lewis was a committed materialist, and his "Should a materialist believe in qualia?" (1995) is an argument that folk psychology contains a thesis materialists must drop. Folk psychology, he says, holds that "we identify the qualia of our experiences. We know exactly what they are - and that in an uncommonly demanding and literal sense of 'knowing what'." He continues: "Call this the Identification Thesis." Spelled out, the knowledge gained in having an experience with quale Q "reveals the essence of Q: a property of Q such that, necessarily, Q has it and nothing else does." Lewis never uses the word "Revelation"; equating the Identification Thesis with Revelation is the later literature's move (Liu 2019; Majeed 2017, abstract: "the identification thesis (revelation)"), and in practice it is uncontested.

Lewis's verdict is blunt: "A materialist cannot accept the Identification Thesis." If qualia are physical properties of experiences, "then it is certain that we seldom, if ever, identify the qualia of our experiences. Making discoveries in neurophysiology is not so easy!" Two consequences matter here. First, a corollary Lewis draws in a footnote: under the thesis, qualia "can have no essential hidden structure - no 'grain' - of which we remain ignorant", so that "if we fail to notice structure, there is no structure there to notice." Strong Revelation forbids a hidden grain. Second, the two "harmless look-alikes" a materialist "can and should accept": that "the broad, de re content of my knowledge does, in the strongest sense, identify the qualia" — one knows *which* property one is acquainted with without knowing its essence — and that one knows what qualia are in an everyday sense. These look-alikes are weak Revelation, stated by a materialist who wanted the strong thesis gone.

Lewis granted the strong thesis folk-theoretical status. Daniel Stoljar, also a physicalist, denies even that. Stoljar's formulation (2009, p. 115, as quoted by Liu 2021) is that "having an experience puts you in a remarkable epistemic position: you know or are in a position to know the essence or nature of the experience"; his position across *Ignorance and Imagination* (2006) and "The argument from revelation" (2009) is, in Liu's summary, that "there is no good reason to think that revelation is intuitive." Liu (2021) takes the opposite side of the intuitiveness question without sharing Goff's conclusions: revelation "is intuitive; it is part of our ordinary, implicit conception of experience", and she lists avowed physicalists who concede the intuition — Lewis, McLaughlin, Braddon-Mitchell, Hill and Papineau. Her further claim belongs on the [meta-problem-of-consciousness](/concepts/meta-problem-of-consciousness/) page: she offers "a rational explanation of the intuition of dualism in terms of the intuitiveness of the thesis of revelation". If experience seems to reveal essences, and no physical essence shows up, dualism will seem true whether or not it is — a point that cuts for and against the Map equally.

## Partial or full: the physicalist replies

The strong form licenses an argument against physicalism. Damnjanovic (2012, *Dialectica*) reconstructs it, in Cortesi's (2023) reproduction of p. 73, around Thomas and the taste of peaches: if the identity holds and Thomas can know the taste's full nature, he can know it is physical; he can know its full nature; he cannot know it is physical; so the identity fails. Damnjanovic's reply, from the abstract, is that "on various plausible interpretations" Revelation is "consistent with physicalism", and that "there is a robust reading of Revelation" that a posteriori physicalists "can, and should, accept." Cortesi (2023), following Stoljar (2009, p. 124), notes that the argument so reconstructed "would function against almost any identity statement".

Trogdon (2017, *Synthese*) draws the distinction that settles the dispute's shape: the direct challenge to physicalism needs its first premise read as *partial* essential characterisation and its linking premise read as *full*, and no single reading satisfies both. On that reconstruction the direct argument fails, while Revelation still "indirectly challenges physicalism" by putting pressure on the account of phenomenal concepts the [phenomenal-concepts-strategy](/concepts/phenomenal-concepts-strategy/) relies on. Díaz-León (2014, abstract) defends a posteriori physicalism against Goff, Levine and Nida-Rümelin; Zhou (2025, abstract) surveys the "radical opacity, soft translucency, and Dual Carving" replies, finds none successful, and proposes that phenomenal concepts are "essence-indicating" and "can refer non-satisfactionally". The dispute has become a dispute about *which* Revelation, and it stays open: Goff (2011) argues that a partial characterisation in terms of a physical property would still be a physical characterisation, a step Trogdon finds "insufficiently motivated".

## Goff's Russellian use and the both-ways turn

Philip Goff is a Russellian panpsychist and, latterly, a cosmopsychist; the Map engages his programme as a rival ([russellian-monism](/concepts/russellian-monism/)), and nothing here enlists him. His 2011 paper, from its abstract, charges that the physicalism of Papineau and Loar "departs from common sense in holding that our phenomenal concept of pain is opaque". Its taxonomy (p. 194, as reproduced by Cortesi 2023) grades concepts by how much of their referent's nature they reveal: *transparent* concepts reveal the full nature, *translucent* concepts reveal part, *mildly opaque* concepts reveal only accidental identifying features, and *radically opaque* concepts reveal nothing. In *Consciousness and Fundamental Reality* (2017), as reported in Stoljar's review, ch. 5 states Phenomenal Transparency as the thesis that "phenomenal concepts reveal the complete nature of the conscious states they refer to" (p. 124), and the argument against physicalism is compressed to: we know what pain is through feeling it, so if pain were c-fibres firing we would know that; we do not; so it is not. Goff's Phenomenal Transparency is strong Revelation under another name, and the engine that carries him from anti-physicalism to the Russellian conclusion.

Between 2017 and 2025 the literature turned the engine round. Majeed (2017, abstract) locates the triangle: Lewis's humility thesis has "a potential exception" that requires both "panphenomenalism (contemporary panpsychism)" and "the identification thesis (revelation)" — together they would let acquaintance identify fundamental properties after all. Botin (2023, abstract) presses the challenge on the physicalist wing of the programme: "Russellian physicalism is either committed to an anti-realist and self-defeating view of phenomenal concepts or it is physically unacceptable." Lin (2025, abstract) presses it on the panpsychist wing: if the thesis is true, panpsychism faces a parallel difficulty; Chalmers's strategy of separating the essence of a phenomenal property from its grounds (Lin's "distinction strategy"; Goff makes the same move at p. 198) faces a dilemma — "either it fails to genuinely rescue panpsychism, or it can equally be used to rescue physicalism." Liu (2021) records the state of play neutrally: "It is not always clear whether revelation also rules out Russellian monism".

The most consequential turn, for this corpus, is Goff's own. His 2018 *Topoi* note (abstract; the full text was not read) observes that little attention has been paid to the problem that Revelation is "a source of pressure in the direction of epiphenomenalism, as introspection does not seem to reveal our conscious states as being essentially causal". If feeling pain reveals pain's complete nature, and no causal power is among what is revealed, then causal power is no part of pain's nature. Goff critiques Mørch's phenomenal-powers reply, on which phenomenal character just is a power, and defends a "consciousness+" reply on which the state has more to it than what is revealed — on the Map's reading, a retreat from full to partial Revelation at the point where the full thesis bites.

## Two senses of phenomenal transparency

The phrase "phenomenal transparency" has two established uses with opposite polarities, and this corpus uses the one that is *not* Goff's. In Thomas Metzinger's sense, developed on the [phenomenal-transparency-opacity-spectrum](/concepts/phenomenal-transparency-opacity-spectrum/) page and used across some twenty pages of the Map, a representation is transparent when its representational character is invisible to the subject: you see the apple, not your model of it, and transparency falls as earlier processing stages become introspectively available. Transparency in that sense is experience *hiding* something, the window that cracks in [depersonalisation](/concepts/depersonalisation/). In Goff's sense, a phenomenal concept is transparent when it *reveals* the full nature of its referent. Metzinger's transparent self-model conceals its own construction; Goff's transparent concept conceals nothing. Where this article says "strong Revelation" it means Goff's transparency; where the rest of the Map says "phenomenal transparency" it means Metzinger's. Cortesi's (2023) split between **C-Rev**, Revelation as a feature of phenomenal concepts, and **A-Rev**, Revelation grounded in "sui generis introspective knowledge by acquaintance", is orthogonal to this and is the axis of [phenomenal-acquaintance](/concepts/phenomenal-acquaintance/).

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Map's existing commitments presuppose weak Revelation and nothing stronger. The rationale for the [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism) tenet is the explanatory gap, which needs only that experience presents a qualitative character no physical description has yet accounted for. The colour page's residue claim — that a qualitative character is *given* in experience, however its address is disputed — needs only that something is revealed. The Russellian hinge stated on the [intrinsic-nature](/concepts/intrinsic-nature/) page, that we know what pain is like by feeling it, is marked there as the programme's most contested premise, and the Map does not rest on it. And the Map's reply to the phenomenal-concepts strategy, in the tenets and on the [phenomenal-concepts-strategy](/concepts/phenomenal-concepts-strategy/) page, claims that the first-person mode of presentation must *be* something beyond what it refers to and that a concept which indicates a brain state while revealing nothing of why it is experienced restates the gap. Each is a claim that experience reveals *something* — [existence, some character](/topics/phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence/#three-layers-of-phenomenal-authority), that the state is not exhausted by its role. None needs the full essence revealed. The Map's objection to opaque phenomenal concepts therefore does not require, and must not drift into, the claim that phenomenal concepts are transparent in Goff's sense.

The Map must deny strong Revelation, for three reasons that are independent of one another. The first is self-opacity. Lewis's corollary — that if we fail to notice structure, there is none to notice — is a consequence of the strong thesis, and the Map holds its negation: [self-opacity](/voids/self-opacity/) argues that consciousness cannot fully know itself, [phenomenal-quality-void](/voids/phenomenal-quality-void/) that the mechanism hides itself either way, and [introspection](/concepts/introspection/) that introspective report is unreliable about its own process. Strong Revelation and self-opacity cannot both be held, and the Map has already chosen. The second is the interface. Tenets 2 and 3 posit a selection power exercised at the quantum interface, and the positions register holds that this interface is "structurally selected to leave only indirect traces" ([P-Q9](/positions/quantum-interface/#p-q9)). If the full essence of a phenomenal state were revealed in having it, and no selection power is revealed, then the power is no part of the state's essence — Goff's epiphenomenalism pressure, applied to the Map's own mechanism. The escapes on offer are Goff's essence-versus-grounds distinction, which Lin argues rescues physicalism just as well, or Mørch's phenomenal-powers view, already the Map's closest competitor on the metaphysics of dispositions. Denying the strong thesis is cheaper than either. The third is the rival. Strong Revelation is the premise that carries Goff from anti-physicalism to Russellian monism. The Map argues that Russellian monism is unstable ([russellian-monism-versus-bi-aspectual-dualism](/topics/russellian-monism-versus-bi-aspectual-dualism/)); it cannot borrow the rival's premise while rejecting the rival's conclusion.

The tier result follows. Weak Revelation is accepted by physicalists such as Damnjanovic and Díaz-León; Goff's direct argument from it is contested, as shown above, and its indirect pressure on the phenomenal-concepts strategy falls where the tenets already mark the dispute open, so it settles nothing. Strong Revelation would discriminate against physicalism — that is its whole appeal — but the Map cannot hold it. So the argument from revelation is not available as support for the Dualism tenet, which continues to rest on the explanatory gap and conceivability; any sentence of the form "experience reveals that the quality is non-physical" would over-reach. For Tenets 2 and 3, the thesis supplies a constraint rather than a result: the interface power is consistent with the Map's epistemology only because the Map holds that what experience reveals is partial, and that holding is a framework commitment, not an independent finding. Liu's explanation of the intuition of dualism cuts the same way: a dualist who grants that the intuition is explained by the intuitiveness of a thesis they themselves deny in its strong form cannot cite the intuition as evidence. The authors who did this work — Lewis, Stoljar, Papineau, Byrne and Hilbert, Damnjanovic, Díaz-León and Zhou on the physicalist side; Goff on the Russellian — all reject the Map's interactionist dualism, and are cited here as rivals who mapped a constraint the Map must respect.
