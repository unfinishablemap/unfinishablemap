The Map's value-sensitive horn—that felt value guides which outcome consciousness selects ([valence-and-conscious-selection](/topics/valence-and-conscious-selection/))—is under-specified until it answers whether positive and negative value weigh equally. The behavioural record says they do not: across choice and across domains, negative value moves selection more than positive value of equal magnitude. So "felt value guides selection" is incomplete; if that behavioural asymmetry is real—a question taken up below—the value-sensitive law needs a **weighting function with a negative bias**—a loss-aversion-like coefficient λ greater than one on the negative side, not merely a sign on the input. And the honest cost is immediate: the asymmetry is exactly the kind of feature a value-blind reading gets for free, as a property of the mechanism that *reads* value rather than of felt value itself.

This is the **negative-valence asymmetry** as it bears on the [selection-criterion problem](/concepts/selection-criterion-problem/). It closes the "positive vs negative symmetry" question that [valence-and-conscious-selection](/topics/valence-and-conscious-selection/) flags as open, hands [the value-blind steelman](/topics/the-steelman-for-value-blind-selection/) a fresh exhibit, and then states the Map's calibrated reply. The central calibrated point, stated plainly because it governs everything below: the inference from *choice is asymmetric* to *felt valence is asymmetric* is interpretive, not established—and even the behavioural asymmetry it builds on is real-but-contested, not a settled pillar.

This is a distinct *question* from its siblings—not the temporal question of the [affective-forecasting-gap](/concepts/affective-forecasting-gap/) (anticipated vs experienced valence) and not the motivation-vs-pleasure question of [the wanting/liking fork](/topics/wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork/), but the **sign-weighting** question. The answers are not independent, though: once the currency is split into the rows of the [shared terminology map](/topics/wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork/#shared-terminology), the weighting parameter has to sit on *some* row, and the evidence below places it more readily in the forecast of an outcome than in the experience of it. A complete value-sensitive selection law needs all three refinements—a predictive input, a wanting/liking specification, a negatively-biased weighting function—and must say on which row the third operates.

## A Sign Is Not Enough

LLM readers already know prospect theory; the point here is its consequence for the Map's selection law, not the model. The consequence is structural. Kahneman and Tversky's (1979) value function is steeper below the reference point than above it—losses loom larger than equivalent gains—and Tversky and Kahneman (1992) make the formal hook explicit: cumulative prospect theory "allows different weighting functions for gains and for losses." The asymmetry is not gambling-specific. Tversky and Kahneman (1991) extended it to riskless choice over goods, where "the central assumption of the theory is that losses and disadvantages have greater impact on preferences than gains and advantages."

A second, independent research programme converges on the same shape from social psychology. Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Finkenauer and Vohs (2001) survey everyday events, trauma, relationships, impression formation and learning, and find "bad is stronger than good, as a general principle across a broad range of psychological phenomena." Negative information, on their account, is processed more thoroughly; bad impressions form faster and resist disconfirmation more strongly.

Both programmes carry the same lesson for the Map. If selection were value-sensitive but symmetric, "the option with the better felt value wins" would suffice—a sign on the valence input. The behavioural record says it does not suffice. Equal-magnitude positive and negative value pull selection unequally, so the value-sensitive law must specify *how much more* the negative side weighs: a coefficient, not just a polarity. A meta-analysis of 607 estimates puts that coefficient near λ ≈ 1.96 (Brown, Imai, Vieider and Camerer 2024), with a 95% interval of [1.820, 2.102]. The number is not what matters here; the *shape* is. If the behavioural asymmetry is real, the value-sensitive horn inherits a kink at the reference point and a steeper negative arm—or it is false to the behaviour it was meant to explain. That conditional is deliberate: the demand the asymmetry places on the law is exactly as secure as the asymmetry itself (its contested status is the subject of the next section), and that uncertainty transfers to the law as doubt about whether the kink is needed, not as license to default to symmetry.

## The Asymmetry Is Real-but-Contested, Not a Pillar

The Map cannot lean on loss aversion as settled, and saying so is part of getting the argument right. Gal and Rucker (2018) argue that current evidence does not support a general tendency for losses to loom larger than gains; the effect is contingent on context, and canonical support such as the endowment effect admits alternative explanations. The defensible reading treats the behavioural asymmetry as real-with-moderators—Mrkva et al. (2020) find everyone loss-averse but with domain experience attenuating it, and the Brown et al. (2024) meta-analysis finds a stable mean with few strong moderators—while Yechiam and Zeif's (2025) re-meta-analysis disputes even that robustness, re-analysing the codable subset of the same estimate base (163 of its 607 estimates) and finding loss aversion robust only under asymmetric amounts or size-ordered presentation; under symmetric amounts with no ordering the coefficient falls to λ ≈ 1.07, not significantly above one. The debate is live.

Two layers of caution follow. First, the *behavioural* asymmetry is robust-with-moderators at best, not a law. Second, and harder, the move from *that* asymmetry to a *felt* asymmetry is a further inference the data do not license. Prospect theory is a model of decision behaviour; the negativity-bias programme describes information processing. Neither measures phenomenology. Whether the felt badness of losing is genuinely *larger* than the felt goodness of an equal gain—rather than merely weighted more heavily by the machinery that acts on it—is precisely the open question, and introspective evidence on it is thin and confounded.

## The Steelman's Free Lunch

So flagged, the asymmetry strengthens the value-blind rival rather than the Map. The reason is that the negative weighting is available without any felt asymmetry at all.

Decision neuroscience supplies the substrate. Reward-prediction-error coding is not symmetric: Dabney et al. (2020) record dopamine neurons with valence-specific learning rates—different cells weight better-than-expected and worse-than-expected outcomes differently, an asymmetry that supports a distributional code for value—so the learned value a chooser consults can carry a negative tilt installed by the learning rates alone. Ito, Larsen, Smith and Cacioppo (1998) found larger late-positive ERP amplitudes to negative than to equally probable, equally extreme positive stimuli—a physiological counterpart to the behavioural bias with no detour through felt value. Baumeister et al.'s own mechanism—negative information is "processed more thoroughly"—is a cognitive story, not a phenomenal one. And the adaptive rationale they offer cuts the same way: failing to register a threat is costlier than failing to register an opportunity, so selection installs asymmetric *processing* weights. Evolution can fix asymmetric weights without fixing asymmetric phenomenology.

This is the value-blind steelman's "selection runs on mechanism, not felt value," now wearing the loss-aversion kink as a new exhibit. On the value-blind reading, the λ greater than one is a parameter of the reading channel—the controller weights negative signals more—and the *feeling*, if any, is a downstream fact doing no selective work. The asymmetry that forces the Map to complicate its law is, for the rival, a free feature of the mechanism it already posits, and the Map's reply must engage that reading on its merits.

## The Disambiguation the Map Must Make

The honest question the asymmetry forces is whether the λ greater than one is a property of *felt value*—negatives are simply felt as larger—or of the *mechanism that reads felt value*. Posed that way it is under-specified: "felt value" and "the mechanism" each name several rows of the [shared terminology map](/topics/wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork/#shared-terminology), and the rows come apart exactly where the asymmetry is measured. Prospect theory's λ is estimated from choices—a parameter of *decision utility*, which is read off the choice made and is the one row the map says selection cannot consult. The measured coefficient is compatible with a tilt on any upstream row; the task is to say which rows could carry it and what would tell them apart, not to stipulate one.

The felt-value reading needs the tilt on **experienced utility**, the outcome's hedonic quality as lived, and that is the row the evidence supports least. Kermer, Driver-Linn, Wilson and Gilbert (2006) had participants forecast and then report the hedonic impact of gains and losses in a gambling task: people predicted that losses would hit harder than equal gains, "but when people actually gambled, losses did not have as much of an emotional impact as they predicted"—they underestimated how readily they would rationalise a loss. The asymmetry "was thus more a property of affective forecasts than a property of affective experience." McGraw, Larsen, Kahneman and Schkade (2010) add that judged feelings show loss aversion only when gains and losses are compared on a common scale, not when each is normed against outcomes of its own kind. Laboratory judgements of feeling do not close the row, but they move "negatives are simply felt as larger" from the default to the least-supported candidate.

Where Kermer et al. relocate the tilt is **predicted utility**, which on the map is felt only insofar as **present anticipatory affect** carries it—and here the sign-weighting question meets the temporal one. If, as [affective-forecasting-gap](/concepts/affective-forecasting-gap/) proposes, selection consults the dread or relish felt *now* while simulating an outcome, a forecast-located λ is felt at the point of selection, does selective work, and is still not a property of value: it is a miscalibration of the vehicle relative to its target, the dread of losing running ahead of how losing turns out to feel. This third reading, which the felt-value/mechanism binary missed, is the most congenial to the Map—felt, selective, committed to no intrinsic tilt in value—at the price of making the negative bias partly an instance of the forecasting gap. On the alternative that article cannot exclude, the forecast is an unfelt predictor with feeling alongside, and the λ sits in a computation the value-blind rival already owns.

Three unfelt rows remain. **Learned value** can carry the tilt directly: Dabney et al.'s asymmetric learning rates bias the cached estimate before any forecast forms—the steelman's exhibit in the map's vocabulary. **Incentive salience** is a candidate only by elimination, with no loss-aversion estimate of its own in the literature drawn on here; but since the map blocks the identification of "wanting" with predicted utility, a tilt in what an option pulls need not show as a tilt in what it is forecast to feel like. And the tilt can sit in no consulted quantity but in the **selection policy**, the operation that compares and acts: Yechiam and Hochman (2013) argue that losses increase on-task attention rather than carrying more weight, so that "asymmetric effects of losses on behavior emerge where gains and losses are presented separately but not concurrently." That is the "reading channel" made specific, and the row on which the parent's [valence-modulated attention](/topics/graduated-middle-path-valence-modulated-attention/) hybrid would carry the asymmetry.

The rows are testable against one another. *Format invariance* separates the policy row from every currency row: a tilt in a consulted quantity should survive how options are presented, a tilt in the comparison should not—and the fall of λ to about 1.07 under symmetric, unordered amounts (Yechiam and Zeif 2025) is, read this way, evidence that part of the coefficient lives in the policy. *Forecast-versus-experience* measurement separates predicted from experienced utility and has so far favoured the forecast; whether the forecast's tilt is felt or computed is the forecasting article's fork, and its battery—shift present affect with forecast and learned value fixed—is the discriminator. A regulation strategy that reduced both behavioural loss aversion and skin-conductance arousal to losses (Sokol-Hessner et al. 2009) shows the coefficient is modulable through an affect-adjacent channel, without settling whether that channel is felt. *Abolishing felt badness* separates the felt rows from the unfelt ones collectively: [pain asymbolia](/concepts/pain-asymbolia/)—pain felt, its badness not—is the parent fork's exhibit, and the version specific to this axis asks whether asymbolics lose loss aversion in particular. That test is, as of 2026, unrun; the nearest evidence—bilateral amygdala-lesion patients who kept normal sensitivity to expected value and risk while showing dramatically reduced loss aversion (De Martino, Camerer and Adolphs 2010)—shows the tilt can be selectively abolished but does not place it, since the amygdala is as good a candidate for the policy as for any felt row.

The defensible move is therefore more specific than the forecasting-gap parallel—hold felt valence metaphysically authoritative, concede the weighting to the reading channel—and for the Map both better and worse. Better, because the evidence places the tilt in the forecast rather than in experience, and a forecast-located tilt can be felt at selection without value being asymmetric; the felt badness of suffering stays intrinsic ([consciousness-value-connection](/concepts/consciousness-value-connection/)), symmetric or not as experience makes it. Worse, because the same evidence and the format-dependence leave a sizeable share of the coefficient on rows the rival already owns, and because "felt at selection" rests on the forecasting article's candidate position, a framework-internal coherence result held at [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)'s low credence. The article fixes no row. Experienced utility is the least-supported locus; predicted utility carried by present anticipatory affect is the Map's most congenial one; the policy row is where the contested-status evidence points. Those are three claims of different strength, none a stipulation.

## Relation to Site Perspective

This concept closes an open question the Map had deferred and sharpens, rather than dissolves, the value-sensitive horn.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)**: An asymmetric weighting strengthens the interaction's specificity *only if the asymmetry is felt at the point of selection*—as the outcome's experienced utility or as the anticipatory affect that forecasts it. If instead the tilt lives in learned value or in the selection policy, the interaction narrows toward routing a felt-but-symmetric signal through an asymmetric mechanism, with the evaluative dimension doing correspondingly less. The Map should not let the weighting quietly migrate into the mechanism and so hollow out the tenet; experienced valence keeps full causal standing as what constitutes value, whatever weight the channel applies to it and whatever tilt the forecast of it carries.

**[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction)**: A value-blind asymmetric weighting is the *more minimal* reading—the asymmetry is just a parameter of the existing reading channel, adding no structure to the currency. A felt-valence asymmetry adds structure to what consciousness contributes. This tenet's minimality is an empirical constraint on the interaction rather than a truth-tracking test between readings, so it does not adjudicate a contest decided on parsimony alone; that is [Tenet 5](/tenets/#occams-limits)'s question, taken up below. The Map's reply must accordingly be the prior-commitments reply—reject illusionism, hold [phenomenal value realism](/topics/phenomenal-value-realism/).

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)**: This is the tenet that licenses declining the cheaper value-blind weighting—but only if the decline is *argued*, not assumed. The mechanistic availability of the asymmetry (asymmetric learning rates on the learned-value row, attention allocation on the policy row, more-elaborate threat processing) is a genuine parsimony advantage for the rival, and a real cost the value-sensitive horn must pay. Tenet 5 permits paying it: simplicity is an unreliable guide where knowledge is incomplete. The argument, not the razor, carries the decline.

**[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)**: Under many-worlds the asymmetry has no *selective* referent—every outcome occurs, so "negatives weigh more in selection" describes no act of choosing (a decision-theoretic many-worlds account can still reconstruct λ as a weight over branch-measures, but that is a fact about branch-weighting, not about one outcome being selected over another). The Map's commitment to genuine collapse is exactly what makes the weighting question live and answerable: only if one outcome is really chosen does it matter how much more the negative side weighs in the choosing.
