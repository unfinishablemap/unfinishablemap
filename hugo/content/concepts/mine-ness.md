Mine-ness is the phenomenal feature by which an experience presents itself as belonging to a subject. Seeing red, feeling pain, recalling a face—ordinarily each conscious state carries an implicit tag that it is *mine*. Mine-ness so understood is narrower than *for-me-ness*, the first-personal givenness Dan Zahavi holds constitutive of every conscious state; Martin Heidegger's *Jemeinigkeit* is an ontological counterpart. The Unfinishable Map treats mine-ness as a **separable phenomenal feature**: a distinct dimension of conscious states that can, in principle and in fact, vary independently of other phenomenal properties. It is not equivalent to the contents of experience, not equivalent to the sense of agency, and not equivalent to reflective self-awareness. Depersonalisation disorder, thought insertion, and certain contemplative states are the natural experiments that pry mine-ness apart from these neighbouring features and expose it as a thing in its own right.

The feature arrives with a limit attached. We cannot introspect the mechanism by which experiences become ours, and every act of introspection is itself first-personally given—for-me-ness is presupposed by the very investigation that would uncover it. The Map names that gap the **ownership void**: a limit on first-person access, not on third-person explanation. This article covers both halves: the feature, its scope and its dissociations, and then the void, [explained below](#the-ownership-void). The separate question of *what grounds* experience's belonging to a subject at all is treated in [consciousness-and-the-ownership-problem](/topics/consciousness-and-the-ownership-problem/).

## Defining the Feature

For-me-ness, on Zahavi's account, is the dimension of pre-reflective self-awareness that accompanies every conscious state: whether a thought, a perception, an emotion, a memory or a bodily sensation, each of my experiences is given to me first-personally. The "for-me" character is not added by reflection or by second-order monitoring. It is constitutive of the experiential character of conscious states from the outset. Marie Guillot argues that such givenness [does not entail](/concepts/thought-insertion/) mine-ness, and reads depersonalisation as mine-ness lapsing while for-me-ness persists; the Map adopts her split.

Three clarifications keep mine-ness from sliding into adjacent notions:

**Mine-ness is not the content of experience.** The content of seeing red is *red*. The content of remembering Paris is *Paris*. Mine-ness is the [orthogonal dimension](/concepts/metacognitive-access-versus-retention/): that this seeing and this remembering present themselves as the seeing and remembering *of a subject*. On the Map's reading, two otherwise similar states can differ in whether they carry the mine-ness tag, as depersonalisation illustrates.

**Mine-ness is not the sense of agency.** Agency is the sense that "I am the initiator" of an action or thought. Ownership is the sense that "this is happening in my experience." Shaun Gallagher's analysis distinguishes the two and argues they come apart in pathology: on his reading of schizophrenic thought insertion, agency is lost (someone else seems to be producing the thought) while ownership is preserved (the thought still occurs in the patient's mind). On the Gallagher-style reading, [depersonalisation](/concepts/depersonalisation/) can disrupt ownership while sparing agency, though active-inference accounts and Billon's deagentivation cluster describe a disturbed sense of control, making the separation one-way.

**Mine-ness is not reflective self-awareness.** Reflective self-awareness is a second-order state: attending to one's own mental states as such. Zahavi's claim is stronger: mine-ness is present in *first-order* experience, before any reflective act. The phenomenal "for-me" structure is built into conscious states at the ground level, not constructed by metacognition on top of them.

Heidegger's *Jemeinigkeit* goes further. Mine-ness is not merely a phenomenal feature of conscious states; it is an ontological structure of Dasein. Being is always "mine to be." Even *inauthenticity*—losing oneself in the anonymous "they"—is a *mode* of mine-ness, not its abolition. The Map does not require Heidegger's full ontological framework, but takes from him the point that mine-ness is *prior* to the subject-object distinction. It is not a relation between two pre-existing things (the experience and the subject who owns it); it is part of how experience is structured from the start.

## Evidence for Separability

The case for treating mine-ness as a distinct feature rests on its **dissociability**—the empirical and phenomenological observation that mine-ness can vary independently of other features of conscious experience.

### Depersonalisation Disorder

[Depersonalisation disorder](/concepts/depersonalisation/) (DPD) is the central piece of evidence. Patients report that experience continues—they see, hear, think, and act—but the experiences feel as though they are happening "behind glass" or to someone else. Alexandre Billon's phenomenological work characterises DPD as "the lack of a phenomenal feature that marks my experiences as mine." The Map's reconstruction, rather than a finding: much phenomenal content persists while felt mine-ness is degraded.

Anna Ciaunica and colleagues describe DPD as a crack in a normally transparent window. On the Map's reading, the window's transparency is its mine-ness: ordinarily we see *through* it to the world. When it cracks the window becomes partly visible—mine-ness becomes a noticed absence rather than an unnoticed presence, and subjects report a fracture between an "observing self" and an "observed self." The pathology converts a transparent feature into an opaque one without destroying consciousness itself.

DPD is also, on the Map's reconstruction, a within-subject case of the [discrimination-problem](/concepts/discrimination-problem/) at the level of a phenomenal feature: the same subject reports mine-ness degraded while much sensory and cognitive content persists.

### Thought Insertion

In schizophrenic thought insertion, patients locate a thought in their own mind yet [deny that it is theirs](/concepts/immunity-to-error-through-misidentification/). Gallagher reads this as the reverse of the DPD dissociation: agency lost, ownership preserved. His is one of five readings that [thought-insertion](/concepts/thought-insertion/) weighs, and the most contested; on two of the others ownership itself fails.

### Contemplative Dissolution

Buddhist *anattā* (no-self) practice systematically searches for [the owner of experience](/concepts/pudgalavada/) and fails to find it. Each element of experience—body, sensation, perception, mental formations, consciousness—is examined, and for each the sense of "mine" is found to be impermanent and unfixable. In Theravāda insight meditation and varieties of Zen, practitioners report states in which experience continues without a felt sense of "someone experiencing it." Advaita Vedānta witness practice differs: what [persists there is the witness](/topics/comparative-phenomenology-of-meditative-traditions/#the-status-of-the-witness). Unlike DPD episodes, such states are more often welcomed.

The contemplative evidence reinforces the depersonalisation evidence: mine-ness can fade while consciousness continues. The overlap between the two is partial. Deane, Miller and Wilkinson trace the experience of selfhood to a "temporally deep generative model" and find "striking experiential similarities and important differences": on their account depersonalisation reflects an inferred loss of control, meditative selflessness a gain. A preregistered survey (Pons et al. 2026) found meditation-triggered and other-triggered depersonalisation-like episodes alike on the Cambridge Depersonalisation Scale but different on every other questionnaire; over a third of meditators had their first episode within 100 hours of practice, and most participants in both groups called their episodes mixed. The loss of mine-ness is neither intrinsically aversive nor intrinsically peaceful, which suggests that its *valence* is a separate feature again, layered on top of mine-ness and varying with context and control. [edge-states-and-void-probes](/voids/edge-states-and-void-probes/) documents similar context-dependence at other cognitive limits.

### Empirical Caveat

Franz Knappik's rival account needs no mine-ness feature at all. He criticises explaining DPD by altered "global features of normal consciousness, such as 'mineness'": depersonalisation and derealisation can occur independently, and most symptoms can be understood as impairments of self-relations that mental states have by virtue of their type. The Map's reply—that each such relation is still a separable phenomenal feature—survives Knappik only if each type-specific relation is itself phenomenal rather than functional, which his paper does not grant.

The separability thesis is in principle defeasible. It would be undermined by cases in which mine-ness co-varies *perfectly* with phenomenal content or with the sense of agency—where every disruption to content also disrupts ownership, or every loss of agency also abolishes ownership. The dissociations above, one-way for agency, break that co-variation; so long as they replicate, the separability thesis stands.

Whether what separates is phenomenal is a further question. Roberto Sá Pereira (2026) argues that the for-me-ness view "lacks evidential support beyond the phenomenological descriptions it was built to interpret," and that depersonalisation and related cases have better explanations without it. The charge reaches separable mine-ness too: the dissociations show that something varies, not that it is a phenomenal mark rather than disrupted affective, interoceptive or self-model processing. Reported loss of mine-ness with those processes measured intact would favour the phenomenal reading.

## Mine-ness and Transparency

A feature that pervades every experience yet normally goes unnoticed is by definition *transparent*—we look through it rather than at it. Thomas Metzinger's self-model theory treats this transparency as architectural: the phenomenal self-model cannot be experienced *as* a model, so its contents present themselves as simply and directly "mine." Metzinger calls this **autoepistemic closure**—the self-model is arranged so as to conceal its own modelling. The Map does not endorse Metzinger's broader physicalist framework, but the structural insight transfers: whatever the underlying nature of mine-ness, it is a transparent feature in normal experience, and its disruption is what makes it perceptible.

Under dualism the opacity may run deeper than concealment by neural architecture. A physical system can in principle be analysed from outside by another physical system; a non-physical subject has no external vantage point from which its own subjectivity could be examined. On that reading the transparency is a consequence of what subjectivity is, rather than a design feature that could have been arranged otherwise.

This places mine-ness on the [phenomenal-transparency-opacity-spectrum](/concepts/phenomenal-transparency-opacity-spectrum/) at the extreme transparent end. Most of the time mine-ness is so transparent that subjects do not notice it at all. Depersonalisation pushes it toward opacity—its absence becomes noticed precisely because its presence was the unnoticed background. Meditation can do something analogous voluntarily, making the feature available for inspection by reducing its grip rather than removing it altogether.

Transparency also explains why mine-ness is easily confused with the bare fact of having experience: a feature always present and never noticed is hard to distinguish from the conditions of consciousness itself. The dissociation data break the confusion, since a feature that can vary is not the bare fact.

## The Ownership Void {#the-ownership-void}

Depersonalisation reports are compatible with mine-ness degrading while experience and its for-me-ness continue—on the [depersonalisation](/concepts/depersonalisation/) verdict, a defensible architecture claim with no metaphysical significance yet—and the degradation shows the patient nothing of mine-ness's mechanism. Patients report that experience stops feeling theirs; they do not report *seeing how ownership was constructed*. To introspection the void shows itself as absence, never as machinery.

Where for-me-ness is concerned, recursion makes the introspective limit structural rather than merely stubborn. Attempting to introspect ownership traps attention in a distinctive way: the attending is itself given first-personally, so for-me-ness is present in the very act of looking for it. Isolating for-me-ness would require a vantage point outside it—an experience that is not "for anyone"—and no such vantage point would be an experience at all. This differs from the regress in [the unobservable self](/voids/self-opacity/), where the observer generates fresh layers of observation; here there is no regress, only a condition that cannot be stepped outside of. If Zahavi is right that for-me-ness is "completely irrelational," and Heidegger right that *Jemeinigkeit* is an existential structure rather than a contingent psychological one, the accounts themselves yield at most this: for-me-ness is elusive to direct introspective thematisation, since every examination presupposes it. Mine-ness, being separable, stays open to contrastive third-person study through perturbation, lesion and modelling.

**Which layer the void occupies.** The opacity sits at the ownership layer—for-me-ness and mine-ness—and is distinct from the parallel opacity at the agency layer ([volitional opacity](/voids/self-opacity/#the-special-case-of-volition)), which concerns how an action becomes mine to initiate. On Gallagher's reading, the two layers separate one-way, so the two voids are structurally distinct rather than one restated in the other's vocabulary.

**One void or many.** Knappik's challenge has a consequence here. If mine-ness is plural rather than a single global feature, and each type-specific relation is phenomenal (which Knappik does not grant), the ownership void is several: each modality of experience may have its own form of appropriation, each opaque to introspection. If the relations are functional, they are open to third-person study, and plurality shrinks the void rather than multiplying it.

**The body as the densest case.** The body is *mine* in a way no external object is, and bodily disownership—the depersonalised "behind glass" body, somatoparaphrenic limb-disavowal—is [among the most vivid disruptions of mine-ness](/concepts/cotard-delusion/). Yet the [interoceptive-void](/voids/interoceptive-void/) shows that the body so owned is mostly silent: most visceral state never reaches awareness, and what does arrives summarised. Felt ownership therefore extends over a substrate the subject has almost no access to. The two voids are distinct—one concerns the appropriation relation, the other the body's opacity to awareness—but the densest instance of the first runs over the territory the second maps.

**Adjacent voids, and what separates them.** The [self-reference-paradox](/concepts/self-reference-paradox/) concerns logical structure; the ownership void is phenomenological, about felt belonging rather than formal reflexivity. [Introspective opacity](/voids/self-opacity/) concerns hidden cognitive processes in general; the ownership void concerns a phenomenal layer that is normally present while its ground stays hidden. The [indexical void](/topics/vertiginous-question/) asks why I am *this* consciousness rather than another, and ownership presupposes an answer, since experiences are "mine" only if there is a determinate "me" to own them. Those two mark faces of one mystery: what makes a subject *this* subject, and how experience becomes *its* experience.

The void is the epistemic residue that remains once the [ownership problem](/topics/consciousness-and-the-ownership-problem/) has been well stated. Ownership is currently unexplained, and the mechanism of appropriation is hidden from within while open to study from without.

## What Mine-ness is Not

**Mine-ness is not personal identity over time.** Personal identity asks what makes tomorrow's person the same as today's; mine-ness is the feature by which this present experience is mine *now*. [The two come apart cleanly](/concepts/quasi-memory-and-the-circularity-objection/): severe dissociative identity might leave mine-ness intact in each moment while diachronic identity fractures, and a continuous person could lose mine-ness in a depersonalised episode and recover it later.

**Mine-ness is not the sense of bodily ownership.** Bodily ownership—the sense that *this body* or *this limb* is mine, manipulable via the rubber hand illusion—is a *sub-type* of mine-ness. Mine-ness in general extends to thoughts, perceptions, and memories with no bodily location.

**Mine-ness is not haecceity.** [Haecceity](/concepts/haecceity/) is the *metaphysical* "thisness" of a particular; mine-ness is the *phenomenal* feature by which experience presents as a subject's. The [vertiginous-question](/topics/vertiginous-question/) is where the two meet.

**Mine-ness is not having a self-concept.** A being can have mine-ness without explicit concepts of "self" or "I." The phenomenal feature is pre-conceptual; the self-concept is its cognitive elaboration.

## Relation to Site Perspective

### Dualism

The [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism) tenet is compatible with, [not evidenced by](/concepts/depersonalisation/#what-the-datum-shows-two-tiers), mine-ness's separability and apparent irreducibility. Zahavi describes for-me-ness as "completely irrelational"—not a subject taking its experience as an object, but a non-objectifying self-givenness built into experience itself. Read as a basic property of consciousness, that givenness fits dualism, though not uniquely: physicalist accounts can describe its functional shadow but not capture its phenomenal character. The Map reads mine-ness as one of the clearest cases where the explanatory gap between functional description and phenomenal character is visible. Metzinger can give a beautiful account of why a self-model would be transparent and feel mine; what he cannot give is an account of why there is something it is like to have a transparent self-model. The ownership void is compatible with the dualist reading but not better explained by it: introspection finding a presence and never machinery is equally expected on self-model and [attention-schema](/concepts/attention-schema-theory/) accounts, whose models omit their own workings.

### Bidirectional Interaction

The [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) tenet connects to mine-ness through the agency/ownership distinction. If consciousness causally influences physical processes—on the Map's account, by biasing among quantum outcomes available at the brain-mind interface—then *agency* is the phenomenal correlate of that causal role. *Mine-ness*, being prior to and separable from agency, may be the more fundamental feature: intrinsic to experience, yet the form in which the non-physical subject claims the physical processes it influences; agency is the form that claiming takes in action. The mechanism of the claiming would then be concealed by the same opacity that hides the interaction mechanism itself. This reading respects the dissociation data (mine-ness without agency on Gallagher's reading of insertion) and the tenet's commitment to a non-physical subject with causal traction.

### No Many Worlds

The [No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds) tenet relates because mine-ness is inherently *indexical*. *My* experiences are mine, not anyone else's; mine-ness picks out a determinate subject from the inside. [Branch-relative indexicality on Many-Worlds accounts](/concepts/many-worlds/#mwis-branch-relative-indexicality-reply) (Saunders, Wallace) can recover a local sense of mine-ness within each branch, since each branch's observer can truly say *these* experiences are mine. The Map's stronger position is that mine-ness is one-branch-only rather than locally articulated within a globally branching structure—a reading that withholds the cross-branch personal-identity relation MWI's Born-rule recovery requires. The phenomenal singularity of mine-ness—the felt fact that *this* experience is mine *here*, with no shadow versions running in parallel—is suggestive of, though not decisive against, MWI's metaphysics. The Map takes the suggestiveness seriously while acknowledging the position is not forced by the phenomenology alone.

### Occam's Razor Has Limits

Predictive processing and self-model accounts give elegant, parsimonious explanations of mine-ness as the output of a transparent self-modelling process. The [Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits) tenet enters because the elegance of these explanations may purchase simplicity at the cost of leaving the phenomenal character unexplained. The Map holds that simpler explanations are not automatically better when the simpler version omits the very feature in need of explanation. Mine-ness is a paradigm case: the physicalist account can be complete at the functional level while still missing what mine-ness *feels like*.
