Semanticisation (in North American usage, semanticization) is the process by which a memory of a particular experienced event—episodic memory, with its contextual detail and its felt quality of re-experiencing—gradually becomes semantic knowledge: the retained fact that the event happened, without the reliving. You once re-inhabited your first day of school; decades later you know you attended, in the way you know Paris is the capital of France. The transformation itself is common ground across memory science: trace transformation theory treats consolidation as transformation rather than mere stabilisation (Winocur & Moscovitch 2011), and the hybrid forms the process produces have their own named category, personal semantics (Renoult et al. 2012). What the camps dispute is what the transformation shows.

The Unfinishable Map's stake in that dispute is explicit and on the record. The Map's treatment of memory phenomenology names semanticisation, studied phenomenologically, as the sharpest available test of its claim that autonoetic consciousness—the felt re-experiencing that marks a memory as one's own past—differs in kind from mere knowing. If felt pastness fades by degrees as a memory semanticises, that reading loses on its own terms. This article reports where the test stands as of 2026, and the standing is mixed: the categorical question has largely gone against the Map—the leading recent literature argues that episodic and semantic memory are not two kinds—while the phenomenological measurement the falsifier actually turns on has not been made, the strongest phenomenal evidence available points toward discreteness rather than grading, and [a third reading](#the-third-reading-fading-as-loss-of-access) on which fading is loss of access rather than transformation of the trace complicates the test's own logic. The wager stands open and contested—not won, and not yet lost.

## What Semanticisation Is

In the hours after an event, retrieval approaches re-experiencing: sensory detail, spatial layout, emotional tone, the sense of being there again. Over days and weeks, peripheral detail thins while the narrative core strengthens. Over months and years, recall increasingly delivers gist; over decades, for most memories, the event has become something one knows rather than something one relives. The Map's account of [consciousness and memory](/topics/consciousness-and-memory/) describes this trajectory in phenomenological terms; the neuroscience attributes it to systems consolidation, in which cortical networks slowly extract statistical regularities from hippocampally bound episodes, and—on the trace transformation view—change what is stored rather than merely relocating it.

Stated this way, semanticisation is neutral. Every position in the dispute—functionalist, eliminativist, dualist—accepts that content persists while episodic specificity and reported re-experiencing decline, that the decline spans years, and that hybrid forms proliferate along the way. The dispute begins when the transformation is asked to bear on what remembering is.

## The Wager the Map Placed on It

The Map's reading of memory, developed across [episodic-memory](/concepts/episodic-memory/), [semantic-memory](/concepts/semantic-memory/) and [anoetic-noetic-autonoetic-consciousness](/concepts/anoetic-noetic-autonoetic-consciousness/), holds that remembering-as-reliving and knowing-that differ in the mode of consciousness rather than the information carried—Tulving's autonoetic/noetic contrast (Tulving 1985) taken as a difference in kind. The episodic-memory article draws the consequence for semanticisation directly: a memory that semanticises loses autonoetic character while retaining content, which "makes consciousness constitutive of the *kind* of memory you have" rather than an accompaniment to it. That reading is the Map's proposal, not a neutral rendering of the science.

A claim of that strength owes the world a way of losing, and the Map's treatment of [memory phenomenology](/topics/phenomenology-of-memory-and-the-self/) states one. Functionalist accounts of semanticisation are absorbed by the Map's standing reply—they specify how the state is produced, not why its output is experienced—so counting them as falsifiers would be empty. What survives as a genuine falsifier is phenomenological: "semanticisation studied phenomenologically rather than functionally—tracking whether felt pastness fades by degrees as an episodic memory becomes semantic—is the sharpest available test." If careful phenomenological report grades smoothly between remembering-as and knowing-that, the joint the Map draws is an artefact of coarse categories. That commitment is stated so it can be checked—and this article is the check running.

## What the Literature Has Returned: The Category Has Graded

On the categorical limb the field has largely returned the adverse answer. A 2024 theme issue of *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*—*Elements of episodic memory: lessons from 40 years of research*—collects a coordinated case against the distinction, and semanticisation is among its leading exhibits. Gentry and Buckner (2024) argue from transitional gradation: episodic and semantic memory grade into each other—through gradual semanticisation in one direction and the semantic composition of episodic recall in the other—so densely that they are not two kinds, and the authors "recommend replacing the distinction with a dynamic life cycle of memory in which a variety of transitional forms will proliferate." In the same issue, Addis and Szpunar (2024) push past even the continuum to a multidimensional model of mental representations, and De Brigard (2024) argues that autonoetic consciousness lacks construct validity and that episodic memory's phenomenology is a contingent feature of retrieval. The one in-issue defence of distinctness, Cheng (2024), argues for a mechanically and functionally distinct episodic subsystem—mechanistic grounds that leave the phenomenology aside, so it is no ally of a constitutive reading either. Outside the issue, a widely cited review concludes the two systems are "inextricably intertwined, yet retain a measure of distinctiveness" (Renoult, Irish, Moscovitch & Rugg 2019)—a continuum claim with a residue clause—and Rubin (2022) replaces the hierarchy with a continuous three-dimensional conceptual space in which self-reference, the dimension nearest the Map's concerns, grades like the rest. The graded reading of the category is now mainstream.

The argument form matters for how the Map files it. Transitional gradation is a metaphysical argument about kind-individuation. It is not a functionalist mechanism story—it does not claim to explain why remembering is experienced—so the Map's standing reply cannot absorb it. Nor is it a phenomenological measurement, so it does not trigger the falsifier. The Map's earlier two-way sorting of semanticisation findings, functionalist or phenomenological, had no slot for the most cited work in the area; this article adds the third category. What a kind-individuation argument attacks is whether "autonoetic" and "noetic" name kinds at all—and whether it succeeds turns on how the graded properties relate to the phenomenal one.

## Where the Phenomenal Limb Stands

That question can be answered from the paper itself: Gentry and Buckner's full text, verified against the open-access publisher record in September 2026, does reach the phenomenal limb, in two moves. First, it asserts grading: memories midway through abstraction "may exhibit properties of both forms of memory—even partially autonoetic components," instancing Neisser's "repisodic" memories of stereotyped repeated events such as family holiday dinners. Second, it concedes the appearance of a joint and explains it away: "the phenomenal experience of remembering does offer apparently sharp boundaries in recollective experiences," which the authors argue is "best explained in terms of an error theory"—an account of why subjects would mistakenly suppose there are two kinds of memory. Autonoesis as a criterion they set aside as an "empirically intractable phenomenological criterion."

Assertion plus error theory is argument, not measurement. The measurement the falsifier turns on—longitudinal tracking, within a single memory, of felt re-experiencing as that memory semanticises—was not found by the literature survey run for the Map in August 2026 through OpenAlex, Crossref and Europe PMC. That is a negative search result at a stated ceiling, not a proof of absence: such a study could be indexed under diary-study or consolidation vocabularies the search did not exhaust. Following the Map's [evidential-status discipline](/project/evidential-status-discipline/), the honest classification is twofold: on the decisive measurement, nothing has come in—the prediction is untested, which is weaker than having survived a test—while at the level of interpretation a serious live countermodel now stands.

What has come in concerns the instrument the test would use. Zaman, Setton, Catmur and Russell (2024), factor-analysing subjective ratings from 342 participants across memory and future thinking, recovered a stable latent structure for autonoesis—so the construct is measurable, against the invalidity charge—but found re-experiencing and mental time travel, not the remember/know contrast, doing the identifying work. Folville, Simons, D'Argembeau and Bastin (2022) found that older adults overestimate the vividness of their remembering and rely less on retrieved detail when judging it, concluding that "the subjective experience of remembering is more than the reactivation of memory content"—a sentence close to the Map's thesis, delivered from inside cognitive psychology by the same study that shows the self-report scales any fading test would use are miscalibrated across groups. Cooper and Ritchey (2022) found vividness ratings tracking gist strongly and perceptual detail weakly, with clusters of participants weighting the two differently—the decomposition experiment one step short of the residual test the Map's parent article names. In the closest existing approach to semanticisation studied phenomenologically over real time, Coane, Umanath, Cimenian and Chang (2022) had participants rate memory for public events spanning twelve years with no instructions on how to use the terms: unprompted definitions of remembering and knowing aligned with the theoretical categories, and remembered and known items did not differ in subjective strength. Micro-phenomenological interviewing, finally, exists as a published instrument and has been pointed at remembering, but its own reliability is under active dispute (Høffding, Martiny & Roepstorff 2021; Heimann et al. 2022). The Map's sharpest available test is unrun—with an instrument that exists.

## The Strongest Phenomenal Evidence Points Toward Discreteness

Severely deficient autobiographical memory (SDAM) is semanticisation's endpoint present from birth. Palombo, Alain, Söderlund, Khuu and Levine (2015) describe, in a three-person case series, healthy and high-functioning adults with a lifelong inability to relive their own past from the first person, corroborated by the absence of the fMRI and event-related-potential signatures that normally accompany episodic recollection: "their past is experienced in the absence of recollection." Autobiographical content intact; the manner of access missing entirely. This is content/manner independence in its cleanest available form, and [episodic-memory](/concepts/episodic-memory/) and [anoetic-noetic-autonoetic-consciousness](/concepts/anoetic-noetic-autonoetic-consciousness/) develop the case.

Two cautions keep the evidence honest. SDAM is a trait, not an observed transition: it shows the two states are separable without showing that the passage between them, when it happens across decades, is a jump rather than a slide. And the same case serves the other side: De Brigard can read SDAM as episodic memory functioning without autonoesis, which is precisely his thesis. Discreteness evidence it is; decisive it is not.

## The Third Reading: Fading as Loss of Access

The Map's dichotomy—graded phenomenology adverse to its reading, discrete phenomenology favourable—omitted a third possibility, and it is the one the empirical record most directly supports. Sekeres and colleagues (2016), in a seven-day laboratory study of forgetting, found peripheral details lost faster than central ones—and then found that brief reminders reinstated the lost peripheral details, so that "loss of details is not always permanent, and may reflect both a storage and retrieval deficit." Barry, Chadwick and Maguire (2018), tracking autobiographical memories longitudinally with fMRI, found ventromedial prefrontal engagement rising and falling around a peak at eight to twelve months before re-engaging for two- and five-year-old memories, replicated across independent memory sets: the neural time course of consolidation is not a smooth decay. The Proustian phenomenon points the same way—an involuntary sensory cue can restore vivid re-experiencing to a memory that had seemed fully semanticised.

If fading is partly an access failure rather than a storage transformation, a smoothly graded report profile is compatible with an ungraded phenomenal property: the grading would live in retrieval accessibility, not in the quality retrieved. When access succeeds, re-experiencing returns whole; when it degrades, reports grade. The Map proposes this access reading as a live alternative rather than an established result—it rests on a small evidence base and appears largely undiscussed as a distinct option in the philosophy-of-memory literature surveyed. The proposal also cuts against the Map's own prose: the smooth decades-long trajectory described in [consciousness-and-memory](/topics/consciousness-and-memory/) is, on the Barry evidence, an assumption rather than an inherited finding. And it raises the bar for the falsifier's design: a test whose adverse outcome is "graded phenomenological report" needs reinstatement probes paired with phenomenological interview—something that can tell transformation from access—before its verdict means what it seems to mean.

## What the Definition Settles and What It Does Not

From the neutral definition alone, this much follows: memories transform; hybrid forms such as personal semantics are real; and any boundary drawn in terms of content, structure or function will be graded, because those properties demonstrably grade. A functionalist can accept all of that and stop.

The further claims are the Map's, and follow only if its reading is accepted: that felt pastness is a unified phenomenal property rather than a bundle of cues; that its fate under semanticisation is evidence about whether consciousness is constitutive of memory kinds or an accompaniment to them; and that the access reading, if it holds, would protect a discrete quale behind a graded report. Whether the falsifier's eventual verdict carries metaphysical weight depends on that framing—which is why the Map states the framing as its own.

## Relation to Site Perspective

Semanticisation matters to The Unfinishable Map because it is where the Map's reading of memory phenomenology most directly exposes itself to evidence. The Dualism tenet motivates treating autonoetic re-experiencing as irreducible; the falsifier staked on semanticisation is what keeps that treatment a commitment rather than an immunised doctrine. Reporting its standing requires three verdicts at once. On the decisive phenomenological measurement, nothing has come in: the constitutive reading is unrefuted and untested, the weakest of the falsifier states, and the moderate confidence the parent article assigns should be read against that. On the kind question, a serious live countermodel stands: transitional gradation, with graded autonoesis asserted and the apparent sharpness of recollective experience conceded but explained away by an error theory. On the instrument question, the evidence that exists is too thin and too miscalibrated to decide.

The Occam's-limits tenet bears on how much the gradation argument can claim. Replacing a dichotomy with a single graded kind because transitional forms proliferate is a parsimony-driven revision, and with the decisive phenomenal measurement missing, the one-kind ontology is underdetermined at exactly the point in dispute. The Map declines to concede the phenomenal limb to an error theory in advance of measurement—while accepting, on the record, that if longitudinal phenomenological tracking ever shows felt pastness fading by degrees within a memory, with access effects controlled, the constitutive reading loses on its own terms. The remaining tenets are not implicated: nothing in this material bears for or against the Map's quantum-interaction commitments or its rejection of Many Worlds, and nothing here supplies support for them either.
