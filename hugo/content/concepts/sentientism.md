**Sentientism is the view that the capacity for valenced experience — experience that feels good or bad to its subject — is both necessary and sufficient for moral status.** Its canonical statement is Bentham's: "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" Its modern development is Singer's extension of equal consideration of interests to every being that has interests at all; its current defence is Perry's (2024) case that sentience should be the *only* basis of status, against pluralists who add agency, empathy or sociality.

The Unfinishable Map holds sentientism in a specific and contestable form: **phenomenal sentientism**, on which the criterion is the presence of felt experience rather than of a functional role that pain and pleasure play in an information-processing system. That qualification is the Map's own, licensed by the [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism) tenet and registered as [P-MS1](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms1); the encyclopedia literature explicitly declines to make it. Its hardest opponent is **biocentrism** — harder than ecocentrism, because the biocentrist concedes that status attaches to individuals and disputes only the experience requirement.

## The Criterion and Its Two Directions of Attack

**Anthropocentrism** restricts moral status to humans. Sentientism's reply is the argument from arbitrariness, which Singer named **speciesism**: privileging *Homo sapiens* as such is a preference for one's own group over relevantly similar outsiders, as racism and sexism do. Any cognitive threshold proposed for the human/non-human boundary — rationality, language, autobiographical memory — excludes some humans, infants and the severely cognitively impaired among them, whose protection almost nobody will withdraw; either the threshold is lowered until it admits many non-human animals, or some humans lose their standing, and species membership cannot save the boundary without conceding that a biological classification does moral work no argument supports. Perry presses the same case against the pluralist grounds now offered alongside sentience: an account resting on sentience alone avoids conceptual redundancy and explains more than its rivals. Infants and the cognitively impaired are sentient, however, so the argument transfers no pressure to a valence criterion; the one human population that criterion itself reaches, the permanently unconscious, is taken up under [disputed implications](#disputed-implications).

**Biocentrism and ecocentrism** press the opposite complaint: sentience is an arbitrarily *narrow* threshold, excluding organisms with goods of their own but no nervous system. The Map's reply to Rolston's ecocentrism — "adaptive value" is either functional description, not yet normative, or a normative property needing the experiential grounding it lacks — is worked out in [the treatment of environmental ethics](/topics/phenomenal-normativity-environmental-ethics/#against-ecocentrism). Both objections turn on whether the experience requirement is principled, which is what the rest of this article is about.

## Biocentrism: The Harder Opponent {#biocentrism}

Ecocentrism must explain how a system with no experiencer in it contains normative properties; biocentrism gives that objection nothing to grip. Paul Taylor's 1981 and 1986 statements are the rigorous version: each individual living thing — animal, plant, or micro-organism — is a "teleological-center-of-life" with a good of its own that can be enhanced or damaged, and every such entity has equal inherent worth. Attfield keeps the ground and drops the egalitarianism by exactly the device the Map itself uses: every living thing has moral standing, and has it equally, while moral *significance* varies with capacities, sentience and self-consciousness foremost (1987, pp. 53–56). That is a threshold set at life with graded stakes above it, a biocentric implementation of the architecture registered as [P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2) rather than a concession to it. Varner's *In Nature's Interests?* (1998) grants interests to every organism but gives priority to those with conscious desires, and above them to certain interests only humans have; his *Personhood, Ethics, and Animal Cognition* (2012) leaves biocentrism behind for Hare's two-level utilitarianism with sentience as the entry condition — the named opponent ends on the Map's side.

A sunflower turning toward light, a bacterium repairing its membrane — each is a system with a direction, and with conditions under which it does well or badly. Sentience adds nothing to that good; it adds only someone to whom it is apparent. Why should the moral weight attach to the awareness rather than to the good?

### The Sentientist Reply: Interests Require a Subject

The sentientist's standard reply is **Joel Feinberg's interests-criterion** (1974). Only entities with interests can have standing, and "interests must be compounded somehow out of conations": a mere thing, "however valuable to others, has no good of its own." Feinberg's list of what mere things lack, though, runs from conscious wishes through "unconscious drives, aims, goals" to "latent tendencies, directions of growth, and natural fulfillments" (pp. 49–50), so on his own wording a plant's tendencies are a form of conative life and the criterion as first stated admits the biocentrist's sunflower. The sentientist verdict returns only when he narrows the criterion to conscious wants: trees, having none, "cannot know satisfaction or frustration, pleasure or pain" (p. 52). The narrowing is the Map's position — wanting rather than merely tending is what requires a subject — but it is the Map's stipulation on a text unstable at exactly this point, not Feinberg's settled view.

Nor does it refute biocentrism: a conation requirement read this way is exactly what the biocentrist denies. It marks a foundational commitment — a system with a direction but no perspective offers a description to be reported, not a perspective to be represented — and locates the disagreement rather than settling it.

The biocentrist's key phrase, "a good of its own", runs together *biological* good, the conditions under which a system persists and reproduces, and *moral* good, a state of affairs there is reason to bring about. The Stanford entry presses the point, following Williams and O'Neill: HIV has a good of its own, so if a good of one's own generates a prima facie duty to promote it, the duty extends to the virus. The biocentrist has machinery for the case — Taylor's priority principles for competing claims (1986, ch. 6) license self-defence against dangerous organisms without appealing to mattering-to-someone — but none of it says why the virus generated a duty needing to be overridden.

Attfield states the inference the HIV case probes as a two-premise argument — every living being has a good of its own; whatever has a good of its own has moral standing — and concedes that the second premise cannot be demonstrated, defending it from the centrality of beneficence: whatever can be benefited or harmed must count (1987, pp. 51–52). The sharpest pressure on that bridge comes from inside naturalism. Basl's *The Death of the Ethic of Life* (2019) grants the biocentrist everything up to the bridge — non-sentient organisms have a welfare, grounded in what selection has shaped them to do — then shows that the same etiological ground confers a welfare on anything with a selection history, so that welfare cannot entail direct moral status without extending status to corkscrews and computers. Attfield had called an artefact's good "derivative"; Basl's point is that selection history, the only subject-free ground on offer, does not respect that line. The Map takes the restriction Basl's dilemma calls for to be the obvious one — a good that is *its own* is a good there is someone for — and reads Basl, who reaches the necessity direction without mentioning felt experience, as an ally on the conclusion rather than on its ground.

The Map holds that the gap is the structural feature of value that [the consciousness-value connection](/concepts/consciousness-value-connection/) identifies: descriptive teleology is everywhere in biology, and normative teleology requires an experiencer for whom the trajectory is good or bad, because the goodness and badness are features of how the state is felt. Korsgaard's "it is a pain to be in pain. And that is not a trivial fact" states the datum, though on Kantian rather than phenomenal-realist grounds.

That premise is an experience requirement on welfare, and Lin (2021) argues that no compelling case for such a requirement exists: the most promising argument — that without it one cannot explain why only sentient beings are welfare subjects — fails because a desire-satisfaction theory of the right shape excludes non-sentient beings on their incapacity for desire. Lin thus concedes the datum the necessity direction needs and threatens the Map's ground for it rather than the direction. The Map's reply to his alternative is the one it gives desire-satisfaction accounts generally — a desire fulfilled with no one any better off for it is a proposition made true rather than a life made better ([welfare subjects without consciousness](/concepts/consciousness-value-connection/#objections-and-responses)) — and Lin can fairly hear that as the requirement restated rather than argued for, since the Map's support for it is the value premise it holds on Dualism's terms ([P-VS3](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs3)). The disagreement sits closer to bedrock than the Feinberg exchange suggested: a contested premise about welfare, not a contested reading of a text.

The requirement must also say *which* experiencer ([two layers](/concepts/no-self-objection-to-phenomenal-value/#implications)). The Map's answer — unrefuted and contested, not tested — is that a momentary experiential locus, whoever this pain is bad for now, suffices for standing; a persisting subject is a further claim ([a locus, not a career](/concepts/no-self-objection-to-phenomenal-value/#maps-reply)) on which sentientism's diachronic stakes draw, and which the Map makes on agency grounds rather than on value.

## Phenomenal Sentientism, Not Functional Sentientism

The distinction the wider literature leaves unmade is the one the Map's position turns on. The Stanford "Grounds of Moral Status" entry uses "consciousness" and "sentience" interchangeably, with no differentiation between phenomenal consciousness and access or functional consciousness; applied work inherits the elision, treating indicators as constitutive of the criterion rather than evidence for it. Two consequences follow if sentience is read functionally.

**First, the criterion inherits whatever indeterminacy the functional analysis has.** If sentience just is a certain organisation of nociception, aversive learning, motivational trade-off, and behavioural flexibility, then "sentient" is a cluster term whose borderline cases are settled by where the thresholds are set. The functionalist need concede only *semantic* indeterminacy. But a precisification is a decision, so "is this insect sentient?" becomes "have we counted enough indicators, on which sharpening?", and what moral status waits on is partly stipulated. The Map's [case-by-case treatments](/topics/consciousness-in-simple-organisms/) read those cases as genuine uncertainty about a real boundary, which requires a real boundary to be uncertain about.

**Second, functional sentientism survives illusionism but loses its rationale.** [Illusionists](/concepts/illusionism/) hold that phenomenal consciousness is an introspective misrepresentation of non-phenomenal properties. Grant illusionism, and the functionalist's criterion is left standing — the illusionist grants the aversive-motivational organisation it picks out. What is not left standing is the reason for caring about it: the case for the criterion has always run through felt badness, so once nothing feels like anything, why that organisation rather than any other generates obligations is reopened. Kammerer's "Ethics Without Sentience" (2022) concedes the loss: if materialism is true, phenomenal consciousness is probably not normatively significant and ethics should be rebuilt on non-phenomenal ground. Frankish's "The Ethical Implications of Illusionism" (2024) denies it: illusionism neither denies that consciousness exists nor entails that it fails to ground ethical value, but gives a different account of what consciousness is and why it grounds value. The Map sides with Kammerer's diagnosis for the reason it presses against ecocentrism above — a functional description is not yet a normative property — which is a commitment of the Map's framework rather than a defect in Frankish's account on its own terms; the register carries his paper as the live attempt, not a defeater landed.

The Map's [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism) tenet dissolves the first consequence and takes a side on the second. If phenomenal experience is irreducible, then valence is a real property of a real subject, and the criterion cannot be eliminated by a correct functional analysis because it was never a functional claim. The exposure runs the other way too: if no state ever actually feels bad, "capacity for valenced experience" names no property. No in-framework refutation of illusionism is on offer here — Dualism denies it, and the denial is a foundational commitment rather than a result — so phenomenal sentientism stands or falls with the tenet. What the metaphysics buys is a different account of hard-case uncertainty — ignorance about a real boundary rather than indecision about where to draw one — and so precaution rather than stipulation.

## Disputed Implications

Some consequences follow from the neutral criterion alone; others only from the Map's phenomenal reading.

**From the criterion alone: overinclusiveness.** The standard objection in the encyclopedia literature is that lowering the standard to sentience puts most animals on a par with most humans, against common moral judgement. This bites against hedonic sentientism most directly.

**From the phenomenal reading: a reply to overinclusiveness, and to disenhancement.** The Map's [phenomenal value pluralism](/topics/phenomenal-value-realism/#beyond-hedonism) holds that hedonic valence is one value-bearing feature of experience among several — aesthetic quality, felt meaning, agency, understanding, connection. Sentience is then the entry ticket to moral status, not the whole measure of what is at stake in a life, so a being with the full range of value-bearing phenomenal features has more at stake than one with valence alone, without species membership doing any work.

That grading collides with the argument from marginal cases used above. Infants and the severely cognitively impaired are sentient, so the ranking implies only that less is at stake on some dimensions of their lives; status is untouched, and so is equal consideration of *equal* interests. The permanently unconscious human is the harder case, the one the valence criterion itself reaches. "Capacity" means the standing capacity: a dreamless sleeper or a patient in reversible coma keeps status ([welfare subjects and standing capacity](/concepts/consciousness-value-connection/#objections-and-responses)). Where the capacity is irreversibly gone, the criterion as stated entails that intrinsic status goes with it, and the Map says so: what remains owed — prior wishes, history, standing relationships — is owed derivatively, concerning that person, not to a patient who retains standing. That is the reading [P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2) adopts, a residue Singer and McMahan accept by other routes. Marginal cases show that species membership cannot ground the boundary, not that every human sits at the top of the ranking.

The mirror-image case presses from the other side. Chalmers holds that consciousness of any kind suffices for status, and tests the valence criterion with philosophical Vulcans — conscious beings who perceive, think and act but feel nothing good or bad. The Map bites the bullet where [the consciousness-value connection](/concepts/consciousness-value-connection/#implications) already does: a Vulcan clears the consciousness bar while having nothing that could go well or badly for it. Smithies (2026) defends that verdict against Chalmers directly, arguing that one cannot explain why consciousness is necessary for moral status without denying that it is sufficient. He is an ally on the necessity direction; the phenomenal reading of the hedonic capacity is the Map's further step.

The same resource bears on the **disenhancement objection**. If suffering is the only bad, a hedonic sentientist appears committed to accepting the genetic removal of farmed animals' capacity to suffer. Against wholesale removal of valence, value pluralism replies directly: the capacity for valenced experience is also the capacity for positively valenced experience, so eliminating it destroys a bearer of value, not merely of disvalue. The proposal actually in the literature is narrower. Shriver's "Knocking Out Pain in Livestock" (2009) targets only the *affective* dimension of pain, leaving nociceptive detection intact. Positive valence is untouched, so pluralism counts *for* it. The line the Map thinks available is narrower still: what negative valence does that no other listed feature does is register damage *to the subject as its own*, and removing it removes the capacity to be harmed in the first person. Whether that outweighs the suffering prevented the Map leaves open.

**From the criterion alone: the criterion does not supply a count.** Saying what has moral standing is not yet saying how many bearers of it a system contains. On the Map's commitments the count is a determinate fact that no inspection can be shown to settle, so applied arguments turning on numbers of patients inherit an assumption the framework cannot discharge ([moral census opacity](/concepts/moral-census-opacity/)).

**Under uncertainty: precaution.** [Birch's *The Edge of Sentience*](/topics/birch-edge-of-sentience-and-the-five-tier-scale/) (2024) defines sentience as the capacity for valenced experience, argues that it suffices for moral consideration, and supplies an action layer where the metaphysics is unsettled — proportionate precaution triggered by credible possibility rather than proof. Birch assigns non-materialist views low credence; the Map adopts the action layer without claiming him as an ally on grounding.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)** is what makes the Map's sentientism phenomenal rather than functional. Under materialism, "sentience" names some functional or physical organisation and inherits that analysis's indeterminacy; under Dualism it names an irreducible property and the moral boundary is discovered rather than stipulated.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)** matters for whether the criterion picks out anything that does causal work. If valence were epiphenomenal, an animal's suffering would be a passenger on the behaviour rather than its driver; because consciousness influences physical outcomes, felt badness is part of the causal story of avoidance.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)** governs the precautionary layer. Dismissing sentience in unfamiliar architectures because "mere mechanism" is the simpler hypothesis is exactly the false parsimony the tenet rejects.

**Acknowledged tension.** Wild animal suffering is a moral concern at a scale that dwarfs human suffering, and the Map has no worked account of what follows practically. The [question of artificial sentience](/topics/ethics-of-possible-ai-consciousness/) is harder still, because the behavioural evidence that grounds attributions in animals is exactly what a trained system can produce without the underlying property ([the gaming problem](/concepts/gaming-problem/)).
