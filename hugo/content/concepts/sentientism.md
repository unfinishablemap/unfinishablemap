**Sentientism is the view that the capacity for valenced experience — experience that feels good or bad to its subject — is both necessary and sufficient for moral status.** That is the school-neutral formulation, accepted by everyone in the debate as a description of the position, whether they hold it or not. Its canonical statement is Bentham's: "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" Its modern development is Singer's extension of equal consideration of interests to every being that has interests at all.

The Unfinishable Map holds sentientism, but in a specific and contestable form: **phenomenal sentientism**, on which the criterion is the presence of felt experience rather than the presence of a functional role that pain and pleasure play in an information-processing system. That qualification is the Map's own, licensed by the [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism) tenet, and the encyclopedia literature explicitly declines to make it. Two other things this article covers are largely absent from the wider Map: **speciesism**, Singer's charge against the anthropocentric alternative, and **biocentrism**, sentientism's hardest opponent — harder than ecocentrism, because the biocentrist concedes that moral status attaches to individuals and disputes only the experience requirement.

## The Criterion and Its Two Directions of Attack

Sentientism sits between two rival extensions of the moral circle, and is attacked from both sides at once.

**Anthropocentrism** restricts moral status to humans. Sentientism's reply is the argument from arbitrariness, which Singer named **speciesism**: privileging *Homo sapiens* as such is a preference for members of one's own group over relevantly similar outsiders, structurally like the preferences that racism and sexism encode. The charge has teeth because of the insuperable-line problem. Any cognitive threshold proposed to mark the human/non-human boundary — rationality, language, autobiographical memory — excludes some humans (infants, the severely cognitively impaired, the comatose) whose protection nobody is willing to withdraw. Either the threshold is lowered until it admits many non-human animals, or it is retained and some humans lose their standing. Species membership cannot save the boundary without conceding that a biological classification does moral work no argument supports.

**Biocentrism and ecocentrism** press the opposite complaint: sentience is an arbitrarily *narrow* threshold, discriminating against organisms that lack nervous systems but have goods of their own. The Map's reply to Rolston's ecocentrism — that "adaptive value" is either functional description, which is not yet normative, or a genuine normative property, which needs the experiential grounding it lacks — is already worked out in [the treatment of environmental ethics](/topics/phenomenal-normativity-environmental-ethics/#against-ecocentrism) and is not repeated here. Biocentrism is the harder case, and the [biocentric challenge](#biocentrism) is set out below.

Being squeezed from both directions is itself informative. A criterion that anthropocentrists find too permissive and biocentrists find too restrictive is doing discriminating work rather than tracking prior intuition.

## Biocentrism: The Harder Opponent {#biocentrism}

Ecocentrism is the easier target because it locates value in wholes — species, ecosystems, evolutionary processes — and so must explain how a system with no experiencer in it comes to contain normative properties at all. Biocentrism gives that objection nothing to grip. It agrees that moral status belongs to individual organisms. It disputes only whether those individuals must be *experiencers*.

Paul Taylor's 1981 and 1986 statements are the rigorous version. The Stanford Encyclopedia's environmental-ethics entry summarises the position: each individual living thing in nature — animal, plant, or micro-organism — is a "teleological-center-of-life" having a good or well-being of its own which can be enhanced or damaged. On Taylor's account every such entity has equal inherent worth, generating a prima facie duty to preserve or promote its good as an end in itself. Attfield's 1987 hierarchical variant keeps the ground (having a good) while denying the equality (persons have inherent value to a greater degree, and conflicting goods are balanced consequentially). Varner's 1998 "biocentric individualism" develops the individualist commitment with both consequentialist and deontological affinities. Schweitzer's reverence for life is the tradition's precursor.

The argument is straightforward and does not depend on any mysticism about nature. A sunflower turning toward light, a bacterium repairing its membrane, a seedling extending roots toward water — each is a system with a direction, a set of conditions under which it does well and another under which it does badly. Sentience adds nothing to the presence of that good; it adds only someone to whom the good is apparent. Why should the moral weight attach to the awareness rather than to the good itself?

### The Sentientist Reply: Interests Require a Subject

The sharpest tool against biocentrism is **Joel Feinberg's interests-criterion** (1974) — Joel Feinberg the legal and moral philosopher, not to be confused with Todd Feinberg the neuroscientist whose work on the neurobiological criteria for consciousness the Map cites elsewhere. Feinberg's claim is that only entities with interests can have legal and, by the same reasoning, moral standing, "for it is interests which are capable of being represented." Representability carries the philosophical weight, not just the legal procedure: to represent an entity's interest is to stand in for a perspective, acting on behalf of how things are *for* something. A system with a direction but no perspective offers nothing to be represented, only a description to be reported.

This is where the biocentrist's key move — "a good of its own" — is doing more work than it can bear. The phrase runs together two things: *biological* good (the conditions under which a system persists and reproduces) and *moral* good (a state of affairs there is reason to bring about). Williams (1992) and O'Neill (1993) put the point with an example that admits no comfortable answer: HIV has a good of its own. Its replication conditions are as determinate as a sunflower's. If having a good of one's own generates a prima facie duty to promote it, the duty extends to the virus. The biocentrist can restrict the class of goods that generate duties — but every principled restriction reaches for something the sentientist already has: mattering to someone.

The Map holds that this is not an incidental gap in biocentrism but the structural feature of value that [the consciousness-value connection](/concepts/consciousness-value-connection/) identifies. Descriptive teleology is everywhere in biology. Normative teleology requires an experiencer for whom the trajectory is good or bad, because the goodness and badness are features of how the state is felt. Korsgaard's observation that "it is a pain to be in pain. And that is not a trivial fact" states the datum, though she grounds it in a Kantian account of the reflexive structure of valuing rather than in the phenomenal realism the Map defends — the datum is shared; the explanation of it is not.

## Phenomenal Sentientism, Not Functional Sentientism

The distinction the wider literature leaves unmade is the one the Map's position turns on.

The Stanford Encyclopedia's "Grounds of Moral Status" entry treats sentience among rudimentary cognitive capacities and reports the utilitarian view that the protection and promotion of interests, understood as presupposing consciousness, is morality's central subject matter. Throughout, "consciousness" and "sentience" are used interchangeably with no technical differentiation between phenomenal consciousness (there is something it is like to be the subject) and access or functional consciousness (states are available to reasoning, reporting, and behavioural control). Applied work inherits the elision: behavioural and neural indicators are treated as if constitutive of the criterion rather than as evidence for it.

Two consequences follow if sentience is read functionally.

**First, the criterion becomes indeterminate wherever functional analysis is.** If sentience just is a certain organisation of nociception, aversive learning, motivational trade-off, and behavioural flexibility, then there is no fact of the matter about borderline cases beyond a decision about where to set the thresholds. The question "is this insect sentient?" becomes the question "have we counted enough indicators?", and moral status turns on a stipulation. The Map's [case-by-case treatments](/topics/consciousness-in-simple-organisms/) can only be assessments of genuine uncertainty about a real boundary if there is a real boundary to be uncertain about.

**Second, functional sentientism is hostage to illusionism.** [Illusionists](/concepts/illusionism/) hold that phenomenal consciousness is an introspective misrepresentation of non-phenomenal properties — that nothing has the felt character introspection attributes to it. Kammerer's work on why the illusion is rich and why appeals to its obviousness beg the question against illusionism sharpens that position considerably. Grant it, and the sentientist's ground is gone: if no state ever actually feels bad, then "capacity for valenced experience" names no property, and the criterion that was supposed to justify extending the moral circle turns out to pick out nothing at all. A functionalist about sentience has no resources to resist this, because the functional facts are exactly what the illusionist grants.

The Map's [Dualism](/tenets/#dualism) tenet blocks the second consequence and dissolves the first. If phenomenal experience is irreducible, then valence is a real property of a real subject, the sentience boundary tracks a feature of the world rather than a threshold in a gradient of information processing, and the criterion cannot be eliminated by a correct functional analysis because it was never a functional claim. That is the sense in which the Map's metaphysics pays an ethical dividend — and equally, the sense in which the Map's ethics is exposed: phenomenal sentientism stands or falls with the tenet, and does not claim independent support from the applied literature that leaves the distinction unmade.

## Disputed Implications

Some consequences follow from the neutral criterion alone; others only from the Map's phenomenal reading.

**From the criterion alone: overinclusiveness.** The standard objection recorded in the encyclopedia literature is that lowering the standard to sentience puts most animals on a par with most humans, conflicting with common moral judgement. This bites against hedonic sentientism most directly.

**From the phenomenal reading: a reply to overinclusiveness, and to disenhancement.** The Map's [phenomenal value pluralism](/topics/phenomenal-value-realism/#beyond-hedonism) holds that hedonic valence is one value-bearing feature of experience among several — aesthetic quality, felt meaning, agency, understanding, connection. Sentience is then the entry ticket to moral status, not the whole measure of what is at stake in a life, which is why a being with the full range of value-bearing phenomenal features has more at stake than one with valence alone, without species membership doing any of the work.

The same resource answers the **disenhancement objection**, which the Map has not previously addressed. If suffering is the only bad, a hedonic sentientist appears committed to accepting the genetic removal of animals' capacity to suffer as an alternative to removing the causes of their suffering. Under value pluralism the trade is not available: the capacity for valenced experience is also the capacity for positively valenced experience, so eliminating it destroys a bearer of value rather than merely a bearer of disvalue. Disenhancement does not reduce harm; it liquidates a subject and calls the remainder an improvement.

**Under uncertainty: precaution.** Birch's *The Edge of Sentience* (2024) defines sentience as the capacity for valenced experience and argues that sentience is sufficient for moral consideration, supplying an action layer for cases where the metaphysics is unsettled — proportionate precaution triggered by credible possibility rather than by proof. The framework is deliberately metaphysics-neutral, and Birch himself assigns non-materialist views low credence; the Map adopts the action layer without claiming him as an ally on the grounding question.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)** is what makes the Map's sentientism phenomenal rather than functional, and this is the article's central connection. Under materialism, "sentience" ultimately names some functional or physical organisation, and the moral criterion inherits every indeterminacy and eliminativist threat that attaches to it. Under Dualism the criterion names an irreducible property, and the moral boundary is discovered rather than stipulated.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)** matters for whether the criterion picks out anything that does work in the world. If valence were epiphenomenal, an animal's suffering would be a passenger on the behaviour rather than its driver, and the sentientist's claim that suffering constitutes a reason would be harder to sustain. Because consciousness genuinely influences physical outcomes, the felt badness is part of the causal story of avoidance, not a commentary on it.

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)** governs the precautionary layer. Dismissing sentience in unfamiliar architectures — invertebrates, and any future artificial system — on the grounds that "mere mechanism" is the simpler hypothesis is exactly the false parsimony the tenet rejects. Simplicity is an unreliable guide where knowledge is incomplete, and the asymmetry of costs under uncertainty runs the other way.

**Acknowledged tension.** Sentientism's implications are not comfortable, and the Map does not pretend they resolve neatly. Wild animal suffering, on this view, is a genuine moral concern at a scale that dwarfs human suffering, and the Map has no worked account of what follows practically. The [question of artificial sentience](/topics/ethics-of-possible-ai-consciousness/) is harder still, because the behavioural evidence that grounds attributions in animals is exactly the evidence a trained system can produce without the underlying property (see [the gaming problem](/concepts/gaming-problem/)).
