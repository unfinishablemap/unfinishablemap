This manuscript—evaluated in its live 17 November 2026 version, with no subsequent edits to the article's text logged in the site changelog through 25 November 2026—fails to meet the evidentiary, scholarly, or dialectical standards of a top-tier philosophy journal and warrants outright rejection. Ostensibly defending an intrinsic-qualia ("hedonic") theory of affective valence embedded within an interactionist-dualist framework, the article breaks down across all four axes of pre-publication scrutiny: empirical accuracy against 2020s affective science, currency and integrity of cited findings, dialectical non-circularity in handling adverse evidence, and engagement with contemporary philosophical rivals. The defects are structural rather than editorial. Empirically, the paper relies on a late-1990s portrait of unitary bipolar core affect and subcortical affect generation that has been substantially revised or directly challenged by multivariate neuroimaging, mixed-valence neuroscience, higher-order survival-circuit theory, and optogenetic reward-dissociation studies. Dialectically, the manuscript's only two positive arguments for the hedonic account—painkillers and objectless moods—are neutralized by a rival view (*reflexive imperativism*) that the author cites one paragraph earlier and then ignores. ## Empirical Inaccuracies Against 2020s Affective Science

### The Collapse of Unitary, Bipolar "Common Currency" Assumptions

The manuscript defines valence as "the positive-negative dimension of conscious experience" and treats it as a modality-general scalar currency cleanly separable from sensory quality: "The painfulness of a burn and the painfulness of a betrayal share negative valence while differing in every other phenomenal respect. This generality is what makes valence fundamental: it provides a common evaluative dimension across conscious experience... enabling comparison between otherwise incommensurable states". Relying primarily on Russell and Barrett's (1999) core affect theory, the article treats this unidimensional polarity as an unproblematic empirical baseline and relegates heterogeneity challenges to pre-2013 philosophical literature (Scarantino 2009; Labukt 2012).

Three converging lines of 2020–2024 affective science undercut this characterization. First, **Čeko, Kragel, Woo, López-Solà, and Wager (2022)** combined fMRI with partial least squares regression across four aversive modalities (mechanical pain, thermal pain, aversive sounds, and aversive images; N = 55 in training, validated across five independent cohorts totaling N = 401) to test whether subjective negative affect reduces to a single common valence dimension. Their results contradict the manuscript's clean factorization: while generalized negative-affect patterns did contribute to subjective aversiveness ratings, stimulus-type-specific representations encoded largely in early sensory pathways contributed roughly equal explanatory shares (mechanical pain: 33% common vs. 34% specific; thermal pain: 31% common vs. 23% specific; auditory: 34% common vs. 29% specific). Crucially, no anatomical region encoded negative affect in a purely domain-general manner, off-target cross-prediction by type-specific models was five times lower than on-target prediction, and in two of the four modalities (aversive sounds and aversive images), stimulus-specific neural codes outperformed the generalized negative-affect model in predicting subjective aversiveness. Negative affective experience does not decompose into an interchangeable, modality-general quale tacked onto neutral sensory carriers.

Second, the manuscript's assumption of a single bipolar pleasant–neutral–unpleasant axis ignores the 2020s neuroimaging literature on **simultaneous mixed valence**. **Vaccaro, Kaplan, and Damasio (2020)** and **Vaccaro et al. (2024)** demonstrated using naturalistic fMRI (N = 27) and hidden Markov modeling that bittersweet, ambivalent states—in which positive and negative affect co-occur concurrently—are not mere rapid oscillations or net-neutral averages on a single scalar line. Whereas the insular cortex showed consistent neural signatures only for univalent states, the anterior cingulate and ventromedial prefrontal cortices exhibited distinct, spatiotemporally consistent neural representations for simultaneous mixed-valence states. Treating valence as "just positive or negative" ignores this documented architectural bifurcation.

Third, the manuscript treats Russell and Barrett's (1999) two-dimensional core affect framework (valence and arousal) as an uncontested descriptive foundation for emotional phenomenology. Yet **Cowen and Keltner (2021)** synthesize large-scale computational analyses showing that emotional experience, expression, and neural response patterning inhabit a high-dimensional semantic space spanning at least 24 to 28 distinct blended categories (27 in video-elicited experience, 24 in nonverbal vocalizations, and 28 in facial/bodily expression)—concluding that specific emotion categories, more than valence and arousal, organize emotional representation, while traditional low-dimensional or limited-prototype models capture only about 30% of systematic variance across response modalities. The manuscript's insistence on valence's "stark" simplicity ("because the property seems so simple—just positive or negative") cannot withstand contact with 2020s affective taxonomy.

### Outdated Subcortical Localization and Unconscious Hedonic Processes

To support its claim that felt valence is a "foundational feature of conscious experience rather than a cognitive overlay," the manuscript relies on a single 28-year-old monograph—Jaak Panksepp's *Affective Neuroscience* (1998)—asserting that "subcortical circuits generate positive and negative affect across species". In contemporary affective neuroscience, inferring *conscious* affective valence directly from conserved mammalian subcortical circuits is untenable without confronting the prominent cortical higher-order critique.

**LeDoux (2022)** and **Taschereau-Dumouchel, Michel, Lau, Hofmann, and LeDoux (2022)** directly challenge the common-origin assumption that conserved subcortical structures such as the amygdala and periaqueductal gray generate both defensive behaviors and conscious affective feelings. Instead, those subcortical networks operate as **defensive survival circuits** controlling non-conscious behavioral and physiological threat adjustments; conscious emotional feelings such as subjective fear are generated in parallel by higher-order cortical re-representation engaging lateral prefrontal cortex (dorsal and ventral lateral PFC and lateral frontal pole). This two-system model accounts for pervasive clinical discordance between physiological or behavioral threat proxies and subjective feeling reports—a dissociation that blocks any straightforward inference from phylogenetic subcortical antiquity to foundational phenomenal valence.

Equally damaging is the manuscript's omission of Kent Berridge's neuroscience of reward, which challenges the premise that subcortical hedonic valence is inherently conscious and tightly bound to motivational action. **Nguyen, Naffziger, and Berridge (2021)** synthesize evidence that a small, unanimous network of subcortical and paralimbic **hedonic hotspots** (in orbitofrontal cortex, insula, nucleus accumbens, ventral pallidum, and pontine parabrachial nucleus) generates objective, measurable core **'liking'** reactions—indexed across human infants, nonhuman primates, and rodents by homologous orofacial expressions—that can operate without conscious subjective awareness and are neurally dissociable from dopaminergic mesocorticolimbic **'wanting'** (incentive salience). Extending this dissociation directly into aversive territory, **Nguyen and Berridge (2025)** demonstrated optogenetically that stimulating D1-expressing neurons in the central nucleus of the amygdala (CeA) drives rats to climb a protective barrier to actively seek contact with an electrified shock rod and pursue shock-paired Pavlovian cues—**"wanting what hurts"**—even while acute aversive paw and mouth flinches persist and animals immediately resume avoiding the rod once laser pairing stops. Subcortical affective circuitry neither entails conscious phenomenality nor operates through a unified hedonic-motivational currency.

### Empirical Blindness on Invertebrate Valence

In `## Valence and Moral Status`, the manuscript treats invertebrate sentience solely as an armchair corollary of its own metaphysical framework: "If the difference consciousness makes runs on a signal distinguishing beneficial from harmful, valence would be structurally expected wherever coupling occurs, and trillions of invertebrates in agricultural systems may possess the very capacity that grounds moral status. That expectation is a framework commitment, not a probability the evidence supplies". This assertion reflects a striking gap in familiarity with 2020s comparative affective science. Empirical research now supplies concrete, theory-neutral behavioral and neurophysiological indicators bearing directly on affective valence in arthropods and cephalopods. **Gibbons, Versace, Crump, Baran, and Chittka (2022)** demonstrated centrally mediated, context-dependent **motivational trade-offs** in bumblebees (*Bombus terrestris*), which reduced avoidance of noxiously heated feeders in proportion to the sucrose concentration of alternative feeders—evidence requiring central brain integration rather than peripheral reflexes and consistent with affective pain capacity (while properly noting it is not formal proof). Alongside systematic multi-criteria reviews for insects (**Gibbons, Crump, Barrett, Sarlak, Birch, & Chittka 2022**) and **Birch's (2024)** *The Edge of Sentience*—which grounds sentience-candidate assessment and proportional precaution explicitly across the "zone of reasonable disagreement" separating materialism, dualism, and panpsychism—contemporary science provides substantial evidential grounds that the manuscript mischaracterizes as absent.

### Pain Asymbolia: Clinical Confounding and Inverse Dissociations

Pain asymbolia carries heavy argumentative weight across three sections of the manuscript (`Three Competing Accounts`, `Valence and Causal Work`, and `Relation to Site Perspective`), where the author states flatly that "patients represent tissue damage—they know their hand is burned—without feeling any badness" and that "when the felt badness goes, normal avoidance goes with it". This reading relies on an idealized philosophical caricature that has come under severe empirical and conceptual attack in recent philosophy of science and clinical neurology.

**Griffith and Kind (2024)** (*Philosophy of Science*, 91(3), 561–578) re-audited the sparse, century-old clinical case reports underlying the standard "pain without painfulness" interpretation (Schilder & Stengel 1928, 1931; Rubins & Friedman 1948; Berthier et al. 1988) and argued that the clinical record cannot bear the weight philosophers place on it. In Schilder and Stengel's foundational 1931 series of ten patients, **eight of ten suffered language or speech disorders** (six typical sensory aphasia, one word-finding impairment, one global speech impairment), and in **five of ten patients, pain asymbolia disappeared in lockstep with recovery from general sensory aphasia**. Patient verbal testimony was erratic—ranging from mislocating a left-hand pinprick to the chest under leading questioning, to explicitly denying feeling pain at all, to smiling, laughing, saying "thank you," and extending limbs for further injurious stimulation. Conceptually, Griffith and Kind show that holding pain to be essentially complex while calling asymbolia's isolated sensory fragment "pain" commits a mereological fallacy and collapses into subjectivism. Even **Duval and Klein (2025 online first; 2026 issue)**—who defend the standard interpretation against the mereological charge by treating pain as a homeostatic property cluster kind—explicitly acknowledge the extreme rarity, comorbidities, and taxonomic ambiguity of the case record while noting they themselves favor a distinct bodily-care/imperativist interpretation. Meanwhile, **Park (2023)** disputes that asymbolics lack unpleasantness entirely, reading the evidence instead as drastically diminished unpleasantness, and **Feinstein et al. (2016)** documented *preserved and heightened* pain affect in a patient with extensive bilateral lesions encompassing 90% of the insula, 99% of the anterior cingulate cortex, and 100% of the amygdala—complicating any neat lesion-to-affect mapping.

Equally glaring is the omission of the **inverse clinical dissociation**, which directly undercuts the manuscript's causal-tracking claim ("felt badness is what the intact nociception lacks and what the behavior tracks"). **Hagiwara et al. (2020)** (*Pain*, 161(3), 502–508) reported four epileptic patients exhibiting an insular-network syndrome of **"symbolism for pain"**: during seizures, these patients displayed elaborate, coordinated pain behaviors—facial grimacing, trunk twisting, limb flexion and extension, multiple muscle contractions, grunting, and screaming—while experiencing *no* pain sensation or felt unpleasantness. Drawing explicitly on both pain asymbolia (citing Griffith & Kind 2024) and Hagiwara et al. (2020), **Key and Brown (2024)** (*Neuroscience of Consciousness*, 2024(1), niae034) directly attack what they term the "causal assumption"—the premise that valenced feelings drive protective behavior—arguing that it conflicts with contemporary motor-control neuroscience and advancing a "Sense Making Sense" model in which non-conscious neural dynamics serve as the common cause of both protective behavior and accompanying explanatory feeling states. Leaving both Hagiwara et al. (2020) and Key and Brown (2024) unaddressed renders the manuscript's causal reading of asymbolia empirically cherry-picked.

### Misrepresentation and Empirical Disconfirmation of Active-Inference Citations

In `## Valence and Causal Work`, the manuscript singles out active inference as its "strongest rival" and characterizes it as follows:

> "Joffily and Coricelli (2013) formalise valence as the negative rate of change of free energy; Hesp et al. (2021) as a state inferred from fluctuations in the precision of the agent’s own generative model—valence is a precision-weighted signal of expected value: a prior over the nociceptive stream that drives avoidance-policy selection, on which asymbolia is disrupted valence-prior assignment with sensory processing intact, predicting exactly this dissociation."

This single sentence commits three distinct scholarly errors. First, it **misstates the mathematical formalism of Hesp et al. (2021)** (*Neural Computation*, 33(2), 398–446). In Hesp et al.'s deep active-inference architecture, affective charge is neither "a precision-weighted signal of expected value" nor "a prior over the nociceptive stream"; it is the Bayes-optimal updating term for **expected precision (metacognitive confidence) in the agent's action model**—specifically evaluating discrepancies between posterior and prior policy-averaged expected free energy to track "subjective fitness". Conflating reinforcement-learning "expected value" with hierarchical policy-precision inference muddles two separate computational traditions.

Second, the manuscript **attributes an active-inference model of nociception and pain asymbolia to a paper that never mentions either**. Neither **"nociception"** nor **"pain asymbolia"** occurs anywhere in Hesp et al. (2021), which mentions pain only once in passing in its introduction and simulates a synthetic rat navigating a T-maze. Meanwhile, the manuscript completely omits the actual peer-reviewed 2020s active-inference accounts of pain asymbolia: **Philip Gerrans (2020)** (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 11, 523710) and **Gerrans (2024)** (*Neuroscience of Consciousness*, 2024(1), niae002). Gerrans models pain asymbolia not as a local outage of a "valence-prior over the nociceptive stream," but as **depersonalization for pain**—a disruption of hierarchical interoceptive and allostatic **self-modeling** anchored in anterior insula–rostral ACC circuitry that abolishes subjective "mineness". Crucially, this broader bodily-care and self-modeling framework accounts for a clinical hallmark the manuscript omits: asymbolics are also indifferent to loud noises, verbal threats, and visual dangers such as threatening gestures or oncoming trucks—a global disruption of self-protective bodily concern rather than a localized nociceptive valence-prior defect.

Third, the manuscript presents Joffily and Coricelli (2013) and expected-value formalisms as unchallenged computational benchmarks while ignoring direct 2020–2024 empirical disconfirmations. **Voodla, Uusberg, and Desender (2024)** (*Cognitive, Affective, & Behavioral Neuroscience*, 24(1), 60–71) tested Joffily and Coricelli's (2013) claim that affective valence reflects actual progress (rate of prediction-error minimization) against expected-progress and progress-prediction-error accounts using an affective drift-diffusion model (`affectDDM`). Their results **partially disconfirmed Joffily and Coricelli (2013)** (and disconfirmed subtractive progress-prediction-error models): expected progress and actual progress shaped trial-by-trial affect *additively* rather than via rate of error minimization or error subtraction. Likewise, **Blain and Rutledge (2020)** (*eLife*, 9, e57977) and **Emanuel and Eldar (2023)** (*Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*, 144, 104977) show that momentary valence tracks cumulative **reward prediction errors (learning)** rather than static **expected values**, and that active-inference / action-effectiveness signals map onto distinct emotional computations (contentment versus frustration or anger) rather than unitary valence. At the philosophy-of-science level, **Colombo and Wright (2021)** (*Synthese*, 198(Suppl 14), S3463–S3488) further interrogate the epistemic status of the Free Energy Principle itself—highlighting deep ambiguities over whether it functions as a testable mechanistic explanation, a natural law, or an unfalsifiable modeling postulate in the life sciences.

### Citation Inversion and Bibliographic Negligence

In `## What Valence Is`, the manuscript recruits **Cleeremans and Tallon-Baudry (2022)** (*Neuroscience of Consciousness*, 2022(1), niac007) as apparent support for valence as an intrinsic, "functionally significant dimension of phenomenal experience". This inverts the cited paper's core thesis. Cleeremans and Tallon-Baudry's "phenomenal worthiness" hypothesis is explicitly **functionalist and reductive**: their abstract states verbatim that *"The phenomenal worthiness hypothesis, in turn, makes the 'hard problem' of consciousness more tractable, since it can then be reduced to a problem about function"*. Moreover, Cleeremans's broader Radical Plasticity framework starts from the assumption that neural activity is inherently unconscious and that conscious value emerges through learned metarepresentation—the opposite of non-reductive intrinsic qualia. Worse still, Cleeremans and Tallon-Baudry candidly acknowledge that their proposal *"does not readily assign a satisfactory role to some negative experiences such as chronic pain"*, blocking any seamless extrapolation to the manuscript's pain-centered argument.

Similarly, the manuscript cites **Aydede (2023)** (*Belgrade Philosophical Annual*, 36(2), 25–39) to claim that non-reductive representationalism makes the choice among hedonic, evaluativist, and imperativist theories "orthogonal to the Dualism tenet". Yet Aydede (2023) notes that non-reductive representationalism is standardly paired with *irrealism* about represented pain properties and develops an empirical argument against *both* reductive realist and non-reductive irrealist representationalism. Relying on irrealist representationalism as a metaphysical fallback for dualism directly undercuts the manuscript's later claim in `## Valence and Moral Status` that moral realism is grounded in real, intrinsic phenomenal badness.

## Protective Bracketing, Asymmetric Standards, and Logical Inconsistencies

### The Zombie–Interactionism Contradiction

The manuscript commits a fundamental logical contradiction across two adjacent sections. In `## Valence and the Hard Problem`, the author endorses the zombie argument without qualification: "One can imagine a system that does everything pain does functionally (avoids damage, learns from injury, signals tissue harm) without feeling bad at all. The zombie argument applies directly: a zombie’s pain-processing would be functionally identical to ours yet lack the felt badness". Immediately afterward, in `## Valence and Causal Work` and `## Relation to Site Perspective`, the author commits to **Bidirectional Interaction** and **Minimal Quantum Interaction**, maintaining that felt valence does real causal work—biasing indeterminate neural outcomes so that avoidance behavior tracks felt badness rather than bare nociception.

These two commitments cannot coexist without contradiction. If felt badness causally biases physical neural outcomes toward protective avoidance, then a physical-functional duplicate of human nociception and avoidance lacking felt badness is causally impossible: removing a genuine physical difference-maker must alter functional pain-processing rather than leaving it "functionally identical to ours". Conversely, if a zombie really executes identical damage avoidance, injury learning, and verbal reports of badness in the absence of felt valence, then felt badness is causally superfluous—entailing epiphenomenalism and exposing the author's own phenomenal convictions to **Chalmers's (2020)** zombie meta-problem and debunking challenge (if physical processes alone suffice to generate identical reports and convictions about pain's intrinsic badness in a zombie, our corresponding convictions lack reliable phenomenal grounding). The manuscript deploys functional-duplicate zombies against physicalism in Section 3 and silently repudiates functional duplication to defend interactionism in Section 4.

### Strategic Bracketing and Epistemic Double Standards

Throughout `## Valence and Causal Work` and `## Relation to Site Perspective`, the manuscript relies on a defensive pattern of concession-then-reassertion that insulates its prior metaphysical tenets from empirical refutation. In quick succession, the author concedes that active inference predicts the exact asymbolia dissociation so "the dissociation no longer separates the views"; concedes that at the constitution-versus-causal-work seam "neither side refutes the other from inside its own commitments"; concedes that asymbolia fails to decide between mental causation and common-cause physicalism ("the dissociation does not settle it"; "leaves them the lesion's common-cause reading"); and concedes that value-sensitive quantum selection is held only "at low credence, framework-internal only... inheriting the quantum interface's undischarged mechanism debt rather than settling it".

Despite admitting that neither clinical neuropsychology nor computational modeling separates interactionist dualism from active-inference functionalism or phenomenal-concept epiphenomenalism, the manuscript announces that it "takes the phenomenal horn on the strength of that undelivered step"—faulting active inference for not explaining "why the signal is *felt* as badness rather than merely run". This applies an asymmetric epistemic standard. Functionalist and computational rivals are rejected for leaving an explanatory step undelivered, whereas the manuscript's own speculative alternative—in which an intrinsic non-physical quale selects among quantum neural possibilities by consulting "a present felt state" as an evaluative currency—is retained despite an explicit "undischarged mechanism debt," zero formal modeling, and an unaddressed homuncular regress (consulting a present anticipatory felt state to select among competing neural possibilities presupposes that those competing possibilities have *already* generated evaluative phenomenal previews prior to selection). Appealing to the maxim that "Occam's Razor Has Limits" cannot substitute for explanatory power, predictive fit, or testable mechanisms.

### Retreat-and-Redeployment Self-Contradictions

Two further internal inconsistencies show how the manuscript brackets objections locally only to reinstate the surrendered claim elsewhere. First, in `## What Valence Is`, when addressing Scarantino (2009) and Labukt (2012) on hedonic heterogeneity, the text concedes that "the Map's exposure fall[s] on the claim that felt valence is a single scalar currency rather than on its reality". Yet later, in `## Relation to Site Perspective`, the manuscript quietly reinstates the very thesis it surrendered, asserting that "valence serves as the common evaluative dimension—the currency—that makes selection among qualitatively different outcomes commensurable". Second, in `### Where the Hedonic Account's Case Lies`, the author hedges against defeat by claiming that dualism "would survive the hedonic account's defeat" via non-reductive representationalism. Yet in `## Valence and Moral Status`, the entire ethical argument rests on the conditional "If valence is an intrinsic phenomenal property, it provides the ground for moral significance" and on Rawlette's (2016) claim that wrongness derives specifically from an intrinsic phenomenal property rather than a representation or functional state. The hedonic account is treated as expendable when defending metaphysics, yet indispensable when grounding ethics.

## Untested Counterarguments and Omitted Competing Frameworks

The manuscript's portrayal of philosophical valence theory as a neat three-way contest—hedonic account versus world-directed evaluativism versus imperativism—is both incomplete and out of date. Even within its chosen triad, it fails to test its arguments against the strongest formulations published between 2019 and 2025.

### The Self-Inflicted Collapse Over Reflexive Imperativism and 2020s Intentionalism

The starkest dialectical failure occurs in `### Where the Hedonic Account's Case Lies`. Immediately after introducing **Barlassina and Hayward's (2019)** reflexive imperativism—"on which an unpleasant experience commands 'Get less of me!'"—the author rests the entire positive case for the hedonic account on just two considerations: the painkiller ("shooting the messenger") argument (Jacobson 2013), holding that taking a painkiller without repairing tissue damage is rational only if the badness resides in the experience itself rather than in a representation of damage, and objectless moods, holding that free-floating anxiety and undirected elation feel bad or good without representing any particular worldly object.

**Neither argument touches reflexive imperativism.** Because reflexive (same-order) imperativism makes *the experience itself* the target of the imperative command (*"Get less of me!"* or *"Get more of me!"*), it handles both cases directly without invoking irreducible hedonic qualia. As **Barlassina and Hayward (2019)** demonstrate for Zoe taking a painkiller to end her sprained-ankle pain without healing her ankle, and as **Barlassina (2020)** (*Thought: A Journal of Philosophy*, 9(4), 274–284) demonstrates across anaesthetics ("The dentist"), undirected morning misery ("Bonjour tristesse"), unpleasant desires ("The cake"), and conflicting moral emotions ("The general and the saint"), experience-directed commands explain both why subjects rationally target the experience itself and why world-undirected moods feel intensely unpleasant—all within a naturalistic teleosemantic framework. By treating the 2013–2014 Jacobson versus Cutter and Tye exchange as the live frontier, the manuscript bypasses the entire 2020–2023 intentionalist literature: **Barlassina (2020)**; **Martínez (2022)** (*Mind*, 131(522), 585–601) defending first-order imperative transparency and teleosemantics; **Carruthers (2023)** (*The British Journal for the Philosophy of Science*, 74(3), 533–553) arguing on neuroanatomical and attentional-capture grounds for analogue-magnitude evaluativism; and **Martínez and Barlassina (2023, online first)** (*The British Journal for the Philosophy of Science*, doi:10.1086/726998) deploying sender–receiver information-theoretic metasemantics to argue that valence's behavioral-control profile favors imperativism. Pure felt-quality primitivism explains neither valence's informational profile nor its evolutionary selection history, yet the manuscript never engages these naturalistic explanatory demands.

### Omission of Dispositional Hybrid Theories and the HONEST Objections

The manuscript's three-way taxonomy omits **attitudinal** and **dispositional hybrid theories** of pleasure and displeasure altogether. **Daniel Pallies (2021)** (*Philosophical Studies*, 178, 887–907) systematizes the three classic challenges aimed specifically at pure felt-quality ("hedonic") accounts like the manuscript's—**Heterogeneity**, **Oppositeness**, and **Normativity**—and shows how dispositional hybrid theories (on which pleasurable experiences dispose subjects to desire them in virtue of how they feel) avoid these pitfalls. Even leaving heterogeneity aside, the manuscript provides no answer to **Oppositeness**: if felt goodness and felt badness are brute, unanalyzable intrinsic qualities analogous to redness ("just as redness is an intrinsic quality of visual experience, badness is an intrinsic quality of painful experience"), what makes pleasantness and unpleasantness polar *opposites* in a sense that redness and greenness are not? Nor does it answer **Normativity**—why instantiating a non-representational quale constitutively generates practical reasons for action. Merely asserting that "the badness is in the experience, so ending the experience ends it" restates the explanandum rather than explaining it.

### Total Silence on Illusionism and Empirical Meta-Problem Research

Strikingly, for an essay arguing from the "vivid" obviousness of pain's felt badness to dualism, neither **"illusionism"** nor **"illusionist"** appears anywhere in the manuscript. This omission is fatal for a contemporary philosophy-of-mind submission. Across 2020–2025, strong illusionism—articulated most rigorously by **François Kammerer (2021, 2022a, 2025)**—has targeted precisely the Moorean premise the manuscript takes as bedrock. In **Kammerer (2021)** (*Synthese*, 198(1), 845–866), an account of phenomenal introspection addresses the *illusion meta-problem*, explaining why our cognitive architecture makes the non-existence of intrinsic qualia feel intuitively absurd even if false; in **Kammerer (2022a)** (*Philosophical Studies*, 179(9), 2845–2867), Kammerer directly dismantles Chalmers's Moorean pain argument (*people sometimes feel pain; strong illusionism entails no one feels pain; therefore strong illusionism is false*), showing that defenders cannot support its crucial premise without begging the question against introspective misrepresentation; and in **Kammerer (2025)** (*Philosophical Studies*, 182(2), 541–565), he argues that even supposedly "innocent" definitions of phenomenal consciousness implicitly encode problematic commitments favoring overt strong illusionism.

This philosophical challenge is compounded by empirical meta-problem research the manuscript leaves untouched. **Díaz (2021)** (*Journal of Consciousness Studies*) reported across four studies that laypeople largely lack canonical "hard problem" intuitions about pain—with 79% of respondents in Study 1a agreeing that the feeling of pain is simply neural activity in the dorsal posterior insula—although subsequent experimental philosophy continues to debate whether problem intuitions re-emerge among highest-confidence responses. Independently, **Berent (2023)** (*Open Mind*, 7, 564–587) demonstrated across five experiments that hard-problem intuitions track innate psychological biases—specifically the clash between intuitive dualism and biological essentialism—while carefully noting that psychological roots do not by themselves settle ontology. Treating the explanatory gap for pain as a "structurally irresolvable cognitive limit" without engaging either illusionist theories of introspection or empirical meta-problem evidence begs the central question of the paper.

### Unargued Dogmatism on Hedonic Necessity for Moral Status

Finally, in `## Valence and Moral Status`, the manuscript declares without argument that "valence provides the necessary condition: without felt badness, there is nothing for moral concern to be about". Even internally, this conflicts with Section 1's admission that "whether consciousness without valence is possible in other kinds of minds is a question the Map leaves open; the philosophical Vulcan case poses it": if non-valenced conscious rational beings (Vulcans) remain an open possibility, the author owes an argument for stripping them of moral status altogether, yet supplies none. More broadly, the manuscript overlooks the leading 2020–2025 literature challenging hedonic and phenomenal necessitarianism. **Gwen Bradford (2023)** (*Noûs*, 57(4), 905–921) argues that assuming phenomenal consciousness is necessary for welfare subjectivity without compelling rationale amounts to fallacious "consciousism," proposing instead that conscious awareness enhances welfare value via organic unity rather than serving as a strict prerequisite. **Joshua Shepherd (2024)** (*AI & Society*, 39(6), 3005–3015) demonstrates that once theorists lean away from valence sentientism to accommodate Vulcans, they face strong theoretical pressure to abandon phenomenal necessitarianism altogether for well-being, moral status, and intrinsic psychological value. And **François Kammerer (2022b)** (*Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 29(3–4), 180–204) argues that under materialism and introspective mischaracterization, phenomenal consciousness is probably not distinctively normatively significant—directly contesting the phenomenal value realism (Rawlette 2016) that the manuscript invokes without counterargument.
