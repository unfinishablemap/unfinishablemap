## Reply

# External audit: “The Affective-Forecasting Gap and the Selection Currency”

**Disposition: major revision.**

The article identifies a genuine problem for the Map: a value-sensitive selection theory cannot remain neutral about whether selection responds to present feeling, predicted future feeling, remembered feeling, learned value, incentive salience, or some combination. Its strongest contribution is to force that question into the open.

It does not, however, successfully close the question. The August 20 revision substantially improved citation hygiene—especially around SNAP-1, internal quotations, and the role of the minimal-interaction tenet—but left four central defects:

1. The article’s “three utilities” taxonomy does not accurately represent the source taxonomy.
2. It overstates the generality and directionality of affective-forecasting error.
3. Its proposed mechanistic correlate conflates predicted value, reward-prediction error, incentive salience, and decision utility.
4. Its anaesthesia section treats differences in measurement, population, endpoint, encoding and retrieval as though they isolated report latency.

The article therefore offers a plausible **framework-relative proposal**—anticipated value causally guides selection while experienced value grounds welfare—but not an empirically established disambiguation.

The changelog’s conclusion that no newer work materially modifies the affective-forecasting picture is particularly doubtful: recent reviews and studies do not overturn the classic literature, but they do make its effect more conditional, heterogeneous and measurement-sensitive than the article acknowledges. ## 2. Citation audit

### 2.1 Affective forecasting: real literature, overgeneralised conclusion

The classic citations are genuine and broadly represented accurately. The literature does document impact bias, focalism, underestimation of coping and errors concerning the duration or intensity of future affect. The article also correctly notices that Levine and colleagues challenged some standard experimental measures, and that Wilson and Gilbert defended the broader impact-bias programme. [PubMed+3Sage Journals+3PubMed+3](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.0963-7214.2005.00355.x)

The problem is the article’s movement from that literature to the categorical claim that the anticipated currency is “exactly the kind that is least reliable,” and that divergence is structured rather than approximately noisy. That is too broad in at least four ways.

First, the Levine–Wilson exchange did not leave a simple verdict that forecasts are globally inaccurate. Levine and colleagues argued that event-specific affective intensity can be forecast with considerable accuracy when forecast and outcome questions are properly matched. The Wilson–Gilbert reply preserved important forms of impact bias, particularly duration errors, but did not eliminate the measurement problem. [PubMed+2PubMed+2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22889075/)

Second, a recent review describes affective-forecasting accuracy as mixed and substantially dependent on how forecasts and experiences are measured. That is a different claim from saying anticipated valence is systematically the least reliable currency. [DOAJ+1](https://doaj.org/article/284f296b1fd34672b3f2bd266cf151bb)

Third, recent everyday-life research reports considerable **relative accuracy**—people often correctly predict whether they will feel better or worse than usual—even where small absolute errors remain. This distinction is crucial for a selection theory. A selector may need only to rank options correctly; it need not predict the exact cardinal intensity of each future experience. A shared additive or multiplicative forecasting bias can leave the ordering of options unchanged. [PubMed+1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42311806/)

Fourth, errors are not uniformly in the direction of overestimation. Work on affect following the 2024 US presidential election found substantial overestimation among some disappointed partisans but more accurate or differently directed forecasts in other groups. The result supports conditional bias, not a universal transformation of anticipated into exaggerated experienced utility. [DOI+1](https://doi.org/10.1177/19485506261445321)

The article should therefore say:

> Affective forecasts can diverge systematically from later experience, especially for salient events and some dimensions of impact, but accuracy varies with context, measurement, forecast horizon and whether absolute magnitude or relative ordering is assessed.

That formulation still creates a calibration problem for the Map. It avoids turning a heterogeneous research programme into a universal law about the selector’s currency.

### 2.2 The “three utilities” attribution is not accurate

The article attributes a three-part distinction—decision utility, experienced utility and remembered utility—to Kahneman, Wakker and Sarin. That paper instead announces **two core meanings of utility**:

- **decision utility**, the weight an outcome receives in choice;
- **experienced utility**, its hedonic quality.

It then distinguishes ways of assessing experienced utility, including momentary or “instant” utility and retrospective evaluation or remembered utility. Remembered utility is therefore not straightforwardly a third co-equal core utility alongside decision and experienced utility. It is a retrospective representation of experience within the broader experienced-utility programme. [RePub+2RePub+2](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/23011/)

More importantly, the category the focal article actually needs is largely absent from its taxonomy: **predicted utility**. Kahneman and Snell explicitly use that term for a person’s forecast of how much a future outcome will be experienced as desirable or undesirable. Predicted utility is conceptually distinct from decision utility, because actual choice can incorporate predicted feeling alongside price, norms, identity, risk, habit, duty, information value and other considerations. [Wiley Online Library+1](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/bdm.3960050304)

This creates a serious error in the article’s claim that selection “runs on decision utility.” Decision utility is normally an **operational description inferred from observed choice**: the option chosen is said to have received greater decision weight. It is not thereby a psychological variable, phenomenal magnitude or neural signal consulted before the choice. Treating it as the causal input risks circularity:

1. an option was selected;
2. therefore it had the greatest decision utility;
3. therefore decision utility explains why it was selected.

The article should instead distinguish at least:

| Quantity | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Instant experienced utility | Hedonic quality experienced at a particular moment |
| Total experienced utility | An aggregation of momentary utilities across an episode |
| Remembered utility | Retrospective evaluation or reconstruction |
| Predicted utility | Forecast of future experienced utility |
| Decision utility | Weight revealed or attributed through actual choice |
| Learned state/action value | Mechanistic estimate associated with states or actions |
| Reward-prediction error | Difference signal used to update learned estimates |
| Incentive salience or “wanting” | Motivational attraction that can diverge from both liking and prediction |
| Present anticipatory affect | A currently felt response generated while imagining a future outcome |

Once these are separated, the article’s headline choice between “anticipated decision utility” and “experienced utility” is revealed to combine distinct dimensions: time, phenomenology, representation, motivation and behavioural measurement.

### 2.3 Experienced utility does not automatically “constitute value” in the required normative sense

The article is entitled to adopt phenomenal-value realism as a Map commitment. It is not entitled to present the experienced-utility literature as independently establishing that total experienced utility constitutes all relevant value.

Kahneman, Wakker and Sarin proposed conditions under which experienced utility might support a normative standard, but that proposal requires substantive evaluative assumptions. Recent analysis argues that moving from hedonic reports to a normative criterion requires an additional value judgment and becomes difficult where outcomes involve agency, meaning, achievement, commitments, effects on other people or values that are not reducible to the temporal sum of pleasure and pain. [Cambridge University Press+1](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/back-to-kahneman/462EF23D9B73A9F3959F0440487F90B4)

This matters because the article currently runs together three claims:

1. experienced valence is phenomenally real;
2. experienced valence constitutes hedonic welfare;
3. experienced valence is the metaphysically authoritative value against which selection should be calibrated.

The first is compatible with the Map’s dualism. The second is a substantive theory of welfare. The third is an even stronger normative and causal claim. Neither the affective-forecasting literature nor the utility taxonomy establishes the progression.

### 2.4 Remembered utility, peak-end effects and duration neglect

The article’s references to the Redelmeier–Kahneman findings are real, and the broad phenomenon is supported beyond the original painful-procedure studies. A meta-analysis found a robust association between retrospective evaluation and peak-end summaries, while duration contributed very little. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749597822000334)

The wording “retrospective judgments track the peak and the end, not the total” is nevertheless too categorical.

A corrigendum to the meta-analysis reported that the overall average of an episode could predict retrospective evaluation at least as well as, and in the corrected estimates better than, the peak-end composite. Duration remained weak, but the evidence does not support treating peak-end integration as the exclusive transformation applied by memory. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597823000547)

More recent experience-sampling work also suggests that peak and end effects depend on how well-being is framed and measured. [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08902070241235969)

The defensible conclusion is that retrospective evaluation is a selective construction influenced by peak, end, average, duration and task framing—not that it universally replaces experience with a fixed peak-end operator.

## 3. Does the proposed disambiguation survive the strongest counterarguments?

Not in its present form.

### 3.1 “Anticipated” and “experienced” are not exclusive kinds of valence

The article reasons that because an outcome has not yet occurred, selection cannot consult experienced valence and must consult anticipated valence. This confuses **what an experience represents** with **whether the representation is itself experienced**.

A person imagining future pain may undergo unpleasant anticipatory affect now. That affect is:

- anticipated with respect to its object;
- presently experienced with respect to its vehicle.

The Map could therefore maintain that selection is influenced by a currently felt phenomenal response to a simulated outcome. It need not choose between a wholly nonphenomenal forecast and the later experience generated after the selected outcome occurs.

This third possibility is especially important for an interactionist theory. It offers a possible route by which consciousness could contribute felt value before selection without requiring backward causation from the eventual experience. Whether that route is correct is empirical and conceptual work still to be done, but its availability defeats the article’s claim that the original question has been closed.

### 3.2 Forecasting bias need not cause selection error

The article treats forecast–experience divergence as evidence that the selection currency is poorly calibrated. That follows only if the divergence changes the comparisons relevant to selection.

Suppose every option’s predicted unpleasantness is exaggerated by 20 per cent. Cardinal predictions are wrong, but the ranking may remain correct. A selector choosing the least unpleasant option could perform perfectly despite systematic magnitude error.

To bear on the selection law, the article needs evidence about:

- rank reversals between predicted and experienced value;
- miscalibration near choice boundaries;
- whether forecast errors differ across competing options;
- whether the errors affect action after controlling for learned value, attention and incentive salience.

The current article relies primarily on absolute forecast–experience gaps and then assumes those gaps compromise selection.

### 3.3 Decision utility cannot serve simultaneously as explanans and behavioural definition

Because decision utility is inferred from choice, it cannot by itself identify the internal selector. A physicalist reinforcement-learning model, a goal-based cognitive model, a phenomenally valenced dualist model and a habit system could generate identical revealed decision utilities.

The article needs to ask which **causal representation** predicts choice over and above the other candidates, not assign the label “decision utility” to whatever wins and then describe that label as the currency.

### 3.4 Many selections do not involve explicit affective forecasting

Habitual action, reflexive avoidance, model-free reinforcement learning, motor competition and well-practised routines can proceed without a reportable forecast of future feeling. Even ordinary deliberation can be guided by rules, identity or commitments that conflict with expected hedonic experience.

A theory that makes predicted phenomenal valence the currency of all selection therefore faces a scope problem. It must specify whether:

- predicted valence is required only for a subset of deliberate choices;
- non-affective mechanisms nominate alternatives while consciousness intervenes only in special cases;
- valence indirectly changes attention or precision rather than directly assigning outcome weights;
- the selector can operate without any explicit prospective representation.

The neighbouring parent article’s attention-mediated middle position is partly an attempt to address this problem, but the focal article largely returns to a direct anticipated-value selector.

### 3.5 Human decision studies do not automatically test quantum selection

The cited empirical literature concerns behavioural choice, learning, reports and neural activity. The Map’s proposed intervention is selection among allegedly quantum-underdetermined neural outcomes. The article acknowledges that no direct neuroscience studies “anticipated phenomenal valence as a selection input,” but it still uses ordinary choice evidence to constrain the quantum selection law.

The missing bridge is substantial. The article needs an argument showing why a systematic relationship at the behavioural level should be attributed to a consciousness-to-physics intervention rather than to ordinary neural prediction, learning and motor-selection processes. Merely labelling both “selection” equivocates between explanatory levels.

## 4. The proposed mechanistic correlate

### 4.1 Reward-prediction error is not predicted utility

The article describes midbrain dopamine reward-prediction error as a quantity that “plausibly biases action selection” and as coding the predictive, economic-value-like component. This is imprecise.

In standard reinforcement-learning terms:

- V(s) or Q(s,a) represents learned state or action value;
- the reward-prediction error δ is the discrepancy used to update those estimates;
- a policy or action-selection process uses the estimates, often with additional motivational and contextual information.

An error signal is therefore not the same thing as the predicted value, and neither is automatically the same thing as predicted hedonic experience. The Map’s own value-blind steelman states the distinction more accurately by treating reward-prediction error as a teaching signal that updates learned values. [ScienceDirect+2The Unfinishable Map+2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211124724011914)

### 4.2 Contemporary dopamine science is more heterogeneous than the article suggests

The familiar scalar reward-prediction-error interpretation remains important, but contemporary work emphasises that dopamine responses can carry feature-specific, distributional, movement-related and other information that is not exhausted by a single integrated value-error scalar. Recent reviews explicitly caution that the original uniform-RPE picture does not explain all phasic dopamine activity. [Uchida Lab+2Nature+2](https://uchidalab.hsites.harvard.edu/publications/explaining-dopamine-through-prediction-errors-and-beyond)

The article need not survey the entire dopamine debate. It should avoid saying, without qualification, that “most midbrain dopamine neurons” provide the sought predictive economic-value signal. At minimum it should distinguish:

- scalar RPE theories;
- heterogeneous or feature-specific error coding;
- learned value representations;
- policy and action-selection mechanisms;
- motivational salience.

### 4.3 Wanting is not equivalent to an affective forecast

The Berridge–Robinson wanting/liking distinction does support a separation between motivational attraction and hedonic impact. It does not map cleanly onto anticipated versus experienced utility.

Incentive salience can become detached not only from pleasure but also from explicit beliefs and learned expectations. A person can strongly “want” something they remember disliking or expect not to enjoy. Thus wanting is not simply a prediction of future liking. [PubMed+1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37543439/)

The focal article also risks equating “liking” with consciously experienced pleasure. Berridge’s terminology distinguishes objective hedonic reactions from subjective conscious pleasure; some “liking” measures need not establish conscious enjoyment. The Map’s dedicated wanting/liking article handles this distinction more carefully than the focal article. [wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork](/topics/wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork/)

Consequently, the cited neuroscience supplies, at most, a **family of mechanism-only rival architectures**:

- learned predictions can differ from outcomes;
- motivation can differ from liking;
- teaching signals can differ from represented values;
- neural value may be distributed rather than held in one currency.

It does not supply a mechanistic correlate of “anticipated phenomenal valence.”

## 5. Episodes that never reach the record

### 5.1 The numerical citations are now substantially accurate

After the August 20 repair, the cited anaesthesia figures have identifiable sources:

- Sanders and colleagues reported connected responsiveness in 12 of 260 patients following intubation, with five signalling pain and none of the postoperatively interviewed patients reporting explicit recall;
- SNAP-1 used a postoperative questionnaire within 24 hours and reported accidental awareness at approximately 1 in 800 general anaesthetics;
- NAP5 found a much lower incidence when relying on spontaneously reported cases. [voids-report-latency-void-2026-08-16](/research/voids-report-latency-void-2026-08-16/)

These studies support a valuable conclusion: **incidence estimates depend greatly on what is measured and how actively it is sought.**

They do not support the article’s stronger causal formulation that the approximately 900-fold difference is “driven by nothing but when and how the question was asked.”

### 5.2 The comparison does not isolate latency

The studies differ in:

- patient populations;
- anaesthetic techniques;
- clinical periods sampled;
- whether the endpoint is command responsiveness, explicit recollection or unsolicited reporting;
- ascertainment intensity;
- inclusion and adjudication criteria;
- opportunities for encoding and consolidation;
- time between event and probe.

The article acknowledges some of these differences immediately after attributing the spread to “nothing but” questioning. The guard therefore contradicts the headline inference rather than merely qualifying it.

Even the within-cohort contrast in Sanders does not show that “latency alone” changed a one-in-twenty-two rate to zero. The immediate endpoint was connected behavioural responsiveness to command. The later endpoint was explicit episodic recall. Those are different capacities. Anaesthetic drugs can disrupt explicit memory encoding or consolidation even where some responsiveness persists. The result therefore establishes a dissociation among connectedness, memory and report—not a controlled effect of elapsed time alone. The Map’s dedicated anaesthesia article makes this decomposition more carefully. [voids-report-latency-void-2026-08-16](/research/voids-report-latency-void-2026-08-16/)

### 5.3 Incidence is sensitive to population and anaesthetic regimen

A 2023 study in young adults found connected consciousness after intubation in 37 of 338 patients, approximately 11 per cent, with incidence differing by sex and anaesthetic management. That does not invalidate Sanders; it shows that the 4.6 per cent figure is not a general constant and that regimen and population can matter substantially. [PubMed+1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35618535/)

A current treatment should include this study, because the focal section uses connected-consciousness incidence to make a general methodological claim.

### 5.4 “No explicit recall” is not equivalent to “no record”

The article divides failures into distorted memory and an absent record. The binary is too simple.

A systematic review found evidence reported as implicit memory in a substantial minority of anaesthesia study cohorts, although the methods and interpretation remain contested. Implicit effects do not prove phenomenal consciousness during anaesthesia, but they show that absence of explicit autobiographical recall is not equivalent to absence of every retained trace. [PubMed+1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34440594/)

At least seven stages should be separated:

1. phenomenal occurrence;
2. sensory or cognitive processing;
3. connected responsiveness;
4. explicit encoding;
5. consolidation;
6. cued or structured recollection;
7. spontaneous report.

Implicit retention adds another possible route. A failure at any later stage cannot by itself locate what happened at the earlier stages.

The article can defensibly say that delayed explicit reports may undercount experiences in states hostile to memory and report. It cannot infer from these studies that latency “deletes” an episode, that a zero report is necessarily an empty training datum, or that the hidden episode had a particular valence.

### 5.5 The pain-memory and mystical-experience comparisons do not supply the missing control

The paediatric pain-memory meta-analysis concerns remembered intensity in children whose experiences were available for ordinary encoding and later report. It does not calibrate memory failure during general anaesthesia. Mild average distortion in that literature cannot establish that the anaesthesia problem concerns pure presence-versus-absence rather than different combinations of encoding, consolidation and retrieval.

Likewise, disclosing that mystical-experience questionnaires were completed hours, weeks or years later is methodologically useful. Delay alone does not quantify the resulting distortion. Psychometric validity, reconstruction, semantic memory and autobiographical interpretation can all coexist. The article turns a disclosure requirement into a general “latency discount” without specifying the discount function or validating it across states.

### 5.6 Forecasts are not trained only on explicit autobiographical reports

The final inference—that anticipated value is trained on a record blind to unreportable episodes—assumes too narrow a learning architecture. Future affective expectations can draw on:

- semantic knowledge;
- other people’s reports;
- implicit learning;
- physiological conditioning;
- generalisation from related experiences;
- model-based inference;
- culturally acquired warnings and expectations.

An absent explicit memory may reduce one information source. It does not make the forecaster wholly blind to the class of outcome.

## 6. Tenet-protective bracketing

The article is unusually candid that the value-blind account receives a parsimony advantage. That is a strength. Several moves nevertheless protect the preferred position without supplying corresponding evidence.

### 6.1 “Calibration failure” is a verdict, not a neutral description

The article says divergence between anticipated and experienced value is a calibration failure rather than evidence that selection is value-blind.

Those alternatives are not mutually exclusive. A mechanism-only theory predicts that choices can be controlled by learned predictors even when conscious feeling is causally idle. Systematic forecast–experience divergence may therefore be evidence favouring that theory over a theory in which felt value directly guides selection. It is not conclusive evidence, but relabelling the divergence “calibration” does not neutralise its likelihood-bearing force.

The appropriate formulation is:

> The divergence is compatible with a miscalibrated value-sensitive selector and with a mechanism-only predictor. Additional evidence is needed to compare those explanations.

### 6.2 Metaphysical authority does not establish causal authority

The article preserves “full causal standing” for experienced valence by saying it constitutes value and serves as the calibration target, even though anticipated valence performs moment-to-moment selection.

But being the standard against which a system is evaluated is not itself a causal role. Temperature can be the target of a thermostat without the future target temperature causing the circuit’s current switching. Similarly, experienced suffering could constitute welfare loss while doing no work in selecting the action that precedes it.

The neighbouring consciousness–value article more carefully distinguishes intrinsic value-grounding from causal or practical tracking. The focal article should preserve that distinction rather than using “constitutes value” to protect the causal standing of experienced valence. [consciousness-value-connection](/concepts/consciousness-value-connection/)

### 6.3 Tenet 5 blocks one argument; it does not support the preferred alternative

The revised article correctly says the minimal-interaction tenet does not make the most minimal model most likely. The limits-on-Occam tenet can likewise establish that parsimony is not decisive.

Neither tenet supplies positive evidence that conscious value participates in selection. At most:

- parsimony does not settle the question;
- the value-sensitive interpretation remains logically available.

Using the tenet to retain a possibility is legitimate. Using it to call that possibility the “defensible move” without further likelihood-bearing evidence is protective bracketing.

### 6.4 Null reports receive asymmetric treatment

The article correctly warns that a null report under anaesthesia is not decisive evidence of absent experience. But the warning is expanded into a general presumption that report channels undercount hidden experience.

That inference is asymmetric. Null reports are discounted as products of inaccessible consciousness, while positive reports are treated as evidence for it. A method that can reinterpret either outcome in favour of experience risks becoming disconfirmation-resistant.

A disciplined version would specify in advance:

- states in which memory impairment independently predicts under-reporting;
- how much under-reporting is expected;
- which physiological or behavioural markers must accompany the hypothesis;
- what evidence would lower confidence that experience occurred.

### 6.5 The proposed experiment is not model-discriminating

The article proposes correcting affective forecasts and asks whether selection changes. It predicts change under an experienced-value-sensitive account but no change if a value-blind predictive channel continues to control behaviour.

That second prediction does not follow. A mechanism-only model can readily predict changed choice when an intervention changes the person’s forecast, representation, attention or learned expectations. Both models can therefore predict the same behavioural result.

The proposal is also weakened empirically by a recent randomised study in which a brief defocusing manipulation did not improve affective-forecasting accuracy. That does not show all debiasing is ineffective, but it makes “defocusing” an unsuitable generic intervention until a manipulation check establishes that it altered the intended representation. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41879054/)

A discriminating test would need orthogonal manipulations of:

- predicted future feeling;
- presently felt anticipatory affect;
- learned action value;
- incentive salience;
- attention or arousal.

It would then require preregistered predictions from at least:

1. a value-blind reinforcement-learning model;
2. a current-phenomenal-valence model;
3. an anticipated-phenomenal-simulation model;
4. the parent article’s valence-to-attention hybrid.

Even that would test behavioural and neural selection. A further bridge would still be required before interpreting the result as evidence of quantum-outcome selection.

## 7. Integration with neighbouring Map articles

The article is well linked in a navigational sense. Its integration is weaker at the level of claims.

### Parent article: *Valence and the Mechanism of Conscious Selection*

The focal article says it “closes” the anticipated-versus-experienced question. The parent article still presents that question as open and describes a graduated position—valence modulates attention and attention performs the selection—as the most defensible current stance. [valence-and-conscious-selection](/topics/valence-and-conscious-selection/)

This is not a minor editorial lag. The two articles allocate causal work differently:

- focal article: anticipated valence is directly consulted by selection;
- parent article: valence may influence the attentional variable that performs selection;
- value-blind horn: mechanistic valuation controls selection while feeling may be downstream.

The focal article should be presented as one candidate resolution within this three-way architecture, not as closure.

### *Wanting, Liking, and the Value-in-Mechanism Fork*

The neighbouring article correctly notes that wanting is separable from both pleasure and reward-prediction learning, and that objective “liking” need not equal conscious pleasure. The focal article collapses these distinctions into a simpler forecast/experience parallel. [wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork](/topics/wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork/)

The two articles need a common terminology table. At present, a reader can receive incompatible mappings of wanting, prediction and phenomenal enjoyment depending on which page is read first.

### *The Steelman for Value-Blind Selection*

The steelman treats reward-prediction error as a teaching signal and learned values as the variables that influence later choice. That is more mechanistically accurate than the focal article’s treatment of reward-prediction error as the predictive currency itself. [the-steelman-for-value-blind-selection](/topics/the-steelman-for-value-blind-selection/)

The focal article should defer to the steelman’s decomposition and identify exactly which mechanistic quantity its phenomenal proposal supplements or replaces.

### *Consciousness–Value Connection*

That article supports the claim that felt badness may intrinsically constitute a form of badness. It does not establish that the same felt property directly selects antecedent physical outcomes. The focal article should explicitly label the missing bridge from **value constitution** to **causal selection**. [consciousness-value-connection](/concepts/consciousness-value-connection/)

### Anaesthesia articles

The dedicated anaesthesia material separates connectedness, phenomenal presence, memory and report. The focal article compresses these into a latency gradient and thereby loses the distinctions the neighbouring material already provides. [anaesthesia-and-the-consciousness-interface](/topics/anaesthesia-and-the-consciousness-interface/)

The focal article should state the philosophical consequence and refer readers to the anaesthesia article for the empirical decomposition, rather than maintaining a weaker parallel analysis.

### Negative-valence asymmetry and marginal-organism scope

If selection requires predicted utility, the negative-asymmetry article must specify whether its negative weighting applies to:

- predicted experienced utility;
- current anticipatory feeling;
- learned action value;
- incentive salience;
- the selection policy itself.

Likewise, requiring prospective affective representation changes the lower boundary for value-sensitive selection. Organisms capable of nociception and learning but not counterfactual affective forecasting may fall outside the theory. That consequence should be propagated into the marginal-organism article rather than left as a one-way backlink.

## Overall conclusion

The article’s central question is stronger than its answer.

It successfully shows that “felt value guides selection” is radically under-specified. It also correctly concedes that a mechanism-only explanation receives genuine support from dissociations among motivation, prediction, memory and experience.

But the proposed resolution succeeds mainly by redefining the roles:

- experienced valence is declared authoritative about value;
- predicted valence is assigned the causal selection role;
- divergence is classified as calibration error;
- the value-blind interpretation is retained as a rival but denied closure through the Map’s prior commitments.

That is a coherent **conditional model**. It is not yet an evidentially supported account of what performs selection, much less what selects quantum-underdetermined neural outcomes. The article should be revised from “the disambiguation the Map must make” to “a provisional layered model and the evidence needed to distinguish it from its rivals.”

## Concrete potential improvements

### Specific article revisions

1. **Rename “Three Utilities, Three Jobs” in the focal article.** Use “Utility and valuation layers,” and state that Kahneman, Wakker and Sarin distinguish two core meanings—decision and experienced utility—while remembered utility is a retrospective evaluation of experience.
2. **Add predicted utility explicitly.** Cite the Kahneman–Snell distinction and reserve “predicted utility” for the forecast of later experience.
3. **Stop calling decision utility the anticipated causal currency.** Define it as a weight inferred from choice unless an independent psychological or neural measure is supplied.
4. **Separate instant from total experienced utility.** The feeling occurring now, the temporal aggregation of an episode and the retrospective judgment of that episode are not interchangeable.
5. **Add present anticipatory affect as a fourth live candidate.** Explain that a future outcome can be represented through a currently experienced positive or negative state.
6. **Replace the binary conclusion with a candidate set:** current anticipatory affect, predicted utility, learned Q/V, incentive salience, attention-mediated valence and combinations of these.
7. **Revise “structured, well-replicated bias” to a calibrated statement.** Incorporate recent evidence that forecasting error varies by context, measure and forecast target, and that everyday relative accuracy can be high.
8. **Add an ordinal-versus-cardinal accuracy subsection.** State the conditions under which a magnitude bias would actually reverse selection.
9. **Update the peak-end discussion.** Cite the meta-analysis corrigendum and acknowledge that overall average experience can predict retrospective judgments as well as or better than a peak-end composite.
10. **Qualify the normative use of experienced utility.** Distinguish phenomenal badness, hedonic welfare and all-things-considered value; address agency, meaning, commitments and effects on others.

14. **Correct the wanting/liking mapping.** State that wanting can diverge from both pleasure and predicted pleasure, and that objective liking reactions do not automatically establish conscious enjoyment.
15. **Remove “driven by nothing but when and how the question was asked.”** Replace it with a statement that ascertainment method, endpoint, population and anaesthetic conditions jointly produce large incidence differences.
16. **Remove “latency alone took a rate … to zero.”** Describe the Sanders result as a dissociation between connected responsiveness and later explicit recall.
17. **Add the 2023 young-adult connected-consciousness study.** Use it to demonstrate population and regimen dependence.

19. **Do not use paediatric pain-memory accuracy as a control for anaesthetic amnesia.** Present it only as evidence about one different kind of retrospective reconstruction.
20. **Narrow the mystical-experience inference.** Require disclosure of report delay, but do not infer a quantified latency discount without validation.

31. **Revise the marginal-organism-scope article.** Examine how much prospective representation the predicted-utility model requires and whether that raises the proposed boundary for value-sensitive selection.

### Site-methodology revisions

32. **Expand citation audits from metadata to entailment.** A verified DOI and an exact quotation do not establish that the source supports the article’s conceptual use of a term.
33. **Audit taxonomies against primary definitions.** Where an argument depends on distinctions such as utility, awareness, report or liking, create a source-derived terminology ledger before drafting.
34. **Replace “find a superseding challenge” currency checks with synthesis checks.** Current literature often modifies boundary conditions without producing one paper that overturns an older paradigm.
35. **Time-limit stability notes.** “Do not reflag” instructions should expire when a relevant neighbouring article changes, a new review appears or the claim is used in a new inferential role.
36. **Do not let caveats immunise claims.** A claim labelled “interpretive” or “speculative” must still be checked for category mistakes and misleading analogies.
37. **Maintain a corpus-wide claim dependency register.** When one article says a question is closed, parent pages and downstream articles should automatically be flagged for status reconciliation.
38. **Separate evidence labels from tenet-coherence labels.** “Compatible with the tenets” should never raise an empirical claim’s evidential status.
39. **Require rival-model prediction tables for proposed tests.** Each experiment should state what every serious rival predicts and identify outcomes with differing likelihoods.
40. **Add a bridge audit for every move from behavioural evidence to quantum selection.** Require the article to identify the intermediate assumptions and possible conventional neural explanations.
41. **Standardise cross-study comparison tables.** Record population, intervention, endpoint, probe timing, case definition and ascertainment method before computing or rhetorically presenting fold differences.
42. **Distinguish null-report disciplines by state.** Anaesthesia, sleep, psychedelia, infant experience and ordinary delayed recall should not inherit one generic latency rule.
43. **Record both positive and negative evidence requirements.** Every hidden-experience hypothesis should specify what observations would lower, not merely preserve, confidence in the experience.
44. **Audit normative bridges separately from empirical ones.** Claims that phenomenology constitutes welfare, that welfare is all-things-considered value and that value causally selects outcomes should receive three separate arguments.
45. **Version terminology across the valence cluster.** A change to “predicted utility,” “decision utility,” “wanting,” “liking” or “reward-prediction error” should trigger review of every article using the affected term.
