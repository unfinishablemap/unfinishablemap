## Overall assessment

The article is a clear and unusually candid statement of how The Unfinishable Map wants evolutionary considerations to support interactionist dualism. The 9 November revision improved it: the page now recognises that a mental influence must be selectable, acknowledges that strict Born-rule preservation may make such selectability impossible, and labels rejection of Many-Worlds as a Map posit rather than an evolutionary result. The bibliography is not obviously fabricated, but it is severely out of date for an article revised in late 2026. The newest external item listed is from 2016, notwithstanding a substantial 2025 literature on the evolution and functions of consciousness and relevant 2026 work on biological agency and teleology. ## 1. The adaptation argument uses an insufficient criterion

### Finding: **High severity; confirmed conceptual error**

The article argues that if conscious influence changes behaviour in ways that alter reproductive success, then consciousness is “genuinely adaptive.” It also introduces a useful selectability proviso: the influence must vary heritably, or at least be stably associated with heritable machinery, before natural selection can act on it. That proviso is necessary, but it is not sufficient. [consciousness-evolution-and-biology](/topics/consciousness-evolution-and-biology/)

At least four distinctions are needed:

- A feature may make a **current causal contribution** to fitness without having been selected for.
- A feature may be an **exaptation**, becoming useful after arising for another reason.
- It may be inseparable from a larger selected complex without itself being the selected trait.
- It may be a by-product correlated with the actual causal target of selection.

Recent work on evolutionary approaches to consciousness stresses precisely this trait-individuation problem: before asking whether consciousness was selected, one must distinguish consciousness proper from attention, learning, reportability, flexible behaviour, affective control and other associated capacities. Merely showing that a conscious organism performs better does not establish that phenomenality, rather than one of its physical or cognitive correlates, was the selected feature. [PubMed Central (PMC)+2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12612694/)

The article’s claim should therefore be weakened from:

> a fitness-shifting phenomenal contribution is genuinely adaptive

to something like:

> a reproducible phenomenal contribution to fitness would make consciousness a candidate target or component of selection, provided that the phenomenal contribution can be individuated from correlated mechanisms and shown to have the relevant history of selection.

This matters particularly for dualism. If a neural architecture and a phenomenal influence always occur together, selection can operate on the package without discriminating between:

- selection for the physical architecture;
- selection for the psychophysical package;
- selection for the phenomenal contribution specifically.

The article currently treats the first demonstration—some fitness relevance—as if it settled the third.

### Gould and Lewontin are also overstated

The statement that Gould and Lewontin “showed” how traits can arise as spandrels overstates what their paper established. Their 1979 article was a critique of overzealous adaptationism and an argument for taking structural constraints and by-products seriously; it did not provide a general test demonstrating that any particular trait is a spandrel. Moreover, “spandrel” is not simply a synonym for any causally inert accompaniment. It originally denotes a feature arising as a structural consequence of other selected arrangements. [PubMed+2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42062/)

A directly relevant paper, *Is Consciousness a Spandrel?*, is absent from the bibliography. Its omission is conspicuous given the article’s framing. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-american-philosophical-association/article/abs/is-consciousness-a-spandrel/30243610CC398D3527500FFE5B8AADB9)

## 2. The burden is not uniquely—or even principally—on materialism

### Finding: **High severity; overstatement**

The zombie passage says that if every behavioural and cognitive function could be implemented without consciousness, “the weight falls on materialism.” That conclusion follows only if one has already accepted a substantial part of the anti-physicalist argument.

Physicalists have several replies:

1. **Identity response:** the conscious state is the relevant physical or functional state, not an additional property whose independent contribution must be isolated.
2. **Constitutive response:** phenomenality is constituted by the physical process, so selecting the process is selecting the conscious state under another description.
3. **Type-A or analytic-functionalist response:** a perfect functional duplicate without consciousness is not genuinely conceivable or metaphysically possible.
4. **Phenomenal-concept response:** an explanatory or epistemic gap need not imply a distinct causally idle property.
5. **Access-theoretic response:** evolutionary theory explains conscious access, flexible availability or metacognitive control while leaving the further metaphysical issue open.

Chalmers’s zombie argument is influential, but the possibility of zombies is precisely what many physicalists deny. It cannot be used as a neutral evolutionary premise and then counted as evolutionary evidence against physicalism. [Consciousness Network+2](https://consc.net/papers/pceg.html)

The article is right that physicalism still faces the hard problem in its familiar form. But that is not the same as showing that physicalism faces an additional evolutionary anomaly. Evolution can explain why a neural or functional organisation proliferated even when philosophers disagree over whether its phenomenal character is identical with, constituted by, realised by or merely correlated with that organisation.

The Map’s own article **“The Evolutionary Case for Mental Causation”** is better calibrated here. It says explicitly that the evolutionary argument is compatible with physicalism as well as dualism and that further arguments are required to establish nonphysical causation. It also preserves the possibility that the physical and phenomenal features were co-optimised or selected as an inseparable package. The target article should inherit those concessions rather than say that the evolutionary weight falls mainly on materialism. [evolutionary-case-for-mental-causation](/topics/evolutionary-case-for-mental-causation/)

## 3. Interactionism permits selection, but does not explain adaptive alignment

### Finding: **High severity; major missing counterargument**

Interactionism can make phenomenal differences causally selectable in a way epiphenomenalism cannot. That is a genuine comparative advantage over **strict epiphenomenalism**. The article, however, often presents it as if this solved the evolutionary problem more generally.

It does not explain why:

- tissue damage feels bad rather than good;
- nutritionally beneficial states often acquire positive valence;
- perceptual similarity structures correspond as well as they do to behaviourally relevant environmental structure;
- pleasure and suffering have the motivational directions they do.

Recent analysis argues that physicalism, dualism and panpsychism all face versions of this adaptive-alignment problem. Interactionist dualism permits consciousness to influence selection, but the bare doctrine predicts no particular psychophysical mapping. A world in which injury produced pleasure and nourishment produced agony appears equally compatible with the mere proposition that mental states can influence neural outcomes. [Springer Link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11097-024-09978-7)

The missing explanatory component is therefore not just **mental causation**, but a theory of:

- psychophysical laws;
- valence-to-action mappings;
- how those mappings vary;
- how such variation is inherited or developmentally stabilised;
- why selection acts on one mapping rather than another.

The article presently moves too quickly from “consciousness can affect behaviour” to “evolution can align consciousness with fitness.” Its own linked material on mental causation and psychophysical laws recognises that co-optimisation remains a live alternative and that the amplification and Born-rule mechanisms are unspecified. [evolutionary-case-for-mental-causation](/topics/evolutionary-case-for-mental-causation/)

This is arguably the article’s most important omitted objection.

## 4. The quantum-invisibility claim is internally unstable

### Finding: **High severity; confirmed tension**

The article says that a minimal quantum bias might not be visible to “standard biology,” while also arguing that such bias can change behavioural and reproductive frequencies enough for selection to act on it. These claims need to be separated.

Evolutionary biology is largely agnostic about the microscopic origin of heritable fitness differences. If a mental influence reliably:

- changes behavioural choices;
- changes survival or reproduction;
- produces population-level frequency differences;

then those downstream effects are in principle accessible to ordinary behavioural ecology, quantitative genetics and population biology even if the underlying microphysical mechanism remains unknown.

What biology might fail to identify is the **nonphysical origin** of the effect, not the fitness effect itself.

Conversely, if the proposed mental intervention preserves all empirically accessible outcome distributions—one strict interpretation of Born-rule preservation—then it supplies no stable frequency difference on which selection can operate. The article now acknowledges this problem, but it does not draw its full consequence: selectability and perfect statistical invisibility cannot both do the required work. [consciousness-evolution-and-biology](/topics/consciousness-evolution-and-biology/)

The appropriate distinction is:

| Claim | Plausibility |
| --- | --- |
| Biology could detect a fitness effect without identifying its quantum mechanism | Yes |
| Biology could identify a nonphysical cause merely from a fitness effect | No |
| A perfectly distribution-preserving intervention could generate a selectable frequency difference | Apparently no |
| A small but non-zero bias could initially escape detection | Yes, but only pending sufficient data |
| A persistent fitness-relevant bias could remain permanently invisible in principle | Not without additional assumptions |

The linked **“Evolutionary Case for Quantum Neural Effects”** is substantially more cautious. It describes quantum biology as establishing non-foreclosure or possibility, not positive probability; it acknowledges that decoherence, amplification and quantitative scaling remain unresolved; and it notes the lack of direct evidence that the proposed mechanisms operate in neural decision-making. The target article’s compressed reference to quantum-biological precedents conveys more evidential support than the sister article permits. [evolutionary-case-for-quantum-neural-effects](/topics/evolutionary-case-for-quantum-neural-effects/)

The external evidence also requires caution. Radical-pair magnetoreception remains an active and plausible research programme, but recent work continues to debate whether realistic noise levels and downstream transduction permit the required sensitivity. Enzyme-tunnelling claims are likewise not an uncontested demonstration that evolution routinely selects behaviourally amplified quantum choices. [PubMed+3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39110232/)

## 5. The history and philosophy of teleology are misrepresented

### Finding: **High severity for historical claim; medium severity for overall argument**

The statement that Darwin “officially banished” teleology is historically too crude. Darwin rejected fixed, externally imposed purposes and species essences, but natural selection also supplied a naturalistic way to explain apparent purposiveness. Darwin himself welcomed Asa Gray’s suggestion that his work had brought teleology back into natural history in a transformed form. [darwin-online.org.uk](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/pdf/1902_Autobiography_F1473.pdf)

The article also identifies teleonomy too closely with shorthand for selection history. That is one influential use, but not the whole field. Biological teleology is now discussed through several partially competing frameworks:

- selected-effect accounts;
- causal-role and contribution-to-capacity accounts;
- organisational accounts based on closure and self-maintenance;
- autonomy and agency accounts;
- goal-directed control and representation;
- evolutionary accounts of internally generated goals.

The organisational tradition associated with Moreno, Mossio, Bich and others explicitly argues that biological organisation supports an intrinsic form of teleology grounded in self-maintaining closure, not merely retrospective selection history. This is especially relevant because the article cites Mossio and Moreno while omitting the implications of their framework. [DOI+1](https://doi.org/10.1093/BJPS/AXP036)

Nor is it current to say that philosophy of biology lacks a framework for genuine purpose. A 2026 *BioScience* analysis distinguishes stronger and weaker forms of agency and goal-directedness and evaluates when purposive explanations are scientifically legitimate. Jablonka and Ginsburg have proposed “mental selection” as a category involving consciously represented goals. These approaches may not establish irreducible phenomenal purpose, but they directly contradict the claim that the relevant conceptual space is simply absent. [PubMed Central (PMC)+2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13486002/)

The defensible Map claim is narrower:

> Existing naturalistic theories of biological goal-directedness do not by themselves establish that phenomenal purpose is irreducible or causally efficacious.

That is quite different from claiming that biology recognises only unconscious teleonomy or lacks conceptual resources for organism-generated goals.

## 6. The theory-of-function taxonomy is incomplete

### Finding: **Medium-to-high severity**

The article contrasts etiological accounts of function, associated with Wright, Millikan, Neander and Godfrey-Smith, with Cummins-style causal-role accounts. It then says neither dominant approach handles consciousness.

There are three problems.

### 6.1 Two traditions are presented as effectively exhaustive

Modern philosophy of biology contains organisational, systems, propensity, contribution-to-fitness and pluralist approaches in addition to the selected-effect/causal-role contrast. Many philosophers treat different notions of function as serving different explanatory tasks rather than expecting one analysis to monopolise all biological function-talk. [DOI+1](https://doi.org/10.1093/BJPS/AXP036)

### 6.2 The criticism is partly question-begging

An etiological theorist can say that the function of a conscious neural organisation is whatever effect explains its selection. A physicalist can identify consciousness with that organisation. The theory therefore “handles consciousness” conditional on the physicalist identity claim. It does not independently solve the hard problem, but theories of biological function are not generally intended to settle constitutive metaphysics.

Likewise, a Cummins theorist can analyse what conscious access contributes to reasoning, learning or behavioural control without purporting to explain why those processes possess phenomenal character.

The article criticises theories of function for failing to answer a question outside their ordinary explanatory remit, then treats that limitation as evidence for the Map’s metaphysics.

### 6.3 Citation completeness is deficient

Millikan and Neander are named in the body but are absent from the reference list. Nagel appears in the bibliography without an evident body-level use. Even where the references exist, the article needs page-specific or work-specific support for its taxonomy rather than a general cluster of canonical names. [consciousness-evolution-and-biology](/topics/consciousness-evolution-and-biology/)

## 7. The organism and strong-emergence section makes unsupported leaps

### Finding: **Medium severity**

The article is entitled to observe that biological individuality and phenomenal unity are different explanatory targets. Autopoiesis, organisational closure and evolutionary individuality do not automatically explain why there is a unified subject of experience.

It goes further, however, suggesting that:

- none of these biological approaches adequately addresses organismal unity in relation to consciousness;
- strong emergence in life and consciousness may indicate a shared explanation;
- the organism concept is incomplete without consciousness.

These claims need significant qualification.

First, enactive and organisational approaches have explicitly attempted to connect self-maintaining organismal unity, sense-making, agency and mind. One may reject their proposed connection, but it is inaccurate to imply that they have simply left it unaddressed. [enactivism-challenge-to-interactionist-dualism](/topics/enactivism-challenge-to-interactionist-dualism/)

Second, a similarity in explanatory form—two phenomena being described as emergent—does not by itself support a common mechanism or ontology. “Strong emergence” can mark the absence of an accepted reduction rather than supply positive evidence for a shared source.

Third, saying that the organism concept is incomplete without consciousness risks excluding apparently nonconscious organisms from full organismhood. The article may instead mean that a complete theory of **conscious organisms** must explain the relationship between biological individuality and the phenomenal subject. That narrower proposition is much more defensible.

## 8. Several rival positions are presented in weakened forms

### 8.1 Integrated Information Theory

The article groups information integration with accounts that offer “only functional explanation.” IIT’s own claim is not merely that integrated information performs a useful function. IIT 4.0 starts from proposed axioms about experience and identifies consciousness with an intrinsic cause–effect structure. That identification may be false, empirically unsuccessful or metaphysically inadequate, but it is not simply a causal-role analysis. [DOI+1](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011465)

The stronger objection is:

> IIT asserts a psychophysical identity between phenomenal structure and intrinsic causal structure, but critics may challenge the axioms, the identity step, the measure, counterintuitive attributions and empirical predictions.

The current article instead dismisses a simpler position than IIT actually advances.

### 8.2 Global Neuronal Workspace Theory

GNWT principally models conscious access: widespread availability, ignition, report and flexible use. Its proponents need not claim that global broadcasting by itself answers every question about phenomenal existence. Treating its failure to settle the hard problem as a straightforward empirical failure misstates its intended explanatory level. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8770991/)

The 2025 COGITATE adversarial collaboration directly tested contrasting predictions of GNWT and IIT. That prominent empirical development should be included in any current discussion of whether consciousness theories provide experimentally discriminating explanations. [Nature+1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08888-1)

### 8.3 Higher-order theories

Higher-order theories are not merely verbal redescriptions of consciousness as representation. They generate empirical hypotheses about the role of metarepresentation, prefrontal processing, confidence, awareness without first-order discrimination and related dissociations. Their metaphysical adequacy remains disputed, but the article should engage the strongest empirical and representational versions rather than listing “higher-order representation” as another unexplained function. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21737339/)

### 8.4 Enactivism

The statement that deep-continuity approaches treat autopoiesis as entailing proto-mentality is too broad. Some enactivists endorse strong life–mind continuity, but others explicitly distinguish basic sense-making from sentience and deny that minimal autopoiesis alone warrants phenomenal attribution. Thompson’s position is richer and more qualified than “life therefore experience.” [PhilPapers+2](https://philpapers.org/rec/THOPOM-8)

The Map’s own enactivism article recognises that these views challenge the separability of life and mind. The target page should distinguish:

- organisational continuity;
- agency or sense-making;
- minimal cognition;
- phenomenal sentience.

Those are not interchangeable.

### 8.5 Russellian monism

The article characterises Russellian monism as holding that the intrinsic nature of matter is experiential. This describes panpsychist Russellian monism but excludes panprotopsychist and neutral variants, in which the intrinsic properties are not themselves experiences. Chalmers explicitly distinguishes these forms. [amherstlecture.org+2](https://www.amherstlecture.org/chalmers2013/chalmers2013_ALP.pdf)

The accurate description is that Russellian monism locates the categorical or intrinsic basis of physical structure in properties capable of grounding consciousness; only some versions identify those properties as experiential.

### 8.6 Illusionism

The article asks why evolution would produce systematic misrepresentation of phenomenality and treats the resulting burden as destabilising illusionism. That is a fair challenge, but it is not unanswered. Illusionists and related theorists have offered evolutionary and metacognitive accounts under which introspective systems represent themselves in simplified, non-relational or apparently intrinsic terms. The phenomenal “illusion” may be a by-product of useful self-modelling, or the representation itself may have cognitive functions. [DOI+2](https://doi.org/10.1515/humaff-2022-0023)

Chalmers’s meta-problem programme also makes the evolutionary explanation of phenomenal judgements a central research target. Importantly, the meta-problem does not disappear under interactionism: an interactionist still owes an account of why particular experiences produce the concepts, reports and theoretical intuitions they do. [Consciousness Network+1](https://consc.net/papers/metaproblem.pdf)

The article should therefore say that illusionism faces a demanding empirical explanatory programme, not that the mere existence of the question renders the view unstable.

## 9. Dualism versus gradualism is a false contrast

### Finding: **Medium severity; contradicted by neighbouring Map pages**

The natural-kinds section associates dualism with a real discontinuity and says materialists tend towards gradualism. Metaphysical position does not determine onset topology so neatly.

A physicalist can hold that consciousness appears once a system crosses a sharp organisational or computational threshold. IIT and some global-workspace interpretations provide obvious examples of physicalist theories with threshold-like consequences. A dualist can hold that psychophysical coupling strength, phenomenal richness or the probability of instantiation changes gradually. [evolution-of-consciousness](/concepts/evolution-of-consciousness/)

The Map’s own **“Consciousness as a Natural Kind”** article handles this more carefully. It distinguishes:

- whether consciousness is present at all;
- whether its contents or intensity are graded;
- whether a sharp metaphysical boundary is epistemically discoverable;
- whether a proposed coupling threshold merely relocates a sorites problem.

It also labels the sharp dualist boundary as a coherent possibility, not an evolutionary result. [is-conscious-being-a-natural-kind](/concepts/is-conscious-being-a-natural-kind/)

The target article should follow that formulation. Dualism may permit a sharp boundary, but does not establish one; physicalism may permit continuity, but does not require it.

## 10. The origin of consciousness is conflated with its evolutionary distribution

### Finding: **Medium severity**

The article argues that if consciousness is nonreducible, its origin lies outside natural selection. This is defensible only after separating several questions:

1. Why does phenomenal reality exist at all?
2. Why do particular psychophysical laws hold?
3. Why did consciousness first become instantiated in terrestrial organisms?
4. Why did particular conscious capacities spread?
5. Why are particular phenomenal qualities correlated with particular biological functions?

Natural selection would not explain why an irreducible phenomenal ontology or fundamental psychophysical law exists. But, conditional on such an ontology and laws, selection could still explain why biological structures capable of instantiating or coupling to consciousness became common.

Calling all of this “the origin of consciousness” obscures the division between a metaphysical ground and a historical evolutionary appearance. The linked **“Evolution Under Dualism”** article is more precise in saying that ordinary evolutionary mechanisms continue to operate on organisms and interfaces under dualism. [evolution-under-dualism](/topics/evolution-under-dualism/)

## 11. The proposed falsifiers are not sufficiently discriminating

### Finding: **Medium-to-high severity**

The article offers two broad defeaters:

- a wholly classical account of neural amplification;
- null results for relevant quantum effects.

Neither is presently a clean falsifier.

A complete classical account of behavioural production would strongly reduce motivation for a quantum interface, but would not logically exclude an additional mental cause unless causal closure, exclusion or the absence of systematic overdetermination were independently established.

Conversely, a null result for one candidate quantum process would not falsify interactionism unless the Map had committed in advance to:

- a particular physical locus;
- a quantitative expected effect;
- an admissible confidence interval;
- a defined class of alternatives;
- conditions under which relocation to another mechanism would be disallowed.

Without these commitments, negative evidence can always be answered by moving the interface elsewhere.

A stronger test architecture would identify:

1. a physical variable whose distribution differs under the interactionist and closed-physical models;
2. the predicted sign and approximate magnitude of the difference;
3. how it amplifies into behaviour;
4. which auxiliary assumptions are required;
5. which result would reduce confidence in the interactionist hypothesis.

The current article provides research directions, not yet genuine discriminating tests.

## Integration with surrounding Map articles

The article is richly linked, but the links sometimes conceal rather than resolve inconsistent commitments.

### “The Evolutionary Case for Mental Causation”

This page is more epistemically disciplined than the target article. It says:

- the by-product reply remains live;
- evolutionary reasoning does not establish nonphysicality;
- physicalism can accept causal consciousness;
- co-optimisation remains an alternative;
- quantum mechanism debt remains unpaid.

The target should import those qualifications. At present, readers following the links encounter a cautious sister article underneath a stronger headline-level argument. [evolutionary-case-for-mental-causation](/topics/evolutionary-case-for-mental-causation/)

### “Evolution Under Dualism”

That article says dualism requires no revision to evolutionary biology and that mutation, inheritance, selection and drift operate normally. The target says standard adaptationist frameworks cannot trace “non-physical selection.” Both can be reconciled, but only by distinguishing:

- the sufficiency of evolutionary mathematics to track phenotypic fitness;
- the metaphysical interpretation of a causal contributor;
- the ability to identify that contributor experimentally.

The current pages do not make that reconciliation explicit. [evolution-under-dualism](/topics/evolution-under-dualism/)

## Tenet-protective bracketing

The article has become more transparent about which propositions are Map commitments. In particular, marking rejection of Many-Worlds as a posit is good practice. ## Final verdict

The article succeeds as a **programmatic dualist essay**: it identifies genuine tensions for epiphenomenalism, asks useful questions about selectability and phenomenal purpose, and now discloses several important assumptions.

It does not yet succeed as an **independent analysis of consciousness and evolution**. Its strongest conclusions depend on:

- an insufficient definition of adaptation;
- a controversial zombie premise;
- conflation of physicalism with epiphenomenalism;
- an unfilled adaptive-alignment gap;
- an unstable combination of selectability and empirical invisibility;
- outdated accounts of teleology, function and competing consciousness theories.

The article should be classified as **requiring substantial revision**, not merely reference refreshing.
