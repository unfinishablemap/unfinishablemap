## Executive Summary

`phenomenal-normativity-environmental-ethics.md` is, on the evidential-status axis, one of the best-behaved applied articles in the corpus: it holds a four-tier graduated confidence framework, keeps precaution in the *policy* register rather than letting it upgrade evidential status, and refuses to inflate a tenet's relevance when that tenet does not bear on the question. Two problems sit on top of that good prose, and neither is a prose problem. First, a **nav-surface defect**: a single link *label* at L71 calls the animal-consciousness literature "scientific consensus" while the article it links to says verbatim that it is not. Second, an **integration gap**: the article links out to 25 distinct targets and receives inbound links from only two live articles, neither of them a cluster owner, and no apex reaches it at all.

## Praise from Sympathetic Philosophers

### The Property Dualist (Chalmers)
The article does not attempt to derive moral status from function. L125: *"If consciousness is irreducible, the moral status of conscious animals is not contingent on our theoretical frameworks—they genuinely experience suffering and flourishing regardless of how we explain it."* The contrast drawn is with functionalist approaches "where moral status depends on debatable criteria for consciousness" — which is the right diagnosis of why the moral-status literature keeps stalling.

### The Quantum Mind Theorist (Stapp)
Little for Stapp here, and the article says so itself rather than manufacturing relevance — see the McGinn/Birch notes below on L127.

### The Phenomenologist (Nagel)
L107 grounds moral epistemology in first-person encounter rather than theory: *"We do not infer that the animal's suffering matters through ethical theory; we perceive its mattering directly."* The move to treat moral perception as *evidence* rather than as a psychological artefact to be explained away is exactly Nagel's methodological complaint against objectivist ethics, applied cleanly.

### The Process Philosopher (Whitehead)
The article is *not* Whiteheadian and does not pretend to be — it puts plants and ecosystems at near-zero. But its reason for doing so is the honest kind: **a mechanistic reason, not a metaphysical prohibition.** L103: *"For plants and ecosystems as wholes, near-zero—no known mechanism for unified [phenomenal experience](/concepts/evaluative-phenomenal-character/)."* A process philosopher can disagree with that verdict on the evidence while conceding the argument is well-formed; what they cannot say is that the article has ruled experience out by fiat. That is the correct shape for a disagreement the Map has not settled.

### The Libertarian Free Will Defender (Kane)
L119 makes agency do real ethical work rather than decorating the conclusion: non-intervention in wild animal suffering is permitted only when it is *chosen* — *"genuine [moral agency](/topics/free-will/) requires choosing non-intervention through deliberation rather than defaulting to it through inattention."* This converts a familiar quietist dodge into a positive obligation to deliberate. Strong, and underexploited elsewhere in the corpus.

### The Mysterian (McGinn)
The whole "Moral Humility and the Boundaries of Consciousness" section is built on cognitive limitation as a *premise*, not a lament: *"We cannot be certain where consciousness ends."* The article then does the mysterian's least common move — it derives a practical policy from the limitation instead of stopping at the limitation.

### The Hardline Empiricist (Birch)
**Load-bearing for this review, and it splits into three praises and one flag.**

*Praise 1 — precaution kept in the practical register.* L105: *"This uncertainty creates a practical asymmetry. The cost of treating a conscious being as if it lacks consciousness (ignoring genuine suffering) is far greater than the cost of treating a non-conscious entity as if it might be conscious (unnecessary caution)."* This is precaution as a *discipline for acting under uncertainty*, and it conspicuously does not conclude that insects are therefore probably conscious. Registers stay separate.

*Praise 2 — the graduated framework is an instrument, not a gesture.* L103 gives four tiers with a distinct reason for each: cortical/thalamic homology for mammals and birds; nociceptors plus aversive learning plus behavioural flexibility for fish and cephalopods; behavioural complexity exceeding reflexive prediction for insects; absence of a known mechanism for plants and ecosystems. Each tier is defeasible by a different kind of finding, which is what makes it a calibration instrument.

*Praise 3 — a tenet declining work it cannot do.* L127, inside "Relation to Site Perspective", concedes: *"For environmental ethics, this tenet is less directly relevant than the others—it constrains the *mechanism* of interaction rather than its *moral significance*."* The tenet sections in this corpus are structurally under pressure to make every tenet load-bearing everywhere. This one refuses. That is the tenet-coherent-not-evidence-elevating pattern applied to the Map's own scaffolding.

*Flag — one register collapse, and it is in nav text only.* See the calibration concern below. Birch would object precisely to relabelling a "realistic possibility" declaration as "consensus", and that is what the L71 link label does.

## Content Strengths

### `obsidian/topics/phenomenal-normativity-environmental-ethics.md`

- **Strongest point**: The dilemma constructed against Holmes Rolston III at L57. Rolston is introduced as *"ecocentrism's most rigorous defence"* and then given a genuine fork — adaptive value is either descriptive (and so not normative without an experiencer) or genuinely normative (and so needs experiential grounding). Opponent parity is real here, not performed.
- **Concession against interest**: L63 declines the easy universalist win: *"Some indigenous ontologies genuinely attribute agency and personhood to rivers and mountains in ways that resist this reinterpretation—the Map acknowledges this tension rather than claiming universal compatibility."*
- **Citation-framing accuracy passes**: L63 closes *"The Map reads such convergence as near-independence rather than a tally of fully independent confirmations (see [P-D3](/positions/arguments-for-dualism/))."* P-D3 does in fact deny full independence (it names the shared Indian milieu and Indo-Greek exchange channels, and declines the Neo-Confucian lineage). The position is cited for a verdict it actually reached.
- **Why it works**: the article's thesis is congenial to putting plants and ecosystems at near-zero, so the interesting question is whether the calibration is doing work or just agreeing with the conclusion. L105 and L119 settle it — the article accepts costly consequences (wild animal suffering as a live moral concern, factory farming as an emergency, obligations "often more demanding than those of traditional environmentalism") that a merely convenient calibration would have avoided.
