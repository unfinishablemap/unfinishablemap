# Outer review: *Depersonalisation and the Separability of For-Me-Ness*

**Article reviewed:** live version last substantively modified 20 October 2026.  
**Changelog reviewed:** live version current through 26 October 2026. [depersonalisation](/concepts/depersonalisation/)

## Overall verdict

**Recommendation: major revision.**

The article gets two important things right:

1. The diagnostic nucleus is broadly accurate: people with depersonalisation/derealisation disorder characteristically retain reality testing despite experiences of detachment or unreality.
2. Its **significance-tier conclusion is admirably restrained**: DPDR does not discriminate dualism from physicalism, and compatibility with an interface model must not be counted as evidence for it.

Its central problem lies between those points. The article repeatedly moves from **preserved insight** to **preserved perception, cognition and phenomenal content**; from the absence of an obvious destructive lesion to an **undamaged content-delivery substrate**; and from the existence of several theoretical explanations to **established mechanistic independence**. None of those stronger transitions is adequately supported.

The result is a defensible weak conclusion wrapped in an indefensible strong one:

- **Defensible:** substantial cognition, reality testing and ordinary action can coexist with profound disturbances of familiarity, presence, ownership, agency and emotional connection.
- **Not established:** exactly the same first-order content remains fixed while a single independently variable phenomenal feature of “for-me-ness” is subtracted from it.

Citation fidelity is generally better than the argument built from the citations. I found no central source that was obviously invented, attributed to the wrong author or quoted in the reverse of its intended sense. The recurring problem is instead **evidential promotion**: an abstract, exploratory factor analysis or theoretical model is accurately quoted and then treated as stronger evidence than it supplies.

## 1. The clinical datum

### 1.1 Retained reality testing: accurate, but too much is inferred from it

The article is right that retained reality testing distinguishes DPDR from psychosis. Current APA material similarly says that during these altered experiences the person remains aware of reality and recognises that the experience is unusual. The article is also right to distinguish the characteristic “as if” form of many reports from a literal delusional belief. [American Psychiatric Association+1](https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/dissociative-disorders/what-are-dissociative-disorders)

But the next sentence is substantially too strong:

> “The patient sees, remembers, reasons and acts normally…”

Reality testing establishes something about **judgement or insight**. It does not establish that:

- perceptual phenomenology is unchanged;
- attention and memory function normally;
- bodily experience and agency are intact;
- all first-order experiential content is preserved;
- ordinary functioning is unimpaired.

DPDR descriptions include visual and auditory alterations, altered spatial distance or flatness, disturbances of time, abnormal familiarity, altered bodily presence, automaticity of action, unusual memory phenomenology, impaired imagery and various reported cognitive difficulties. There is debate over whether all perceptual descriptions should be taken literally, but there is no basis for excluding them in advance. Knappik specifically notes the range of perceptual symptoms and argues that there is no independent reason simply to reinterpret all of them as figurative descriptions of a lost ownership tag. [Springer Link+1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11097-022-09872-0)

A person can therefore know that the world is not literally unreal while it appears distant, flattened, dreamlike or strangely unfamiliar. **Preserved belief about reality and preserved phenomenal content are different claims.** The article currently collapses them.

There is also an internal tension. The page presents the Cambridge Depersonalisation Scale factors as anomalous bodily experience, emotional numbing, anomalous subjective recall and alienation from surroundings. Those are not naturally summarised as perception and cognition being wholly intact. [depersonalisation](/concepts/depersonalisation/)

A clinically safer formulation would be:

> Reality testing is characteristically retained, and many ordinary perceptual, cognitive and practical capacities remain available, despite marked alterations in affect, familiarity, embodiment, agency and the phenomenal presentation of self and world.

### 1.2 The diagnostic references are substantively sound but methodologically stale

The article cites the 2013 DSM-5 and transcriptions of both DSM and ICD criteria through secondary sources. That is candidly disclosed, but it is no longer best practice. DSM-5-TR is the current American standard, while WHO published the ICD-11 Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements in March 2024 as the current clinical diagnostic manual. Both should be consulted directly for a page whose architecture depends on fine distinctions among insight, experience, impairment and exclusion criteria. [American Psychiatric Association+1](https://www.psychiatry.org/psychiatrists/practice/dsm)

The article’s abbreviated presentation also omits important aspects of disorder status. DPDR is not merely the long-lasting endpoint of an otherwise ordinary experience. Clinical diagnosis additionally concerns distress or impairment and exclusion of relevant substances, medical conditions and better explanations. The article’s sentence that “the disorder is the chronic form of a common experience” is therefore at most a dimensional gloss, not an adequate clinical characterisation.

### 1.3 Prevalence: the old quotation is real, but the inference needs updating

The Hunter, Sierra and David 2004 figures are quoted fairly. The difficulty is that the article combines a very broad estimate for **ever having transient depersonalisation symptoms** with an estimate for clinically significant disorder, then treats the combination as evidence of one smooth progression.

A newer systematic review identified 23 prevalence studies using standardised tools. In general-population studies, most estimates were approximately 0.76–1.9%, rather than providing a new confirmation of the 26–74% lifetime figure for any transient experience. The newer review also demonstrates how strongly estimates depend on sampling and operationalisation. [UCL Discovery+1](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10135448/3/David_The%20prevalence%20of%20DDD_Final.pdf)

The defensible claim is that transient depersonalisation-like experiences are relatively common while persistent clinically significant DPDR is much less common. Prevalence figures alone do not show that the disorder is simply the quantitatively intensified form of the transient state. Duration, distress, comorbidity, precipitating conditions and qualitative symptom organisation may matter.

### 1.4 The four-factor structure is not a settled psychometric architecture

The description of Sierra et al. 2005 is accurate. It was an exploratory factor analysis of 138 consecutively assessed patients and yielded the four factors the article names. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/abs/unpacking-the-depersonalization-syndrome-an-exploratory-factor-analysis-on-the-cambridge-depersonalization-scale/9A4486F31B3A8DC290B61CCE25A0CDAB)

The article nevertheless makes too much of it when it calls that structure “the psychometric fact any one-feature reading has to accommodate.” It is **one exploratory solution**, not an established decomposition of the syndrome. A later study of 394 affected adults obtained five factors, separating unreality of self from perceptual alterations. A Spanish-language community study explicitly reported that CDS factor structures had been inconsistent across studies, even though it selected a four-factor solution for its own sample. [Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai+1](https://scholars.mssm.edu/en/publications/de-constructing-depersonalization-further-evidence-for-symptom-cl-2)

More importantly, factor analysis identifies covariance among questionnaire items. It does not by itself determine:

- whether factors are causally independent;
- whether one factor is upstream of the others;
- whether there is a common latent cause;
- whether the factors are phenomenal rather than functional;
- whether any factor corresponds to a detachable metaphysical constituent.

The four-factor result therefore pressures a simple one-symptom account, but it does not establish a multi-channel ontology.

### 1.5 The affective and neural generalisations are too categorical

The statement that limbic activation to aversive stimuli “is reduced” is directionally related to an influential corticolimbic model, but it is anatomically and evidentially too broad. A recent systematic review found **some evidence** for diminished subjective responsivity to aversive images and sounds, alongside hyperactivation in circuits associated with emotional regulation during aversive viewing. That is more qualified than a general claim of reduced limbic activation. [King's College London](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/behavioural-autonomic-and-neural-responsivity-in-depersonalisatio/)

The page should distinguish at least:

- subjective emotional response;
- autonomic response;
- activation in particular regions during particular tasks;
- regulatory versus generative circuitry;
- trait DPDR from experimentally induced depersonalisation;
- medicated, comorbid and diagnostically heterogeneous samples.

The affective model remains empirically serious, but the present wording makes it look more uniform and settled than the experimental literature supports.

### 1.6 “Without damage to the substrate” is ambiguous and misleading

The architecture tier says that something comes apart from content “without damage to the substrate that delivers the content.” Read as **without a required gross destructive focal lesion**, this is plausible. Read as **without relevant alteration of the neural or functional substrate**, it is false or at least wholly unsupported.

The neighbouring clinical-dissociation article already supplies the right distinction: the relevant systems may be **undamaged, not unchanged**. It discusses altered connectivity, activation and coordination rather than the absence of neural change. The depersonalisation page should use exactly that discipline. [clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence](/topics/clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence/)

This matters philosophically. A physicalist explanation need not predict tissue destruction. It may posit altered precision weighting, salience, interoception, affective regulation, network dynamics or self-modelling in structurally intact tissue. The absence of a lesion does not create a gap between experience and its substrate.

## 2. Do the cited sources say what the article says they say?

### Diagnostic manuals

The central diagnostic claims about detachment, unreality and retained reality testing are correct. The weakness is sourcing them through secondary transcriptions and citing DSM-5 rather than DSM-5-TR, not misrepresentation of the criteria. [depersonalisation](/concepts/depersonalisation/)

### Hunter, Sierra and David 2004

The quoted prevalence range is faithfully taken from the older review. The error is inferential and temporal: heterogeneous estimates of transient experiences are treated as though they establish a straightforward continuum into current diagnostic disorder.

### Sierra et al. 2005

The four labels and “distinct but related” conclusion are accurate. What is not warranted is treating this exploratory result as the unique or settled psychometric architecture. [Cambridge University Press+1](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/abs/unpacking-the-depersonalization-syndrome-an-exploratory-factor-analysis-on-the-cambridge-depersonalization-scale/9A4486F31B3A8DC290B61CCE25A0CDAB)

### Gerrans 2019

The quotation about cognition and perception otherwise being intact is faithful to Gerrans’s account. But the article introduces it as “a formulation every reading accepts.” That is not established. It is part of Gerrans’s theoretical reconstruction, and it is much more plausible if “intact” means sufficient for retained insight and ordinary object recognition than if it means phenomenally or neurocognitively unchanged. [Springer Link+1](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s13164-018-0415-2.pdf)

### Billon

The article represents Billon unusually carefully. It correctly reports his proposal that DPDR involves diminished subjective character or mineness, distinguishes the persisting subject from a missing feature of experience, and—crucially—reports his explicitly neurophysiological conclusion rather than recruiting him as a dualist. It also properly treats his position as a philosophical interpretation of the syndrome rather than pretending that Billon endorses the Map’s metaphysics. [depersonalisation](/concepts/depersonalisation/)

The remaining problem is auditability. The bibliography says the 2016 and 2023 works were quoted from preprints without page numbers and that the Simeon–Abugel patient report was not consulted directly. These are acceptable provisional practices when conspicuously labelled, but they are weak foundations for a page whose argument turns on exact differences among subjective character, ownership, actual character and present character.

### Seth, Ciaunica, Deane and the active-inference literature

The article’s paraphrases are broadly faithful. Seth, Suzuki and Critchley propose an interoceptive predictive-coding account of presence; Ciaunica and colleagues propose altered precision modulation and sensory attenuation; Deane and colleagues relate temporally deep generative models, allostatic control and contrasting forms of selflessness. [DOI+2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00395)

The distortion is in epistemic status. These works are theoretical or modelling proposals. Seth’s paper presents itself as a hypothesis-and-theory contribution and as a step toward a mechanistic account. Ciaunica and Deane likewise formulate explanatory frameworks and predictions. The article upgrades these into:

- “fully specified physicalist mechanisms”;
- a result “predicted by a single parameter”;
- a “worked mechanism”;
- “mechanistic independence”.

That is too strong. A computationally expressible proposal is not yet an empirically fitted, uniquely supported or interventionally validated mechanism. The page should distinguish:

1. conceptual mechanism;
2. formal model;
3. fit to observational data;
4. comparative model superiority;
5. causal confirmation by intervention.

The cited predictive-processing literature mostly occupies the first two levels.

### Zahavi, Kriegel, Henriksen and Parnas

The universalist view is represented fairly: minimal for-me-ness is not a detachable introspectible quale but part of what it is for an experience to be consciously given. The article also responsibly acknowledges that some cited discussions concern thought insertion rather than DPDR and that their application is an extension. [depersonalisation](/concepts/depersonalisation/)

Calling the disagreement an “introspective standoff,” however, understates the universalist argument. It is partly conceptual: the very awareness and report of alienation may demonstrate that minimal first-personal givenness remains. Billon can answer by distinguishing minimal givenness from the felt mineness allegedly diminished in DPDR, but that distinction must be defended; it is not a neutral parsing of the datum.

### Knappik

Knappik’s localist challenge is accurately and substantially represented. The article correctly identifies his claim that different state types bear different subject-relations and that their impairments can vary independently. It also candidly admits that the Map can turn this into a “family of separable phenomenal features” only by establishing that those local relations are phenomenal rather than merely functional—a premise Knappik does not grant. [Springer Link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11097-022-09872-0)

This is arguably the article’s strongest treatment of an opponent. The problem is that the concession is not consistently propagated to the neighbouring mine-ness page.

## 3. The readings of what comes apart

There is a minor counting problem. The section is headed “Four Readings,” but the page presents:

1. the affective reading;
2. Billon’s subjective-character reading;
3. active inference and self-modelling;
4. universalism;
5. localism.

Universalism and localism share a subsection, but they make opposite claims and cannot usefully be treated as one reading. The clearer structure would be **“The Map/Billon interpretation and four rivals.”**

### 3.1 Billon and the Map: a serious interpretation, not the clinical datum itself

Billon provides the strongest route to the article’s preferred architecture. His reading is phenomenologically intelligible: the subject and ordinary intentional objects remain, while a quality of subjective character or mineness is diminished.

But this is an interpretation of patient reports. It is not independently established that:

- the missing element is one feature;
- it is present across all experience;
- other phenomenal content remains qualitatively identical;
- it is distinct from affect, familiarity, agency or embodied regulation;
- it varies independently of all functional change.

The page sometimes preserves that distinction, calling the Map’s view a reconstruction. Elsewhere it states separability as the documented result in terms already loaded toward Billon.

### 3.2 The affective reading: fairly stated, too quickly discounted

The article correctly presents affective attenuation as a genuine alternative rather than a superficial symptom account. It also identifies reasonable challenges: DPDR is heterogeneous, and emotional numbing can occur without the full syndrome.

Its two main objections do not decide the matter, however.

First, finding emotional numbing as one questionnaire factor among several does not show that it cannot be a common cause, a precipitating condition or part of a hierarchical causal sequence. Factor analysis is not causal analysis.

Second, cases of trauma-related emotional numbing without full DPDR show that affective alteration may be **insufficient by itself**. They do not show that affective alteration is irrelevant or that no affect-plus-appraisal account can explain DPDR.

The fair conclusion is that a pure “lack of emotional colour and nothing else” account is under pressure, while broader affective-regulatory models remain live.

### 3.3 Predictive processing and active inference: the strongest physicalist rival, but not a finished explanation

The article is right to regard these accounts as especially important. They attempt to explain why disturbances of interoceptive precision, agency, allostatic control and self-prediction could produce a globally altered sense of presence while retaining insight.

But “single precision parameter” is too tidy. Precision appears in these models as part of a larger architecture involving hierarchical prediction, sensory attenuation, interoception, agency attribution, control and explanatory inference. Nor has the article shown that a single independently measured parameter predicts the syndrome’s full breadth across patients.

The page should also separate:

- **generic self-model accounts**, on which subjective character depends on the structure or transparency of a self-representation;
- **predictive-processing accounts**, which specify prediction-error and precision dynamics;
- **active-inference accounts**, which additionally concern action and expected control;
- **constitutive claims**, on which these processes realise presence;
- **causal-production claims**, on which they generate or disrupt it.

Currently those levels are bundled together as one “worked mechanism.”

### 3.4 Universalism: not refuted by pathology

The universalist can concede that DPDR severely alters ownership, familiarity, embodied self-affection and reflective self-recognition while denying that minimal for-me-ness disappears. On this reading, alienation is itself given first-personally.

The Map’s proposed reconciliation—minimal reporting position retained, additional felt feature diminished—is coherent. But it is also precisely the disputed architecture. The DPDR reports do not independently label one element “minimal givenness” and another “felt for-me character.” The page should present this as a discriminating hypothesis, not as a decomposition already delivered by introspection.

### 3.5 Localism: the strongest challenge to the strong architecture tier

Localism fits several awkward facts:

- symptoms vary across patients;
- depersonalisation and derealisation can dissociate;
- bodily, episodic, affective, perceptual and agentive disturbances need not covary perfectly;
- different state types possess different forms of self-relation.

It does not follow that localism is correct. A global feature could have multiple partially independent manifestations. But the page has no decisive evidence for choosing that model over the converse possibility that several local disturbances create the impression of one global loss.

The current response—plural for-me-ness is still a family of separable phenomenal features—risks making the architecture claim unfalsifiable. If one global feature is found, the thesis is confirmed; if several local relations are found, they become a family of features and the thesis is again confirmed. A useful theory needs conditions under which the Map would conclude that there is **no distinct for-me component**.

## 4. The two-tier conclusion

### 4.1 A weaker architecture tier is defensible

DPDR supports a significant dissociation claim:

> Retained reality testing and substantial cognitive access can coexist with profound, persistent alterations in how self, body, agency, memory and world are experientially presented.

That is clinically and philosophically important. It prevents simple identification of self-presence with explicit self-belief, object recognition or the ability to make first-person reports.

### 4.2 The stronger architecture tier is not adequately argued

The page’s stronger conclusion requires four further propositions:

1. **Content invariance:** the same first-order phenomenal content persists.
2. **Feature unity:** one global for-me feature changes rather than several state-specific relations.
3. **Causal independence:** the feature can vary independently of affective, predictive, interoceptive and self-modelling functions.
4. **Substrate preservation:** the relevant content-producing substrate is unchanged rather than merely non-lesioned.

The reviewed evidence establishes none of these.

Most importantly, “mechanistic independence is supplied by every rival” is a category mistake. The rival mechanisms generally propose **dependencies**:

- the affective account makes alienation depend on emotional attenuation;
- predictive accounts make it depend on precision and self-modelling;
- transparency accounts make it depend on altered presentation of normally transparent processes;
- localism denies that there is one independent global feature.

The fact that several competing mechanisms have been proposed does not demonstrate that the alleged feature is mechanistically independent. It demonstrates underdetermination among explanations.

### 4.3 The significance tier is the article’s strongest section

The page correctly says that even genuine phenomenological separability would not imply metaphysical irreducibility. Its proposed bridging premise—that something varying independently of content or a particular function must be contributed from outside functional organisation—is recognisably the dualist thesis under another description. Rejecting that inference as question-begging is exactly right. [depersonalisation](/concepts/depersonalisation/)

It also fairly records three inconvenient facts for the Map:

- physicalist rivals predict much of the same phenomenology;
- Billon himself favours neurophysiological explanation;
- compatibility with dualism does not constitute support for dualism.

That is genuine adversarial discipline, not cosmetic balance.

## 5. Is DPDR an instance of the discrimination problem?

Not in the strong sense used by the site’s own discrimination-problem article.

That article characterises the problem through cases in which alternatives produce the same effects in the relevant detection or reporting channel. DPDR is not such a case. The syndrome changes reports, experienced affect, behaviour in at least some contexts, questionnaire responses and measurable psychophysiological or neural variables. [discrimination-problem](/concepts/discrimination-problem/)

Nor has the depersonalisation article established that two phenomenally different states have exactly the same first-order content. It has established, at most, that much object-level access and reality judgement can survive despite altered self/world presentation.

DPDR is therefore better described as:

- a **within-subject dissociation**;
- an **underdetermination case** between rival decompositions;
- a possible source of **matched-task experiments** in which object recognition or judgement is held approximately constant.

Calling the syndrome itself the “best within-subject exhibit” of the discrimination problem overstates the match between the case and the site’s formal concept.

## 6. Tenet-protective bracketing

### At the significance tier: little or none

The article does not protect dualism by declaring physicalist accounts impossible, incomplete by definition or explanatorily irrelevant. It openly grants them evidential parity and refuses to count compatibility as confirmation. This is a strong feature.

### At the architecture tier: moderate bracketing

Three subtler protective moves remain.

**First, all decompositions become evidence for separability.** If affect separates, that supposedly supports separability; if precision changes, a computational self-component separates; if local relations fail, a family of phenomenal features separates. This makes “separability” so permissive that almost any structured explanation confirms it.

**Second, the Map says it “adopts the mechanics without the metaphysics.”** That is legitimate only after showing why the relevant mechanics fail to constitute the phenomenon they explain. Otherwise, physicalist causal machinery is accepted as evidence for the Map’s architecture while its constitutive interpretation is simply bracketed.

**Third, the thesis retreats from a single global feature to a family of local phenomenal relations when pressed by Knappik.** Such a retreat may be correct, but it changes the hypothesis. The article needs criteria for deciding between the original and revised versions rather than treating both as continuous confirmation.

Tenet 5 is applied more honestly. The page acknowledges that the simplest one-feature story may be precisely what symptom heterogeneity and localism count against. That self-application should be retained. [depersonalisation](/concepts/depersonalisation/)

## 7. Integration with neighbouring pages

### Mine-ness

This is the largest cross-site inconsistency. The mine-ness article makes stronger claims than the depersonalisation page can sustain: that qualitatively identical experiences can differ only in mine-ness, that content is preserved, and that a pluralised family of phenomenal self-relations survives the localist challenge. [mine-ness](/concepts/mine-ness/)

Those should be marked as the Map’s reconstruction, not as the clinical result. In particular, “same content” needs an operational definition:

- same represented object?
- same explicit judgement?
- same discrimination performance?
- same perceptual qualities?
- same affective and bodily presentation?

The evidence supports some of these identities in some tasks, not all of them globally.

### Cotard delusion

The Cotard page is comparatively careful. It reports the proposal that some Cotard cases may involve depersonalisation-like anomalous experience plus a change in belief evaluation, while acknowledging rival accounts and the absence of metaphysical discrimination. [cotard-delusion](/concepts/cotard-delusion/)

The depersonalisation page’s claim that the relation is a continuum “on the majority reading” is insufficiently supported. Billon’s “as-if Cotard” formulation and Davies and Coltheart’s qualified proposal are serious models, but they do not by themselves establish a majority clinical consensus. Cotard syndrome is heterogeneous, and “as if” becoming literal belief should remain one proposed pathway rather than the default developmental sequence.

### The discrimination problem

As noted above, the integration is conceptually overdrawn. The discrimination page concerns output-equivalent alternatives; DPDR is a syndrome with changed reports and processing. The link should point to a possible **experimental strategy**—holding a specified discrimination constant while symptom intensity varies—not claim that the clinical syndrome already instantiates the formal problem.

### Clinical dissociation as systematic evidence

That page contains the more defensible formulation “undamaged, not unchanged,” and it explicitly recognises the absence of targeted perturbational evidence. Those qualifications should govern the depersonalisation page. [clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence](/topics/clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence/)

Conversely, the clinical-dissociation page still inherits some of the overstatement when it treats DPDR as a clean separation of mine-ness from preserved content. The revision needs to propagate in both directions.

### The recent PTSD integration

The changelog records a newly created treatment of the dissociative subtype of PTSD that runs against the Map’s preferred trauma interpretation and presents a relatively well-specified physicalist production account. Yet its integration into the depersonalisation and clinical-dissociation pages consisted only of zero-word piped links; the depersonalisation page remained at 3,498 words under its hard 3,500-word limit. [changelog](/workflow/changelog/)

This illustrates a broader methodological problem. The site’s changelog and source-verification machinery are unusually transparent, but strict word ceilings can cause **link insertion to substitute for argumentative integration**. An adverse result is not genuinely absorbed merely because a phrase now points to another page.

3. **Depersonalisation:** replace “without damage to the substrate” with “without a required gross focal destructive lesion,” followed immediately by the clinical-dissociation page’s rule: **undamaged does not mean unchanged**.
4. **Depersonalisation:** update DSM-5 to DSM-5-TR and replace both secondary diagnostic transcriptions with direct citations to current APA and 2024 WHO ICD-11 CDDR sources.
5. **Depersonalisation:** replace the sentence that DPDR is simply the chronic form of a common experience with a dimensional but clinically qualified statement mentioning persistence or recurrence, distress or impairment, relevant exclusions and heterogeneity.
6. **Depersonalisation:** retain Hunter et al. 2004 for historical comparison but make the newer prevalence systematic review primary; do not combine transient-experience and disorder estimates as though they measured the same outcome.

8. **Depersonalisation:** state explicitly that factor structure demonstrates item covariance, not causal modularity, phenomenal separability or metaphysical component structure.
9. **Depersonalisation:** replace the blanket reduced-limbic-activation claim with a task-specific summary of diminished subjective aversive response, regulatory-circuit findings and the limitations of the experimental evidence.

12. **Depersonalisation:** replace “fully specified physicalist mechanisms,” “worked mechanism,” “single parameter” and “mechanistic independence” with more accurate descriptions such as “candidate theoretical mechanism,” “formal explanatory framework” or “testable computational proposal.”
