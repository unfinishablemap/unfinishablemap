## Executive Summary

The article's real contributions survive: the five-faces analysis of operation-to-consciousness opacity, the honest functionalist concession at L111, and the 09-07 hedges. Its weakness is that the conclusions have not caught up with those concessions.

Two High issues sit in Relation to Site Perspective. Three tenet checks flagged both, and neither was ever queued.

- **L157** presents Tenet 3 as an observed fact: "is mental causation producing physical outcomes". It says translation is work that "cannot be delegated to unconscious processing". The article's own L117–L127 says the operation is sub-personal and that LLMs, which the Map holds lack phenomenal experience, perform it at junior-human level.
- **L155**, with its premise at L107, infers *in-principle* untranslatability from a remainder that the article's own evidence shows to be *in-practice*. L97 lists circumlocution as a working option, and L125 says first-person experience "cannot adjudicate" the in-principle question. Cassin, whose *Dictionary of Untranslatables* the article cites, disowns the reading in her introduction: "in no way implies that the terms in question … are not and cannot be translated".

There is one new philosophical misattribution. L125 says Davidson "resisted" Quine's no-unique-translation conclusion, but Davidson endorsed indeterminacy (SEP). Three empirical claims are stated as settled when they are contested or misdescribed:

- the Mandarin vertical-time effect (ten failed replications; the Map's own 10-26 research note forbids presenting it as settled);
- English versus Spanish agentivity (English allows "The cup broke");
- the L77 sequence in which meaning is grasped first and words found after (the Macizo & Bajo 2006 data favour "horizontal" translation).

## Critiques by Philosopher

### The Eliminative Materialist (Patricia Churchland)

"Your evidence base is translators' self-reports. L65 says 'Translators report', L85 says 'Experienced translators report', and you cite no study for either. A whole field records what translators actually do: translation-process research with think-aloud protocols, keystroke logging and eye-tracking. You use none of it. Where experimenters have looked, the folk story you tell at L75, in which the meaning is grasped first, held 'in a pre-linguistic form', and then given words, is the 'vertical' model. Macizo & Bajo (2006) found that when people read *for translation*, target-language lexical properties already affect comprehension, and they conclude their data 'provides support for horizontal theories of translation'. The felt sequence is not the processing sequence. Your own L117 grants this: 'the translator does not experience the translating'. So why does L77 take the felt sequence as evidence for an intermediate meaning-state?" (See Issue 7.)

### The Hard-Nosed Physicalist (Daniel Dennett)

"L85: 'This is not an inference but a felt quality.' Twenty-six lines later you grant my reading, that the felt loss is 'a high-level report about representational mismatch' (L111). A report of mismatch *is* the product of a comparison. You cannot hold both the flat denial at L85 and the concession at L111. Then look at L157. You list 'grasp, hold, search, compare, reject, and choose' as a 'cascade of conscious acts'. But L119 says 'the ranking of candidate mappings, and the rejection of disanalogies happen below introspective access'. On your own account the comparing and rejecting that matter happen sub-personally, and what the translator receives is 'the felt-fit of the chosen rendering — the output' (L119). That is my view, and it is your L117–L129. Your Relation section then contradicts it." *(The bedrock functionalist standoff is not re-flagged. ### The Quantum Skeptic (Max Tegmark)

Choosing between "longing" and "nostalgia" is a macroscopic, content-laden selection lasting seconds to minutes (L67 "milliseconds or minutes"). ### Issue 3: L125 misdescribes both Quine and Davidson (Medium-High)

- **Location**: L125. "There is no uniquely correct translation between any two languages — multiple translation manuals can be empirically adequate … Davidson resisted this conclusion by arguing that any genuinely cognitive system must be in principle inter-translatable. The Quine-Davidson disagreement turns on whether translation-difficulty is *unbridgeable in principle* or *unbridgeable only in practice*. The empirical untranslatable-terms corpus and the phenomenology of the remainder sit closer to Quine's pole".
- **Problem**:
  - **Davidson did not resist the no-unique-translation conclusion; he adopted it.** SEP "Donald Davidson" (Malpas, rev. 2023), §3.4: "It is this failure of uniqueness that Davidson terms the 'indeterminacy' of interpretation and which provides a counterpart to the 'indeterminacy of translation' … in Quine … It nevertheless remains an ineliminable feature of all interpretation." What Davidson rejected was the idea of an *untranslatable language*, in the same entry, §4.2: "the idea of an untranslatable language … cannot be given any coherent formulation". That is a different target.
  - **Quine's indeterminacy is a surplus of correct translations, not a shortage.** SEP "Willard Van Orman Quine" (Hylton & Kemp, rev. 2023), §6.2: "there might be different ways of translating a language which are equally correct but which are not mere stylistic variants." Neither philosopher holds that translation is "unbridgeable in principle". On Quine's view *saudade* has many equally correct English renderings, not none. So the Lomas corpus and the felt remainder do not "sit closer to Quine's pole". They do not bear on indeterminacy at all.

  *Both entries were fetched today and the quoted strings grepped from the extracted text.*
- **Provenance**: The 05-09 and 06-02 deep reviews marked this characterisation "correctly characterised" on a source-conclusion pass that fetched no source, so it was never actually verified. The conflation comes from `research/voids-translation-void-2026-05-09` L111 ("Quine's stance allows for genuinely Unexplorable cross-lexical territory"). That note's own key-points bullets (L109–L110) state each thesis correctly. `voids/conceptual-scheme-void` L104 gets Davidson right.
- **Recommendation**: Rewrite the face so that it opposes the right things. Quine: no fact of the matter fixes which of several adequate manuals is correct. Davidson: no language could be untranslatable, though he accepts indeterminacy. The translator's felt sense that one rendering is *the* right one is then the interesting datum, because indeterminacy says no such fact exists. That keeps the face, which the 06-20 note says must not be collapsed, and makes it accurate. Adjust Further Reading L178 ("The conceptual-scheme face Quine and Davidson disagree about") to match. Roughly length-neutral.

### Issue 6: L101 and L103 say English lacks a construction it has (Medium)

- **Location**:
  - L101: "English foregrounds agents ("I broke the cup"); Spanish and Japanese allow events to occur agentlessly ("The cup broke itself," "The cup broke")."
  - L103: "When the translator converts an agentless Japanese construction into agent-foregrounding English, they are not preserving the same meaning in different packaging — they are performing a phenomenal transformation".
- **Problem**:
  - "The cup broke" is the article's own gloss, and it is grammatical English, so "allow" implies a contrast that does not exist.
  - The documented difference is a *usage preference for accidental events*. Fausey & Boroditsky (2011), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 18(1): 150–157, DOI 10.3758/s13423-010-0021-5: "English and Spanish speakers described intentional events similarly, using mostly agentive language … However, when it came to accidental events English speakers used more agentive language than did Spanish speakers." *PubMed abstract verbatim, PMID 21327361.*
  - The Japanese half was not verified this pass; the likely lead is Fausey et al. 2010, *Frontiers in Psychology*, which is unverified.
  - L103's "converts" is therefore optional. A translator can keep the intransitive. The "phenomenal transformation" is a choice the translator makes, not something the language pair forces.
- **Recommendation**: "English speakers describe accidents agentively more often than Spanish speakers do (Fausey & Boroditsky 2011)." Change L103's "converts" to "chooses to render … as". Net +5 plus one reference line (~25).

- **Location**: L75–L77. "holding it in what might be called a pre-linguistic form". Then: "If meaning were identical to its linguistic expression, there would be nothing to hold between languages. - **Problem**:
  - **Wrong rival.** No one in the cognitive-phenomenology debate identifies meaning with linguistic expression. Computational accounts posit language-independent content: Fodor's language of thought, and interlingua machine translation. ## Counterarguments to Address

### The untranslatable remainder supports dualism (L107, L155)
- **Current content says**: Physicalist meaning would make translation complete in principle, so the remainder points to a phenomenal dimension.
- **A critic would argue**: Physicalists already agree that translation is always possible in principle. Quine and Davidson both do; Davidson calls an untranslatable language incoherent. The remainder the article shows is lost compression and naturalness, which is the in-practice cost of lexical mismatch, and Cassin herself says untranslatables can be translated.
- **Suggested response**: Hold the in-practice claim. The remainder is *felt* as a loss of meaning, not of form, and that felt character is the datum for the phenomenal account. The functionalist explains it as a mismatch report (L111). It is a standoff, honestly stated.

### Translation as a showcase of mental causation (L157)
- **Current content says**: Translation is "perhaps the clearest everyday example" of conscious work that cannot be delegated.
- **A critic would argue**: It is perhaps the clearest everyday example of cognitive work that *has* been delegated. The article itself says LLMs do it at junior-professional level.
- **Suggested response**: Move the claim to where it is defensible: literary translation, framed as the Map's wager (L87's register), with the deflationary reading admitted (L147).

### Meaning held before words (L75–L77)
- **Current content says**: The intermediate state shows meaning is not identical to expression, and so supports cognitive phenomenology.
- **A critic would argue**: The language of thought and LLM latent spaces both contain such states without phenomenality. Experimentally, translators activate target words during comprehension (horizontal route).
- **Suggested response**: Run the phenomenal-contrast argument (Strawson's), which targets the right rival. Concede that the felt order may misdescribe the processing order.
