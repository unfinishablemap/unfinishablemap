## 1. The central thesis is much stronger than the evidence

The article moves among at least four claims:

1. Motivation sometimes influences cognition.
2. Motivational orientation pervades much ordinary cognition.
3. Some appetitive structure is necessary for any cognition.
4. Desire may be inseparable from consciousness itself.

The first claim is well supported. The second is plausible but requires qualification. The third and fourth are not established by the evidence presented.

The decisive inferential gap is between **causal influence** and **constitution**. Evidence that fear affects height estimates, that goals alter information search, or that action selection incorporates expected value shows that motivation can help determine cognitive processing. It does not show that every percept, inference, memory, or state of awareness is partly made of wanting. Yet the article opens with the claim that desire “may constitute” cognition, repeatedly describes escape as impossible, and later entertains inseparability from consciousness. [voids/appetitive-void](https://unfinishablemap.org/voids/appetitive-void/)

### The article lacks a stable definition of “desire”

“Appetite” or “desire” variously means:

- consciously felt wanting;
- incentive salience;
- a biological set point;
- persistence or conatus;
- craving, in a Buddhist sense;
- Lacanian lack;
- affective perspective;
- a preference distribution in active inference;
- epistemic value or uncertainty reduction;
- a trained objective in an artificial system;
- any goal that influences attention.

These are not interchangeable constructs.

If “desire” means any goal, prior preference, regulative norm, set point, or optimization criterion, the thesis becomes nearly trivial: most adaptive systems are organized around some objective. It would apply to thermostats and homeostatic controllers and would no longer establish anything distinctive about consciousness.

If it means phenomenal wanting, the neuroscience and active-inference evidence does not establish its ubiquity. If it means a specifically human existential structure, evidence about optimization or dopamine is not enough. The article therefore obtains apparent breadth partly by changing the meaning of its central term.

The paper needs to say which of the following it is defending:

- **Phenomenological constitution:** every conscious episode feels appetitively directed.
- **Representational constitution:** every cognition represents some outcome as preferable.
- **Functional constitution:** every cognition exists within a goal-directed control architecture.
- **Causal modulation:** motives frequently influence processing without constituting it.
- **Metaphysical constitution:** cognition is an expression of will, lack, conatus, or a comparable fundamental principle.

At present it draws support from the weaker functional and causal claims while rhetorically advancing the stronger phenomenological and metaphysical ones.

## 2. Citation and attribution audit

### 2.1 Schopenhauer: broadly faithful doctrine, inadequate locator, suppressed counterexample

The article’s general presentation of Schopenhauer is defensible: he treats will as primary, intellect as secondary, and ordinary intellect as serving willing. The cited source also discusses consciousness as grounded in longing and the intellect as usually subordinate to the will. [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_World_as_Will_and_Idea/Volume_2/Chapter_19)

There are two problems.

First, the bibliography gives Schopenhauer’s work without enough information to verify the article’s exact English wording. An edition, translator, volume, chapter and page are needed. The wording appears to come from later material associated with Volume II rather than being straightforwardly locatable in the original 1819 text as bibliographically represented.

Second, Schopenhauer explicitly allows exceptional cognition in which knowing becomes detached from willing and “purely objective.” The article acknowledges exceptional aesthetic contemplation but then says that the exception proves the rule. It does not. If the thesis is strict constitutivity—no cognition without appetite—then even a rare genuine exception is a counterexample. If the thesis is instead that ordinary cognition is normally will-serving, the article should state that weaker and much more defensible claim. [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_World_as_Will_and_Idea/Volume_2/Chapter_19)

**Verdict:** broadly accurate at the doctrinal level, but overextended and inadequately sourced as a quotation.

### 2.2 Buddhism: the most serious substantive misrepresentation

The article cites no primary Buddhist text, translation or specialist source for its Buddhist section. That omission matters because it attributes a strong constitutional thesis to “Buddhist philosophy” as though the traditions spoke uniformly.

In the early Buddhist dependent-origination formula, consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense bases, contact and feeling occur before craving. This does not map neatly onto a modern cognitive model, and the relation is not merely a simple temporal sequence, but it does prevent the article from treating early Buddhism as straightforwardly teaching that craving constitutes perception itself. Craving arises conditioned by feeling within the canonical formulation. [The Buddha's Words+1](https://thebuddhaswords.net/sn/sn12.2.html)

The alleged “foundational paradox” of desiring an end to desire also conflates distinct Buddhist categories. The aspiration or intention to abandon craving is commonly represented by **chanda**, whereas compulsive craving is **taṇhā**. In SN 51.15 the apparent regress is raised and answered directly: the desire used to reach a goal can cease once the goal has been attained. The text uses an instrumental analogy rather than treating the issue as a fatal paradox. [SuttaCentral+1](https://suttacentral.net/sn51.15/en/sujato)

The article’s cessation wording is recognizable as standard Buddhist language, but no source or translator is supplied, so the quotation is **not verifiable as printed**.

This section also collapses early Buddhist, Abhidharma, Yogācāra and other positions into a single “Buddhist” view. Some Buddhist arguments may support pervasive affective conditioning; others provide precisely the alleged counterexample—a conscious condition no longer organized by craving.

**Verdict:** unsupported generalization, conceptual conflation and missing primary references. This section requires rewriting, not merely an additional citation.

### 2.3 Spinoza: primary propositions verified, inference overstated

The attribution of conatus to *Ethics* III, proposition 7 is accurate: each thing strives to persevere, and this striving is described as its actual essence. Spinoza also distinguishes appetite from desire, with desire commonly characterized as appetite together with consciousness of it. [Spinoza's Ethics 2.0+1](https://archive.spinoza.ca/elements/3P7)

The article then conflates these levels:

- **Conatus** applies to finite things generally.
- **Appetite** is conatus considered in relation to mind and body.
- **Desire** is the conscious form or awareness of appetite in the relevant scholium.

It therefore does not follow that removing conscious desire would remove being. A process might retain conatus without phenomenally experienced wanting. Nor is desire “the substance of conscious existence” a felicitous Spinozist formulation: individual minds are modes, not substances.

The discussion of adequate ideas as a “partial exit” is also misleading. For Spinoza, adequate understanding transforms passive affects and permits more active forms of striving; it does not suspend conatus. Reason reorganizes appetite rather than leaving all directedness behind.

**Verdict:** the cited propositions are genuine; the article’s extrapolation from conatus to conscious desire is not.

### 2.4 Nietzsche: quotation genuine, context changes its force

The perspectivism passage comes from the third essay of *On the Genealogy of Morality*. Nietzsche does indeed reject a view from nowhere and associates knowing with interpretive, affect-laden perspectives. [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Genealogy_of_Morals/Third_Essay)

But the immediate argument is not merely that cognition is an appetitive imposition. Nietzsche says that permitting more affects and perspectives to speak can increase objectivity and completeness. The article takes the perspectival premise while omitting this constructive epistemic response.

Accordingly, Nietzsche supports something like:

> Cognition always proceeds through situated affective perspectives.

He does not straightforwardly establish:

> Cognition is nothing but appetite, or discovery is displaced by imposition.

The statement that “will to power drives all knowledge” also needs a precise passage. Generic references to *Beyond Good and Evil* and the *Genealogy* are insufficient, especially given the textual complications surrounding posthumously assembled “will to power” material.

**Verdict:** the main quotation is authentic, but the interpretation is selectively one-sided.

### 2.5 Lacan and Žižek: one clear bibliographic error

The wording that fantasy “constitutes our desire” or “provides its coordinates” is associated with Žižek’s “The Seven Veils of Fantasy,” published in *The Plague of Fantasies* in 1997. It is not properly sourced to *The Sublime Object of Ideology* from 1989, as the article’s bibliography suggests. [No Subject+1](https://www.nosubject.com/Articles/Slavoj_Zizek/the-seven-veils-of-fantasy.html)

That reference should be corrected.

The larger formulations attributed to Lacan—that desire is the structure of consciousness, that *objet petit a* ensures that wanting always misidentifies its target, and that subjects cannot know what they truly want—are compressed interpretive summaries. The supplied citation to Seminar XI does not provide an adequate locator for those formulations. Lacan distinguishes need, demand and desire, and does not simply identify all cognition or all consciousness with desire.

These claims are therefore **not adequately verifiable from the article’s bibliography**. They need exact seminar/session or page references, translation details, and more restricted wording.

**Verdict:** one demonstrable misattribution and several insufficiently located interpretive claims.

### 2.6 Predictive processing and active inference: formal unification is not conceptual identity

The active-inference literature does integrate pragmatic and epistemic considerations within expected-free-energy formulations. It also describes epistemic activity as motivationally structured. That supports the limited claim that belief formation and action selection need not operate independently of goals. [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10115009/3/Friston_All%20Thinking%20is%20%27Wishful%27%20Thinking_AAM.pdf)

It does not “collapse the boundary between prediction and preference” in the stronger sense required by the article. The formalism retains distinctions among:

- beliefs about states;
- preferences over outcomes;
- posterior beliefs about policies;
- epistemic value or information gain;
- pragmatic value.

Putting two quantities into a common objective does not make them the same psychological phenomenon. More importantly, a technical “prior preference” is not automatically a felt desire. A nonconscious controller can embody outcome preferences without experiencing wanting.

Current motivational science makes the same distinction from another direction. Berridge’s work separates incentive salience or “wanting” from hedonic “liking” and from learned predictions of outcome value. Wanting can occur consciously or unconsciously and can diverge markedly from prediction. [LSA Technology Services](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2023/09/2023-Berridge-Separating-desire-from-prediction-TICS.pdf)

Thus the article’s active-inference passage commits a level shift:

> An agent’s policy selection is formally sensitive to preferred outcomes.

does not entail:

> Every cognition is phenomenally constituted by desire.

**Verdict:** a legitimate theoretical connection, presented in language that overstates what the formalism proves.

### 2.7 Cognitive penetration: the article acknowledges the controversy but understates it

The article deserves credit for saying that motivational effects on perception are contested. But its acrophobia example is presented too straightforwardly. Studies in which fearful participants report greater heights do not, by themselves, establish that early visual perception has changed. Alternative explanations include attention, memory, judgment, response criteria, experimental demand and task interpretation.

Firestone and Scholl’s major review argues that many celebrated examples of top-down visual penetration fail to exclude such alternatives; fear-and-height cases form part of that dispute. [Yale Perception Lab+1](https://perception.yale.edu/papers/16-Firestone-Scholl-BBS.pdf)

More recent review work offers a less binary picture: evidence and vulnerability to cognitive penetration differ by sensory modality and experimental design, rather than yielding a simple result that desire penetrates perception as such. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44159-024-00382-1)

The sentence should therefore say something like:

> Fear is associated with larger reported height estimates in some paradigms, although whether this reflects perceptual experience, post-perceptual judgment or task effects remains disputed.

The article mentions the dispute but does not cite its principal skeptical literature. Firestone and Scholl are also absent from the bibliography.

**Verdict:** cautiously worded in places, but the evidential example still carries more argumentative weight than the field warrants.

### 2.8 Motivated reasoning: influence is supported, total appetite is not

The motivated-reasoning literature supports selective search, asymmetric evaluation and goal-sensitive stopping rules. But it also finds important roles for accuracy motivation and mixtures of accuracy and directional goals.

A 2023 investigation of information search found substantial effects from both accuracy and desired conclusions: an accuracy-only account best described many participants, while a combined account fit another substantial group. The findings support heterogeneous, task-dependent motives rather than a universal replacement of truth-seeking by appetite. [Nature+1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-29429-8)

This presents a dilemma for the article:

- If accuracy motivation counts as “appetite,” every truth-directed correction becomes confirming evidence and the theory is difficult to falsify.
- If accuracy motivation does not count, then the evidence indicates competing cognitive orientations rather than appetite’s universal dominion.

The cited psychology supports motivationally influenced cognition, not the claim that cognition is constituted by desire.

## 3. Important counterarguments remain underdeveloped

### 3.1 The physicalist fractionation objection

Contemporary motivational neuroscience does not reveal a single process corresponding to the article’s cross-traditional “appetite.” It increasingly distinguishes:

- incentive salience;
- hedonic impact;
- reinforcement learning;
- reward prediction;
- effort allocation;
- homeostatic need;
- habitual action;
- model-based planning;
- conscious desire.

Berridge’s distinction among wanting, liking and learning is especially damaging to the article’s unitary treatment. Wanting can dissociate from expected value, pleasure and conscious report. [LSA Technology Services](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2023/09/2023-Berridge-Separating-desire-from-prediction-TICS.pdf)

Recent work continues this fractionation. A 2024 human pharmacological study found a more qualified pattern than simple dopamine-equals-wanting accounts: opioid manipulation affected medial orbitofrontal responses during consumption, while the anticipated dopamine effect was not clearly observed behaviourally. A 2025 study distinguished D3-receptor-related motivational functions from D1-receptor-related reinforcement within ventral-striatal circuitry. [PubMed+1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38445523/)

This does not refute a broad philosophical idea of appetite. It does show that the article cannot treat neuroscience as converging on a single appetitive essence. The physicalist alternative is not merely “the same data with a different ontology.” It also supplies a more fine-grained decomposition of what the article groups together.

### 3.2 The nonconscious-regulation objection

A system can be goal-directed without consciously wanting its goal. Homeostatic regulation, habitual control, attentional capture and learned action policies can proceed without phenomenal desire. Therefore, even universal functional directedness would not demonstrate that desire constitutes consciousness.

The article should address three separate possibilities:

1. Goal-directed cognition without conscious wanting.
2. Conscious experience without current goal-directed cognition.
3. Conscious wanting as one content within, rather than a condition of, awareness.

At present it moves too quickly from organismic regulation to phenomenal appetite.

### 3.3 The accuracy-motivation objection

An agent can desire truth, but that does not make its resulting belief epistemically suspect. A motive can be truth-conducive. More importantly, the fact that inquiry was initiated by a desire does not imply that every intermediate representational state is constituted by desire.

The paper needs to distinguish:

- the motive for initiating an inquiry;
- the procedures used during inquiry;
- the representational content produced;
- the phenomenology accompanying it;
- the standards by which it is evaluated.

Otherwise, “you wanted to know” becomes an all-purpose answer to every proposed case of non-appetitive cognition.

### 3.4 The contemplative-counterexample objection

The article treats meditative or aesthetic states asymmetrically. Their apparent reduction of appetite is allowed as suggestive phenomenology, but they are denied counterexample status because:

- they are temporary;
- a prior desire may have motivated entry into them;
- a subsequent desire may arise to recover them.

None of these points establishes that appetite was present during the state itself. A desire to fall asleep does not show that sleep phenomenology contains that desire; wanting to recreate an experience does not retroactively alter its content.

A serious test would require preregistered phenomenological and physiological criteria for the target state and an answer to what result would count against appetitive constitution.

### 3.5 The artificial-systems objection

An artificial model can have been optimized by an external training objective without itself representing or experiencing that objective. Selection history, current control structure, represented goal and phenomenal desire must be separated.

Saying that every output is “appetitively structured” because optimization occurred during training risks confusing the designer’s or training procedure’s objective with the system’s present mental architecture. This is particularly important for the Map’s AI-consciousness discussions.

### 3.6 The predictive-processing countermodel

A predictive-processing account can accommodate:

- top-down expectations;
- precision allocation;
- salience;
- action-oriented inference;
- interoceptive regulation;
- motivationally biased evidence accumulation.

None of those requires phenomenal appetite to be ubiquitous. Nor does the existence of a formal objective decide whether the system is conscious. The article concedes that predictive processing can explain the same observations physically, but it does not identify any observation on which the physicalist and dualist accounts make different predictions. [voids/appetitive-void](https://unfinishablemap.org/voids/appetitive-void/)

### 3.7 The genealogy-versus-justification objection to its Occam discussion

The article suggests that attraction to simplicity may itself be appetitively conditioned. That may be psychologically true. It does not challenge the epistemic case for simplicity.

A belief can have a motivated causal history and still be supported by predictive performance, compression, generalization or Bayesian model comparison. To avoid a genetic fallacy, the article must distinguish:

- why a thinker likes a simple theory;
- whether simplicity has independent evidential or methodological value.

Its existing hedge—that this does not by itself refute Occam’s razor—is correct, but the implication for the Map’s fifth tenet remains underdeveloped.

## 4. Tenet-protective bracketing

The article is better than many metaphysically committed essays in one important respect. It explicitly says that salience-shaping evidence is ontologically neutral, that predictive processing can cover the same ground without dualism, and that the dualist interpretation is an addition made by the Map rather than an empirical consequence. That is a genuine methodological virtue. [voids/appetitive-void](https://unfinishablemap.org/voids/appetitive-void/)

Nevertheless, several protective devices remain.

### 4.1 Ubiquity is used to explain the absence of independent evidence

The “void” is said to be invisible because it structures every attempt to observe it. This may describe a genuine transcendental problem. But it also means that failure to find a desire-free condition is confirming evidence, while any alleged desire-free condition can be redescribed as the product of a desire to find one.

That is self-sealing unless the paper specifies observations that cannot be assimilated in this way.

### 4.2 Counterexamples are redescribed rather than tested

Schopenhauerian contemplation, Buddhist cessation and meditative quiet are treated as temporary or motivated exceptions that supposedly reinforce the general rule. This protects the thesis by changing “no cognition without appetite” into “ordinary cognition generally returns to appetite.”

The latter may be true, but it is a different claim.

### 4.3 The proposed falsifiers are not diagnostic

The article gestures at possible falsification through:

- a demonstrably desire-free cognition;
- artificial cognition without appetitive structure;
- the absence of patterned motivational bias.

But each test is presently insulated:

- “Desire-free” cannot be demonstrated if every experimental instruction, attentional target or reporting act automatically counts as appetite.
- An artificial system always has some training objective if external optimization history counts as desire.
- Patterned motivated reasoning demonstrates motivational influence, not constitution.

The paper must specify which auxiliary explanations it would forbid itself from introducing after a negative result.

### 4.4 Dualism does not uniquely predict desire-free consciousness

The suggestion that dualism opens the possibility of nonphysical, desire-free awareness is speculative but not discriminating.

A physicalist can posit a neural condition with awareness but little or no motivation. A dualist can hold that every nonphysical mind is inherently appetitive. Whether consciousness can occur without desire is logically orthogonal to whether consciousness is physical.

Consequently, “appetite as a feature of incarnation” is an imaginative possibility, not an inference from the evidence.

### 4.5 “Bidirectional interaction turned inward” risks a category shift

The Map’s bidirectional-interaction tenet concerns causal relations between consciousness and physical processes. Motivational modulation within cognition is not automatically the same phenomenon “turned inward.” The phrase rhetorically aligns the article with the tenet without supplying a mechanism that connects the two.

The article should either formulate that mechanism or omit the phrase.

## 5. The convergence argument is overstated

The paper says, in effect, that Schopenhauer, Buddhism, Spinoza, Nietzsche, psychoanalysis and predictive processing repeatedly identify an appetitive structure, and treats this as something like a replicated introspective result. [voids/appetitive-void](https://unfinishablemap.org/voids/appetitive-void/)

This is not a valid characterization of the evidence.

These sources employ very different methods and posit different objects:

- Schopenhauer advances a metaphysics of will.
- Early Buddhist texts present a causal-soteriological account of craving and suffering.
- Spinoza develops a deductive metaphysics of conatus.
- Nietzsche offers a perspectival and genealogical analysis.
- Lacan constructs a clinical and structural theory of desire.
- Active inference offers a formal model of belief and policy selection.

They are not independent replications of one introspective observation. Active inference is not an introspective report at all. Spinoza’s proposition is not based on contemplative phenomenology. Buddhist claims cannot be treated as interchangeable with Lacanian lack merely because both can be translated using the English word “desire.”

Nor does cross-cultural recurrence “rule out” cultural construction. It reduces the likelihood that the idea is peculiar to one author, but common human problems, intellectual transmission, broad translation choices and selection of congenial traditions remain live explanations.

The Map’s own convergence position is more restrained: its positions register treats convergence primarily as framework-internal coherence rather than independent confirmation. The Appetitive Void should cite and conform to that restriction. [positions/voids-as-evidence](https://unfinishablemap.org/positions/voids-as-evidence/)

The article should replace “one well-replicated introspective report” with something like:

> A family of partly overlapping claims, derived through heterogeneous and non-independent methods, repeatedly portrays human cognition as affectively or motivationally situated.

That would remain philosophically interesting without manufacturing evidential independence.

## 6. Reference currency

The historical works are not “stale,” but the article’s empirical scaffolding is incomplete for a text substantively revised in September 2026.

Its central empirical sources are mostly foundational or pre-2023. It does not incorporate:

- Berridge’s recent synthesis of wanting, liking, learning and incentive salience;
- post-2022 studies distinguishing accuracy from directional motivation;
- newer modality-sensitive assessments of cognitive penetration;
- recent human pharmacological evidence complicating simple reward-neurotransmitter mappings;
- recent dissociations among motivational, reinforcement and spontaneous-movement functions in dopaminergic systems. [PubMed+4LSA Technology Services+4Nature+4](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2023/09/2023-Berridge-Separating-desire-from-prediction-TICS.pdf)

This matters because the newer literature generally moves away from a unitary motivational substance and toward separable mechanisms.

There is also an internal currency problem. The Map’s own “Wanting and Liking” article was deeply reviewed shortly before “Appetitive Void” received its latest substantive revision. That article now explicitly distinguishes wanting, liking, prediction and learning and gives a physicalist mechanism-only rival. Yet “Appetitive Void” neither cites nor links it. The changelog records that modernization work. [topics/wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/wanting-liking-and-the-value-in-mechanism-fork/)

This is not just a missing hyperlink. The neighbouring article contains distinctions that would force a substantial reconstruction of the present article’s central category.

The article is usefully connected to:

### What is missing or inconsistent

It should be linked near the first definition of appetite, not relegated to an implicit background. Its construct distinctions should govern the entire article. **2. The convergence section should link to [P-V1](/positions/voids-as-evidence/#p-v1).**  

The article currently uses convergence more aggressively than the positions register permits. [P-V1](/positions/voids-as-evidence/#p-v1) says convergence is not independent confirmation. [positions/voids-as-evidence](https://unfinishablemap.org/positions/voids-as-evidence/)

**3. The article should also link to [P-V3](/positions/voids-as-evidence/#p-v3).**  

The Map has suspended cumulative evidential claims while its candidate ledger lacks rejected cases and negative controls. The Appetitive Void’s language of robust recurrence should therefore be explicitly non-cumulative. [workflow/changelog](https://unfinishablemap.org/workflow/changelog/)

**4. Reciprocal links are incomplete.**  

The article presents the Self-Maintained Void as a motivational cousin, but that relationship is not comparably developed from the neighbouring article back toward appetite. The cluster reads as a set of analogies rather than a mapped dependency structure. [voids/self-maintained-cognitive-limits](https://unfinishablemap.org/voids/self-maintained-cognitive-limits/)

**5. Its relation to the Affective Void needs sharper boundaries.**  

Is appetite one affect, a generator of affect, a valenced control state, or an orientation that can exist without felt affect? The Affective and Appetitive voids currently risk redescribing the same material at different levels. The Affective Void’s older basic-emotion and neural-substrate language also deserves modernization alongside this revision. [voids/affective-void](https://unfinishablemap.org/voids/affective-void/)

**6. The “pre-conceptual knowing” terminology should be normalized.**  

If the link is intended to point to the Acquaintance Void, the displayed name, article title and glossary terminology should make the relationship explicit rather than appearing to name a different article.

## Overall verdict

The article contains a strong philosophical prompt and one exemplary caveat: it openly admits that the motivational evidence does not adjudicate between physicalism and dualism. That substantially improves its intellectual honesty.

But the core argument remains under-supported. The article assembles several defensible claims about motivated cognition, organismic regulation and situated inquiry, translates them all into “desire,” and then infers that desire may constitute every thought or consciousness itself. That inference is not licensed.

Its most serious defects are:

- equivocation among several meanings of desire;
- the unsupported and partly erroneous Buddhist treatment;
- the Žižek bibliographic misattribution;
- overstatement of active inference;
- under-engagement with wanting/liking/learning dissociations;
- treatment of heterogeneous traditions as replications;
