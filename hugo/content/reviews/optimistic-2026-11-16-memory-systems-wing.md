## Praise from Sympathetic Philosophers

### The Property Dualist (Chalmers)
`semantic-memory` L171 extends the explanatory gap from sensory qualia into "the seemingly 'cold' domain of information retrieval", and L85 hands the rival reading to the page's own cited authority (Schwartz 2002 on tip-of-the-tongue states as metacognitive inference) and concedes "the phenomenology does not by itself decide between the accounts." The gap is argued for, with the opposing account stated at full strength. `memory-anomalies` L82 keeps the localisation result and the hard-problem residue apart in one sentence: stimulation shows that a substrate is implicated, and is "silent on the further question of why stimulation should elicit a felt anything."

### The Quantum Mind Theorist (Stapp)
The wing is restrained here, which is the right register. `implicit-memory` L185 says how delegation works physically "remains speculative" and offers the Zeno mechanism as "one proposed mechanism". `semanticisation` L84 says outright that nothing in the article bears on the quantum-interaction commitments "in either direction". `consciousness-and-memory` L186 files the tenet as "consistent with the dissociation observed here", no more.

### The Phenomenologist (Nagel)
`semanticisation` is the wing's model of first-person method taken seriously: L44 picks out a phenomenological falsifier (does felt pastness fade by degrees within one memory?) and L72 specifies the experiment from published parts, noting that binary remember/know data cannot distinguish "a graded within-memory fade and a discrete flip". `memory-anomalies` L114 states the governing rule cleanly: introspective reports are "evidence about phenomenology, not about the underlying causal facts".

### The Process Philosopher (Whitehead)
`implicit-memory` L153 to L159 gives a process reading of why monitoring disrupts skill: conceptual prehension injected where physical prehension sufficed. It is offered as illumination and carries no evidential upgrade. That is the right use of the persona: compatible at any tier, never the engine of a tier change.

### The Libertarian Free Will Defender (Kane)
The choking material treats agency as something with measurable consequences. `implicit-memory` L88 already records that Beilock and Carr's own dual-task training "left subsequent choking unchanged", a correction against the page's interest, and L101 concedes the common-cause reading before resting the case on the predicted character of the interference.

### The Mysterian (McGinn)
`consciousness-and-memory` L176 leaves the Proustian question (recovered past qualia, or vivid new simulation?) as possibly unanswerable. `memory-anomalies` L98 generalises cryptomnesia into a structural limit: every "original" thought "arrives without a packing slip".

### The Hardline Empiricist (Birch)
This persona has the most to praise and the most to flag.

Praise: `semanticisation` L32 and L82 keep three verdicts separate (the kind question largely adverse, the phenomenal measurement unmade, the instrument too miscalibrated to decide) and call the Map's reading "unrefuted and untested, the weakest of the falsifier states". `memory-anomalies` L110 says the trio "is not by itself evidence for or against dualism", and L118 places the cumulative-convergence move at *live hypothesis* while admitting the article is part of the convergence it counts. `implicit-memory` L191 and `semantic-memory` L177 both decline to draw No-Many-Worlds support from their material. `consciousness-and-memory` L180 says the cases "share one reading rather than confirming it independently". Each of these is a place where a tenet could have been used as an evidence upgrade and was not.

Flags (calibration concerns, each carried into the Priority List or Ideas for Later):

#### 1. `concepts/semantic-memory` L111: the N400 claim is one side of a split literature, stated as fact, uncited

- **Anchor** (occurs once): `In attentional blink paradigms, when targets don't reach consciousness, semantic processing fails to complete. The N400 ERP component (a marker of semantic processing) requires the word to reach conscious awareness.`
- **Evidence**: the classic attentional-blink ERP studies report the opposite. Vogel, Luck and Shapiro (1998), abstract: "No suppression was observed during the attentional blink interval for ERP components corresponding to sensory processing (the P1 and N1 components) or semantic analysis (the N400 component)." Luck, Vogel and Shapiro (1996), abstract: words in the blink "are analysed to the point of meaning extraction, even though these extracted meanings cannot be reported". Rolke et al. (2001), abstract: for missed primes "the N400 effect was present". Kiefer (2002), abstract: "N400 priming effects can be reliably obtained from unconsciously perceived masked words". The page's side has support too: Batterink et al. (2010), abstract: "N400 amplitude was significantly reduced during the AB period, and missed targets did not elicit an N400." The sentence entered with the article on 2026-01-17 from `research/semantic-memory-consciousness-2026-01-17` L81 to L82, which gives no source.
- **Proposed text** (replaces the anchor and the sentence after it, `Subliminal priming activates associations, but conscious processing is needed for genuine understanding.`, which the next paragraph restates): "How far processing runs without awareness is contested at the N400, the ERP marker of semantic analysis: Vogel, Luck and Shapiro (1998) found it unsuppressed during the attentional blink, while Batterink et al. (2010) found missed targets eliciting none."
- **Dependent sentence**, L113 anchor (once) `This suggests a two-stage model:` becomes "The Map reads this as a two-stage model:". The closing claim that one "cannot unconsciously *understand* a sentence" then stands as the Map's reading, which is what the evidence licenses.
- **References**: add Vogel, Luck & Shapiro 1998 (*JEP: HPP* 24(6), 1656-1674) and Batterink, Karns, Yamada & Neville 2010 (*J. Cogn. Neurosci.* 22(11), 2514-2529). Martinelli et al. 2013 and Visser et al. 2012 are in the list with no citation in the body (surname count in body: 0 and 0) and can be swapped out.

#### 2. `topics/consciousness-and-memory` L106: "appears to require reduced consciousness" ignores consolidation in wakeful rest

- **Anchors** (each once): `Consolidation does not merely require reduced sensory input—it appears to require reduced *consciousness*.` and `Cellini and Capuozzo's (2018) review reaches the same stage-dependent conclusion.`
- **Evidence**: the page's support is a null for *cued* reactivation in waking (Hu et al. 2020, abstract: "TMR was not effective during REM sleep nor during wakefulness in the present analyses"). Uncued consolidation in awake, conscious participants is established: Dewar et al. (2012), abstract: "wakeful resting led to significant enhancement of memory after a 15- to 30-min period and also after 7 days"; Wamsley (2019), abstract: "brief periods of rest after learning facilitate consolidation of new memories"; Carr, Jadhav and Frank (2011), abstract: replay "occurs frequently in the awake state" and "consolidation occurs in both the awake and sleeping animal". The corpus has no mention of any of this (grep for awake replay, wakeful rest, waking rest, Wamsley, Dewar across topics, concepts, voids, apex: 0 relevant hits). The page is also in tension with itself: L160 gives REM, where "vivid phenomenal experience returns", a role in emotional memory processing.
- **Proposed text**: first anchor becomes "Sleep consolidation does not merely require reduced sensory input—it appears to require reduced *consciousness*." After the second anchor add: "The requirement is not general: ten minutes of wakeful rest after learning improved recall a week later (Dewar et al. 2012), so consolidation may need release from the task rather than absence of experience."
- **Why this helps the Map's own argument**: L108 says the channel disengages because "its mode of operation—serial selection—appears incompatible with the parallel integration consolidation requires". Rest releases selection without removing experience, so the wakeful-rest result fits the interface reading better than the stronger wording did. Nothing here concedes epiphenomenalism; it narrows what withdraws from "consciousness" to "attention-mediated selection".
- **Leave alone**: the Hu et al. attribution and the Cellini and Capuozzo "review" wording (2026-08-06 Stability Notes); the section heading (anchors may exist).
- **Reference**: add Dewar, Alber, Butler, Cowan & Della Sala 2012 (*Psychological Science* 23(9), 955–960).
- **Length**: +35 body, +20 reference as "Dewar, M. et al."; must be paid for (shared budget below).

#### 3. `topics/consciousness-and-memory` L138: infantile amnesia as "architectural destruction" contradicts the sibling page's access reading and the primary literature

- **Anchor** (once): `reveals this as architectural destruction rather than mere forgetting: immature hippocampal circuits are physically remodelled, and without an autonoetic self-concept, infant memories were never stored in formats the adult retrieval system can access.`
- **Evidence**: Guskjolen et al. (2018), abstract, pose the question directly: are infant memories "permanently erased (i.e., storage failure) or become progressively inaccessible with time (i.e., retrieval failure)", and find that "optogenetic stimulation of tagged dentate gyrus neurons recovered 'lost' infant memories up to 3 months following training". This is from the laboratory whose neurogenesis result (Akers et al. 2014) underlies the "physically remodelled" wording. In humans, Yates et al. (2025), abstract: hippocampal activity at encoding relates to later memory "beginning around 1 year of age", which "implies that postencoding mechanisms, whereby memories from infancy become inaccessible for retrieval, may be more responsible for infantile amnesia."
- **Sibling conflict**: `concepts/semanticisation` L66 to L72 argues that fading may be "loss of access rather than transformation of the trace", and the same paragraph of the hub page says Proustian recalls suggest "some hippocampal traces persist but are not normally accessed". The trajectory's endpoint is then described in the destruction register, one sentence later, with no link between the two readings.
- **Proposed text** (compact variant, replaces the anchor): "examines the barrier, which may be one of access: infants encode individual memories hippocampally from about age one (Yates et al. 2025)." The preceding words `the [[infant-consciousness|infant consciousness void]]` stay. A fuller variant adds: "and 'lost' infant memories have been recovered in adult mice (Guskjolen et al. 2018)".
- **Secondary file**, `voids/infant-consciousness` L67 (2392 words; voids gate 3000; headroom 607, so not net-zero). Anchor (once): `ongoing neurogenesis that overwrites early memory traces`. "Overwrites" is the storage-failure reading that Guskjolen et al. tested and did not find in mice. Suggested: "ongoing neurogenesis that remodels hippocampal circuits and speeds forgetting (Akers et al. 2014), though in mice the forgotten memories can be reactivated in adulthood (Guskjolen et al. 2018), which points to retrieval failure rather than erasure". The void's sentence two lines earlier ("in formats that the adult retrieval system cannot access") already leans the access way; the fix makes the paragraph agree with itself. References to add there: Akers et al. 2014 (*Science* 344(6184), 598-602), Guskjolen et al. 2018 (*Current Biology* 28(14), 2283-2290), Yates et al. 2025 (*Science* 387(6740), 1316-1320).
- **Species caveat to keep**: the recovery result is in mice and the human result concerns encoding; "may be" is the right strength.
- **Length** (hub): compact variant −11 body, +17 reference.
