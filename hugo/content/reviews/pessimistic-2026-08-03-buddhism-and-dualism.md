## Executive Summary

The compatibility thesis is bought at a price the article does not book. It restates Tenet 1 three times as a claim about **physical descriptions**, then uses that reading to accommodate Yogācāra idealism — but Tenets 2 and 3 require a claim about **physical processes**, an actual world with quantum indeterminacies to bias. The article's own opening says the Map commits to "consciousness interacting with matter"; its L131 says the position is compatible with idealism. Both cannot stand.

Two further findings are contradictions with sources that name this article. `tenets.md` L121 cites it for the Madhyamaka deflationary route being "a genuine bedrock disagreement, not an in-framework defect the Map can refute" — the article's Tenet 4 entry says "Compatible." And `concepts/yogacara-alaya-vijnana-storehouse-consciousness` L64 says "the Map must not conscript it as evidence for its own position" — the article's L79 says Yogācāra "provides another bridge" and "aligns with the Map's rejection of physicalism."

The article is well written, honest in several places to a standard the corpus should copy, and its one live empirical citation verifies clean at the publisher. The defects are architectural: it is the Map's canonical treatment of the tradition the register now names as its principal rival on individuation, and it reads as though that rivalry did not exist.

## Critiques by Philosopher

### The Buddhist Philosopher (Nāgārjuna)

The most damaging critic here, and the one the article most needs. My objection is not the one the article answers.

The article's "Emptiness Challenge" section frames me as asking whether consciousness can be "fundamental" if all phenomena are empty, then answers with two-truths: the Map operates at the conventional level, so the compatibility holds. But look at what that concession does. If the Map's irreducibility claim is conventional, it is on a footing with "the chariot exists" — true enough for practical purposes, false under analysis. The Map does not want that. Tenet 1 says there is "something beyond the material." That is a claim about *how the world is*, not about how it is usefully described. You cannot both retreat to *saṃvṛti-satya* to escape my analysis and keep the ontological claim the tenet asserts. Response 1 says it plainly and does not notice: "Irreducibility is a relation between descriptions." Descriptions are conventional designations. You have conceded the point and called it compatibility.

Second: the "asymmetric emptiness" response attributes to "some Buddhist interpreters" the view that consciousness has special status as that which recognises emptiness. *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* chapter 3 examines the seer, the seeing and the seen, and finds no self-existent seer. That is not an oversight I made which a later interpreter corrected — it is the argument. The article names no interpreter, cites no text, and presents a contested minority reading as one of three equally available responses.

Third, and most seriously: the article says the Map's Tenet 4 is "compatible" with Buddhism because "Buddhist emphasis on *this* moment, *this* life, *this* path to liberation resonates." That is soteriological urgency, not metaphysical indexicality. The tenets page already understands this — it says the no-self analysis takes the "I" to be an aggregate of conditions that already includes the branch, so "why am *I* this one?" is not a well-formed question. The register agrees: P-I1 commits the Map to closed individualism *over* empty individualism, which is my position. So the Map has already decided I am a rival on the very point this article calls compatible. The article is more accommodating toward me than the Map's own foundations are, and the accommodation is achieved by swapping a metaphysical claim for a devotional one.

### The Eliminative Materialist (Patricia Churchland)

The article stages a contrast it cannot sustain. It says Buddhism "doesn't say consciousness is an illusion (that would be eliminativism, which Buddhism rejects)" — but the whole apparatus of *anattā*, aggregates and dependent origination is an elimination programme. Buddhism eliminated the self, which is a folk-psychological posit of exactly the kind I target. The article then draws a line: eliminating the self is "congenial," eliminating phenomenal consciousness is not, because "phenomenal evidence has a different epistemic standing than theoretical posits about what underlies it." That is the whole dispute stated as a premise. Two-and-a-half millennia of trained introspectors report that the self dissolves under sustained attention. The article treats that as evidence. When the same method is pointed at phenomenal properties and Madhyamaka reports that they too lack inherent existence, the article calls it an over-extension. You cannot license the method by its results and then disqualify the results you dislike.

The Fox et al. finding is the sharpest thing in the article and it cuts against you. Meditators' introspective reports correlate better with two-point discrimination thresholds and somatosensory cortical area — that is, trained introspection tracks *neural organisation*. The study's own reference class for accuracy is the brain. You cite it to establish that introspection is trainable and it establishes that what accurate introspection converges on is cortical magnification.

### The Hard-Nosed Physicalist (Daniel Dennett)

The section titled "What Remains in Tension" concedes the witness problem and then repairs it in the same breath: "the phenomenology of witnessing — consciousness observing its own contents — supports the irreducibility claim." Three sentences earlier the article says mainstream Buddhism denies any observer persists. So the phenomenology being cited as support is the phenomenology the tradition under discussion regards as a construction to be seen through. You have taken a seeming that your interlocutor specifically identifies as a seeming and read it as evidence of what it seems to be. That is the heterophenomenological error in its purest form, and it happens in the article's own concession section, where the guard should be highest.

Second: "Mental states matter; consciousness does something" (L87). The Map's own tenets page says articles asserting consciousness does "real work" inherit a debt they do not discharge, and "should read no more confidently than this paragraph does." This article reads considerably more confidently, and it does so while claiming a shared structure with a tradition that has no theory of the interface at all.

### The Quantum Skeptic (Max Tegmark)

I am mentioned nowhere in this article, and that is the finding. The article offers Buddhism "a mechanism (quantum selection) for how mental causation might work" and parallels *cetanā* with "conscious selection at moments of physical indeterminacy." Then its Relation to Site Perspective section covers Tenets 1, 3, 4 and 5 and skips Tenet 2 entirely. The tenet whose page carries the decoherence-timescale dispute, the 10⁻¹³-to-10⁻⁴-second range, and the admission that the mechanism is "empirically indistinguishable from chance" is the one tenet the article invokes in the body and omits from the audit. What is being offered to Buddhism is the least-secured thing the Map holds, offered without its price tag.

### The Many-Worlds Defender (David Deutsch)

The article's Tenet 4 entry is where I would press, and it presses itself. It says the Middle Way "rejects both eternalism and annihilationism," similarly to the Map rejecting "soul-substance eternalism and physicalist deflation." That is a rhetorical parallel between two rejection-pairs with nothing in common. Meanwhile the Map's actual argument against many-worlds is the indexical one, which — as your own register now concedes at P-I2 — is conditional on a determinate subject. The Madhyamaka reading dissolves that. So the tradition this article calls compatible with Tenet 4 is the tradition that removes Tenet 4's argument. If you are willing to grant that "I" is an aggregate of conditions, you should grant that the aggregate can include the branch index, and the vertiginous question stops having a target. Your article grants the first and denies the second without noticing they are the same grant.

### The Empiricist (Karl Popper's Ghost)

What observation could distinguish "consciousness is irreducible and impermanent" from "consciousness is a physical process that changes"? The article's answer is the convergence of contemplative traditions on structural features. But it also concedes — correctly and to its credit — that "all human introspection shares one instrument, so the circle is narrowed rather than broken." Having conceded that, the convergence argument is doing no confirmatory work, and the article's own compatibility thesis rests on nothing that could come out otherwise. The "comparative advantages" section is the tell: it lists what each framework *offers* the other, which is a compatibility audit, not a test. Nothing in this article could have concluded "incompatible."

## Critical Issues

### Issue 1: Tenet 1 equivocates between "physical processes" and "physical descriptions," and the descriptions reading cannot carry Tenets 2 and 3

- **File**: `obsidian/concepts/buddhism-and-dualism.md`
- **Location**: L60, L66, L111, L131 against L36
- **Problem**: Tenet 1 as stated on `tenets.md` L53 reads: "consciousness (or mind, or experience) is not reducible to **physical processes**. There exists something **beyond the material**." The article restates it four times as a claim about representations: "cannot be *reduced to* physical **descriptions**" (L60); "no physical **description** captures" (L66); "Irreducibility is **a relation between descriptions**, not a claim about inherent existence" (L111); "the Map's dualism is between consciousness and *physical descriptions* … This is **compatible with both physical realism and idealism**" (L131). This is the epistemic/metaphysical equivocation in its load-bearing form. The *epistemic* reading (no physical description captures what experience is like) is what buys compatibility with Madhyamaka emptiness and with Yogācāra mind-only. The *metaphysical* reading (there is something beyond the material) is what Tenet 2 and Tenet 3 require: Tenet 2 commits to biasing "otherwise indeterminate outcomes" in an actual physical world; Tenet 3 commits to "influence over which definite outcome becomes actual." Under the idealism L131 declares compatible there is no independent physical relatum to bias — the article raises this objection itself at L123 and then dissolves it by retreating to the descriptions reading. The article's own opening (L36) states the Map "commits to irreducible consciousness **interacting with matter**." L131 says the position is neutral between matter existing and not. These are not both true.
- **Severity**: High
- **Recommendation**: Split the readings. Say which one Tenet 1 asserts (the tenets page is unambiguous: "there exists something beyond the material"), and mark the compatibility result as holding on the epistemic reading only, with the consequence stated — that Yogācāra idealism is accommodated at the cost of the relatum Tenets 2 and 3 need. `concepts/yogacara-alaya-vijnana-storehouse-consciousness` L64 already writes this correctly and can be quoted rather than re-derived.

### Issue 2: The Tenet 4 "Compatible" verdict contradicts both the tenets page and the register — and the tenets page cites this article as the source of the rival reading

- **File**: `obsidian/concepts/buddhism-and-dualism.md`
- **Location**: L161 (Relation to Site Perspective, No Many Worlds)
- **Problem**: `tenets.md` L121 reads: "A deflationary route stays open at the framework boundary: the Madhyamaka no-self analysis (`buddhism-and-dualism`, `indian-philosophy-of-mind`) takes the 'I' to be an aggregate of conditions that already includes the branch, so 'why am *I* this one?' is not a well-formed further question. **That is a genuine bedrock disagreement, not an in-framework defect the Map can refute.**" `positions/individuation-and-subjecthood` **P-I1** (adopted 2026-08-03) commits the Map to closed individualism explicitly *over* "empty individualism (no persisting self; boundaries conventional)" and holds subject boundaries to be "a real, determinate metaphysical fact rather than a perspectival illusion or a convention." **P-I2** states that retiring P-I1 "leaves the objection with nothing to discriminate among — on the Madhyamaka reading the tenets page already concedes." The article says: "Buddhist emphasis on *this* moment, *this* life, *this* path to liberation resonates … Compatible." The compatibility is manufactured by substituting soteriological immediacy ("this life matters") for metaphysical indexicality ("why am *I* this determinate subject"). Two other corpus articles get this right — `social-construction-of-self-vs-phenomenal-self` L105 marks the no-self exchange as "resting on that commitment rather than as an in-framework refutation," and `yogacara-alaya-vijnana-storehouse-consciousness` L64 marks the rivalry — so this article is the outlier. This is **boundary-substitution running in the unusual direction**: not a tenet-appeal dressed as refutation, but a bedrock disagreement dressed as agreement.
- **Severity**: High
- **Recommendation**: Rewrite the Tenet 4 entry as an honestly-marked framework-boundary disagreement, citing P-I1, P-I2 and the `tenets.md` L121 passage that names this article. The Madhyamaka reading is the Map's registered rival on individuation; the article should say so and drop the "resonates" parallel, which does no argumentative work. This is also the article's largest missed opportunity: it is the natural home for the corpus's fullest statement of that disagreement.

### Issue 3: Yogācāra is conscripted as an ally in the paragraph lead, against the sibling article that forbids it — and against a clause in this article's own next sentence

### Issue 4: Agent causation is attributed to a no-self tradition, contradicting the Map's own definition of it and this article's own thesis

- **File**: `obsidian/concepts/buddhism-and-dualism.md`
- **Location**: L89
- **Problem**: "Both frameworks require what philosophers call `agent-causation` — the capacity of a conscious being to initiate causal chains, not merely transmit them." The Map defines agent causation as substance causation: `concepts/agent-causation` description — "persons originate actions directly as **substances exercising causal powers**"; `tenets.md` L57 — "Agent causation needs a **persisting subject** that exercises causal powers in its own right; a property-only reading … without a substance-bearing subject struggles to ground that exercise." Buddhist *pratītyasamutpāda* is paradigmatically event-causal: conditions conditioning conditions, with *cetanā* a *cetasika* arising conditionally, not a substance-agent initiating a chain. The article therefore attributes to Buddhism the persisting substance-bearing subject that its own section "What Buddhism Actually Denies" (L46–50: Permanence, Substantiality, Ownership-centricity) says Buddhism denies. It is an internal contradiction against a section forty lines earlier, and an external one against two canonical sources.
- **Severity**: High
- **Recommendation**: The structural parallel the article wants is *mental causation* — mind affects world — which Buddhism does hold and which Tenet 3 asserts. Say that, and drop the agent-causation identification. Then note the genuine divergence: the Map's mental causation runs through a persisting subject and Buddhism's does not, which is the same fault line as Issue 2 and should be cross-referenced to it.
- **Defect family**: This is not confined to one file. `concepts/moral-responsibility` L147 runs the same move ("Agent causation can accommodate this: the agent sources its choices without constituting an unchanging essence"), and `concepts/quantum-interpretations` L159 runs a variant ("the 'observer' needn't endure, but can be each moment selecting its own outcome"). Per the standing lesson that a review's locus list is a sample and not the population, the coupling should be re-grepped across `obsidian/`, `archive/` and `hugo/content/` before the family is closed.

## Counterarguments to Address

### Whether the two-truths retreat costs the Map its tenet

- **Current content says**: "the Map's framework operates at what Buddhism calls the conventional level … The compatibility thesis holds at the conventional level where both frameworks actually operate." (L115)
- **A critic would argue**: Then Tenet 1 is a conventional truth, on a footing with "the chariot exists." The Map asserts something stronger — "there exists something beyond the material." Compatibility purchased by demoting the tenet is not compatibility with the tenet.
- **Suggested response**: Distinguish the *scope* of a claim from its *status*. The Map can claim its dualism is a conventional-level claim in Madhyamaka's technical sense (a claim about dependently-arisen phenomena) while still being a claim about how those phenomena are, not merely how they are described. That distinction is available and is exactly what Response 1 currently forecloses by saying irreducibility is "a relation between descriptions."

### Whether the convergence argument survives the article's own concession

- **Current content says**: convergence across traditions "weakens one specific deflationary objection: that irreducibility is merely a cultural assumption" (L66), immediately hedged with "all human introspection shares one instrument, so the circle is narrowed rather than broken."
- **A critic would argue**: The hedge is fatal to the use. If the circle is not broken, convergence is consistent with a species-universal cognitive bias — which is the deflationary objection, restated.
- **Suggested response**: The hedge is right and should stay; what needs adding is what the convergence *does* establish. It rules out the narrower charge of *tradition-specific* construction (inherited from Cartesian metaphysics) while leaving the species-level charge open. That is a real and modest result, and stating it makes the hedge look like calibration rather than retraction.

### Whether the Map's parallel between *cetanā* and quantum selection is a parallel at all

- **Current content says**: "The Buddhist emphasis on *cetanā* … parallels the Map's emphasis on conscious selection at moments of physical indeterminacy: in both, what matters is that events originate in something like deliberate choice." (L89)
- **A critic would argue**: "Something like deliberate choice" is doing all the work. *Cetanā* is a conditioned mental factor with karmic valence; quantum selection is an actualisation event at a physical indeterminacy. Nothing in Buddhist doctrine locates volition at a point where physics is open, and nothing in the Map's mechanism assigns moral valence to the selection.
- **Suggested response**: Keep the parallel at the level it holds — both frameworks deny that mental states are causally inert — and drop "originate," which imports the agent-causation claim Issue 4 identifies.

## Strengths (Brief)

The one live empirical citation **verifies clean at the publisher**. Fox, Zakarauskas, Dixon, Ellamil, Thompson & Christoff (2012), "Meditation Experience Predicts Introspective Accuracy," *PLOS ONE* 7(9): e45370 — title, full author list, journal, year, issue and article number all correct, and the paraphrase matches the study (body-scanning meditation; ratings across body regions correlated against two-point discrimination thresholds and primary somatosensory cortical area; experts significantly more accurate than novices). Only the "objective thresholds" phrasing needs tightening, noted above as Low.

More importantly, the article's **hedge on that citation is model-grade** and should be preserved verbatim through any revision: "This is narrower than a claim that meditators introspect consciousness as such more accurately, but it counts against the view that introspective skill is untrainable." That is exactly the discipline the corpus asks for — the finding stated at the width the study supports, with the inference it *does* license named separately.

L66's convergence hedge is the second-best sentence in the article: "all human introspection shares one instrument, so the circle is narrowed rather than broken." L40's disclaimer that the compatibility thesis "represents one coherent reading, not the only Buddhist position," with a working named anchor to the emptiness challenge, is correct style-guide practice installed early where truncation cannot remove it. L117's refusal to declare victory — "These responses don't dissolve the tension" — is the right register.
