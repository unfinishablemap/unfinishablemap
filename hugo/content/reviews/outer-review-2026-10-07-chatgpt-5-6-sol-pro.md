## Reply

# Independent critical audit: “Assent Void”

**Audit date:** 7 October 2026  
**Overall verdict:** **major revision required**

The article contains a potentially valuable observation: ordinary belief acquisition often lacks the felt, action-like authorship associated with deciding, intending or choosing. It also correctly resists turning that observation into evidence for interactionist dualism. But the present article does not establish the stronger claim announced in its opening—that consciousness “never catches itself coming to believe.” Its three supporting literatures concern different phenomena, and the article has not shown that they converge on a single, phenomenally absent event of assent. [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/)

The most serious defect is empirical. The article says that competing accounts of truth encoding agree that a first truth-assignment occurs below deliberate judgment. One of its own principal sources explicitly describes a Cartesian alternative in which, when processing capacity is unavailable, the proposition remains **untagged**. The claimed common ground is therefore not common ground. [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/)

Most of the direct quotations I checked are genuine. I found no fabricated cited work. The principal problem is **inferential fidelity rather than textual fidelity**: accurate sentences are repeatedly made to support broader conclusions than their sources warrant. Two references also remain openly unverified at primary-text level in the live bibliography.

## 2. Source-by-source citation audit

### Descartes and the history of doxastic voluntarism

The association of Descartes with strong doxastic voluntarism is defensible, but the article’s bare citation to 1641 is inadequate for a contested historical interpretation: it supplies no edition, translation, meditation, page or passage. Recent scholarship includes a forceful defence of the view that, for Descartes, every belief involves choice, but that is an interpretive position rather than an uncontested historical datum. [PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/BOEWEB)

The historical framing is also too compressed. Strong accounts of assent and responsibility for belief precede Descartes, including Stoic, Augustinian and medieval traditions. The current literature does not present Descartes as the unambiguous beginning of the issue. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/doxastic-voluntarism/)

**Verdict:** the reference exists and the association is reasonable, but the historical claim needs a primary passage, a modern scholarly source and an acknowledgement of interpretive and pre-Cartesian context.

### Williams, Alston and the alleged impossibility of believing at will

The article fairly invokes the familiar Williams-style argument that an attempt to believe for non-evidential reasons would undermine its own success. It also correctly distinguishes direct from indirect influence over belief.

But it omits a crucial qualification found in the very contemporary overview on which it relies: Williams’s argument is widely regarded as unsuccessful **as stated**, with Bennett’s “Credamites” case providing a prominent counterexample. The philosophical taxonomy is also less settled than the article implies. Doxastic voluntarism ordinarily needs only the voluntary control of **some** beliefs, whereas universal involuntarism says that none are voluntarily controllable. Establishing that ordinary subjects usually cannot believe arbitrary propositions on command does not establish the article’s stronger claim about “any believer” or every kind of belief. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/doxastic-voluntarism/)

Alston-style distinctions between direct, indirect and long-range control concern agency. They do not independently support the phenomenological conclusion that belief formation is unfelt. A nonvoluntary occurrence can be phenomenally vivid; pain, surprise and recognition are elementary counterexamples to the inference pattern.

**Verdict:** broadly accurate argumentative history, but presented as more conclusive than current scholarship allows and made to support an unrelated phenomenological conclusion.

### Hieronymi

The article’s Hieronymi quotation is accurate, and her distinction between ordinary action control and evaluative control is relevant. But the treatment omits two matters that materially alter the result.

First, Hieronymi explicitly accepts that Bennett’s Credamites case defeats Williams’s original argument as given. She then develops a narrower account of why belief is not produced as an ordinary action. Second, on her positive account, answering the question whether *p* positively is itself constitutive of believing *p*. The immediacy is conceptual or constitutive, not evidence that a hidden causal event has already ended before conscious judgment begins. [eScholarship+1](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt3ms6v6bz/qt3ms6v6bz_noSplash_c2cb95fc802bec02853a1ae73d9b289c.pdf)

Hieronymi also allows substantial evaluative and managerial agency over attitudes. Her view is therefore not well summarized as leaving the subject with “nothing to push on.” It rejects one model of action-like production while preserving important forms of control.

**Verdict:** quotation accurate; argumentative use materially incomplete. Hieronymi may support identifying judgment with assent rather than inserting an inaccessible assent event before judgment.

### Gilbert and the default-belief experiments

The article’s description of Gilbert and colleagues’ load experiments is substantially accurate. Participants exposed to propositions later marked false showed effects of those propositions under conditions designed to interrupt rejection, including effects on sentencing and interpersonal evaluation. [DTG at Harvard](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Gilbert%20et%20al%20%28EVERYTHING%20YOU%20READ%29.pdf)

The evidential scope is nevertheless narrower than the article claims. These paradigms measured later memory and behaviour. They did not directly measure:

- a discrete moment of belief formation;
- a phenomenal marker or its absence;
- whether participants possessed an outright belief rather than a transient representation;
- whether a missing falsity tag, response bias or source-memory failure caused the effect.

The experiments therefore support some combination of automatic processing, imperfect source tagging and resource-dependent correction. They do not by themselves reveal the phenomenology of “assent.”

**Verdict:** empirical summary mostly accurate; interpretation overextended.

### Mandelbaum

The cited paper and its central thesis are genuine. Mandelbaum argues for unusually automatic and liberal belief acquisition and against a clean separation between merely entertaining a proposition and believing it.

But describing it as the “strongest philosophical defence” is an uncited editorial ranking. More importantly, Mandelbaum’s broad functional treatment of belief does not identify a unique subpersonal truth-assignment event, much less demonstrate that such an event is phenomenally absent. His account could instead undermine the article’s event ontology: if thinking itself normally constitutes believing, there may be no additional assent operation for consciousness to miss.

**Verdict:** citation and attributed general position accurate; the claimed support for a discrete hidden event is not present in the source.

### Hasson and colleagues

The quotation and result are accurately represented. Hasson and colleagues argue that comprehension need not always entail belief and that suspension is possible. [Gwern](https://gwern.net/doc/culture/2005-hasson.pdf)

This source is awkward for the article’s synthesis. It helps defeat a simple universal Spinozan model but does not replace it with a universal subpersonal truth-assignment. If comprehension can occur without belief, an encountered proposition need not immediately pass through the event the article calls assent.

**Verdict:** citation accurate; its implications are less favourable to the article than the exposition suggests.

### Nadarevic and Erdfelder

This is the article’s clearest substantive citation error.

Nadarevic and Erdfelder describe the Cartesian model as involving an initial representation followed, where processing resources permit, by a true or false tag. When resources are unavailable, the representation can remain **unaltered and untagged**. Their results were presented as contradicting the Spinozan account. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13421-012-0251-z.pdf)

The article instead claims that both sides of the dispute grant a first truth-assignment below deliberate judgment. That is not what the cited Cartesian model says. “First truth-assignment” is the article’s attempted common denominator, not a result agreed by the competing models.

This error matters because it supports two further claims:

1. that a hidden assignment event is empirically unavoidable; and
2. that disagreement about its default value is evidence that first-person access is blank.

Once the untagged option is restored, neither claim follows.

**Verdict:** work exists, but the article’s central synthesis is contradicted by it.

### Later source-memory literature omitted from the article

The bibliography is stale relative to the article’s September 2026 publication date. A 2019 follow-up by Nadarevic and Erdfelder reported results inconsistent with the simple Spinozan account and favoured more flexible, context-dependent tagging. A 2024 preregistered model-based study with 208 participants found no predicted truth/falsity memory asymmetry across base-rate conditions and judged its results more compatible with a Cartesian account than with either a Spinozan or expectation-violation model. [Springer+1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-019-00940-6)

These later results do not settle the field, but they make it untenable to present the dispute using 1993, 2005, 2013 and 2022 sources alone and then announce a consensus survivor.

**Verdict:** material literature-update failure.

### Vorms

The quotation from Vorms is accurate: the paper argues that implausible propositions can appear to be encoded as false rather than accepted by default, challenging a simple Gilbertian model. [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10139921/1/vormsEtAl_2022_includingSMs.pdf)

What does not follow is that all propositions receive an immediate, prior-weighted truth value. Showing that plausibility affects encoding in some cases neither establishes a universal tag nor identifies the tag with outright belief. Vorms’s intervention increases theoretical underdetermination; it does not produce the clean common denominator claimed by the article.

**Verdict:** citation accurate; inference too strong.

### Shah and Velleman

The quotation concerning the transparency of doxastic deliberation is accurate. Their thesis is that deliberating whether to believe *p* is normally settled by considering whether *p*, rather than by consulting one’s preferences about believing it. They also carefully distinguish belief from assuming, imagining and accepting. [JSTOR+1](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/30043693.pdf)

The article moves from this claim to a stronger one: that the subject is “never at” the belief or cannot observe belief itself. That does not follow. Transparency is principally a thesis about how a deliberative question is answered. It does not establish:

- that standing beliefs cannot be introspected;
- that feelings of conviction are absent;
- that subjects cannot notice a transition in confidence;
- that conscious judgment occurs only after belief formation;
- that all forms of self-knowledge use the outward route.

Indeed, if consciously answering whether *p* constitutes judging or believing *p*, transparency places consciousness at the transition rather than proving its exclusion from it.

**Verdict:** quotation and narrow doctrine accurate; universal phenomenological conclusion unsupported.

### Evans

The paraphrase of Evans’s familiar outward-looking account is substantially faithful to the relevant passage. The live article nevertheless acknowledges that it was taken through Shah and Velleman rather than checked directly, which should not remain true in a finished scholarly article. Later discussion also treats the outward route as applicable over a significant range rather than as a necessary method for every belief self-ascription. A subject can sometimes report having no view, recall a settled commitment or notice uncertainty without freshly settling whether *p*. [JSTOR+1](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/48655748.pdf)

**Verdict:** broadly faithful but insufficiently checked and generalized beyond its defensible range.

### Ginet

The cited position exists, and later literature treats Ginet as a serious defender of direct doxastic voluntarism. But the live page expressly states that the examples were reported through the Stanford Encyclopedia and that the primary source was not examined. [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/)

That omission is especially problematic because recent work published before the article directly revisits Ginet and offers a modest defence of direct voluntarism for some existentially significant beliefs. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-026-05615-9)

**Verdict:** bibliographic existence confirmed, but the article has not completed its own primary-source check.

### Moore’s paradox

The article invokes Moorean absurdity as evidence that belief cannot be inspected as an inner state in the relevant way. This is too quick. Moore’s paradox has competing explanations involving assertion, rational coherence, self-knowledge, expression and the norms of belief. The oddity of “*p*, but I do not believe *p*” does not establish that belief formation lacks phenomenology or that standing belief cannot be introspectively accessed.

**Verdict:** relevant phenomenon, but presently an uncited and under-argued transfer from assertoric paradox to formation opacity.

### Continued influence and artificial believers

The reference to continued influence is plausible as a broad empirical observation, but it is uncited and does not discriminate among belief, memory accessibility, source confusion and inferential habit. It therefore cannot independently establish an initial act of assent.

The phrase extending the result to “any believer,” including artificial systems, is wholly unsupported. No theory is provided for when an artificial state counts as belief, what would constitute direct control in an artificial architecture, or whether an agent could possess read/write metacognitive access to its own confidence states. The article’s research note expressly recorded that this extension had not been investigated. [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/)

**Verdict:** both claims need either dedicated evidence and operational definitions or deletion.

## 3. The three faces do not yet identify one event

The article says that control, timing and transparency “conjoin on one event.” At present this is asserted rather than demonstrated. [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/)

| Face | Actual target of the literature | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Control | Whether a subject can intentionally acquire an outright belief | Whether acquisition has phenomenal character |
| Timing | How presented propositions and truth/falsity information are encoded and later remembered | That the encoded state is an outright belief or a discrete assent |
| Transparency | How a subject answers a deliberative or self-knowledge question | The causal timing or phenomenology of belief formation |

Four bridge premises are missing:

1. A source-memory truth tag must be the same state as outright belief.
2. Every belief must pass through one discrete first truth-assignment.
3. The inability to produce that state as an ordinary action must imply inability to experience it.
4. Looking outward when answering whether *p* must imply that no inward awareness accompanies judging or believing *p*.

None is supplied by the cited literature.

The site’s own conjunction-coalescence standard requires more than thematic similarity. The conjunction should add a joint structural claim that the components do not separately provide and should survive attempts to explain them by different mechanisms. Here the three components can readily concern different mechanisms: agency, memory encoding and rational self-knowledge. [conjunction-coalesce](/apex/conjunction-coalesce/)

There is also a deeper target-instability. “Assent” alternately denotes:

- a truth tag;
- an outright belief;
- a conscious judgment;
- an endorsement;
- a decision;
- confidence or certainty;
- the transition into a dispositional state.

If conscious judgment is itself assent, Hieronymi’s account places the subject at the event. If assent is only a source-memory tag, it may not be belief. If it is a feeling of conviction, the article has not established that it is always downstream. If belief acquisition is gradual rather than punctate, there may be no single event whose absence constitutes a void.

The current article risks **reifying an event and then treating failure to locate that reified event as a discovery**.

## 4. Problems with the control argument

The weaker premise—that ordinary subjects generally cannot acquire arbitrary beliefs merely by deciding to acquire them—is well supported as a mainstream position. The stronger universal premise is not.

Contemporary reference work describes direct voluntarism as controversial and widely rejected, but also emphasizes disagreement over the relevant conception of control, the difference between belief and neighbouring attitudes, and the existence of nonstandard but serious voluntarist theories. Empirical work on ordinary intuitions is also mixed. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/doxastic-voluntarism/)

Moreover, several works available before 29 September 2026 should have prevented a consensus presentation:

- Crowe defends a modest form of direct doxastic voluntarism for some existentially motivated cases. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-026-05615-9)
- Singh treats belief as involving a volitional commitment to truth, while distinguishing that from arbitrary belief at will. [OUP Academic+1](https://academic.oup.com/book/62410/chapter/555618642)
- Mugg develops an architecture-sensitive, graded form of direct belief control rather than a binary voluntary/involuntary classification. [OUP Academic+2](https://academic.oup.com/book/62410/chapter/555618793)

These accounts may ultimately fail, but they must be engaged because they challenge exactly the universal formulation used by the article.

Most importantly, **absence of action-like control is not evidence of phenomenal absence**. The article moves from “I cannot make myself believe *p* just by deciding” to “there is no felt event of believing *p*.” That inference is invalid without an additional theory connecting voluntariness to phenomenal accessibility.

## 5. Problems with the timing argument

The article treats several competing models as though they all identify a hidden initial verdict and disagree only about whether its default is true, false or prior-weighted. The literature does not support that taxonomy.

A more faithful map is:

- A strong Spinozan model posits initial acceptance followed, where resources permit, by rejection.
- A Cartesian tagging model permits an initially represented but **untagged** proposition.
- Plausibility-sensitive models permit prior knowledge to affect encoding.
- Flexible or context-dependent models resist a single invariant default.

This theoretical plurality does not leave a universally accepted “first truth-assignment” behind as a residue.

Nor does scientific disagreement about encoding show that introspection is blank. Researchers may disagree because:

- tasks have weak construct validity;
- later memory is an indirect indicator;
- response or guessing biases differ;
- “belief” is operationalized differently;
- multiple processes produce similar behavioural effects.

The article’s statement that the dispute is itself evidence for a first-person blank is therefore a non sequitur. A disputed third-person construct does not become real merely because introspection does not settle the scientific dispute.

The timing evidence supports a more modest conclusion: **some belief-relevant or source-relevant processing is automatic, resource-sensitive and poorly available to retrospective report**. That is important, but it is not yet the Assent Void.

## 6. Problems with the transparency argument

Transparency is the most philosophically substantial face, but it is overinterpreted.

When asked whether *p*, a subject often answers by considering *p*. That can explain why belief self-ascription need not involve inspecting an inner object. It does not prove that no first-person awareness accompanies:

- judging;
- resolving doubt;
- a rise in conviction;
- recognition;
- inferential closure;
- discovering that one already has a settled view.

Recent phenomenal-belief work presents a direct counterposition. Smithies argues that belief has a phenomenal dimension associated with feelings of conviction and that occurrent conviction can itself constitute belief rather than merely report a prior state. Such conviction can arise unbidden, so this view is compatible with involuntariness while rejecting phenomenal absence. [OUP Academic+2](https://academic.oup.com/book/62410/chapter/555618894)

That is an especially damaging omission because it separates the two propositions the article conflates:

> A belief can be nonvoluntary and yet consciously felt.

The article also says certainty is necessarily downstream. Neither Shah and Velleman nor Evans establishes that temporal ordering. A feeling of rightness, resolution or conviction might terminate inquiry, accompany the judgment or partly constitute it. To call every such report “downstream” by definition makes the claim immune to counterevidence.

## 7. Strongest missing counterarguments

### The judgment-itself objection

The strongest objection is that conscious judgment may itself be the relevant assent. On Hieronymi’s view, positively answering whether *p* is not a report of an earlier belief-making event; it constitutes believing *p*. The article assumes the opposite temporal architecture without defending it. [eScholarship](https://www.escholarship.org/content/qt3ms6v6bz/qt3ms6v6bz_noSplash_c2cb95fc802bec02853a1ae73d9b289c.pdf)

### The no-single-event objection

Belief acquisition may be gradual, distributed or heterogeneous. Perceptual belief, remembered belief, testimony, inference, self-interpretation and habit need not terminate in the same kind of event. A universal “moment of assent” may be no more theoretically warranted than a unique moment at which a person becomes convinced during slowly accumulating evidence.

### The phenomenal-conviction objection

Feelings of conviction, rightness, resolution and confidence may be genuine aspects of believing rather than merely post hoc annotations. Smithies supplies a current philosophical version of this view, while the Map’s own Noetic Feelings article makes related claims. [OUP Academic+2](https://academic.oup.com/book/62410/chapter/555618894)

### The attitude-pluralism objection

Belief, acceptance, judgment, credence, assumption and practical commitment are not interchangeable. The article uses distinctions among them to reject voluntarist counterexamples but relaxes those distinctions when treating source-memory tags as beliefs. That is asymmetric. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/doxastic-voluntarism/)

### The construct-validity objection

The behavioural experiments infer processing from memory errors or later decisions. They do not measure the putative assent event. The article needs to show that competing explanations—source confusion, familiarity, guessing, retrieval availability and residual influence—do not explain the findings.

### The counterreport objection

The article acknowledges but does not examine contemplative or phenomenological reports of observing belief crystallize. It then suggests that the accuracy of such reports is precisely what is doubtful. This is circular: evidence contrary to a universal introspective-absence claim is excluded because the claim implies that such evidence is unreliable.

The correct response would be to investigate the reports, distinguish prospective from retrospective methods and specify independent criteria for error. Until that work is done, the article should say that ordinary introspective access is uncertain or uncommon, not impossible.

### The architecture objection

The conclusion is framed as substrate-independent, but every substantial argument concerns human psychology or human norms of rational deliberation. An artificial cognitive architecture could, in principle, log confidence updates, expose internal state transitions or intentionally write to states satisfying an operational belief criterion. Whether those states should count as beliefs is a substantive issue, not something the human literature has already settled.

## 8. Tenet-protective and void-protective bracketing

The article deserves credit for one important restraint. It explicitly states that the proposed void is framework-independent, supplies no evidence for dualism, and offers no placement for minimal quantum interaction. It does not pretend that automatic or opaque cognition is inexplicable under physicalism. This is consistent with the site’s P‑V2 discipline. [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/)

The principal problem is therefore not straightforward **dualist tenet protection**. It is **void protection**:

- disagreement between empirical models is redescribed as evidence for the void;
- voluntarist cases can be reclassified as acceptance rather than belief;
- felt conviction can be reclassified as downstream rather than assent;
- first-person counterreports can be dismissed as unreliable without examination;
- Self-Opacity can be invoked as background support for the specific opacity that helps constitute Self-Opacity.

These moves create a structure in which almost any result preserves the void:

- no feeling → assent is absent;
- a feeling → it is downstream;
- voluntary control → the state was not genuine belief;
- no voluntary control → assent cannot be an action;
- competing encoding theories → the first-person blank explains the dispute;
- introspective reports → introspection is precisely what is unreliable.

That is not a genuine falsification regime.

The site’s “Voids as Evidence” page already recognizes this danger. It says cumulative evidential weight should remain suspended until the site maintains candidate alternatives, rejected cases, retired voids and negative controls. The Assent Void presently lacks those safeguards and should not be counted as an established framework-independent datum. [voids-as-evidence](/positions/voids-as-evidence/)

## 9. Coherence with surrounding Map articles

### Resistance Void

The Resistance Void says that attempts to alter belief encounter “something that refuses to move” and describes a distinctive felt character of resistance. The Assent Void says there is “nothing to push on” and no experienced failure corresponding to an inner force. [phenomenology-of-resistance-across-domains](/topics/phenomenology-of-resistance-across-domains/)

These claims can be reconciled only by distinguishing:

- the felt frustration of an unsuccessful higher-level attempt;
- the lower-level mechanism responsible for belief stability;
- initial acquisition from later revision.

The current pages do not make that distinction clearly enough. As written, one page says resistance has a distinctive phenomenology while the other uses lack of phenomenology as part of its conclusion.

### Noetic Feelings

The Noetic Feelings article says that feelings of rightness can terminate conscious inquiry, confidence can gate action, and a felt “click” of having decided can register a transition. It also allows some of these feelings to operate below fully reportable judgment. [noetic-feelings-void](/voids/noetic-feelings-void/)

This is in direct tension with the Assent Void’s claim that certainty is always downstream and that consciousness lacks a marker at the transition. The site needs a common taxonomy:

- Is a feeling of rightness evidence used in forming belief?
- Is it the phenomenal form of judgment?
- Is it a metacognitive estimate after belief?
- Can different cases instantiate different relations?

Without that taxonomy, the articles selectively classify the same sort of noetic feeling as central in one context and epiphenomenal reporting in another.

### Suspension Void

The Assent Void says the Suspension Void should carry a reciprocal qualification or link. The current Suspension page does not yet implement that integration. [suspension-void](/voids/suspension-void/)

The relation is philosophically important. If some propositions remain untagged or suspended, the absence of assent may reflect a genuine third attitude rather than an inaccessible assignment. Suspension is therefore not merely a neighbouring topic; it is a direct countermodel to the article’s timing synthesis.

### Self-Opacity

The Self-Opacity article presents limitations on access to one’s own mental processes as constitutive. The Assent Void can serve as a case study within that larger category, but the two pages cannot provide independent support for one another unless their evidential dependencies are made explicit. [self-opacity](/voids/self-opacity/)

At present there is a risk of circular accumulation:

1. Self-Opacity predicts inaccessible formation.
2. Assent is interpreted through that prediction.

## 10. A defensible replacement thesis

The evidence reviewed would support the following narrower formulation:

> Ordinary human subjects often cannot acquire an outright belief merely by deciding to acquire it. Some processing of propositions and source information occurs automatically and is imperfectly available to retrospective report. When deliberating whether to believe a proposition, subjects commonly settle the matter by considering whether the proposition is true. These facts reveal limits on action-like control and metacognitive access, but they do not yet establish a single, discrete or universally phenomenally absent event of assent.

That would preserve the article’s useful core without claiming more than its sources support. It would also leave open three genuine research questions:

- whether conscious judgment sometimes constitutes belief formation;
- whether feelings of conviction accompany some forms of belief acquisition;
- whether different belief-forming processes have different phenomenological profiles.
