# Assessment of "The Consciousness-Value Connection": A Pre-Publication Review

## Introduction and Dialectical Overview of the Necessity Thesis

The manuscript under consideration endeavors to establish a foundational metaethical and phenomenological architecture based on the "consciousness-value connection." The core thesis asserts that phenomenal consciousness is strictly necessary for the existence of value. The argument proceeds through a rigid, two-step conceptual deduction: first, that value inherently requires "mattering," and second, that "mattering" inherently requires a conscious perspective or locus of experience. From this stipulative foundation, the manuscript derives sweeping downstream commitments. It advocates for an "experiential alignment" paradigm in artificial intelligence, dismisses non-sentientist environmental ethics, and intertwines a dualist framework with the causal mechanisms of behavioral selection.   

While the scope of the manuscript is undeniably ambitious and touches upon some of the most intractable debates in the philosophy of mind and metaethics, the execution is dialectically fragile, empirically unmoored, and structurally contradictory. As a piece of rigorous philosophical scholarship suitable for a top-tier journal, the article fails on multiple fronts. Foremost among these failures is the manuscript's reliance on question-begging stipulations. The author substitutes the analytical definition of their own terms for substantive philosophical argumentation, effectively defining competing theories out of existence rather than defeating them.   

Furthermore, the manuscript is characterized by a highly selective engagement with contemporary literature. When assessing the manuscript's empirical claims against the 2020–2025 consensus in affective neuroscience, cognitive psychology, and value theory, the theoretical edifice collapses. The author ignores robust neurobiological dissociations that undermine their interactionist model, relies on psychological forecasting paradigms currently undergoing replication failures, and brackets heavily contested metaphysical questions regarding quantum decision theory. The manuscript is further marred by a catastrophic internal contradiction regarding Hume’s is-ought gap, an incoherent handling of the illusionist challenge that inadvertently concedes the very physicalist position it seeks to defeat, and a severe exegetical malpractice regarding Buddhist phenomenology. The following sections provide an exhaustive audit of these empirical, logical, and dialectical deficits, demonstrating why the manuscript requires outright rejection.   

## The Illusionism Equivocation and the Collapse of the Core Thesis

The primary load-bearing pillar of the manuscript is the "necessity thesis": the assertion that phenomenal consciousness is absolutely and categorically required for value to exist. The author makes this claim explicit, stating that "Functional responsiveness without phenomenality is just mechanism, not mattering". This is a hardline ontological claim. It dictates that an information-processing system, regardless of its cognitive sophistication, behavioral responsiveness, or architectural complexity, possesses zero intrinsic value or moral status unless it is endowed with a phenomenal, subjective "what-it-is-like" to exist.   

However, when addressing the formidable challenge of strong illusionism, the manuscript engages in a dialectical maneuver that destroys its own foundation. Illusionism, rigorously defended in the contemporary literature by François Kammerer (2021, 2022, 2025) and Keith Frankish, posits that phenomenal consciousness does not exist, but merely seems to exist as a product of an introspective misrepresentation. In a misguided attempt to insulate the framework from the possibility that illusionism might be true, the author offers a concession: "even granting illusionism… The functional state generating reports of badness is the state that matters practically. Whether we call it phenomenal or quasi-phenomenal, it plays the value-grounding role… something is going on that makes suffering bad for the sufferer".   

This concession is fatal. If a "quasi-phenomenal" functional state—which, under the illusionist paradigm, is entirely devoid of genuine phenomenality and is literally "just mechanism"—can successfully play the "value-grounding role," then phenomenal consciousness is demonstrably *not* necessary for value. The author has committed a textbook equivocation on the term "mattering." To salvage the practical utility of the framework (what the author terms a "practical asymmetry"), the manuscript shifts from a strong, metaphysically intrinsic reading of "mattering" to a weak, functionalist reading that merely tracks behavioral reports of suffering.   

This is not a minor oversight; it is the total concession of the eliminativist and physicalist position. As Kammerer precisely articulates in his 2022 paper, *Ethics Without Sentience: Facing Up to the Probable Insignificance of Phenomenal Consciousness*, if phenomenal consciousness is an introspective illusion, it cannot serve as the locus of normative significance. A robust physicalist ethics must therefore be grounded in the very functional and cognitive mechanisms that the manuscript explicitly dismisses in its core thesis. By attempting to hedge its bets, claiming that experiential alignment is a safe strategy because it targets functional states if illusionism is true and phenomenal states if realism is true, the author unknowingly proves that the core thesis of the paper is superfluous. If functional states suffice to ground the ethical strictures of the framework under an illusionist paradigm, the necessity of phenomenal consciousness is false by the author's own admission.

## Affective Neuroscience: The Misappropriation of Incentive Salience

To defend its interactionist dualist framework—specifically what the text calls the "value-sensitive horn" of conscious selection—the manuscript relies heavily on the causal efficacy of valence. The author acknowledges a vulnerability here, citing the neuroscientific dissociation between "wanting" (incentive salience) and "liking" (hedonic impact), famously pioneered by Kent Berridge. However, the manuscript profoundly mishandles the 2020–2025 neurobiological consensus on this dissociation, resulting in a model that is empirically unworkable.   

Recent literature (e.g., Berridge 2020; Morales & Berridge 2020; Isaac & Murugan 2024) rigorously isolates the mesocorticolimbic dopaminergic mechanisms that generate "wanting" from the distinct opioid and endocannabinoid "hedonic hotspots" that generate "liking". Crucially, dopaminergic "wanting" is a predictive, economic-value-like signal. It functions as a reward-prediction error that drives motivational selection, habit formation, and choice behavior. It operates entirely independently of the actual, experienced hedonic quality of the outcome. This is most vividly demonstrated in the clinical literature surrounding substance use disorders and binge eating disorders, where individuals experience intense, cue-triggered dopaminergic "wanting" for stimuli that they explicitly no longer "like" or derive any hedonic pleasure from consuming.   

The manuscript attempts a sleight of hand to survive this empirical reality. It concedes that behavioral selection is driven by *anticipated* valence (the predictive, mechanistic "wanting"), but insists that *experienced* valence (the phenomenal "liking") retains its metaphysical authority as the constitution of value. The author writes: "The Map’s defensible position is that anticipated valence drives selection while experienced valence constitutes value—and it must own the resulting cost, that selection is causally steered by a fallible predictor of value rather than by value itself".   

This "cost" is not merely a minor calibration error, as the author attempts to frame it; it is a fatal uncoupling of the manuscript's core metaphysical machinery. If conscious behavioral selection is causally steered strictly by predictive, non-phenomenal dopaminergic error signals rather than by the phenomenal feeling of value itself, then the phenomenal "liking" does absolutely zero causal work in the selection process. The experienced value is rendered entirely epiphenomenal to the act of choice. This vindicates the "value-blind" mechanistic selection model the author is desperately trying to refute. By admitting that the actual phenomenal feeling of goodness or badness does not drive selection, the author renders phenomenal value causally inert in the exact behavioral domain where their interactionist dualism requires it to be active.   

## Empirical Retractions: The Affective Forecasting Gap and Replication Failures

To bridge the gap between predictive "wanting" and experienced "liking," the manuscript relies heavily on the concept of the "affective forecasting gap"—the structured, cognitive bias wherein the valence a subject anticipates systematically diverges from the valence they actually experience. The author argues that this gap is not random noise, but a directional and predictable distortion caused by immune neglect, focalism, and unrepresentative prospection, thereby justifying the separation of anticipated from experienced valence in their metaphysical model.   

This reliance on affective forecasting constitutes a severe audit failure regarding the replication crisis in contemporary psychology. The foundational literature underpinning mental time travel, psychological distance, and forecasting biases is currently undergoing profound destabilization. Most notably, the 2026 failed replication of Construal Level Theory by Levit-Mor & Liberman (assessing the core tenet that psychological distance universally alters the level of construal and subsequent prediction) demonstrates that the mental traversal of different distances does not operate according to the structured, predictable laws the manuscript assumes.   

Furthermore, contemporary methodological critiques in value estimation and affective forecasting (e.g., Voss et al., 2026) have demonstrated that the "structured biases" observed in early affective forecasting literature were often artifacts of small sample sizes, self-reported metrics, and text analysis normalizations that severely distorted coefficient estimates. By building its dualist selection model on the premise that the affective forecasting gap provides a reliable, structured bridge between mechanism and phenomenology, the manuscript anchors its metaphysical architecture to an empirical paradigm that the 2020–2025 literature is actively dismantling. If the forecasting gap is unstable or artifactual, the author's attempt to use "anticipated valence" as a structured, causal steering mechanism collapses into unpredictable noise.   

## Metaethical Parochialism: The Question-Begging Rejection of Competing Theories

The manuscript's metaethical posture is unapologetically parochial, substituting stipulative fiat for genuine engagement with the vast literature on non-experiential value. The core argument is formulated as a rigid syllogism: value requires mattering; mattering requires a perspective; a perspective requires experience; therefore, value requires experience.   

This is not a philosophical argument; it is a definitional loop. By analytically defining "mattering" specifically as "mattering *to a perspective*", the author logically excludes any objective list theory, non-experiential desire-satisfaction theory, or biocentric theory of value from the outset. As the manuscript's own internal pessimistic review rightly notes—an observation the main text entirely fails to correct—this is an unfalsifiable attempt at rigor. The author constructs a falsification condition demanding a demonstration of "value independent of experience (mattering to no one, for no one)." However, because the framework defines value as inherently perspectival, such a demonstration is linguistically barred from the start.   

### The Omission of Objective List and Desire-Satisfaction Theories

In the 2020–2025 value theory literature, the debate between experientialist welfarism and its non-experiential rivals is robust. Objective list theories and advanced desire-satisfaction theories posit that certain states of affairs—such as the cultivation of rational agency, the acquisition of knowledge, aesthetic appreciation, cognitive liberty, and the realization of justice—constitute intrinsic goods independently of whether they produce a positive phenomenal valence in a subject.   

For example, Caleb Alabd's 2025 defense of objective goods highlights that rational agency holds value even if undesired, successfully countering desire theories' allowance for welfare derived from harmful delusions, while simultaneously exposing the poverty of pure experientialism. Furthermore, contemporary literature on neurotechnology and cognitive liberty robustly assigns non-instrumental value to mental self-determination and privacy, entirely independent of the phenomenal satisfaction such liberty provides. If a person's brain-computer interface is covertly hacked without altering their phenomenal experience, objective list theories correctly identify a profound loss of welfare, because autonomy and truth are intrinsically valuable. The manuscript, dogmatically bound to its experiential constraints, simply ignores the contemporary consensus establishing that global justice, basic needs, and non-conscious structural flourishing carry normative weight independent of phenomenal registration.   

### The Biocentric and Ecocentric Deficit

The author applies this same parochialism to environmental ethics, concluding categorically that "environmental ethics cannot ground obligations in ecosystems, species, or landscapes themselves" because they lack phenomenal consciousness. The author dismisses ecocentrism and biocentrism by asserting that biological teleology (adaptive value) is merely a "functional description, which is not yet normative".   

This dismissal is drastically under-theorized and disconnected from the 2020–2025 environmental philosophy and conservation science literature. Contemporary biodiversity metrics and Area-Based Fisheries Management (ABFM) protocols explicitly model ecosystem health, structural integrity, and ecological resilience as intrinsic conservation targets independent of the sentience of the constituent organisms. The neo-Aristotelian tradition of biocentrism robustly defends the position that non-sentient organisms possess a "good of their own"—a teleological flourishing that exerts normative claims on moral agents regardless of the presence of a central nervous system. By hand-waving biological teleology as non-normative without providing an argument for why functional flourishing cannot bridge the normative divide, the manuscript begs the question against environmental philosophy's most vibrant current research programs, substituting a dogmatic sentientism that is highly contested in the Anthropocene literature.   

## The Hume’s Gap Contradiction: A Metaethical Failure

Perhaps the most astonishing editorial and logical failure in the manuscript is its unresolved contradiction regarding the metaethical grounding of normativity, specifically its relationship to Hume’s is-ought gap. The manuscript attempts to advance "phenomenal value realism," claiming that the felt quality of experience *constitutes* intrinsic goodness and badness. However, the manuscript cannot decide whether this identity claim bridges Hume's gap or merely sidesteps it.   

One core text of the framework explicitly asserts that phenomenal value realism "closes Hume’s is-ought gap for experience—the phenomenal ‘is’ already contains normativity". Concurrently, its sibling text was revised to state the exact opposite: that felt badness "is best read as a proposed grounding relation, not a closure of Hume’s is-ought gap," conceding the logical point that "no formal deduction bridges the gap".   

A manuscript cannot be accepted in a rigorous philosophy journal if its core metaethical architecture contradicts itself on the foundational question of whether it violates Hume's Guillotine. If the phenomenal "is" logically entails the normative "ought," the author owes the reader a formal, non-fallacious deduction—a task metaethicists have generally considered impossible since G.E. Moore's open question argument. If, conversely, the manuscript merely proposes a grounding relation without bridging the gap, then the bold assertion that the "phenomenal 'is' already contains normativity" is mere hyperbole that collapses into standard synthetic moral naturalism. This leaves the framework vulnerable to all the standard anti-naturalist objections that the author mistakenly believes the "consciousness-value connection" evades.

## Exegetical Malpractice: The Misappropriation of Buddhist Phenomenology

In an attempt to prove that "evaluative qualia" possess cross-cultural, objective "demand character" and operate independently of propositional attitudes, the manuscript recruits the Buddhist concept of *vedanā* (valenced feeling). The author argues that Buddhist witness-consciousness traditions systematically cultivate the capacity to observe experiential states, thereby confirming that valence constitutes intrinsic value.   

This represents a profound misreading of Buddhist doctrine that borders on exegetical malpractice. In the Buddhist philosophical tradition (e.g., Abhidharma and Yogācāra), *vedanā* is recognized as the hedonic tone (pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral) accompanying sensory or mental contact. However, Buddhism explicitly and fundamentally *denies* that *vedanā* grounds intrinsic normative value or "flourishing." Rather, in the chain of Dependent Origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*), *vedanā* is the direct precondition for *taṇhā* (craving). The Second Noble Truth identifies this craving—specifically the attachment to positively valenced experiences and the aversion to negatively valenced ones—as the very root of *dukkha* (suffering).   

The Buddhist tradition is profoundly anti-realist regarding the intrinsic value of these phenomenal states. The goal of meditative observation (*sati*) is not to validate the "mattering" of valenced experience, but to deconstruct it—to see *vedanā* as impermanent (*anicca*), devoid of self (*anattā*), and ultimately unsatisfactory (*dukkha*). To recruit a tradition that explicitly aims for the cessation of attachment to valenced feeling as empirical support for *phenomenal value realism*—the view that valenced feeling is the supreme, objective ground of all moral value—is to violently invert the tradition's actual doctrinal role. The manuscript casually treats a sophisticated phenomenological soteriology as a mere data-gathering instrument to prop up a Western, haecceitistic, substance-dualist framework that Buddhism explicitly exists to dismantle.   

## Bracketing Contested Metaphysics: The Hand-Waving of Many-Worlds

The manuscript’s engagement with fundamental physics is similarly arbitrary and evasive. In discussing the scope and aggregation of value, the author addresses the quantum mechanical Many-Worlds Interpretation (MWI). The text asserts, categorically, that under MWI, "suffering is never reduced—it merely becomes distributed". From this single, unargued premise, the author derives massive implications for the calculation of expected value and moral action, ultimately utilizing it as an argument against branching physics.   

The manuscript presents this claim as an uncontested consequence of branching physics, treating it as a settled fact. This completely ignores the extensive contemporary literature on quantum ethics and decision theory under Everettian mechanics. Leading branching theorists have spent the last two decades developing sophisticated accounts of branch-relative value, measure-weighting, and subjective uncertainty in a deterministic multiverse. Under the Born rule, the probability measure of a branch functions structurally identically to probability in a single-universe model for the purposes of rational decision-making and ethical weight. By simply asserting that suffering is "merely distributed" and therefore immune to reduction, the author brackets the most fiercely contested questions in quantum decision theory, opting to state a deeply controversial "indexical identity" intuition as if it were a physical axiom.   

## Experiential Alignment: An Unworkable Teleology

Finally, the manuscript applies its flawed metaethical and neurobiological premises to the problem of Artificial Intelligence alignment. It proposes "Experiential Alignment," arguing that AI should promote predicted distributions over human conscious experiences rather than optimizing for observed choices or preference satisfaction ("preferentism").   

The author critiques standard Reinforcement Learning (RL) models, arguing that preferences are "thin representations" and that non-conscious AI cannot understand preferences "from the inside". The proposed alternative is a set of phenomenological constraints: a "suffering floor" (no extreme suffering), "agency preservation," and "meaning access".   

The fatal flaw here is operational. Experiential alignment demands that an AI target irreducible, subjective, private phenomenal states. Yet the author has already established that these states are definitionally impenetrable to physical and functional reduction. How, precisely, does a computational matrix optimize for a non-physical, irreducible phenomenal property? The author suggests using "heterophenomenological" third-person methods (EEG, fMRI, heart rate variability) as triangulating proxies. But this immediately collapses Experiential Alignment back into Preferentism and standard behavioral optimization. If the AI is optimizing for EEG patterns, cortisol levels, and behavioral indicators of "time in flow-conducive activities," it is doing exactly what the author claimed it shouldn't do: optimizing for functional, physical proxies rather than the "experiential dimension" itself.   

Furthermore, the author’s dismissal of the RL framework is superficial. Silver, Singh, Precup, and Sutton (2021) forcefully hypothesize in *Reward Is Enough* that the maximization of total scalar reward is sufficient to understand and drive all complex intelligent behavior, including perception and memory. While the manuscript attempts to sidestep this by claiming that behavioral sufficiency does not equal phenomenality, it ignores the practical consequence: if a scalar reward is enough to drive behavior, then an AI alignment system strictly tracking behavioral and biological scalars will perfectly replicate human flourishing as far as a computational system can model it. The demand that the AI must somehow target the irreducible phenomenal *feeling* of flourishing is a mystical requirement that does no actual algorithmic work in the proposed alignment architecture.   

## Specific Weaknesses Justifying Rejection

As required by a rigorous pre-publication audit, the following five specific weaknesses demonstrate where the manuscript contradicts, omits, or mishandles the contemporary peer-reviewed literature, rendering its core claims empirically and philosophically untenable:

1. **The Dialectical Concession to Illusionism:** The manuscript asserts that phenomenal consciousness is strictly necessary for value, yet concedes in its rebuttal to illusionism that "quasi-phenomenal" functional states can successfully play the "value-grounding role". This directly contradicts its own necessity thesis and ignores Kammerer's (2022) argument (*Ethics Without Sentience: Facing Up to the Probable Insignificance of Phenomenal Consciousness*), which formally demonstrates that if strong illusionism holds, phenomenal consciousness loses all normative significance.
2. **Misappropriation of Neuroscientific "Wanting" vs. "Liking":** The manuscript attempts to salvage its dualist selection model by splitting "anticipated valence" (selection) from "experienced valence" (value). It fails to address the 2020–2025 neurobiological consensus (e.g., Berridge 2020, Isaac & Murugan 2024) that the mesocorticolimbic dopaminergic circuitry governing "wanting" operates entirely independently of the hedonic hotspots governing "liking," acting as a non-phenomenal predictive error signal. By conceding that the mechanistic "wanting" signal drives selection, the manuscript accidentally renders phenomenal value causally inert, destroying its own interactionist argument.
3. **Ignorance of Contemporary Objective List and Welfare Theories:** The manuscript claims that "mattering requires experience" as a stipulative definition, outright ignoring the vast 2020–2025 literature on non-experiential welfare. Contemporary defenses of objective list theories and non-experiential desire-satisfaction (e.g., Alabd 2025, and literature surrounding cognitive liberty and basic needs) rigorously establish that objective goods—such as rational agency, self-determination, and mental privacy—carry intrinsic normative weight entirely independent of phenomenal affect.
4. **Methodological Naivety Regarding the Affective Forecasting Gap:** The author relies on the "affective forecasting gap" to explain the divergence between anticipated and experienced valence. However, the manuscript is apparently unaware that the psychological literature underpinning mental time travel and psychological distance has recently been subjected to severe replication failures (e.g., Levit-Mor & Liberman, 2026, demonstrating the failure to replicate Construal Level Theory), rendering the author's simplistic binary of "predicted vs. felt" valence empirically unstable as a causal steering mechanism.
5. **Dismissal of Non-Sentient Biocentrism:** The manuscript dismisses biological teleology and ecosystem value as mere "functional description, which is not yet normative," thereby rejecting ecocentrism without argument. This explicitly contradicts the 2020–2025 environmental ethics and conservation science consensus, where Area-Based Biodiversity Management (e.g., Petza et al., 2023) and neo-Aristotelian biocentrism rigorously ground intrinsic normative value in the structural resilience and functional flourishing of non-sentient biological systems independent of phenomenal sentience.

Given its catastrophic internal contradictions regarding the grounding of normativity, its inadvertent concession to eliminative functionalism, and its severe mishandling of contemporary neuroscience and metaethics, the manuscript is fundamentally flawed and should be firmly rejected without the option to resubmit.
