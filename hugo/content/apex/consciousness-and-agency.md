We believe ourselves to be authors of our actions. When you choose to speak rather than stay silent, to help rather than ignore, the choice seems genuinely yours. You could have done otherwise. You are responsible for what you did. These convictions structure our lives—our relationships, our institutions, our sense of who we are.

Yet they face a formidable challenge: if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, where is there room for consciousness to contribute anything? If your decision was determined by neural events stretching back before your birth, in what sense did *you* decide?

The Unfinishable Map's answer is agent-causal: consciousness authors action by selecting among quantum possibilities that physics, on collapse-involving readings, leaves undetermined. The agent determines which of several genuinely open alternatives becomes actual, and that selection grounds free will and the moral responsibility that depends on it.

## The Authorship Problem

The [trilemma of selection](/topics/trilemma-of-selection/) frames the challenge: determinism eliminates genuine alternatives, pure indeterminism gives randomness rather than authorship, and only mental causation preserves the sourcehood agency requires. The trilemma is a heuristic, not an exhaustive partition—event-causal libertarianism and sophisticated compatibilism each claim a fourth place—so the Map's position rests on the arguments below.

The [source-versus-leeway taxonomy](/concepts/source-versus-leeway-incompatibilism/) sorts the two canonical incompatibilist arguments. Van Inwagen's [Consequence Argument](/topics/the-consequence-argument-for-incompatibilism/) is a *leeway* argument: if our acts are consequences of the remote past and the laws of nature, we cannot do otherwise. Pereboom's [manipulation argument](/topics/the-manipulation-argument-and-hard-incompatibilism/) is a *source* argument that never mentions alternatives: an agent whose action traces to factors beyond his control is no more responsible than one covertly manipulated. [Frankfurt cases](/topics/frankfurt-cases-and-the-principle-of-alternate-possibilities/) pushed much of the field from leeway toward source framings. The Map sits at *wide* source incompatibilism (Timpe): sourcehood is fundamental, but genuine open alternatives come with it. The taxonomy does not prove that location, and source incompatibilism is not by itself libertarian: Pereboom shares the source premise yet judges indeterministic origination mere luck. The Map parts from him only on whether that origination is real.

Compatibilism is the philosophical mainstream, and its strongest versions—Fischer and Ravizza's *reasons-responsive mechanism*, Wolf's Reason View, Dennett's "freedom worth wanting"—are formidable. The [Compatibilist Symmetry Challenge](/concepts/compatibilist-symmetry-challenge/) concedes much of this: sophisticated reasons-responsive determinism plausibly secures the same deliberative phenomenology, the same praise-and-blame practices, and the same character-formation arc the Map describes, on a different metaphysical scaffold. What agent causation distinctively delivers is narrower—ultimate desert and the metaphysics of genuinely open alternatives—and the Map adopts the libertarian framing for its coherence with the other tenets, not because it uniquely explains moral practice. Within that residue the objection is this: reasons-responsiveness without sourcehood cannot distinguish an agent who acts for reasons from a mechanism that *processes* reasons. Fischer's account explains why an action is appropriate given the agent's reasons; it does not explain why those reasons are *the agent's* if the responsive mechanism is itself the product of causes the agent never chose.

The Map's libertarianism has a specific mechanism: consciousness selects among quantum possibilities. Quantum mechanics specifies probabilities but not which outcome occurs—the [definite outcomes problem](/topics/quantum-measurement-and-consciousness/)—and on collapse-involving readings that gap is real. But quantum randomness by itself gives no free will; a random choice is no more *mine* than a determined one. This is the [luck objection](/concepts/quantum-indeterminacy-free-will/), the central challenge any libertarian position must answer, and the sections that follow answer it in three moves: how consciousness operates, what kind of cause the agent is, and why selection is not arbitrary.

## The Selector Model

Consciousness, on the Map's framework, is primarily a *selector*. The brain does the computational work—representing possibilities, simulating consequences, preparing motor programs—and consciousness arbitrates among the alternatives rather than constructing them, on the [control-theoretic reading](/concepts/control-theoretic-will/) by biasing which trajectory the neural machinery settles into. Two tenets converge here. [Minimal quantum interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) demands that consciousness's influence be as small as possible; [bidirectional interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) requires that it genuinely affect outcomes. Selection among options physics has already prepared satisfies both.

A genuine difficulty follows. Selecting among neural firing patterns means influencing *macroscopic* brain states, not single quantum events. The proposed answer is amplification—small quantum biases cascading through sensitive neural dynamics into large-scale pattern selection ([mental causation](/concepts/mental-causation-and-downward-causation/) discusses coupling)—and whether it is physically realistic remains open. The objection that warm neural tissue decoheres superpositions in ~10⁻¹³ seconds (Tegmark 2000) is contested but unresolved: Babcock et al. (2024) report ultraviolet superradiance across tryptophan mega-networks, and Khan et al. (2024) find the microtubule-stabiliser epothilone B delays anaesthetic-induced unconsciousness in rats, but these are laboratory and behavioural results, not demonstrations of sustained coherence in an intact functioning brain.

Most cognition is therefore *nonconscious scaffolding*, and the channel through which consciousness acts has limited bandwidth. [Attentional economics](/concepts/attentional-economics/) reads agency as the allocation of a finite attentional resource, so that freedom scales with attentional skill; [skill delegation](/concepts/skill-delegation/) shows the selector engaging during learning, withdrawing from routine execution, and re-engaging for novel decisions—a pattern "choking under pressure" illustrates, though the [friction reading](/concepts/interface-friction/) concedes that standard motor-control models predict the degradation.

Two refinements keep the selector from being a passive menu-reader. Consciousness also *generates* options: as [the agent-causation article](/concepts/agent-causation/) argues, each movement of attention opens possibilities that did not exist before, so the agent shapes which options exist as well as which become actual. And consciousness can *refrain* from selecting, entering a [witness mode](/concepts/witness-consciousness/) in which alternatives present without resolution—a passive recipient of random events could not choose to be passive.

## Agent Causation: The Agent as Source

Most causal explanations trace event to event: the desire caused the decision; the decision caused the movement. [Agent causation](/concepts/agent-causation/) is different. The agent—a persisting substance, not a collection of events—directly causes the action, and the regress of causes stops there. [Event-causal libertarianism](/topics/event-causal-libertarianism/), on which agent-involving mental events cause actions nondeterministically with no agent-as-substance added, does not fully deliver sourcehood: if prior mental events you did not choose cause your decision, the *disappearing-agent* objection bites and you remain a channel for causes rather than their origin. The seam shows in the tradition's leading figure: Kane (2024) now rejects the event-causal *label*, insisting his theory involves no event-causal reduction of agency, yet still declines to posit an agent-substance cause. The Map takes the step Kane will not: why did you choose A rather than B? Because *you* exercised causal power in favour of A. It is this step, not the [dualism tenet](/tenets/#dualism)—which requires only irreducibility—that commits the framework to a [substance-bearing subject](/concepts/substance-property-dualism/); a reader who accepts irreducible consciousness but rejects substance-bearing agents parts company with the Map here.

Critics find "the agent caused it" a name for the problem rather than a solution. The objection presupposes event-causal reduction—that every genuine explanation must trace back to prior events—and agent causation rejects that assumption: explanation must terminate somewhere, and "the agent selected for reasons" is a legitimate terminus. E.J. Lowe and Timothy O'Connor go further: *all* causation is fundamentally [substance causation](/concepts/agent-causation/#substance-causation-the-metaphysical-foundation), so agent causation is paradigmatic rather than exceptional, a conclusion Whitehead's [process philosophy](/concepts/process-philosophy/) reaches from different premises. The question shifts from "How can consciousness possibly cause anything?" to "Why did we think it couldn't?"

Sartre supplies a structural precondition. The ability to stand apart from your own mental states—to reflect on your anger and decide whether to act on it—is his *pour-soi*; a being that merely *is* its states cannot evaluate or override them. Sartre is no substance dualist and his radical freedom owes nothing to quantum indeterminacy, but he names what the Map requires: consciousness distanced from its contents so as to select among them. That distance varies—full in [authentic choice](/topics/authentic-vs-inauthentic-choice/), attenuated in habit—so selection runs along a continuum.

## Escaping the Luck Objection

Van Inwagen's rollback argument states the objection precisely: replay time after an agent chooses A. Since the choice was undetermined, different outcomes appear across replays, yet the agent was identical until the moment of decision. The difference seems pure luck.

The Map distinguishes consciousness-selected indeterminism from lucky indeterminism through three features. First, selection is *reasons-guided*: consciousness selects A because A serves goals and expresses values. Reasons do not *determine* the outcome but they *guide* it.

Second, selection carries the [phenomenology of effort](/concepts/phenomenology-of-choice-and-volition/). Hard choices feel hard, and the felt difficulty tracks measured cognitive load—a correlation that would be coincidental if phenomenology had no functional role and is expected if it tracks real causal engagement. This is a coherence consideration, not a parsimony argument ([Tenet 5](/tenets/#occams-limits) declines to treat simplicity as evidence either way), and as the [positions register](/positions/agency-and-will/) records, deliberative phenomenology on its own is compatible with a compatibilist reading too.

Third, selection shows distinctive neural signatures. Willed attention and movement carry greater frontal theta and frontoparietal coherence than their instructed counterparts, and neural populations commit to a selected action roughly 280 ms before movement onset—signatures consistent with consciousness engaging at the selection stage, though they do not by themselves establish it. Neurosurgical stimulation adds a dissociation: one area produces conscious intention *without* movement, another movement *without* any feeling of intending. Deciding is neurally separable from execution, as a selector operating at the interface rather than in downstream motor circuits would predict.

Together these answer the rollback: the agent explains the difference between replays because the agent *selected* for reasons, with phenomenal engagement, through mechanisms that distinguish selection from reception. Reasons-guided selection, though, requires the capacity to represent alternatives before choosing.

## Counterfactual Reasoning and Deliberation

[Counterfactual reasoning](/concepts/counterfactual-reasoning/)—representing alternatives and evaluating what would follow from each—is *how* consciousness engages with the options quantum indeterminacy provides. Without it, selection would be a coin flip in phenomenological clothing; through it, selection becomes reasons-responsive deliberation. Comparative psychology shows what the capacity demands. A sated chimpanzee will not store food for future hunger because it cannot hold its current state and a counterfactual hungry-future-self together—a working-memory limit of roughly 2±1 items (Read, Manrique & Walker 2022) against about 4±1 for humans (Cowan 2001). The ape figure is a review's synthesis of the comparative data rather than a single measurement, and the cross-species comparison is contested ([the capacity gap](/concepts/working-memory/#the-capacity-gap)), but even this conservative two-fold expansion is what lets situation, counterfactual premise, and consequence be held at once.

The [counterfactual void](/voids/counterfactual-void/) marks the capacity's architectural limits. Byrne's research shows we mutate nearby features of actuality—actions over inactions, controllable over uncontrollable factors—rather than freely exploring possibility space, and radical alternatives thin to schematic labels. These constraints clarify rather than undermine the framework: agent causation requires evaluating *nearby* alternatives sufficient for practical deliberation, not surveying all metaphysical possibility—a narrow interface to action-relevant alternatives is what [Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) predicts.

## Mental Causation and the Exclusion Problem

Agent causation requires that mental states actually cause physical effects, and [mental causation](/concepts/mental-causation-and-downward-causation/) faces Kim's exclusion argument: if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, mental causes are either identical to brain states or idle. The Map offers two independent responses.

The first denies causal closure at quantum indeterminacies. There physics provides necessary but not sufficient causes; consciousness selects among outcomes physics leaves open, causing physical events without competing with physical causes. Where the closure premise fails locally there is no second sufficient cause to compete with, so the overdetermination worry [dissolves rather than needs answering](/topics/overdetermination-dissolution-under-selection-only-interactionism/). [Type-token causation](/concepts/type-token-causation/) locates the traction: sufficient physical causation holds at the type level (Born-rule statistics across instances) but fails at the token level (which outcome occurs in *this* measurement).

The companion worry—that any such influence must inject energy and violate conservation—is answered by selection without injection, the constraint [Tenet 2](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction) fixes. Among *energy-degenerate* outcomes, biasing which possibility actualises adds nothing; where outcomes are not degenerate, selection that preserves Born frequencies leaves the ensemble energy expectation intact, and conservation—a statistical, local law—is tested at the ensemble level, not trial by trial ([the conservation-laws article](/concepts/conservation-laws-and-mental-causation/) develops the accounting). A stronger reply circulates: conservation laws derive from symmetries, a non-physical influence would break the relevant time-translation symmetry, so expecting conservation at the interface begs the question against interactionism (Cucu and Pitts 2019). The Map uses that point only dialectically—an objector cannot *presuppose* conservation at the interface—and does not adopt symmetry-breaking as its own answer: conservation failure at the interface sits outside the current framework and would require revising Tenet 2.

The second response draws on Bradford Saad's [delegatory dualism](/topics/delegatory-dualism/) (2025): experiences *[preempt](/concepts/delegatory-causation/)* rather than overdetermine the default causal profile of brain states, and Saad's distinction between *universal* and *[observational closure](/concepts/observational-closure/)* parallels the quantum framework—mental causation can be genuine without producing detectable anomalies.

[Clinical neuroplasticity](/topics/clinical-neuroplasticity-evidence-for-bidirectional-causation/) is suggestive empirical support. Meta-analyses find CBT and medication achieving comparable outcomes through *different* neural pathways—top-down cortical-to-limbic versus bottom-up neurochemical. If mental causation were physical causation redescribed, the two should converge on one route; the physicalist can reply that different inputs take different routes, but must then explain why the asymmetry is so consistent across conditions. Suggestive rather than decisive: Schwartz's foundational OCD study was small, lacks same-method replication outside the UCLA group, and its protocol overlaps with standard exposure therapy.

The [argument from reason](/topics/argument-from-reason/) offers a route to mental causation that depends neither on the phenomenology of effort nor on any quantum premise. Rational inference requires tracking normative relationships—what follows from what—whereas neurons fire because of electrochemical gradients, not because firing would be *justified*. If all beliefs are fully explained by nonrational physical causes, no belief is rationally justified, physicalism included. The argument does presuppose the [phenomenal intentionality thesis](/concepts/intentionality/#phenomenal-intentionality-theory)—that grasping a conclusion as *following from* premises requires phenomenal consciousness—but on that premise it is the framework's failsafe: were the effort phenomenology doubted and the neural signatures contested, rationality itself would still argue that consciousness causes something.

## Time, Collapse, and Selection

A timing challenge remains. The Libet experiments show neural activity "predicting" decisions before conscious awareness; if the brain decides before consciousness knows, how can consciousness be the author? The neuroscience is less decisive than it looks. Schurger et al. (2012) proposed that the readiness potential reflects neural noise rather than decision preparation, and Sjöberg (2024) adds clinical data pointing the same way: patients with surgical resection of the supplementary motor area, which generates the readiness potential, retain their sense of voluntary action. Sjöberg is himself no dualist and reads the cases as removing a defeater rather than confirming conscious causation, but the Libet challenge is weakened on its own terms.

The Map's fuller resolution reframes the problem. Quantum mechanics is time-symmetric at the fundamental level, and retrocausal interpretations take this seriously: quantum events involve "handshakes" between past and future boundary conditions that are themselves atemporal. On this reading selection does not happen *at* a moment; it fixes which lived sequence becomes actual, and the selected transaction includes the neural history that preceded awareness. What looks like neural activity predicting the decision is the selected history. [The temporal article](/topics/time-collapse-and-agency/) holds this reading weakly, subordinate to a forward-in-time mechanism preference; the core argument does not depend on it.

A further extension is speculative and deliberately narrow. Where consciousness participates in [collapse](/concepts/collapse-and-time/), it may help constitute the structure of *experienced* time—the interpenetration of retained past and anticipated future that Whitehead's "concrescence" describes. The Map does not claim that consciousness creates time's arrow: its [background commitment](/tenets/background-commitments/) to objective single-outcome actualisation has prebiotic matter resolving to definite outcomes long before any observer. What survives is a local claim about lived temporal becoming, held as hypothesis.

## Moral Responsibility Grounded

If consciousness genuinely authors action, [moral responsibility](/concepts/moral-responsibility/) gains metaphysical grounding—and the symmetry challenge fixes where. For much of moral practice a compatibilist account plausibly does equivalent work, and Strawson's [reactive-attitudes](/topics/reactive-attitudes-and-strawsonian-responsibility/) account shows how much the two share: if responsibility just *is* the aptness of resentment and gratitude, the metaphysics enters only where those reactions presuppose that the agent could have originated otherwise. Where agent-causal authorship does separating work is *ultimate desert*: a wrongdoer really deserves blame because they really originated the act, with open alternatives genuinely rejected rather than merely routed elsewhere. Sophisticated compatibilism grounds desert in real capacities—identification with [higher-order desires](/concepts/frankfurt-hierarchical-mesh-theory-of-the-will/), mechanism-level reasons-responsiveness, normative competence—but these emerge from prior states the agent never authored, whereas under agent causation the agent is the irreducible source. The contrast is irreducible-versus-derivative, not metaphysical-versus-pragmatic.

That residue is contested from the opposite flank too. Pereboom (2014) argues that we could give up the *desert-based* reactive attitudes—resentment and indignation above all—while retaining much of what matters in interpersonal life. Since ultimate desert is exactly what libertarian selection distinctively underwrites, the skeptic's challenge lands on the Map's narrowest claim: if a desert-free moral life is livable, the libertarian addition buys less than it appears to. The Map holds that retribution stays intelligible only if the agent originated the act, but owns that this is where its case is thinnest.

The [consciousness-value connection](/concepts/consciousness-value-connection/) grounds candidacy for moral *patienthood*—who can be harmed—directly; moral *agency* requires the further capacities agent causation presupposes—control over conduct, access to reasons, a genuine range of options, competence to recognise what is at stake—which infants, most animals, and any minimally-phenomenal subject lack. Consciousness is necessary for that standing without conferring it, and the asymmetry helps, because patienthood grounds obligations toward beings who cannot be held responsible. Under agent causation the connection becomes practical: agents act *for the sake of* value because they are acquainted with it through experience.

Retribution becomes intelligible: the wrongdoer exercised causal power to harm when they could have caused good. Mitigation has limits, since prior causes influenced but did not determine, and character responsibility follows because character is partly self-created—cultivating attentional skill is itself an exercise of agency. That agents act against their own better judgment—[weakness of will](/topics/akrasia-and-weakness-of-will/)—fits the selector model as a lapse in selection rather than evidence against it: by the agent's own report, the selector failed to enforce what deliberation endorsed—one lens on the will, not a proof. The [full moral implications](/topics/moral-implications-of-genuine-agency/) extend to obligation, moral luck, and the demands genuine agency places on moral theory.

## Synthesis

The components—agent causation, mental causation, quantum indeterminacy, counterfactual reasoning, atemporal selection—each face objections in isolation yet form a mutually supporting structure in which each answers objections to the others. That support would be viciously circular if every element's credibility depended on accepting the rest. It is not, because some anchors are grounded outside the circle—though fewer, and less evenly weighted, than the framework's coherence makes it feel.

The **hard problem**—argued through zombies, the knowledge argument, and the explanatory gap—needs neither agent causation nor quantum mechanics. The **argument from reason** reaches mental causation with no premise about effort or collapse and survives even if the selector model is wrong. The **neural data** and **clinical neuroplasticity** are findings consistent with the framework that do not presuppose it, though the latter's foundational study is thin. The **phenomenology of effort** and the [near-independent cross-cultural convergence](/concepts/cross-cultural-convergence-on-mental-causation/) on substance-causal accounts of persons are real but discounted: the [cognitive science of intuitive dualism](/topics/cognitive-science-of-dualism/) offers a common-cause explanation of why humans everywhere find agent causation natural, which screens off the inference from universal conviction to truth. The convergence claim therefore rests on the genuinely independent subset—the neural data and the argument from reason—with the rest contributing coherence.

Around those anchors, counterfactual reasoning and creative generation make selection reasons-responsive rather than arbitrary. Significant uncertainties remain. The amplification mechanism is empirically unresolved; the temporal reading is speculative; compatibilism plausibly matches the framework across most of moral and phenomenological life, leaving libertarian agency's distinctive contribution confined to ultimate desert and open alternatives; and the [agency-void](/voids/agency-void/) adds a limit shared by every account of mental causation—consciousness cannot verify its own causal role from within, since every check is performed by the faculty under investigation. The framework's strength lies in unifying independently grounded claims, not in first-person certainty.

If the framework is correct, you are neither a passive spectator of determined events nor a victim of random fluctuations but an agent—a conscious being who selects among genuinely open possibilities for reasons that matter to you. Your selections constitute your life, shape your character, ground your responsibility. The everyday sense of authorship, so easily dismissed as folk psychology, tracks the structure of mind and world.

## Evidence and Dependency

This synthesis leans on lines of support of very different standing, and their coherence must not be counted twice. The willed-versus-instructed neural signatures, the intention/execution dissociation, and the clinical pathway divergence are externally evidenced—they hold whatever one thinks of the tenets—though each is consistent with the framework rather than probative of it, and the Schwartz programme lacks same-method replication outside its originating group. The argument from reason and the hard problem are independently argued; the former presupposes phenomenal intentionality but neither effort phenomenology nor any quantum premise, and the latter alone carries the framework's dualism. The selector model, the conservation accounting, and the atemporal reading of Libet are inherited from Tenets 2 and 3. The exclusion-argument response inherits Tenet 4's collapse realism, and the rejection of many-worlds on agency grounds rests on sourcehood *plus* a global-exclusion condition the Map [posits rather than derives](/tenets/background-commitments/). The phenomenology of effort and the cross-cultural convergence are mutually coherent only, once the common-cause discount is applied. The moral-responsibility material inherits its scoping from the [symmetry challenge](/concepts/compatibilist-symmetry-challenge/) and its value grounding from [the moral-architecture synthesis](/apex/moral-architecture-of-consciousness/); the two pieces share one premise package rather than corroborating each other.

## What Would Challenge This View?

The [falsification roadmap](/topics/falsification-roadmap-for-the-interface-model/) works the tenets through one by one; the challenges specific to the *combined* agency position are these. Near-perfect prediction of voluntary choices from prior neural states—current accuracy for simple binary choices is around 60%—would close the gap the framework requires. A materialist solution to the hard problem that explains rather than redescribes would remove the foundation. Systematic dissociation of felt effort from cognitive load would collapse the phenomenological evidence. Confirmation of many-worlds would leave no collapse to influence—though the agency case against many-worlds would fall not to that result but to the global-exclusion posit failing, since an Everettian can reproduce branch-local agency in full. And a compatibilist account delivering genuine sourcehood—why the reasons are *the agent's* in a metaphysically robust sense—would drain the motivation for agent causation. Failure of any particular mechanism, such as quantum approaches to the interaction channel, would not: it would demand another gap in physical causation, not abandonment of a case resting on phenomenology, the hard problem, and the argument from reason rather than on any specific physics.

## Relation to Site Perspective

This article draws on all five tenets. **[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)**: agent causation requires consciousness to be ontologically distinct from physics—otherwise it inherits determinism or random indeterminism—though the substance-bearing subject is the agent-causal step's commitment, not the tenet's. **[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)**: the selector model is a direct application—consciousness causally influences physical outcomes through quantum selection. **[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction)**: the influence works within what physics allows—selecting among outcomes, never injecting energy. **[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)**: the objection here is about authorship and desert, not phenomenology. An Everettian can grant the agent a branch-local history of having chosen; what branching withholds is the global exclusion of unchosen alternatives on which desert's "genuinely rejected" rests. That exclusion condition is a [posit the Map adopts](/tenets/background-commitments/) alongside sourcehood, asserted rather than derived from the agency case, so the rejection of many-worlds on agency grounds stands or falls with it. **[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)**: the tenet does not license the agent-causal posit; it refuses to let parsimony veto a posit that earns its keep by answering the disappearing-agent objection event causation cannot.
