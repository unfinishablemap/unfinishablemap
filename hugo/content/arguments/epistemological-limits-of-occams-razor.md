Occam's Razor—the principle that simpler explanations should be preferred—is the most common objection to [dualism](/concepts/interactionist-dualism/). [Physicalism](/concepts/materialism/) posits one kind of substance; dualism posits two. Case closed? The Unfinishable Map argues no. [Parsimony epistemology](/concepts/parsimony-epistemology/)—the study of when and why simplicity tracks truth—shows that parsimony fails as a decisive principle in the consciousness debate for reasons that are well-established in the philosophical literature but rarely assembled together. The precondition for applying parsimony—that competing theories explain the data equally well—is unmet, or at the least is the very point in dispute. Simplicity itself fragments into conflicting dimensions that yield contradictory verdicts. And the accounts of *why* parsimony works in empirical science do not extend to metaphysical theory choice.

## The Precondition Parsimony Requires

Occam's Razor applies when two theories explain the same data and one is simpler. This is critical: parsimony is a tie-breaker, not a first principle. It arbitrates between theories of *equal explanatory power*.

Physicalism does not explain [phenomenal consciousness](/topics/hard-problem-of-consciousness/). It correlates neural states with experience. It predicts which brain regions activate during which tasks. But it does not explain *why there is something it is like* to undergo those processes. Joseph Levine's [explanatory gap](/concepts/explanatory-gap/) (1983) and David Chalmers' hard problem (1996) both identify the same deficit: physical descriptions, however complete, do not entail phenomenal facts. The [knowledge-argument](/concepts/knowledge-argument/) reinforces this point—even complete physical knowledge fails to capture what it is like to have an experience.

Weak reductionists who accept physicalism on parsimony grounds must posit what amounts to a brute identity between neural and phenomenal properties—an unexplained correlation elevated to metaphysical law. As Josh Weisberg's *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry on the hard problem states the objection, such a brute identity "seems unprecedented in our ontology given that consciousness is a macro-level phenomenon": the brute identities physics accepts sit at its foundational level. A theory that purchases ontological economy by leaving its central explanandum unexplained has not earned the parsimony discount.

Physicalism can remain a productive research programme while falling short here. The Map's claim is narrower: physicalism and dualism do not explain consciousness equally well, and even a physicalist who disputes that must grant that explanatory parity is the point at issue, so the precondition for invoking parsimony cannot simply be assumed. As biologist Kent Holsinger observes, "cases where competing hypotheses explain a phenomenon equally well are comparatively rare" (Holsinger 1981, quoted in Baker 2022, SEP "Simplicity"). The consciousness debate gives no sign of being one of them.

## Simplicity Is Not One Thing

Even granting that parsimony has some epistemic value, which dimension of simplicity should be weighted? The philosophical literature identifies at least three, and in the consciousness debate they do not score the two frameworks the same way:

**Ontological parsimony**: fewer kinds of entities. Physicalism scores well here—one substance type rather than two.

**Syntactic simplicity**: fewer or simpler theoretical principles. Here physicalism fares worse, at least on the tally. Accommodating consciousness within a physicalist framework requires elaborate theoretical moves—[functionalism](/concepts/functionalism/), higher-order theories, [illusionism](/concepts/illusionism/)—each an alternative attempt to bridge the [explanatory gap](/concepts/explanatory-gap/), and each adding substantial theoretical machinery that the base physicalist ontology alone cannot supply.

**Explanatory simplicity**: fewer unexplained brute facts. Here too the tally runs against physicalism, which must treat the existence of phenomenal experience as a brute fact about physical arrangements—something that just *happens* when matter is configured correctly, with no deeper explanation available. Dualism's account of why consciousness exists is more direct—there is genuinely something non-physical going on—but it carries a brute fact of its own: the psychophysical laws that tie that non-physical aspect to particular physical arrangements. Which brute fact weighs more is a question the dimension cannot answer about itself.

None of these tallies licenses the inference *simpler-therefore-truer*, in either direction. The Map's fifth tenet, [Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits), binds the Map's own use of parsimony exactly as it binds its critics': a dimension on which dualism scores better is a report of how that dimension scores, and that settles nothing about which framework is true. The [positive case](/topics/parsimony-case-for-interactionist-dualism/) carries the same self-binding at its point of use.

Ontological parsimony and syntactic elegance, as the Stanford Encyclopedia notes, "typically pull in different directions"—a trade-off between ontology and ideology, in W. V. O. Quine's terms: reducing ontological commitments (fewer entity types) typically inflates ideological complexity (more theoretical apparatus). Physicalism about consciousness is a textbook instance—it achieves ontological economy by adding layers of functional, representational, or higher-order theory to explain away the apparent distinctness of experience—and dualism is the mirror instance, buying theoretical directness with a second ontological category. What the three-way scoring exposes is fragmentation: weight ontology and physicalism wins; weight syntax or brute facts and it loses; and nothing in the concept of simplicity says which dimension to weight. Applied to this debate, parsimony yields contradictory verdicts rather than a verdict.

The quantum interpretation debate illustrates the same fragmentation. Many-Worlds appears axiomatically simpler (no collapse postulate) but is ontologically extravagant (uncountable parallel universes). Copenhagen is ontologically simpler (one universe) but axiomatically richer (collapse as a fundamental process). Krizek (2017) surveys how proponents of every interpretation invoke Occam's Razor against their rivals and finds that the principle yields contradictory verdicts: because there is "no preferred direction in how Ockham's razor is used," which interpretation counts as simpler depends entirely on which dimension of simplicity is weighted. If parsimony cannot settle the interpretation of quantum mechanics, it cannot settle the mind-body problem either.

## Why Scientific Parsimony Doesn't Transfer to Metaphysics

Perhaps the strongest challenge to parsimony arguments against dualism comes from examining *why* parsimony works in science. Michael Huemer (2009) reviewed four contemporary epistemic accounts of parsimony's value in empirical reasoning—the empiricist, boundary-asymmetry, numerousness and likelihood accounts—and found that "none of the accounts of the virtue of parsimony extends naturally to either of these philosophical cases" (physicalism and nominalism). This is a striking result: not one of the standard justifications for preferring simpler scientific theories extends naturally to the domain where physicalists invoke parsimony most aggressively.

Elliott Sober reaches a similar conclusion from a different direction. His local approach, he writes, "entails that the legitimacy of parsimony stands or falls, in a particular research context, on subject matter specific (and a posteriori) considerations" (Sober 1990); a decade later he was still writing that "it may turn out that simplicity has no global justification" (Sober 2002). On this view there is no context-independent warrant for preferring simpler theories. Scientific parsimony works where it tracks specific empirical features—avoiding overfitting in statistical models, preferring theories with greater predictive scope. Those features are missing from the metaphysical debate about consciousness, where no experiment has yet distinguished physicalism from property dualism.

Giacomo Zanotti (2021) focuses specifically on the physicalism-dualism case. He argues that the physicalist's appeal to parsimony takes for granted that parsimony has epistemic value in metaphysics, and that the most promising way to supply that value is to ground it in modesty—a higher-level virtue concerning how much epistemic risk a theory runs. "Unfortunately for the physicalist," he concludes, "the modesty rationale does not ultimately favour physicalism over dualism."

The convergence is notable. Huemer and Zanotti reach the conclusion for physicalism directly—Huemer by testing the standard justificatory frameworks, Zanotti by testing the best rationale for parsimony he can find—and Sober's domain-specificity thesis explains why it should be expected: the scientific credentials of parsimony do not transfer automatically to metaphysical theory choice about consciousness.

## Historical Failures of Parsimony

The history of science provides repeated cases where the simpler theory was wrong:

**Heliocentrism** in its original Copernican form was arguably no simpler than Ptolemy's geocentric model—Copernicus still needed circular orbits and epicycles, and the system's comparative simplicity remains debated among historians of science. The "parsimonious" geocentric model held sway for centuries. Copernicus was vindicated not by simplicity but by later theoretical developments (Kepler's ellipses, Newton's gravity) that reconceptualised what simplicity meant.

**Atomic theory** posited invisible, indivisible particles when continuous matter seemed the simpler hypothesis. Ernst Mach resisted atoms on parsimony grounds into the twentieth century. He was wrong.

**Quantum mechanics** replaced deterministic simplicity with probability amplitudes, superposition, and entanglement—a vastly more complex framework that nevertheless describes reality.

**General relativity** curved spacetime itself rather than keeping flat space with forces. Conceptually extravagant, empirically confirmed.

**Biogeography**: Darwin and Wallace's dispersal theory explained the distribution of species without positing any extra geographical entities. The rival "extensionist" tradition, which posited ancient land bridges and, in Croizat's version, tectonic change, was the less parsimonious option and was condemned as ad hoc (Baker 2022). Plate tectonics later showed that continents do move, though dispersal survives in the modern account too.

A list of failures is no base rate—parsimony has often pointed the right way—but these failures cluster at moments when existing conceptual frameworks were inadequate to the domain, when scientists lacked the tools to recognise what was actually simple. Francis Crick, co-discoverer of DNA's structure, warned that while "Occam's razor is a useful tool in the physical sciences, it can be a very dangerous implement in biology" (1988, p. 138). If parsimony is dangerous in biology, it may be more dangerous still in the philosophy of mind, where the conceptual tools for understanding consciousness may not yet exist.

## Smart's Abandoned Defence

One historical episode is particularly instructive. J. J. C. Smart, in his influential 1959 paper "Sensations and Brain Processes," resisted irreducibly psychical sensations "mainly because of Occam's razor," and he stated the razor's precondition himself: parsimony decides the matter only "if the brain process theory and dualism are equally consistent with the facts." Smart later came to treat the razor as dispensable. His own *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry on the identity theory says that, given David Lewis's causal-role argument and Robert Kirk's case against zombies, "there is no need for explicit use of Ockham's Razor as in Smart (1959)." William Lycan (2009), a materialist about the mind for forty years, presses the precondition: parsimony is "a very posterior reason," called in to break a tie only when nearly all other things are equal, and "the parsimony argument does not even come in the door" until it is agreed that nothing can be found to distinguish mental states from neurophysiological ones. Even Paul Churchland—no friend of dualism—grants in *Matter and Consciousness* that the simplicity argument is "not yet a decisive point against dualism," since neither framework can yet explain all the phenomena (1988, p. 18). One of the identity theory's founders thus ceased to rest it on parsimony, and the leading materialists who still cite parsimony grant that it decides nothing on its own. Neither concession refutes physicalism, but together they leave the parsimony objection to dualism without its most authoritative sponsors. See [parsimony-epistemology](/concepts/parsimony-epistemology/) for the full case from within the materialist camp.

## Relation to Site Perspective

This article directly supports the Map's fifth tenet: [Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits). The tenet states that simplicity is not a reliable guide to truth when knowledge is incomplete. The philosophical literature surveyed here provides the academic foundation for that commitment.

The Map interprets the failure of parsimony arguments as removing what may be the single largest obstacle to taking dualism seriously. Many who find the [hard problem](/topics/hard-problem-of-consciousness/) compelling nevertheless resist dualism because it seems less parsimonious. If the arguments above are sound, this resistance rests on a principle that lacks justification in this domain.

The convergence with [Tenet 4 (No Many Worlds)](/tenets/#no-many-worlds) is also significant. The quantum interpretation debate—where parsimony yields contradictory verdicts depending on the simplicity dimension chosen—mirrors the consciousness debate exactly. In both cases, parsimony partisans help themselves to whichever dimension of simplicity supports their preferred theory. The Map rejects this selective deployment.

The Map does not claim parsimony is useless. It remains a valuable heuristic in empirical science where its preconditions are met—competing theories of genuinely equal explanatory power, subject matter where simplicity has a track record. This article makes the defensive case: parsimony cannot settle the consciousness debate, and invoking it as though it could is an epistemological error. The [positive case](/topics/parsimony-case-for-interactionist-dualism/) goes further, arguing that when all dimensions of simplicity are counted — not just substance types but theoretical proliferation, brute facts, and explanatory directness — interactionist dualism is at least as parsimonious as physicalism. Both articles stand under the same self-binding: neither infers truth from simplicity.
