## The Central Question, Answered

### Does a Bayesian treatment break the underdetermination tie?

**For genuinely empirically equivalent rivals: no, and provably not.** If P(E | H₁ ∧ K) = P(E | H₂ ∧ K), the likelihood ratio is 1, and Bayes' theorem returns the prior odds unchanged. This is arithmetic, not a contested reading. Bayesian confirmation theory gives contrastive underdetermination a precise expression; it does not dissolve it. Earman (1993), as SEP's `scientific-underdetermination` reports him, presses the point from the other side: he "regards some version of Bayesianism as our most promising form of inductive methodology" and then shows that challenges to the *long-run reliability* of Bayesian methods can be motivated by empirical indistinguishability of hypotheses stated in any language richer than that of the evidence — i.e. Bayesianism inherits the underdetermination problem rather than escaping it.

**For the holist (blame-distribution) form: yes, conditionally.** The standard Bayesian treatment of auxiliary hypotheses — SEP `confirmation` §3.5 names Dorling (1979) and Howson & Urbach (2006, 92–102) as its source, and Rowbottom (2010), Strevens (2001) and Worrall (1993) as its critics — represents auxiliaries as themselves more or less probable, so blame for a failed prediction can be distributed non-arbitrarily. Strevens (2001) argues the *standard* version "begs the question against those who claim that the problem has no solution" and offers a replacement. So Bayes offers a solution to Duhem's problem that is real but prior-relative. `duhem-quine` relies on the holist form throughout, which means this is the part of the Bayesian machinery the article actually needs — and it does not deliver a tie-break between metaphysical cores.

**Does Bayesianism vindicate the predict/accommodate asymmetry?** Only at a cost the Map should not want to pay. SEP `confirmation` §3.5 sets out the Surprising Prediction theorem and then draws the consequence explicitly: under the Principle of Total Evidence, "relevance confirmation turns out to embed a squarely predictivist version of hypothetico-deductivism," which "neutralizes the charge of underdetermination, yet it comes at the usual cost, namely, the old evidence problem" — no statement already known to be true can ever confirm anything. The Bayesian predictivist's escape (Garber-style credence over the *logical* fact that H ∧ K entails E) is charged by Earman (1992, 131) with changing the subject. Dropping Total Evidence to rescue old evidence brings the underdetermination problem straight back.

This matters for the Map specifically: **the Map's evidential base is almost entirely old evidence.** Conceivability, knowledge, Nagel-subjectivity and Kripke arguments; introspective report; the persistence of the explanatory gap — none of it is temporally novel. A Bayesian predictivism strong enough to break the physicalism/dualism tie is a predictivism under which the Map's own evidence stops confirming. That is a self-undermining move, and it is the single most useful thing this reading turned up.

### Is the asymmetry itself secure?

No — and the article should not lean harder on it than the literature supports. The mainstream position is **weak predictivism**: prediction is at best a fallible symptom of some other epistemically relevant property, not itself evidence.

- **Hitchcock & Sober (2004)** float the hypothesis "that accommodation is a defective methodology only when the methods used to accommodate the data fail to guard against the risk of overfitting" — the asymmetry is real but derivative, and vanishes when overfitting is controlled for.
- **Barnes (2008)** argues that successful prediction "testifies to the general credibility of the predictor" rather than directly to the theory — again derivative.
- **White (2003)** defends **Strong Predictionism** — that we typically have stronger evidence when a theory predicted data than when it was constructed to accommodate them — but frames it as a thesis facing "powerful objections" whose "existing arguments in support of it are seriously flawed," offering a new defence. A minority position, defended as such.
- **Syrjänen (2023)** surveys the alleged defects of accommodation (overfitting, association-hunting, fudging) and concludes "there is currently insufficient evidence to conclude that the problems of accommodation result in a predictivist advantage in theory confirmation."
- **Dellsén (2023)** argues the asymmetry can run the *other way*: knowing data were accommodated rather than predicted suggests the data were less likely to have been manipulated or fabricated, which can outweigh whatever predictivist advantage exists.

Anything the Map writes here must say that the asymmetry is contested, in the same register the article already uses for Laudan–Leplin and for Douglas's direct/indirect distinction.

### The consciousness-specific layer

Three papers, and only three, do real work here beyond generic philosophy of science:

- **Negro (2024)** — already cited by the article. His model's first criterion *is* the prediction/accommodation distinction; he adopts the Lakatos–Zahar **use-novelty** reading (a fact is novel if it was not among the facts that motivated the theory's construction) over the temporal reading, illustrating with Mercury's perihelion and then translating the debate into consciousness science via Sperling's partial-report paradigm. His "Lakatos and Bayes" subsection concedes that a formal model "will be based on some form of Bayesianism," that "a Bayesian account could complement and ameliorate the Lakatosian framework presented here," but that "a full-blown combination of Lakatos and Bayes might be hard to achieve," with quantification of his notions (disruptive power, boldness) left to future work.
- **Kleiner & Hoel (2021)** — a dilemma that bites *underneath* predictivism in this field. If a theory's predicted experience and the experience inferred from report are independent, any minimally informative theory of consciousness is automatically falsified; if they are strictly dependent, the theory is unfalsifiable. Since consciousness science infers the explanandum from report, the predictivist criterion cannot be applied cleanly here even if it is sound in general.
- **Mckilliam (2024)** — the most direct published answer to the Map's live question, and it is negative: he explains "why a theory-driven approach to consciousness science, *even one sensitive to Lakatosian norms and updating in a Bayesian manner*, may lead to divergence rather than convergence," and proposes natural-kind reasoning (on the model of the history of thermometry) as a theory-neutral alternative.

Mckilliam is the strongest single citation the Map does not yet carry. He is also the one most in tension with the article's Lakatosian framing, which is a reason to cite him, not a reason to avoid him.
