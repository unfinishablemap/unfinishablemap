## Executive Summary

Kant defines a postulate of pure practical reason as a theoretical proposition that cannot be demonstrated but is inseparably attached to an unconditional practical law (5:122). There are three: immortality, freedom considered positively, and the existence of God (5:132). They are licensed by four conditions, all of which Kant states: theoretical reason can neither prove nor refute the proposition; the need behind it is a need of *pure* practical reason grounded in the moral law, never in inclination; the resulting assent extends reason's use "in a practical point of view" and yields no synthetic theoretical knowledge; and the status is belief (*Glaube*), which is neither knowledge nor opinion. Kant contrasts a postulate with a *hypothesis*, which a need of speculative reason yields and which reaches at most "the most rational opinion". The Map's three background posits (a persisting subject, objective single-outcome actualisation, global exclusion) share the first condition and the openly declared non-demonstrability. They fail the second: they are motivated by an account of agency the Map chooses, with no apodictically certain law behind it. They also break the third in one place, since the Map's subject posit is meant to do explanatory work and could acquire empirical determination through the subject index, which Kant's postulates by construction never could. The scholarship is divided on whether even Kant's own postulates succeed (Gardner 2011 finds an "unresolved tension"; the SEP calls the argument "bold but dubious").

## Key Sources

### Kant, *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788), Dialectic, ch. II, §§III–VIII

- **URL**: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/5683 (Abbott translation, raw text retrieved and grepped this session)
- **Type**: Book (primary)
- **Translation note**: Verbatim quotes below are **Abbott** unless marked. The Gutenberg Abbott text carries no Akademie pagination. Akademie loci are taken from SEP "Kant's Account of Reason" and Gardner (2011), both of which quote the **Gregor** (Cambridge 1996) translation; where a Gregor wording is given it is quoted *as reproduced in* those sources, not from the Cambridge volume itself.
- **Pagination verified at secondary source**: primacy section 5:119–121 (SEP); definition of a postulate 5:122 (SEP); list of the three postulates 5:132 (SEP), which confirms the task's lead; "only for practical purposes" 5:133 (Gardner); preface note on the term "postulate" 5:11n (Gardner); "keystone" passage 5:3–5 (Gardner); Wizenmann footnote 5:143n (Gardner); need of pure practical reason 5:142 (SEP, Kant's Philosophy of Religion). **Not verified**: the exact Akademie page of the hypothesis/postulate contrast that opens §VIII (5:142 is the lead), of "a faith that is commanded is nonsense" (5:144 is a lead), and of the paralogisms sentence in §VI (it falls between the 5:132 list and the 5:133 passage).
- **Key points and quotes**:
  - *Definition* (§IV; 5:122). Abbott: immortality "is a postulate of pure practical reason (by which I mean a theoretical proposition, not demonstrable as such, but which is an inseparable result of an unconditional a priori practical law". Gregor, as quoted in SEP: "a theoretical proposition, though one not demonstrable as such, insofar as it is attached inseparably to an a priori unconditionally valid practical law".
  - *The three* (§VI; 5:132). Abbott: "These postulates are those of immortality, freedom positively considered (as the causality of a being so far as he belongs to the intelligible world), and the existence of God."
  - *Source in the moral law, which is itself no postulate* (§VI). Abbott: "They all proceed from the principle of morality, which is not a postulate but a law". And: "These postulates are not theoretical dogmas but, suppositions practically necessary".
  - *The link to the Paralogisms* (§VI). Abbott: the postulates lead "To that one in the solution of which the latter could do nothing but commit paralogisms (namely, that of immortality), because it could not lay hold of the character of permanence, by which to complete the psychological conception of an ultimate subject necessarily ascribed to the soul in self-consciousness, so as to make it the real conception of a substance, a character which practical reason furnishes by the postulate of a duration required for accordance with the moral law". ("The latter" is speculative reason.) This is the single most useful passage for the Map: Kant names *permanence* as what the Paralogisms could not deliver and says practical reason supplies it by postulate.
  - *No extension of theoretical cognition* (§VI, 5:133; §VII). Abbott: "Is our knowledge, however, actually extended in this way by pure practical reason... Certainly, but only in a practical point of view. For we do not thereby take knowledge of the nature of our souls, nor of the intelligible world, nor of the Supreme Being, with respect to what they are in themselves". From §VII: "the admission of this reality does not render any synthetical proposition possible" and "we can make no theoretical rational use of them at all".
  - *Hypothesis versus postulate* (§VIII). Abbott: "A want or requirement of pure reason in its speculative use leads only to a hypothesis; that of pure practical reason to a postulate". Of the speculative case: "the highest degree of certainty to which this pre-supposition can be brought is that it is the most rational opinion for us men."
  - *The bar on wish-based assent* (§VIII). Abbott: "we are in nowise justified in assuming, on account of what we wish on merely subjective grounds, that the means thereto are possible or that its object is real."
  - *Reply to Wizenmann* (§VIII note; 5:143n). Abbott: "I quite agree with him in this, in all cases where the want is founded on inclination". The moral case differs because it is "a want of reason springing from an objective determining principle of the will, namely, the moral law, which necessarily binds every rational being".
  - *Primacy* (§III; 5:119–121). Abbott: theoretical reason must accept such propositions "as something offered to it from a foreign source, something that has not grown on its own ground, but yet is sufficiently authenticated", but only where the practical interest is pure. If practical reason were "dependent on pathological conditions", then "Mohammed's paradise, or the absorption into the Deity of the theosophists and mystics would press their monstrosities on the reason according to the taste of each".
  - *Preface note* (5:11n). Abbott: the certainty of a postulated possibility "is not a known necessity as regards the object, but a necessary supposition as regards the subject... It is, therefore, merely a necessary hypothesis."
  - *Freedom's special status* (Preface; 5:3–5). Abbott: "Freedom, however, is the only one of all the ideas of the speculative reason of which we know the possibility a priori (without, however, understanding it), because it is the condition of the moral law which we know." God and immortality "are not conditions of the moral law, but only conditions of the necessary object of a will determined by this law". The footnote adds that "freedom is the ratio essendi of the moral law, while the moral law is the ratio cognoscendi of freedom."
  - *Choice within the postulate* (§VIII). Abbott: "the manner in which we have to conceive this harmony of the laws of nature with those of freedom has in it something in respect of which we have a choice, because theoretical reason decides nothing with apodeictic certainty about it".
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral on Tenets 1–4 as doctrine; structurally close to Tenet 5 in refusing to let theoretical silence count as denial, and structurally opposed to the Map wherever a posit is used to explain.

### Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Canon, "Of Opinion, Knowledge, and Belief"

- **URL**: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/4280 (Meiklejohn translation, raw text grepped). The Map's Kant page quotes Guyer/Wood; the two should not be mixed in one article without a note.
- **Type**: Book (primary)
- **Pagination**: roughly A820–831/B848–859; "doctrinal belief" near A825–827/B853–855 (**unverified**, from memory of standard pagination; SEP confirms A828/B856 for the "hateful in my own eyes" sentence, which it reports as "contemptible").
- **Key points**:
  - Kant recognises a *theoretical* analogue of practical belief. Meiklejohn: "we find in purely theoretical judgements an analogon of practical judgements, to which the word belief may properly be applied, and which we may term doctrinal belief." His example is extraterrestrial life: "I should not hesitate to stake my all on the truth of the proposition—if there were any possibility of bringing it to the test of experience—that, at least, some one of the planets, which we see, is inhabited."
  - Doctrinal belief guides inquiry without explaining: "the word belief refers only to the guidance which an idea gives me, and to its subjective influence on the conduct of my reason".
  - It is unstable: "mere doctrinal belief is, to some extent, wanting in stability."
  - SEP (Kant's Philosophy of Religion) reports that the doctrinal-belief argument for a future life "is not an argument that endures beyond the 1780s".
- **Relevance**: doctrinal belief is defined by a counterfactual test of experience. That is nearer to the Map's subject posit, with its conditional empirical route, than the practical postulate is.

### Gardner, "Kant's Practical Postulates and the Limits of the Critical System" (2011)

- **URL**: https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1343785/ (author manuscript PDF, full text read); DOI 10.1017/s0263523200000227
- **Type**: Paper. *Bulletin of the Hegel Society of Great Britain* 32 (no. 63), 187–215.
- **Key points**:
  - Reconstructs the early objection from Wizenmann, Flatt, Feder and Schulze: the moral theology "interpolates a normative relation in the absence of any rational connection".
  - Grants Kant a partial reply: the moral argument uses "a rule of inference with one instance of application only, viz. of inference from moral interest", and so "does not authorise empirical inferences from wishes or hopes that p to beliefs that p."
  - Holds that a residue of the objection survives: "the subject's understanding of why he accepts them must fall short of giving him grounds for thinking that what he believes is true", so that "reason is in one respect opaque to itself".
  - Conclusion: "There is an unresolved tension between the cognition at which the moral theology aims, and the ultra-thin subjective basis provided for it within Kant's system."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. Useful as the considered case that even Kant's own postulates carry a cost.

## Major Positions and Debates

- **Kant's position (practical postulation under strict conditions).** Where an unconditional practical law requires an end that is possible only if a theoretically undecidable proposition is true, reason is entitled to hold that proposition true, for practical purposes only. The Map's agency-motivated subject posit has the same *shape* and lacks the same *ground*.
- **The wishful-thinking objection** (Wizenmann 1787, Feder 1790, Flatt, Schulze, as reported by Gardner): a need for a belief is no evidence for it. It bears on the Map harder than on Kant, who concedes it wherever the need rests on inclination and escapes only through the moral law's universality. Gardner's verdict is that the direct objection fails and a residue stands.
- **Deflationary and "as if" readings** (Vaihinger; Ferreira 2014, Allison and Rawls per SEP). The Map does not hold its posits "as if": the tenets page calls them "chosen starting points" and asserts them.
- **Is freedom a postulate like the other two?** The 5:132 list includes it; the Preface gives it a separate status as the condition of the moral law. The Map's subject posit is motivated by agency, so its nearest Kantian relative is the freedom postulate, the one Kant grounds most firmly and the one whose ground the Map most plainly lacks.

## The Comparison (the Map's own reconstruction)

The Map's three posits, from [background-commitments](/tenets/background-commitments/): a numerically determinate subject that persists through time; objective single-outcome actualisation; global exclusion of unchosen alternatives. The register entries ([P-I1](/positions/individuation-and-subjecthood/#p-i1), [P-I2](/positions/individuation-and-subjecthood/#p-i2), [P-SC1](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc1), [P-SC3](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc3)) and the Kant page already establish that these are posited, that Tenet 4 is "argued conditionally", and that persistence has empirical discriminability *none*.

| Kant's condition | Kant's postulates | The Map's posits |
|---|---|---|
| Theoretically undecidable (neither provable nor refutable) | Yes, by the first *Critique* | Claimed; the subject posit is held open to future evidence, which is weaker than undecidable |
| Ground is an apodictically certain practical law | Yes (the moral law, "not a postulate but a law") | **No.** The ground is that agent causation needs a persisting bearer, and agent causation is itself a Map commitment |
| Need is universal, not inclination | Yes ("necessarily binds every rational being") | **No.** An Everettian or a Madhyamaka analyst does not share the need |
| No theoretical use; explains nothing | Yes ("no theoretical rational use of them at all") | **No.** The subject posit is used in the indexical objection, in survival puzzles and in the census argument |
| Status: belief, neither knowledge nor opinion | Yes | Nearest Kantian category is hypothesis or doctrinal belief |
| Openly declared as non-demonstrable | Yes | Yes |

**Similarities that survive.**
1. *Declared non-demonstrability.* Both name the proposition as unproved and say why it is held anyway.
2. *Dependence structure.* Kant's God and immortality "attach themselves" to freedom; the Map's Tenets 1, 3 and 4 share one root posit. In both systems the list of commitments is shorter than it looks, and both say so.
3. *Theoretical silence is not denial.* Kant: "I cannot prove these by my speculative reason, although neither can I refute them." This is the same move as the Kant page's use of Tenet 5 as a named premise that stops parsimony counting against the posit without counting for it.
4. *Permanence by posit.* Kant supplies the soul's permanence by postulate after showing no inference yields it. The Map's diachronic clause is in the same position, by the Kant page's own third exposure.

**Disanalogies that must be stated.**
1. *No moral law.* This is the central one. Kant's entire licence runs through a law he holds apodictically certain. The Map has no counterpart. "Agency needs it" is conditional on wanting the Map's account of agency.
2. *The Wizenmann test.* Kant's reply concedes the objection for inclination-based needs. Unless the Map can show its need is a need of reason as such, it falls on the conceded side. The honest statement is probably that the Map's posits are choices, which the tenets page already says ("chosen starting points"), and that Kant offers them no licence.
3. *Explanatory use.* Kant's postulates may not be used to explain anything in nature. The Map's subject does explanatory work. This is the "framework boundary" the Kant page already marks at A772/B800; the practical postulates do not soften it.
4. *Empirical determinability cuts both ways.* If the subject index left a subject-indexed signature, the posit would become an empirical hypothesis and stop being postulate-like at all. A Kantian postulate that could be tested would, for Kant, never have been a postulate. So the Map's posit is either weaker than a postulate (no law behind it) or of another kind (a hypothesis awaiting test), and never the same thing.
5. *Scope of what is postulated.* Kant postulates endless duration of "the same rational being"; the Map posits persistence through a life and is silent on survival. Kant's postulate is stronger in content and weaker in use.
6. *Persistence stays out of reach on both routes.* The register grades persistence's empirical discriminability *none*. So the diachronic clause is the one part of the Map's posit that cannot become a hypothesis and has no moral law to make it a postulate. It is the purest case of a bare choice in the corpus.

**Where the Map's posits sit in Kant's own taxonomy.** Three candidates: (a) *transcendental hypothesis*, permitted only defensively (already on the Kant page); (b) *hypothesis from a need of speculative reason*, reaching "the most rational opinion"; (c) *doctrinal belief*, a firm theoretical assent that guides inquiry and is defined by what one would stake "if there were any possibility of bringing it to the test of experience". Reading (c) fits the subject-index route best and brings Kant's own warning that such belief is "wanting in stability". None of the three is a postulate.
