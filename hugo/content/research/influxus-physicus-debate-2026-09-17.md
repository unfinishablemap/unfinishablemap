## Executive Summary

Between roughly 1723 and 1755, German academic philosophy reversed itself on mind-body causation. Physical influx (*influxus physicus*) — the doctrine that finite substances genuinely act on one another, so that the soul really does move the body — went from disreputable scholastic leftover to the position argued for by Gottsched, Knutzen, Reusch, Crusius and the pre-critical Kant, displacing the Leibnizian pre-established harmony that had been the prestige option. This is the one period in a major philosophical culture in which something very close to the Map's Tenet 3 was the winning academic view, which makes it a case study rather than a curiosity. The Tenet 5 lesson is real but is **not** "parsimony failed" — what actually did the work is set out under [Key Debates](#what-actually-did-the-work-the-tenet-5-question-answered-honestly). The sharpest single find is that Knutzen, the leading influxionist, conceded in print that the mechanism of mind-body influx "cannot be distinctly understood by us on account of the nature of the thing."

## Major Positions

### Physical influx (*influxus physicus*)
- **Proponents**: Johann Christoph Gottsched, **Martin Knutzen** (1713-1751), Johann Peter Reusch, **Christian August Crusius** (1715-1775), the **pre-critical Kant** (1747, 1755, 1756); earlier, Andreas Rüdiger.
- **Core claim**: finite substances genuinely act on one another. The soul really causes bodily motion and the body really causes changes in the soul. Wolff's own gloss (SEP, verbatim): "one substance produces a state in another directly through its own activity."
- **Key arguments** (as reconstructed from Lorini and Watkins 2003):
  1. **Deflate the term.** Gottsched: "influx" is metaphorical, not a literal flowing — it just names the power of a substance to act directly on another. This single move dissolves the ancient migration-of-accidents objection by denying that anything migrates.
  2. **From self-motion to other-motion.** Knutzen: a simple substance with the power to move itself must also have the power to move other simple substances; impenetrability supports this (1735, §§28-29).
  3. **Homogenise the forces.** Crusius solves the heterogeneity problem by "identifying a specific force that both minds and bodies have, namely the force to move" (Watkins 2003, p. 10, citing Crusius 1745, p. 680).
  4. **Turn Leibniz's physics against Leibniz's metaphysics.** The conservation objection assumed the Cartesian quantity of motion; Leibniz himself showed that it is *vis viva* that is conserved. Knutzen adds that Leibniz derives the conservation law from the principle of inertia, "which cannot hold for the soul" (1735, §53).
- **Relation to site tenets**: This is the **closest historical precedent for Tenet 3 (Bidirectional Interaction) winning an academic argument**, and argument 4 is a remarkable structural ancestor of the Map's own position in `concepts/conservation-laws-and-mental-causation` — both say the conservation objection smuggles in a premise about the *kind* of system under discussion. The Map should be candid that Knutzen's version rests on physics the Map does not endorse.

### Pre-established harmony
- **Proponents**: Leibniz; in the eighteenth century **Alexander Gottlieb Baumgarten** and **Georg Friedrich Meier** (Meier, *Beweis der vorherbestimmten Harmonie*, Halle 1743); **Wolff only weakly** (see correction above).
- **Core claim**: no intersubstantial causation; each substance unfolds by its own internal law in exact correspondence with the others.
- **Relation to site tenets**: The Map already covers this well — `concepts/occasionalism` §Sibling Non-Interactionist Solutions and `topics/history-of-the-interaction-problem` §Pre-Established Harmony, plus `research/pre-established-harmony-2026-07-14.md` and `research/non-interactionist-dualisms-2026-07-12.md`. **The new article must not re-explain pre-established harmony**; it should link to those and cover only the eighteenth-century *reception*.

### Wolff's own position (the corrective)
- **Core claim**: pre-established harmony is *the more probable hypothesis* about the soul-body relation specifically, but little turns on it; the monadology is doubtful.
- **Why it matters**: the standard "Wolffian pre-established harmony versus physical influx" framing — which the task note reproduces and which is genuinely widespread — overstates Wolff's investment. Both SEP and Lorini contradict it. An article that repeats it uncritically would be repeating a textbook error that two of my four best sources explicitly flag.

## Key Debates

### What actually did the work? (the Tenet 5 question, answered honestly)
- **Sides**: not a debate among the historians so much as a question the task poses. The driver asked me to say what did the work rather than assert a clean parsimony-fails moral. Here is what the evidence supports, in descending order of confidence.
- **It was not empirical.** No observation, experiment or measurement decided between pre-established harmony and physical influx. Both were, and remained, empirically equivalent about every observable event. Whatever happened, it was not data.
- **It was substantially theological and moral.** The Pietist case against Wolff was that pre-established harmony entails fatalism and therefore destroys the imputability of action — if my deeds were laid down at creation, I cannot be praised or blamed for them. Crusius's later anti-Wolffian metaphysics runs on the same engine: he "attacked determinism mainly on the score of the moral evils that must flow from any system of determinism," and held against Wolff that a sufficient reason suffices only for free actions *insofar as they are free*. **Influx won partly because it was the option that let you hold people responsible.**
- **It was substantially institutional and coercive.** The 1723 edict gave pre-established harmony's leading defender 48 hours to leave Prussia on pain of hanging; Wolff returned only in December 1740, under a new king. Knutzen held his chair in Pietist Königsberg. A doctrine need not be refuted if its professors can be exiled. ⚠️ Keep SEP's hedge — the king was "allegedly persuaded" by the deserters argument; a reported episode, not a documented deliberation. ⚠️ Also unverified: my claim that the exile was spent at Marburg is from background knowledge, not from anything I read this session.
- **And it was philosophical, but by redefinition rather than refutation.** This is the subtlest strand and the one most worth the article's space. Influx did not answer the classical objections so much as *change what it was claiming*, until the objections no longer had a target: Gottsched made "influx" metaphorical so nothing flows and no accident migrates; Knutzen and Reusch argued that Leibniz's and Wolff's own commitments never actually forbade reading the grounding relation causally. The theory that won in 1755 was not the theory that had been refuted in 1700.
- **Current state**: The historiography (Watkins 1995, 1998, 2005) treats the transition as settled fact; the *explanation* of it is where the interest lies, and I could not read the paper that gives Watkins's own explanation.

### Was Knutzen really the decisive influence on Kant?
- **Sides**: the traditional story makes Knutzen Kant's teacher and the man who converted him to influx and to Newton. A revisionist current, associated with **Manfred Kuehn**, trims this substantially.
- **The evidence I found**: Watkins 2003, p. 10 n. 7, reports that "Kuehn argues that while Kant is referring to Knutzen in this passage, the reference is to be understood as a **sarcastic put-down, not as a compliment**" (of the *True estimation*, citing Kuehn 2000, pp. 21-27). An encyclopedia summary likewise holds that recent research confines Knutzen's influence to the *True estimation*'s solution to the problem of the interrelation of substances and to Kant's acceptance of Newtonian attraction.
- **Also**: Watkins 2003 shows Kant's *solution* differs from both Knutzen's and Crusius's — Kant defines force generally, as activity, rather than attributing the same particular force (the force to move) to minds and bodies, so he need not homogenise mind and matter the way they do.
- **Current state**: live. **The article should not assert that Knutzen converted Kant to influx.** The safe, supported claim is that Kant argued for influx in a milieu Knutzen had helped make arguable, and that his own argument was not Knutzen's.

### Did Wolff retract pre-established harmony in 1724?
- **⚠️ FLAGGED AS UNVERIFIED — and I nearly recorded it as fact.** A search summary asserted "Wolff publicly retracted the pre-established harmony in 1724, but there were doubts as to his sincerity." When I fetched the SEP Wolff entry and extracted its passages on pre-established harmony verbatim, **this sentence was not among them** — what SEP gives instead is the "more probable hypothesis" line, which reads oddly if he had retracted it. ## The Kant material, quotable

All from Watkins 2003, pp. 11-12, quoting **Kant, *Theoretical Philosophy 1755-1770*, Cambridge University Press, 1992**, with Akademie pagination.

- **The principle of succession (1:410)**: "Substances can change only insofar as they are connected with other substances; their reciprocal dependence determines the mutual change of state."
- Kant introduces it, with the principle of coexistence, as a principle "extremely rich in consequences and derive[d] from the principle of the determining ground" (1:410).
- **The first argument for it (1:410)**: "Suppose that some simple substance . . . were to exist in isolation. I maintain that it could undergo no change of its inner state. The inner determinations, which already belong to the substance, are posited in virtue of inner grounds, which exclude the opposite. Accordingly, if you want another determination to follow, you must also posit another ground. But since . . . no external ground is added to it, it is patently obvious that the new determination cannot be introduced."
- Watkins's gloss: Kant gives **three** arguments, "all based on the idea that the kind of causally isolated substances invoked in pre-established harmony are incapable of undergoing change given the way in which determinations are posited by grounds in a substance."
- ⚠️ **The ellipses are Watkins's**, reproduced as they stand in his text. If the article quotes these, it should quote them from the Cambridge Edition directly, or attribute the elision to Watkins. Do not silently close the ellipses.

## The single best find for the Map

Watkins 2003, p. 10, n. 9, translating **Knutzen 1745, *Systema causarum efficientium*, §42**:

> "The very specific mode of physical influx or action by which the mind influences its body and the body in turn influences the mind, cannot be distinctly understood by us on account of the nature of the thing."

Watkins's own gloss in the body text: Knutzen "expresses a certain degree of skepticism about our ability to understand the specific causal mechanisms that pertain to mind-body interaction in particular and thus about a detailed solution to the heterogeneity problem."

**Why this is the find.** `topics/history-of-the-interaction-problem` §What the Debate Reveals was edited *today* to add the Régis horn — the interactionist who "kept dualism and genuine causation but sacrificed intelligibility, declaring the interaction real yet incomprehensible." Knutzen occupies that horn a century later, and is a better instance than Régis for the Map's purposes because he is the man who **won**: the leading influxionist, at the moment influx was carrying the field, conceded that the mechanism could not be distinctly understood. That is a sharper datum for Tenet 5 than any story about parsimony — the winning theory was not the better-explaining theory — and it sets up the Map's own position, since the quantum-selection proposal attempts what Knutzen declined.

⚠️ **Attribution discipline**: this is **Watkins's English rendering of Knutzen's Latin**, quoted from a footnote. Cite it as "Knutzen, *Systema causarum efficientium* §42, trans. Watkins (2003), p. 10 n. 9". It has not been checked against the Latin.
