## Executive Summary

The **epistemic condition** (EC) on moral responsibility asks what an agent must be aware of to be praiseworthy or blameworthy. The SEP entry is Rudy-Hiller's (first published 12 Sep 2018, substantive revision 4 Oct 2022). It sorts the field into:
- volitionism/internalism (Zimmerman, Rosen);
- weakened internalism;
- epistemic-vice accounts;
- quality-of-will theories (Arpaly, Harman, Talbert, with Angela Smith as an outlier);
- capacitarianism (Sher, Clarke, FitzPatrick, Rudy-Hiller).

**Neil Levy's consciousness thesis** (*Consciousness and Moral Responsibility*, OUP 2014) is the leading defence of a consciousness requirement. In his words it is "the thesis that consciousness of at least some of the facts that give our actions or omissions their moral significance is a necessary condition of moral responsibility" (p. vii). The consciousness in question is explicitly "an informational state, not phenomenal consciousness" (OSO ch. 2 abstract). Levy's operational notion is **personal availability**: information available for "easy and effortless recall" (p. 34). He grounds it in a broadcasting version of global workspace theory. He sets the hard problem aside ("the hard problem is not our problem", p. 38) and says zombies "lack nothing in the way of awareness" (p. 38).

**So Levy cannot be enlisted for dualism, nor for a phenomenal reading of the Map's existing claims.** On his account a zombie duplicate would satisfy the condition.

The opposition has two families, and **the queue task's lead conflates them.**
- **Attributionists / quality-of-will theorists** hold that responsibility tracks what an act or attitude expresses about the agent. Examples are Scanlon and Smith's self-described "rational relations view", with Arpaly's Huckleberry Finn as the signature case.
- **George Sher** (*Who Knew?*, OUP 2009) denies the awareness requirement on different grounds. His account is capacitarian and rests on "origination". The SEP files him under capacitarianism (§3.4), not quality of will (§3.3). Levy treats Smith and Arpaly in his "real self" chapter and Sher separately in his "control" chapter. Smith herself attacks Sher's purely causal origination relation (SEP-secondary, quoting A. Smith 2010: 523).
- "Attributionism" is **Levy's own 2005 label** for Scanlon and Smith ([V-full], JESP).

**Implicit bias** is the standard test case.
- Levy argues that implicit attitudes have "thin, and morally empty, content" and cannot ground responsibility (p. 102).
- Holroyd (2012) and Washington & Kelly (2016) argue that agents can sometimes be blameworthy for their influence.
- Smith (2012, fn 11) reads "subconscious racist reactions co-existing alongside consciously endorsed egalitarian views" as compatible with her view.
- The empirical base is weak and contested. IATs were "poor predictors" (Oswald et al. 2013). Changes in implicit measures "did not mediate changes in explicit measures or behavior" (Forscher et al. 2019). On the other side, Greenwald, Banaji & Nosek (2015) and Kurdi et al. (2019, r = .37 under favourable design versus .02) reply.
- People predict their own IAT results "surprisingly" accurately (Hahn et al. 2014). So the relevant unawareness is unawareness of an attitude's *influence on a given act*, not of the attitude's existence. Levy's own *Noûs* paper concedes "we appear regularly to be aware of the content of our implicit attitudes".

**A phenomenal version already exists in print.** Alex Madva ("Equal Rights for Zombies? Phenomenal Consciousness and Responsible Agency", 2019) argues on Strawsonian grounds that "the capacity for phenomenal consciousness is necessary for moral responsibility" [V-abs]. That is a *capacity* thesis about responsible agents, not a phenomenal version of the EC. Opponents of any consciousness requirement include:
- King & Carruthers (2012): theories of responsibility "should be framed in such a way as to be free of any commitment to the existence of conscious attitudes";
- King & Carruthers (2022): consciousness is "important ... [but] not a necessary condition";
- Semler (2024, *Canadian Journal of Philosophy*): nonconscious moral agency, including artificial agency.

**Levy's 2014 position on occurrent awareness** (do not flatten it):
- The SEP classes Levy 2014 as *dispositionalist* (2014: 34) [V-SEP].
- SEP note 3 records a shift: in *Hard Luck* (2011, chs. 5–6) Levy sided with Zimmerman and Rosen on **occurrent, de dicto** awareness. In 2014 he "clearly denies the necessity of occurrent beliefs for blameworthiness (2014: 31)", and his content is plausibly **de re** [V-SEP].

### Rudy-Hiller, F. "The Epistemic Condition for Moral Responsibility." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*. First published 12 Sep 2018; substantive revision 4 Oct 2022. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-responsibility-epistemic/ [V-SEP]

**Structure**: §1 contents and kinds of awareness; §2 revisionism (the regress argument); §3 responses (weakened internalism, ignorance and epistemic vices, quality of will, capacitarianism); §4 conclusion.

**[verbatim]** §1.1 frames three questions, the third being "whether awareness is actually required at all or whether there can be, in Sher's (2009) phrase, 'responsibility without awareness'".

**§3.3, quality of will** [V-SEP]:
- **[verbatim]**: "The key insight that quality-of-will theorists bring to bear on the debate over the EC is that an agent need not believe that her action is right/wrong ... for the action to express the quality of her will and thus for her to be praise-/blameworthy in performing it." The ellipsis drops a parenthesis.
- **[verbatim]** Arpaly's Huck: Huck "is not capable of bringing to consciousness his nonconscious awareness" of Jim's humanity. This is *SEP-secondary*, quoting Arpaly 2003: 77.
- **[verbatim]** The "Angela Smith 2005 & 2017 disagrees" line says failures of factual awareness, "such as forgetting a friend's birthday, sometimes reveal inadequate moral concern and thus are blameworthy". Smith is the outlier because most quality-of-will theorists require factual awareness.

**§3.4, capacitarianism** [V-SEP]:
- The best formulation of the EC "is disjunctive (Sher 2009: 87)". Agents satisfy it if they are aware, "or if they should and could be aware of them given the available evidence, the opportunity to adequately process it, and their cognitive capacities."
- Sher's "Hot Dog" case (Sher 2009: 24) involves the van and the forgotten border collie.
- Sher's positive relation is **origination**. Unawareness counts when it is "caused by the agent's constitutive attitudes, dispositions, and traits" (SEP paraphrase of Sher 2009: 86–88, 118).
- **Criticisms**:
  - **[verbatim]** Levy: "that responsibility consists in origination" is implausible (2014: 127). SEP-secondary; Google Books returned 0 for the phrase, an index limit, so unconfirmed in Levy's text.
  - **[verbatim]** A. Smith 2010: a purely causal connection "cannot establish the right kind of connection between an agent and her wrongdoing" (523). SEP-secondary.
  - Moore & Hurd 2011.

**§4 conclusion** [V-SEP]: part of the dispute rests on different conceptions of responsibility: accountability (volitionists, capacitarians) against attributability (quality of will).
- **[verbatim]**: "the consensus seems to be that the different conceptions are all valid, since each of them captures different aspects of our responsibility practices (Watson 1996; Shoemaker 2011; Zimmerman 2015)."

**Taxonomy placement of Sher (lead correction).** Sher sits under **capacitarianism**, not quality of will.

### Madva, A. (2019). "Equal Rights for Zombies?: Phenomenal Consciousness and Responsible Agency." [V-abs via OpenAlex/PhilArchive OAI record MADERF; venue LEAD]

- **[verbatim]**: "Neil Levy argues that phenomenal consciousness—the qualitative feel of conscious sensations—is entirely unnecessary for moral responsibility. - **Two cautions.**

## Major Positions {#positions}

### Volitionism / internalism (Zimmerman 1997; Rosen 2003, 2004; Levy 2011 *Hard Luck*)

- **Core claim.** Blameworthiness for ignorant wrongdoing must trace back to a culpable act done in awareness. The regress argument then makes blameworthiness rarer than common sense supposes. This is revisionism.
- **Relation to the tenets.** It is neutral, but it sits closest to the Map's "Self-knowledge: Responsible agents know what they're doing" (`moral-responsibility` L89). Its tracing structure is the logic of the gradient's "negligent depletion" (L108).

### The consciousness thesis (Levy 2013, 2014)

- **Core claim.** Consciousness of at least some of the morally significant facts is necessary. Consciousness here is a personally available informational state that is globally broadcast and integrated.
- **The two arguments.**
  - The real-self argument (ch. 5): only consciously available contents are "assessed for conflict and consistency" with the agent's attitudes.
  - The control argument (ch. 6): without consciousness one does not control the act's moral significance.
- **Relation to the tenets.** Tenet-neutral. It fits the Map's claim that consciousness matters for responsibility at the level of the word "consciousness" only. In Levy's sense a zombie duplicate satisfies it.

### Quality of will / attributionism (Scanlon 1998; Smith 2005, 2012; Arpaly 2003, 2015; Harman 2011; Talbert)

- **Core claim.** Responsibility tracks what an act or attitude expresses: regard or disregard, or a rational connection to evaluative judgments. Most theorists in this family still require factual awareness and deny that de dicto moral awareness is needed. Smith extends responsibility to "patterns of awareness" and unintentional omissions.
- **Relation to the tenets.** It denies the awareness requirement without denying consciousness's existence or role. It is neutral on dualism. It is the Map's Strawsonian neighbour (see `reactive-attitudes-and-strawsonian-responsibility`).

### Capacitarianism (Sher 2009; Clarke 2014; FitzPatrick 2008; Rudy-Hiller 2017)

- **Core claim.** The EC is disjunctive: aware, *or* should and could have been aware. Sher adds origination in constitutive psychology.
- **Relation to the tenets.** Neutral. **It is structurally closest to the Map's gradient**, which grades by capacity and does not excuse lapses of intact capacity (gradient L61: such lapses are "not thereby excused").

### No consciousness requirement (King & Carruthers 2012, 2022; Semler 2024)

- **Core claim.** Responsibility theory should not depend on conscious attitudes (K&C), or moral agency does not require phenomenal consciousness (Semler).
- **Relation to the tenets.** This is the direct rival to the Map's "Moral responsibility requires consciousness" and to the AI page's culpability-gap ground. Engage it; do not dismiss it.

### Phenomenal-capacity requirement (Madva 2019)

- **Core claim.** The capacity for phenomenal consciousness is necessary for responsibility. The argument runs through the qualitative character of the reactive attitudes.
- **Relation to the tenets.** It is the published view nearest to what the Map would want. It is still a capacity thesis, and nothing in the abstract makes it depend on dualism.

### Externalism about the EC (Washington & Kelly 2016)

- **Core claim.** Responsibility-relevant knowledge can be outside the head, in what the agent's community makes available.
- **Relation to the tenets.** Neutral. It is relevant to the AI page's tracing analysis.

## Key Debates {#debates}

### 1. Is awareness necessary at all?

- **Sides.** Levy, the volitionists and Madva (in the phenomenal-capacity form) say yes. Quality-of-will theorists (for moral awareness), Smith (even for some factual awareness), Sher, King & Carruthers and Semler say no or not strictly.
- **State.** The debate is open. The SEP conclusion locates part of it in competing conceptions of responsibility (accountability against attributability) and records a pluralist "consensus" that both are valid. If the EC differs by type of responsibility (Mason 2015 and Zimmerman 2017, per the SEP), the dispute partly dissolves.

### 2. What kind of awareness?

The options are occurrent, dispositional, or Levy's personal availability; and de dicto or de re. Levy moved from occurrent de dicto (2011) to personal availability with de re content (2014), according to SEP note 3 and his p. 30.

### 3. Implicit bias as test case

- **Levy.** Implicit attitudes have thin associative content and are not belief-like enough to express the real self, so the agent is not responsible. Awareness of their content is not sufficient (*Noûs*).
- **Holroyd.** Sometimes liable to blame.
- **Washington & Kelly.** Responsible, because relevant knowledge is socially available.
- **Smith.** Compatible with responsibility if the reactions embody evaluative takings.
- **Evidence status.** It is weak at the measurement level (Oswald; Forscher; Schimmack) and contested (Greenwald et al. 2015; Kurdi et al. 2019). The awareness premise is undercut by Hahn 2014.
- **Lesson for the article.** The test case is *conceptual*: an agent influenced by an association whose influence on this judgment she does not notice. It must not lean on IAT effect sizes, and it must not assume implicit attitudes are unconscious.

### 4. Access or phenomenal?

Levy holds that an informational state is enough and zombies lack nothing. Madva holds that the capacity for phenomenality is necessary. Semler holds that phenomenality is not necessary for moral agency. **This is the one debate where a dualist framework has something at stake.**

- **(a) Levy gives the letter of claim 1, not its dualist reading.** If his thesis is right, "responsibility requires consciousness" is true in the informational sense. The Map cannot read L83/L89 phenomenally on Levy's authority: his p. 38 says a phenomenally unconscious duplicate lacks "nothing in the way of awareness".
- **(b) A phenomenal EC would be the Map's own extension, and it has costs.** The extension: the morally significant facts must be phenomenally present, or phenomenally available (gradient L69's word). Madva 2019 is the published precedent for a phenomenal requirement, and it is a *capacity* thesis. The costs:
  - In ordinary agents, access and phenomenality co-occur, so no ordinary excuse case separates them. Levy pp. 66–67 engages Block's claim that phenomenal content can outrun access; do not overclaim either way.
  - The extension must deliver a verdict on a functional duplicate that lacks phenomenality.
  - Blindsight-type dissociations are contested evidence, not a clean separating case.
- **(c) The Map's own pages lean capacitarian on lapses.** Gradient L61 refuses to excuse an intact capacity whose recognition lapses. That is Sher's and the capacitarians' verdict, and it conflicts with Levy's ch. 5 revisionism, which excuses "ordinary agents who just happen not to be conscious" of the relevant facts. The Map currently asserts a consciousness requirement (L83) *and* a capacitarian verdict on lapses (gradient L61) without reconciling them. **The article should name this tension and offer the reconciliation honestly.** One candidate: the consciousness condition governs the agent's *standing* (capacity), while the epistemic condition on particular acts is disjunctive. That is roughly Madva plus the capacitarians. It is the Map's synthesis and must be labelled so.
- **(d) The AI page's culpability gap does not follow from the EC.** If the EC is satisfied by personal availability and global broadcast, a system with workspace-like architecture might satisfy it. Semler 2024 argues for nonconscious moral agency, explicitly including artificial agents. The AI page's verdict rests on the Map's interface/amplifier posit, not on any epistemic condition. The new article must say this rather than present the EC as support for the culpability gap.
- **(e) Strawsonian seam.** Strawson's excuse "He hadn't realized" (`reactive-attitudes` L40) is an epistemic excuse. Madva's argument runs through the reactive attitudes. That is a natural cross-link.
- **(f) The alief seam.** `alief-and-acting-against-belief`'s "noticing condition" (L109–113) is a felt-effort condition, not an EC. The new article should cite it for the structure of implicit attitudes and the measurement record, and keep the two conditions distinct.
