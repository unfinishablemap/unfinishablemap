## Executive Summary

The early Confucian texts answer the question "how long should grief go on?" with the rite, graded by the relationship, and not with the mourner's feeling. In *Analects* 17.21 Zai Wo proposes one year; Confucius asks whether he would then "feel at ease" eating rice and wearing embroidery, and when Zai Wo says yes, treats the answer as showing his "want of virtue". Xunzi's "Discourse on Ritual" (*Xunzi* 19, repeated almost word for word as the *Book of Rites* chapter "Sannian wen") says the three-year rite was "established in accordance with the strength of the emotions" (稱情而立文), yet ends it in the twenty-fifth month while "The sorrow and pain were not yet ended", on the ground that the time has come "for the resumption of their duties to the living"; for a cultivated mourner the feeling, followed, "will prove to be inexhaustible". Ritual works in both directions, to "cut off that which is too long and stretch out that which is too short". A run of *Book of Rites* anecdotes applies the term against the mourner's own readiness: Zilu "cannot bear" to stop and is told the reluctance is universal; Boyu weeps past the year and is told it is "excessive"; Zixia ends with grief "not yet forgotten" and Zizhang with grief gone, and both keep the same term. On the Map's reading this **relocates** Marušić's puzzle rather than dissolving it: it moves the normative question from the feeling to the rite, concedes that the reason (and, for the worthy, the feeling) persists, and justifies the rite's end by the needs of the living, which is the consideration Marušić says explains grief's fading without justifying it. Its clearest contribution is to self-knowledge: neither ease nor reluctance is trusted as a sign of where grief's proper course ends, and Zhu Xi glosses Confucius's own "Is it excessive?" over Yan Hui as grief at its height not knowing itself (哀傷之至，不自知也). ## Key Sources

### *Analects* 17.21 (Zai Wo and the three-year mourning)
- **Text**: Chinese Wikisource 論語/陽貨第十七, §17.21: 宰我問：「三年之喪，期已久矣！ … 舊穀既沒，新穀既升，鑽燧改火，期可已矣。」 … 於女安乎？」曰：「安！」 … 予之不仁也！子生三年，然後免於父母之懷。
- **Translation**: Legge (1872), Book XVII ch. XXI [read, OCR scan].
- **Key points**:
  - Zai Wo asks about the three years, "saying that one year was long enough". His reasons are social (three years without ritual practice and music will ruin them) and cosmological (in a year the old grain is used up, the new has ripened, and the woods for fire-drilling have gone through the seasons).
  - Confucius: "If you were, after a year, to eat good rice, and wear embroidered clothes, would you feel at ease?" Zai Wo says he would. Confucius: "If you can feel at ease, do it." But "a superior man, during the whole period of mourning, does not enjoy pleasant food which he may eat, nor derive pleasure from music which he may hear".
  - After Zai Wo leaves: "This shows Yu's want of virtue. It is not till a child is three years old that it is allowed to leave the arms of its parents." And: "Did Yu enjoy the three years' affection for his parents".
  - Legge's notes: the three years "comprehended properly but twenty-five months, and at most twenty-seven months"; "Tsae Wo is strangely insensible to the home-put argument of the Master"; and Legge's own verdict that the reason Confucius gives "must be pronounced puerile".
- **Zhu Xi's commentary** (*Lunyu jizhu* juan 9, Wikisource) [read]: on the ease question, 夫子欲宰我反求諸心，自得其所以不忍者。故問之以此，而宰我不察也 ("The Master wanted Zai Wo to look back into his own heart and find for himself what he could not bear; so he asked him this, and Zai Wo did not perceive it", note's translation). He glosses Zai Wo's cycle argument as 言期年則天運一週，時物皆變，喪至此可止也 ("after a full year heaven's course has made one round and the season's things have all changed, so mourning can stop here", note's translation). He cites Fan Zuyu: 喪雖止於三年，然賢者之情則無窮也。特以聖人為之中制而不敢過，故必俯而就之。非以三年之喪，為足以報其親也 ("Although mourning stops at three years, the feeling of a worthy person is without end. It is only that the sage set a middle measure for it which one dares not exceed, so one must stoop to meet it. It is not that three years of mourning is enough to repay one's parents", note's translation).
- **SEP "Confucius"** (Csikszentmihalyi 2024) [read]: Confucius "explains the origin of length of the three-year mourning period to be the length of time that the parents had given their infant child support (17.21)"; and on ritual generally, "Knowing the details of ritual protocols is important, but is not a substitute for sincere affect in performing them."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral.

### Other *Analects* passages on mourning
- 3.4 (林放問禮之本 … 喪，與其易也，寧戚): "In the ceremonies of mourning it is better that there be deep sorrow than a minute attention to observances." (Legge 1872)
- 3.26 (臨喪不哀): Confucius cannot bear "mourning conducted without sorrow" (Legge 1872). SEP "Mencius" quotes Slingerland's rendering of the same passage.
- 19.14, Ziyou (子游曰：「喪致乎哀而止。」): "Mourning, having been carried to the utmost degree of grief, should stop with that." Legge's 1872 text heads this saying "Tsze-hea" (Zixia) while his note and the Chinese assign it to Ziyou; follow the Chinese. Zhu Xi glosses it 致極其哀，不尚文飾也 ("carry grief to its utmost and do not prize ornament", note's translation): it limits *display* by feeling, not *duration*.
- 11.9 (Wikisource §11.9; Slingerland and some editions number it 11.10): 顏淵死，子哭之慟。從者曰：「子慟矣！」曰：「有慟乎？非夫人之爲慟而誰爲？」 Legge (1872): the disciples said "Sir, your grief is excessive"; Confucius: "Is it excessive"; "If I am not to mourn bitterly for this man, for whom should I mourn". Zhu Xi (*Lunyu jizhu* juan 6) on 有慟乎: 哀傷之至，不自知也 ("At the height of grief he did not know it of himself", note's translation). He then cites Hu: the extreme of grief, applied where it is fitting, is the correctness of feeling and nature.

### Xunzi, "Discourse on Ritual" (禮論, *Xunzi* ch. 19)
- **Text**: Chinese Wikisource 荀子/禮論篇 [read in full]. **Translation**: Dubs (1928), Book XIX, on the rules of proper conduct (*Li*) [read in the mourning sections; the running head is OCR-damaged]; the rendering of 19.9a in SEP "Xunzi" (Goldin 2025) [read].
- **Key points**:
  - **Two-directional measure.** 禮者、斷長續短，損有餘，益不足: the rules of proper conduct "cut off that which is too long and stretch out that which is too short; they diminish that which is too much and increase that which is insufficient" (Dubs). Dubs's note glosses the opposites as different people: the unworthy son's natural sorrow needs deepening, while "the worthy son needs to have his feelings controlled" so that he does not injure himself.
  - **The course of mourning.** 變而飾，動而遠，久而平: at each stage the dead are adorned, moved further off, and "with the lapse of time he returns to the ordinary course of life, thereby to tranquillize life" (Dubs; Chinese 久而平，所以優生也).
  - **Excess condemned.** 相高以毀瘠，是姦人之道，非禮義之文也，非孝子之情也: competing in emaciation is the way of a wicked man, not ritual's form and not a filial son's feeling. Dubs's table of contents summarises this section as funeral rites that "give a standard of normal emotional observance" (the scan's first word is OCR-damaged, so only the rest is quoted).
  - **The three years matched to feeling.** 三年之喪，何也？曰：稱情而立文 … 創巨者其日久，痛甚者其愈遲. Dubs: "three years of mourning arises because ceremonies were established in accordance with the strength of the emotions". SEP "Xunzi" (Goldin 2025): "When a wound is colossal, its duration is long; when pain is profound, the recovery is slow. The three-year mourning period is a form established with reference to emotions; it is the means by which one conveys the acme of one's pain." The entry glosses the rite as "providing suitable forms for us to express emotions that are so deep as to be potentially debilitating".
  - **The rite ends while the feeling has not.** 三年之喪，二十五月而畢，哀痛未盡，思慕未忘，然而禮以是斷之者，豈不以送死有已，復生有節也哉！ Dubs: "but the sorrow and distress is not yet ended; the thinking about and longing for the dead is not yet forgotten". (Dubs renders the closing clause as a further question, "Why should they not terminate at the funeral…"; Legge's *Lî Kî* version of the parallel text, below, reads it as a justification, which fits the Chinese 豈不以…也哉 ("is it not because…", note's gloss). The note follows Legge.)
  - **The bird and the gentleman.** Even birds and beasts circle and cry before leaving a lost mate; man's "sorrow for his parents remains unexhausted even till death" (Dubs). A depraved man forgets by evening a death in the morning; for a cultivated gentleman the twenty-five months pass like a four-horse team glimpsed through a crack (若駟之過隙), and "even if the time of mourning were extended, he would never reach its end" (Dubs; 然而遂之，則是無窮也). So the sage kings "established a mean" (故先王聖人安為之立中制節，一使足以成文理，則舍之矣).
  - **The one-year base and its doubling.** 至親以期斷: for the closest kin mourning breaks at one full year, because heaven and earth have changed (天地則已易矣) and all things begin anew. Three years is reached by doubling it to magnify the rite (加隆焉，案使倍之，故再期也; Dubs's wording here is OCR-damaged and is not quoted). Text note: Wikisource's *Xunzi* reads 四時則已無矣 at this point; the *Liji* parallel reads 四時則已變矣.
  - **After mourning, sacrifice.** 祭者、志意思慕之情也: "Sacrifice is because of the emotions produced by memories, ideas, thoughts, and longings" (Dubs). The section ends 事死如事生，事亡如事存 ("serve the dead as one serves the living, the departed as the present", note's translation). The longing that outlives the mourning term has a standing ritual form.
  - **Ritual produces the feeling too.** In ch. 20 ("On Music") mourning clothes and the sound of weeping "cause people's hearts to be sad" (Dubs).
- **Authorship of the parallel**: Dubs notes that this section is the whole of *Book of Rites* ch. 35 and "it fits in so well with the argument of this book that it seems plainly to be" Xunzi's composition. That is Dubs's judgment; the direction of borrowing is not settled here.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral.

### *Book of Rites* (禮記), "Sannian wen" (三年問, "Questions about the Mourning for Three Years")
- **Text**: Chinese Wikisource 禮記/三年問. **Translation**: Legge (1885b), Book XXXV [read].
- **Key points** (the *Xunzi* passage above, in Legge's wording):
  - "The different rules for the mourning rites were established in harmony with (men's) feelings."
  - "The greater a wound is, the longer it remains; and the more pain it gives, the more slowly is it healed."
  - The mourning ended with the twenty-fifth month. "The sorrow and pain were not yet ended", and the "longing loving thoughts were not yet forgotten", and, of the ending of the mourning dress, "was it not shown that there should be an end to the duties rendered to the dead, and that the time was come for the resumption of their duties to the living?" The *Liji* has 服以是斷之 (the mourning *dress* is cut off here) where *Xunzi* has 禮以是斷之 (the *rite* is cut off).
  - A depraved man, "when a kinsman dies in the morning, he will forget him by the evening". For the superior man the twenty-five months "will seem to pass as quickly as a carriage drawn by four horses is whirled past a crevice. And if we continue to indulge the feeling, it will prove to be inexhaustible."
  - "Therefore the ancient kings determined the proper medium for mourning, and appointed its definite terms. As soon as it was sufficient for the elegant expression of the varied feeling, it was to be laid aside."
  - "in the case of the nearest kindred, there is a break in it at the end of a year", because "the four seasons have gone through their changes"; three years comes "From the wish to make it greater and more impressive, the time is doubled, and so embraces two round years."
  - "the feeling of man on the death of his kindred remains unexhausted even till death".

### *Book of Rites*, "Tangong shang" (檀弓上): applying the term against the mourner
- **Text**: Chinese Wikisource 禮記/檀弓上. **Translation**: Legge (1885a), Book II [read].
- **Key points**:
  - **Confucius after the *xiang* sacrifice** (孔子既祥，五日彈琴而不成聲，十日而成笙歌): "Confucius, after the service at the close of the one year's mourning, in five days more (began to) handle his lute, but brought no perfect sounds from it; in ten days he played on the organ and sang to it". Legge's 1872 *Life* tells it of the end of three years' mourning for his mother: "Five days after the mourning was expired, he played on his lute but could not sing." Legge's two renderings disagree on which sacrifice is meant (Zhu Xi's note on 17.21 places the "lesser" *xiang* 小祥 at one year, 期而小祥); state only that the lute failed after a ritual milestone.
  - **Zilu and his sister** (子路有姊之喪，可以除之矣，而弗除也 … 吾寡兄弟而弗忍也 … 先王制禮。行道之人皆弗忍也): Zilu "might have ended his mourning for his eldest sister, he still did not do so". He says: "I have but few brothers, and I cannot bear to do so." Confucius replies (Legge's bracketed reading): "they could not bear (to cease mourning) even for (ordinary) men on the roads". Zilu then "forthwith left off his mourning". The Chinese 行道之人皆弗忍也 can also be read as "everyone who walks the Way cannot bear it" (note's translation); on both readings the reluctance is not special to Zilu and is not a reason to continue.
  - **Boyu and his mother** (伯魚之母死，期而猶哭 … 嘻！其甚也): Boyu "kept on wailing for her after the year". Confucius: "Ah! (such a demonstration) is excessive." Boyu "forthwith gave up wailing". Legge's note: "it has been supposed that his mother had been divorced", which would shorten the term owed; the case may concern rank rather than duration as such.
  - **Zisi** (先王之制禮也，過之者，俯而就之；不至焉者，跂而及之): "those who would go beyond them should stoop down to them, and those who do not reach them should stand on tip-toe to do so".
  - **Zixia and Zizhang** (哀未忘也，先王制禮而弗敢過也 / 先王制禮，不敢不至焉): given a lute after mourning, Zixia "tried to tune it, but could hardly do so; he touched it, but brought no melody from it. He rose up and said, 'I have not yet forgotten my grief. The ancient kings framed the rules of ceremony, and I dare not go beyond them". Zizhang tuned it easily and said: "The ancient kings framed the rules of ceremony, and I do not dare not to come up to them." (Only the *Liji* version of this pair was read.)
  - **The man of Pian** (哀則哀矣，而難爲繼也。夫禮，爲可傳也，爲可繼也，故哭踊有節): of a man weeping like a child for his mother, Confucius says: "This is grief indeed, but it would be difficult to con- tinue it. Now the rules of ceremony require to be handed down, and to be perpetuated. Hence the wailing and leaping are subject to fixed regulations." The measure is set by what can be sustained and transmitted, not by the strength of one mourner's grief.

### *Classic of Filial Piety* (孝經), ch. 18 "Mourning for Parents" (喪親)
- **Text**: Chinese Wikisource 今文孝經: 三日而食，教民無以死傷生，毀不滅性 … 喪不過三年，示民有終也. **Translation**: Legge (1879), SBE 3 [read].
- "the living should not be injured on account of the dead"; "The period of mourning does not go beyond three years, to show the people that it must have an end."

### *Mencius* 3A2, 3A5, 7A39
- **Text**: Chinese Wikisource 孟子/滕文公上, 孟子/盡心上. **Translation**: Legge (1875) [read].
- **3A2** (Duke Wen of Teng): Mencius says three years' mourning, coarse cloth and thin congee "have been equally prescribed by the three dynasties, and are binding on all, from the son of Heaven to the common people". The duke's kin and officials object that no former ruler of Lu or Teng kept it; the duke keeps it, and at the burial those who come to condole, seeing his grief, are "greatly pleased".
- **3A5** (against the Mohist Yi Zhi): burial began when people who had left their parents' bodies in a ditch passed by and saw them being eaten. "The perspiration started out upon their foreheads, and they looked away, because they could not bear the sight." (其顙有泚，睨而不視。夫泚也，非為人泚，中心達於面目.) "The emotions of their hearts affected their faces and eyes". Mencius grounds the rite in spontaneous feeling, the opposite pole to Xunzi's emphasis on artifice.
- **7A39**: King Xuan of Qi "wanted to shorten the period of mourning"; Gongsun Chou suggests "To have a whole year's mourning is better than doing away with it altogether", and Mencius compares that to telling a man twisting his elder brother's arm to do it gently. For a prince prevented from mourning his mother, Mencius says it is a case "where the party wishes to complete the whole period, but finds it impossible to do so", and even a day more is better than none (是欲終之而不可得也，雖加一日愈於已). The obligation concerns the will to complete the term, not the feeling's course.

### The Mohist critique
- **SEP "Mencius"** (Van Norden 2024) [read]: Mozi criticised the Confucians, "regarding as wasteful such Confucian practices as elaborate funerals, lengthy mourning periods, and musical performances".
- *Mozi* "Jiezang xia" (節葬下, Chinese Wikisource) [read]: the three years leave mourners unable to work; 計久喪爲久禁從事者也 ("reckon long mourning as a long prohibition of work", note's translation). This is a consequentialist measure of duration, against which the Confucian texts argue.

### Modern scholarship (abstract-level unless marked)

## Major Positions

### Rite-measure (Xunzi; the *Book of Rites*)
- **Proponents**: *Xunzi* 19; *Liji* "Sannian wen" and "Tangong"; Fan Zuyu and Zhu Xi in commentary.
- **Core claim**: grief's form and duration are fixed by ritual graded to the relationship (closeness and rank: 別親疏貴賤之節), calibrated by the sage kings to the feeling appropriate to that relationship, working both to extend shallow grief and to cut off deep grief.
- **Key arguments**: the feeling of a cultivated mourner is inexhaustible, so a term cannot be read off it; the feeling of a depraved one is momentary, so a term cannot be read off that either; the living must return to life; what can be transmitted and continued must have fixed measures.
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral. A naturalistic, socially grounded account; no tenet engaged.

### Feeling-root (Mencius; the feeling side of the *Analects*)
- **Proponents**: Mencius 3A5; *Analects* 3.4, 3.26, 19.14; Liu (2006) on 17.21.
- **Core claim**: the rites grow from and must express genuine feeling; ritual without grief is worthless.
- **Relation**: compatible with the rite-measure view on duration. The *Analects* combines both: the test is put in terms of feeling (ease), the term is not adjusted to the answer.

### Consequentialist shortening (Mohists; Zai Wo's social argument; King Xuan)
- **Core claim**: long mourning costs work, health and social practice; a year suffices.
- **Relation**: the Confucian texts reject it; Xunzi concedes the one-year base (the cosmic round), then doubles it.

### Modern readings
- **Mixed-affect process** (Chan et al. 2026): ritual sustains, guides and transforms sorrow over time.
- **Sincere vs sarcastic permission** (Choo 2019; Foust 2021): whether "do it" is real advice.
- **Critiques**: Legge (1872) called the 17.21 reasoning "puerile"; Fraser (2013) says Xunzi's ritual theory does not face death as natural and inexorable; Ing (2012) shows the *Liji* itself treating ritual's outcomes as uncertain.

## Key Debates

### Is the measure set by feeling or by form?
- **Sides**: 稱情而立文 and *Analects* 3.4 point to feeling; the twenty-fifth-month end while the sorrow is not yet ended, the Zilu, Boyu and Zixia/Zizhang anecdotes, and Fan Zuyu's remark that the feeling of a worthy person is without end (賢者之情則無窮) point to form.
- **Core disagreement**: whose feeling the form is matched to. Chenyang Li's neutral reading of *qing* ("people's normal feelings") answers: a typical, situation-appropriate feeling, not the individual's.
- **Current state**: open in the literature; the texts read here support reading the form as fixed and calibrated to a normal feeling.

### Is the length principled or conventional?
- **Sides**: the texts derive one year from the cosmic round and double it to magnify the rite (Xunzi) or "From the wish to make it greater and more impressive" (Legge's *Liji*); 17.21 derives three years from infancy in the parents' arms. Against: Legge's "puerile"; the Mohists; Zai Wo's cosmic-round argument is the *same* argument Xunzi uses for the one-year base (this parallel is the note's observation; Zhu Xi glosses Zai Wo in nearly the same words as Xunzi, but no source read draws the connection).
- **Current state**: the derivation is partly conventional on its own terms; the doubling is justified by honour, not by feeling.

### Can ritual guide grief reliably?
- Ing (2012): the *Liji* shows ritual failure as ambiguous. Fraser (2013): Xunzi's approach does not address death as natural. The Map should not present the rite as a solved external instrument.

## The Map-Specific Question {#map-question}

Everything in this section is the Map's analysis. No source read connects Confucian mourning to Marušić.

### 1. Dissolve, relocate, or beg?

**It does not dissolve the puzzle.** Marušić's puzzle is about the *feeling*: grief fades while its reason is unchanged, and the fading does not seem mistaken. The Confucian texts do not show that the fading of feeling is fitting. On the contrary, they deny that the best mourner's feeling fades on schedule: "if we continue to indulge the feeling, it will prove to be inexhaustible"; Fan Zuyu's 賢者之情則無窮 (the feeling of a worthy person is without end; note's translation).

**It relocates it.** The texts move the normative question from the feeling to the rite. What ends at twenty-five months is mourning: the dress, the diet, the abstentions, the withdrawal from office. Its end is justified by "the resumption of their duties to the living", by the need that "the living should not be injured on account of the dead", and "to show the people that it must have an end". The feeling that outlasts the term is not declared mistaken. It is given another ritual home, sacrifice, which is "because of the emotions produced by memories, ideas, thoughts, and longings". This sits close to the re-establishment reading the bereavement void credits to Fuchs: the relation continues in another form.

**On one point it begs the question, or rather denies Marušić's premise.** The reasons it gives for ending mourning are the needs of the living, which is exactly the class of consideration Marušić says *explains* the fading without *justifying* it. The Confucian answer works only if obligations to the living are reasons that bear on how one should respond to the dead, that is, only if grief's response is answerable to more than the loss. That is a substantive disagreement with Marušić's framing, not a solution within it. A second dependence: the measure's authority rests on the sage kings' calibration (立中制節), and the texts' own derivation of the length is partly conventional (the one-year round is doubled for honour). Ratcliffe, Richardson and Millar (2023, via the PGD note) dissolve the puzzle a different way, by taking grief's object to be a loss of possibilities engaged with over time; Olberding's "it is not death, but loss that is the spur to grief" points in a similar direction, but she does not address Marušić.

### 2. Can a mourner know from inside when grief has run its proper course?

The texts answer no, in both directions, and they do so by cases.
- **Premature ease** (17.21): Zai Wo's ease after a year is treated as a defect, and Zhu Xi glosses it as his failure to perceive what he could not bear (宰我不察).
- **Universal reluctance** (Zilu): the feeling of not being ready is shared by everyone and is therefore not evidence that this mourner's grief has further to run.
- **Excess unseen from inside** (Boyu; Confucius over Yan Hui): Boyu's wailing past the year is called "excessive" by another; Confucius has to ask "Is it excessive", and Zhu Xi reads the question as grief at its height not knowing itself (哀傷之至，不自知也). Confucius then justifies the grief by its object ("for whom should I mourn"), not by inspecting it.
- **Different residues, one term** (Zixia and Zizhang): one mourner's grief outlasts the term and another's ends before it; both keep the term.
- **Residue shown in performance, not introspection**: Confucius's lute "brought no perfect sounds from it" after a ritual milestone; Zixia "could hardly" tune his. Where the texts register that grief remains, they register it in the hand and voice.

Two cautions keep this from overstating. The feeling still matters: mourning "without sorrow" is intolerable (3.26), deep sorrow beats correct observance (3.4), and feeling sets a ceiling on *display* (19.14). And the external measure is not presented as infallible: Ing (2012) reads the *Liji* as making ritual failure ambiguous. So the Confucian position is better put as: the inner signal does not set the term, and the term is set by a form calibrated to the normal feeling of a relationship. This parallels what `prolonged-grief-disorder-2026-11-19` found in the clinical criteria (a line drawn from outside by duration and norms), with the difference that the Confucian line ends *mourning* and diagnoses nothing.

### 3. Relation to the tenets

No tenet is supported. The texts are naturalistic: Fraser (2013) describes both Xunzi's and the *Zhuangzi*'s approaches as making "no appeal to a conception of divinity or a personal afterlife" (abstract; the phrase is in his abstract, not quoted in the fold). The material does not bear on dualism, and the fold must say so. The mourner's limited self-knowledge is a psychological point that a physicalist account accommodates. **Tenet 5** has at most a thematic echo, which the Map should not draw: the "simpler" view that grief ends when it fades is not the view these texts take. **No Many Worlds** has a thematic link only, as in the bereavement void: the grief is for this parent, and Olberding's "none but my parent can be to me a parent" (in her *Aeon* essay; not quoted in the fold) states it.

```markdown
## Mourning and the Measure of Grief {#mourning-measure}

Early Confucian texts treat mourning for a parent as the central case of feeling given ritual form, and on how long grief should be given that form they answer with the rite, graded to the relationship, rather than with what the individual mourner feels. In *Analects* 17.21 the disciple Zai Wo argues that a year of mourning is enough, since by then the old grain is gone and the new has ripened. Confucius asks, "If you were, after a year, to eat good rice, and wear embroidered clothes, would you feel at ease?" Zai Wo says he would. Confucius tells him to do it, since a gentleman in mourning takes no pleasure in fine food or music, and once Zai Wo has left he says the answer shows his "want of virtue": a child is not "allowed to leave the arms of its parents" until it is three (Legge 1872). The test appeals to feeling, but Zai Wo's report of ease counts against him, not against the rite.

Xunzi's "Discourse on Ritual" (*Xunzi* 19) generalises the point. Ritual is to "cut off that which is too long and stretch out that which is too short" (Dubs 1928), deepening a shallow mourner's grief and restraining a deep one's. The three-year rite is said to match feeling (稱情而立文), yet it closes in the twenty-fifth month although, in the parallel *Book of Rites* text, "The sorrow and pain were not yet ended", because the time has come "for the resumption of their duties to the living". For a cultivated mourner the feeling, if indulged, "will prove to be inexhaustible" (Legge 1885b). The *Book of Rites* applies the rule against mourners' own sense of readiness. Zilu will not end his mourning for his sister because he "cannot bear" to, and Confucius answers that the reluctance is universal and the former kings set the term regardless. Zixia comes out of mourning barely able to tune a lute, his grief "not yet forgotten", and Zizhang able to play, and each says he kept to the term the former kings set (Legge 1885a). Mencius roots burial in spontaneous feeling, the sweat that broke out on the brows of people in high antiquity who saw their unburied parents' bodies being eaten, yet he opposed a king who wanted to shorten the mourning period (Legge 1875).

On the Map's reading these texts relocate the puzzle that [[bereavement-void#the-lapse|the bereavement void]] takes from Berislav Marušić rather than dissolve it. Marušić asks why grief's fading is not a failure when its reason, the loss, is unchanged. The Confucian texts separate the rite from the feeling. What ends on schedule is mourning, the feeling need not end, and the end is justified by the needs of the living, which is the kind of consideration Marušić says explains the fading without justifying it. The texts say more about self-knowledge. Neither Zai Wo's ease nor Zilu's reluctance is taken as evidence of where grief's proper course ends; the relationship and the rite fix that from outside. When Confucius was told that his grief for his disciple Yan Hui was excessive, he asked, "Is it excessive?" (*Analects* 11.9), and Zhu Xi's commentary takes the question to mean that at the height of grief he did not know it of himself. None of this bears on the mind-matter question this article is about.
```
