## Key Sources

### SEP, "Scientific Objectivity" (§3, "The Value-Free Ideal")

- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-objectivity/
- **Type**: Encyclopedia
- **Key points**:
  - Frames the **Value-Free Ideal (VFI)**: scientists should strive to minimise the influence of *contextual* values on scientific reasoning, in gathering evidence and in assessing and accepting theories.
  - Distinguishes **cognitive** values (also called epistemic or constitutive — predictive accuracy, scope, unification, explanatory power, simplicity, coherence with accepted theory) from **contextual** values (moral, personal, social, political, cultural). Cognitive values are widely regarded as benign because they are taken to be truth-indicative; the VFI targets only contextual values.
  - Attributes to Kuhn (1977) the claim that cognitive values define the shared commitments of science, while noting that subjective differences in ranking and applying them do not vanish — "a point Kuhn made emphatically."
  - Reconstructs the argument from inductive risk through Rudner (1953), Hempel (1965), and Douglas (2000), and both classical rebuttals (Jeffrey 1956, Levi 1960).
  - Closes §3 sceptically rather than triumphally: "In the absence of a general theory about which contextual values are beneficial and which are pernicious, the VFI might as well be as a first-order approximation to a sound, transparent and objective science." (*sic* — the entry's own text.)
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The entry is metaphysically uncommitted throughout; nothing in it requires or excludes phenomenal consciousness.

### SEP, "Social Dimensions of Scientific Knowledge" (§5, Longino)

- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-knowledge-social/
- **Type**: Encyclopedia
- **Key points**:
  - Longino's starting point is an underdetermination argument — a *semantic* gap between data-statements and hypothesis-statements, meaning evidential relations "cannot be formally specified" and are mediated by background assumptions. This is close kin to the Duhem-Quine material the Map already has.
  - The decisive sentence for this research: on Longino's account "the only check against the arbitrary dominance of subjective (metaphysical, political, aesthetic) preference in such cases is critical interaction among the members of the scientific community or among members of different communities."
  - Four community norms for objectivity: (1) venues for critical interaction; (2) uptake — demonstrated change in the community's belief distribution over time in response to criticism; (3) public accessibility of the standards regulating discourse; (4) tempered equality of intellectual authority.
  - Longino (2002) extends this: the cognitive processes of science, observation and reasoning included, are *themselves* social processes.
- **Tenet alignment**: **Conflicts** with the use the review proposed. Longino classes aesthetic preference alongside metaphysical and political preference as the thing objectivity must be protected *from*. Her programme relocates epistemic authority from the individual knower to the community — a move that makes individual phenomenal response epistemically inert by design.

### Rudner (1953), "The Scientist Qua Scientist Makes Value Judgments"

- **Citation**: *Philosophy of Science* 20(1): 1–6. doi:10.1086/287231 — **Crossref-verified** (title, author, volume, issue, pages, year).
- **Key points** (paraphrase from SEP's reconstruction; primary text not obtained — see gaps):
  - Premise one: any adequate account of scientific method must hold that the scientist *qua* scientist accepts or rejects hypotheses.
  - Premise two: no hypothesis is ever confirmed beyond reasonable doubt; some probability of error always remains.
  - Conclusion: accepting or rejecting therefore requires weighing how serious each of the two possible errors would be — which is an ethical judgement. The worked contrast is deaths from an unsafe drug wrongly judged safe versus deaths from a withheld treatment wrongly judged unsafe.
- **Structural observation, and the crux of this note**: Rudner's argument is **a priori and normative**. It concerns what *justifies* an acceptance decision under non-conclusive evidence. It would hold with full force for an idealised reasoner with no phenomenology whatsoever, and it makes no claim about what causally produces any actual scientist's judgement.

### Douglas (2000), "Inductive Risk and Values in Science"

- **Citation**: *Philosophy of Science* 67(4): 559–579. doi:10.1086/392855 — **Crossref-verified**.
- **Key points** (via SEP, which cites Douglas 2000: 563–565):
  - Extends inductive risk beyond the acceptance of theories, which is only "one of several places for values to enter scientific reasoning." Values also enter experimental design, methodology, characterisation of data, choice of statistical method, and interpretation of findings.
  - This is what defeats Jeffrey's rebuttal: you cannot quarantine value-free evidence-gathering from value-laden acceptance, because the value judgements are distributed across the whole inferential pipeline.
  - Case study: dioxin carcinogenicity in rats. Contextual values (safety, risk aversion) entered twice — in classifying pathological samples as benign or cancerous, and in extrapolating from high-dose experimental to realistic low-dose conditions.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral, and neutral in a way that matters. The dioxin case study is an argument about *institutional epistemic policy*, not about phenomenal causation.

### Douglas (2009), *Science, Policy, and the Value-Free Ideal*

- **Citation**: University of Pittsburgh Press — **publisher-verified** at https://upittpress.org/books/9780822960263/ (title and author confirmed on the press's own page).
- **Key point**: the **direct/indirect role distinction**. Values may legitimately play an *indirect* role — setting how much evidence is required before a claim is accepted, in light of the consequences of error — but may not play a *direct* role, acting as reasons in themselves to accept or reject a claim.
- **Why this matters to the Map**: a standing metaphysical commitment operating as a reason to accept a metaphysical thesis is a textbook *direct* role. Douglas's account, the single most-cited framework in this literature, **prohibits** the move the Map's `duhem-quine-underdetermination-consciousness` line 94 makes.
- **Contestation, honestly noted**: SEP records that the distinction may not be sharp enough to police legitimacy, citing Elliott (2011: 320–321): a scientist who finds the consequences of wrongly accepting *H* undesirable and therefore picks a statistical model likely to favour ¬*H* may be exercising reasonable conservativeness, or may be "reasoning to a foregone conclusion, and ... treating values as evidence." So the constraint is live and contested rather than settled — which is the appropriate weight class for the Map to give it.

### Ward (2021), "On value-laden science" — **the single most important source here**

- **Citation**: *Studies in History and Philosophy of Science Part A* 85: 54–62. doi:10.1016/j.shpsa.2020.09.006 — **Crossref-verified**. Note the date ambiguity: OpenAlex records an online publication date of 2020-10-21; Crossref's issued date is 2021-02. Cite by volume and pages, which are unambiguous.
- **Key points** (from the publisher-supplied abstract, retrieved via Semantic Scholar):
  - "Philosophical work on values in science is held back by widespread ambiguity about how values bear on scientific choices."
  - Ward distinguishes **four** relations a value can bear to a choice: values can **motivate**, **justify**, **cause**, or **be impacted by** the choices we make.
  - Applying the taxonomy: "The claim that non-epistemic values must play a role in scientific choices that run inductive risk makes most sense as a claim about values being needed to **justify** such choices."
- **Why this is decisive**: Ward supplies, from inside the literature and for the literature's own reasons, exactly the discriminator the Map needs — and it separates the argument from inductive risk (justification) from the claim the Map requires under Bidirectional Interaction (causation). The Map does not have to import this distinction or defend it; it is the going view of what the inductive-risk argument is about.

### Resnik and Elliott (2023), "Science, Values, and the New Demarcation Problem"

- **Citation**: *Journal for General Philosophy of Science* 54: 259–286. doi:10.1007/s10838-022-09633-2 — **Crossref-verified**.
- **Key point**: names the field's current organising problem. Once the VFI is abandoned, the question is no longer *whether* values influence science but *which* influences are legitimate. Companion literature: Koskinen and Rolin (2022), *Studies in History and Philosophy of Science* 91: 191–198, doi:10.1016/j.shpsa.2021.12.001; Cabrera (2022), *Canadian Journal of Philosophy* 52: 810–827, doi:10.1017/can.2023.19.
- **Tenet alignment**: **Constrains** the Map. This is the axis on which the Map's existing inference is exposed.

## Major Positions

### The Value-Free Ideal (VFI)

- **Proponents**: the received view through the mid-twentieth century; defended in contemporary form by Betz (2013), *European Journal for Philosophy of Science* 3(2): 207–220, doi:10.1007/s13194-012-0062-x.
- **Core claim**: contextual values should be minimised in evidence-gathering and theory assessment; cognitive values are permitted.
- **Betz's specific defence** (via SEP): scientists can largely avoid contextual value judgements by carefully expressing the uncertainty attached to their evidential judgements, on a scale from purely qualitative expert judgement to precise probabilistic assessment. SEP notes the proposal does not address value judgements at earlier stages of inquiry.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral on dualism. Worth noting for the Map's own conduct: the VFI's "report your uncertainty rather than accept or reject" strategy is structurally the same move the Map makes with its five-tier evidential scale under [P-M1](/positions/methodology-and-calibration/#p-m1) — a convergence in *method*, not in metaphysics.

### The Argument from Inductive Risk

- **Proponents**: Rudner (1953); Hempel (1965) — who, per SEP, distinguishes value-free *judgements of confirmation* from value-laden *judgements of acceptance* and coins the residual "inductive risk"; Douglas (2000, 2009).
- **Core claim**: because evidence never conclusively establishes a hypothesis, deciding to accept it requires weighing the consequences of being wrong, and that weighing is an ethical judgement.
- **Key rebuttals** (both via SEP, both unverified at primary source): Jeffrey (1956) — lawlike hypotheses in fundamental science have general scope not tied to any application, so the scientist should simply refrain from accepting or rejecting and report probabilities instead. Levi (1960) — scientists commit to community standards of inference on entering the profession (for example, a 5% significance threshold), and those standards, not the individual's ethical judgement, determine acceptance.
- **Relation to site tenets**: **This is the position the review identified as convergent with Bidirectional Interaction, and it is not.** Three reasons, in order of force:
  1. **Wrong relation.** Ward's taxonomy separates *justifies* from *causes*. The inductive-risk argument establishes that a value judgement is needed to justify acceptance. Bidirectional Interaction is a claim about causal history — that a phenomenal state figures in the aetiology of a physical event. Nothing in the justificatory claim entails the causal one.
  2. **Wrong values.** The literature's target is contextual values — moral, social, political. Aesthetic response and felt coherence are, on the standard taxonomy, cognitive values (elegance, simplicity, coherence), and cognitive values are the *uncontroversial* part that the VFI never disputed. The formalised post-Kuhnian literature is about the part of the terrain the Map is not standing on.
  3. **Functionalist availability.** Even granting a causal reading, nothing in the literature requires values to be phenomenally realised. Levi's rebuttal locates them in *community standards*; Betz's in a *calibrated uncertainty scale*; Douglas's in *institutional* decisions about thresholds. Every mechanism proposed on either side of this debate is implementable without phenomenal consciousness. The literature therefore supplies a premise that is **neutral** between the Map and physicalism, not one the Map can recruit.

### Longino's Critical Contextual Empiricism

- **Proponents**: Longino (1990), *Science as Social Knowledge: Values and Objectivity in Scientific Inquiry*, Princeton University Press; Longino (2002), *The Fate of Knowledge*, Princeton University Press.
- **Core claim**: objectivity is a property of *communities*, not individuals, secured by structured critical interaction meeting the four norms above.
- **Relation to site tenets**: **Conflicts** with the proposed use, and the conflict is worth stating plainly rather than softening. The Map would like individual phenomenal response to be an epistemically respectable guide. Longino's entire apparatus is built to prevent individual preference — explicitly including *aesthetic* preference — from determining outcomes, on the grounds that there is "no higher authority or transcendent aperspectival position" from which foundational assumptions can be adjudicated. An article that cited Longino as supplying a premise the Map needs would be misreading her.
- **A more honest use is available**: Longino's underdetermination-plus-background-assumptions structure is genuinely close to the Map's `duhem-quine-underdetermination-consciousness`, and her "no transcendent aperspectival position" claim is close to the Map's reflexivity material. Cite her for *that*, as a parallel diagnosis, and record that her prescription runs the other way.

### The Aesthetic-Values Strand (adjacent, thinner, and not the same literature)

- **Proponents**: McAllister (1996), *Beauty and Revolution in Science*, Cornell University Press; Ivanova (2017), "Aesthetic values in science", *Philosophy Compass* 12(10), doi:10.1111/phc3.12433 — Crossref-verified.
- **Why it is listed separately**: this is the literature that actually concerns aesthetic response in theory choice, and it is *not* the inductive-risk literature. Conflating the two is the error the review's framing invites. If the Map wants to engage the aesthetics-of-theory-choice question, this — not Douglas — is the correct body of work, and it connects to existing Map content (`aesthetics-and-consciousness`, `consciousness-and-mathematics`, `epistemic-emotions`) rather than to the philosophy-of-science cluster.

## Key Debates

### Is the value-free ideal defensible?

- **Sides**: Betz (2013) and the uncertainty-articulation strategy versus the inductive-risk tradition (Douglas 2000, 2009) and the social-epistemology tradition (Longino 1990, 2002).
- **Core disagreement**: whether contextual value judgements can be quarantined from the epistemic core of science, or whether they are distributed across the inferential pipeline in a way that makes quarantine impossible.
- **Current state**: ongoing, with the centre of gravity having moved decisively away from the VFI, but with SEP itself declining to declare it dead — it retains the VFI as a serviceable first-order approximation in the absence of a general theory of which contextual values are beneficial.

### The new demarcation problem

- **Sides**: Douglas's direct/indirect criterion; Elliott's (2011) objection that it is not sharp enough; Koskinen and Rolin's (2022) transdisciplinary criteria; Cabrera's (2022) Rawlsian proposal; Brown's (2020) "moral imagination" ideal (*Science and Moral Imagination: A New Ideal for Values in Science*, University of Pittsburgh Press, open access — publisher page confirms OA availability).
- **Core disagreement**: given that values do influence science, which influences are legitimate?
- **Current state**: actively open. Resnik and Elliott (2023) frame it as the field's central problem; Hufendiek and James (2026), "Science, Values and Ideology", *Philosophy Compass* 21, doi:10.1111/phc3.70088, is the most recent survey located and indicates the debate is still generating primary literature.
- **Why the Map should care**: this is the axis on which the Map is exposed. It is not enough to establish that theory choice is value-laden; the Map's inference needs the *particular* value influence it wants — a standing metaphysical commitment functioning as a reason — to land on the legitimate side of a criterion that the literature is currently in the business of drawing.

## The [P-M2](/positions/methodology-and-calibration/#p-m2) Discipline, Stated Explicitly

The harvest mint required this note to carry the discipline rather than conclude that the values-in-science literature supports dualism. It does, and it goes further.

**[P-M2](/positions/methodology-and-calibration/#p-m2) as written** discounts "any convergence across N traditions, substrates, or methodologies as a *single observation read N times* — framework-internal coherence rather than N-fold independent triangulation — *until* the convergence passes a structurally distinguishing test that rules out a shared upstream architecture."

**The finding here is that [P-M2](/positions/methodology-and-calibration/#p-m2) does not get to apply**, because its precondition fails. [P-M2](/positions/methodology-and-calibration/#p-m2) governs cases where two lines of inquiry reach the same proposition and the question is whether they reached it independently. Here they do not reach the same proposition: one literature establishes that value judgements are needed to *justify* acceptance under uncertainty; the Map needs phenomenal states to *cause* theory choice. Discounting a convergence to framework-internal coherence would be too generous a verdict, because it would concede that a convergence exists.

The correct register is therefore not "convergent but discounted" but **"not convergent — the apparent agreement is an equivocation across Ward's four relations."** Any article built on this note should say so in those terms.

### What would a distinguishing test look like?

The mint asked that a distinguishing test be named or its absence recorded. Two candidates are nameable, and one structural obstacle must be recorded alongside them.

**Candidate 1 — substrate dissociation.** If an AI system with no phenomenal aesthetic response reproduces the full pattern of value-guided theory choice, *including* the feature Kuhn emphasised most — that competent practitioners rank and apply the same cognitive values differently and rationally reach different conclusions — that is evidence the phenomenality is not doing the work. A systematic residual no functional specification captures would be evidence the other way. This test is available now, and it can go against the Map, which is what makes it a real test rather than a confirmation exercise. It connects to existing Map positions on AI consciousness.

**Candidate 2 — population dissociation, on the pattern the Map already uses.** `phenomenal-contrast-method` uses aphantasia in exactly this shape, and does so well: the population-scale contrast supports cognitive phenomenology's reality while the dissociation between phenomenal report and task performance *bounds* what it does. The analogue here would be individuals with markedly attenuated epistemic or aesthetic feeling. The discriminating question is whether theory-choice behaviour tracks phenomenal report *beyond* what functional competence predicts. If it does not, the phenomenal-causation reading loses its evidence.

**The structural obstacle, recorded honestly.** Under Tenet 2, any phenomenal influence on physical outcomes is exercised minimally at the quantum level and preserves Born statistics exactly. The Map already registers the consequence: such influence is empirically indistinguishable under aggregate-statistics tests ([P-Q2](/positions/quantum-interface/#p-q2), [P-Q7](/positions/quantum-interface/#p-q7), and the framework-stage calibration in [P-M4](/positions/methodology-and-calibration/#p-m4)). Both candidate tests above are aggregate-behavioural. So on the Map's own commitments, neither test can discriminate the *mechanism* — at best they discriminate whether phenomenal report carries information that functional description does not. A distinguishing test for the causal claim proper is blocked by the same mechanism debt that blocks the Map's other interface claims, and this note should not be read as having found a way around it.
