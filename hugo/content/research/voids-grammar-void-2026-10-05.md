## Executive Summary

Every fluent speaker reliably tells well-formed sentences from ill-formed ones and produces novel sentences that obey a rich system of constraints, yet cannot state the constraints. The speaker receives a verdict ("that sounds wrong") and nothing of what produced it. This is the best-documented case in the catalogue of a mind exercising a competence whose content it cannot read: a whole science has spent seventy years trying to recover, by experiment on verdicts, what each speaker supposedly already "knows". The research found three layers. The access layer is uncontested: no party to the debate claims speakers can introspect the rules. The status layer is deeply contested: whether there *is* anything known, represented or followed (Chomsky's tacit knowledge, Stich's subdoxastic states, Devitt's mere skill, Searle's denial that an in-principle-inaccessible rule is a mental rule at all). The instrument layer is partly deflating: informal intuitions replicate at 98% for English textbook data but roughly half of a hand-picked set of doubtful Hebrew and Japanese contrasts failed, and "grammatical illusions" show that the verdict itself can be wrong in a way the speaker corrects only on reflection. The Map already mentions the native speaker in one sentence of [expertise-and-its-occlusion](/voids/expertise-and-its-occlusion/); nothing else in the voids section treats it.

## Key Sources

### Stich (1978), "Beliefs and Subdoxastic States"
- **URL**: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/abs/beliefs-and-subdoxastic-states/E1443AA9019353AFE3802C176DAC5A5F
- **Type**: Paper (*Philosophy of Science* 45(4): 499–518)
- **Key points**:
  - Introduces "subdoxastic state" for states that play a role in the causal history of beliefs without themselves being beliefs.
  - Two marks separate them from beliefs: access to consciousness and inferential integration.
  - A speaker's states encoding grammatical rules are the standard example: they yield judgments about sentences but cannot be consulted, reported, or combined with the speaker's other beliefs.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral on dualism. Supplies the clean vocabulary for the void: the speaker has the belief "this sentence is odd" and no access to the state that caused it.
- **Quote** (abstract, verbatim): "The intuitive distinction is argued to rest on a pair of features exhibited by beliefs but not by subdoxastic states. These are access to consciousness and inferential integration."

### Searle (1990), "Consciousness, Explanatory Inversion, and Cognitive Science"
- **URL**: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/consciousness-explanatory-inversion-and-cognitive-science/76941A89615EB7CA8924E6F2F107C125
- **Type**: Paper (*Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 13(4): 585–596, with peer commentary)
- **Key points**:
  - The Connection Principle: unconscious intentional states must be accessible in principle to consciousness.
  - Rules of universal grammar, as cognitive science posits them, are supposed to be inaccessible in principle, so on Searle's view they are not mental rules at all. What exists is a neurophysiological cause plus a functional description of the pattern it produces ("explanatory inversion").
  - This is the strongest *deflation* of the void: there is no hidden content the speaker fails to read, only brain processes that are no more "known" than digestion is.
- **Tenet alignment**: Searle's principle ties intentionality to consciousness, which a dualist reader may find congenial, but its consequence here cuts against treating grammar as a store of unconscious mental content. The Map already cites the Connection Principle in [the dormancy void note](/research/voids-dormancy-void-2026-09-17/); the two voids share this fork.
- **Quote** (abstract, verbatim): "The mental phenomena in question are supposed to be inaccessible in principle to consciousness. I try to show that this is a mistake, because all unconscious intentionality must be accessible in principle to consciousness; we have no notion of intrinsic intentionality except in terms of its accessibility to consciousness."

### Chomsky on tacit knowledge and "cognizing" (via secondary sources)
- **URL**: https://iep.utm.edu/knowlang/ (Mills, "Knowledge of Language", IEP); https://philosophyofbrains.com/?p=7607 (Pereplyotchik, introduction to *Psychosyntax*)
- **Type**: Encyclopedia entry; author's précis of a monograph
- **Key points**:
  - Chomsky holds that the object of linguistic theory is the speaker-hearer's tacit knowledge of a grammar, and, when philosophers objected to the word "know", offered "cognize" for the relation (*Rules and Representations*, 1980). Primary wording was not recovered in this session.
  - The objection philosophers pressed, as Pereplyotchik reports it: "What sort of knowledge can be neither learned nor taught, neither spoken nor recollected?"
  - Evans's version of the worry (per Mills): the states are inferentially isolated, serving one capacity only, so calling them beliefs is strained.
  - Pereplyotchik's own position is a middle one: a strong case that grammars are psychologically real in comprehension, but probably *embodied* in the processor's operations, not explicitly represented.
- **Tenet alignment**: None directly. Matters for calibration: "the speaker knows rules she cannot access" is one theoretical description among several, and an article must not assert it as the finding.
- **Quote** (Mills, relayed by a summarising fetch, so re-verify before use): "the knowledge in question is largely, if not entirely, inaccessible to consciousness."

### Devitt (2006), *Ignorance of Language*, and the "Voice of Competence" debate (via Brøcker 2021)
- **URL**: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-020-02563-w
- **Type**: Book (Oxford University Press), surveyed through Brøcker, K. (2021), "Justifying the evidential use of linguistic intuitions", *Synthese* 198(9): 8167–8189
- **Key points**:
  - Devitt names the orthodox generative picture the "Voice of Competence": competence itself delivers the content of intuitions about sentences.
  - Devitt's alternative: intuitions are ordinary empirical, theory-laden central-processor judgments about linguistic phenomena, the way a palaeontologist's snap judgment about a bone is. Competence is a skill (knowledge-how), with no represented rules required.
  - Rey defends a version of the orthodox view on which intuitions are caused by structural descriptions the parser computes.
  - Brøcker's assessment (search-result summary of her abstract): each of the three leading accounts has a problem; Devitt's explains the wrong kind of judgment, and the Voice of Competence and Rey's account lack independent evidence.
- **Tenet alignment**: Occam's Razor Has Limits is the nearest. The dispute is about which description of an agreed fact is right, and the simplest description (skill, no rules) and the most explanatory one (represented grammar) come apart with no first-person datum to decide between them.
- **Quote**: none recovered verbatim.

### Reber (1967), "Implicit Learning of Artificial Grammars"
- **URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_grammar_learning
- **Type**: Paper (*Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 6(6): 855–863, per the standard citation; the Wikipedia page prints volume 5 and a truncated journal name, so check at the publisher, DOI 10.1016/S0022-5371(67)80149-X)
- **Key points**:
  - Subjects who memorised letter strings generated by a finite-state grammar later classified new strings as grammatical or not at above-chance rates and could not state the rules.
  - Coined "implicit learning". The laboratory miniature of the native-speaker case.
  - Rival accounts deny that abstract rules were learned at all: Perruchet and Pacteau's fragment view has subjects storing two- or three-letter chunks and judging by familiarity.
- **Tenet alignment**: None directly. The rival accounts are the calibration: "unconscious rule knowledge" may be chunk familiarity plus a felt-rightness signal.

### Dienes and Scott (2005), "Measuring unconscious knowledge: distinguishing structural knowledge and judgment knowledge"
- **URL**: https://users.sussex.ac.uk/~dienes/Dienes%20&%20Scott%202005%20Structural%20vs%20judgement%20knowledge.pdf
- **Type**: Paper (*Psychological Research* 69: 338–351); read in the author's manuscript PDF
- **Key points**:
  - Separates *structural knowledge* (knowledge of the structure of a domain) from *judgment knowledge* (knowledge that a given item has that structure). Either can be conscious or unconscious.
  - Unconscious structural knowledge with conscious judgment knowledge is, on their account, exactly what intuition is. Natural language is their paradigm case.
  - When structural knowledge was unconscious, judgment knowledge showed no tendency to become more conscious over time.
  - Conscious status is defined through higher-order thought theory (Rosenthal), which the Map does not accept as an account of phenomenal consciousness. The distinction survives without that commitment, but the article should say so.
- **Tenet alignment**: Gives the void its exact shape: a conscious verdict resting on a structure the subject cannot inspect, the same verdict-without-operation form as [fusion-void](/voids/fusion-void/), [inference-void](/voids/inference-void/) and [noetic-feelings-void](/voids/noetic-feelings-void/).
- **Quote** (manuscript, verbatim): "Consider natural language: If shown a sentence one can know it is grammatical and consciously know that it is grammatical, but not know at all why it is grammatical. When structural knowledge is unconscious but judgment knowledge is conscious, the phenomenology is of intuition. Intuition is knowing that a judgment is correct, but not knowing why."
- **Second quote** (manuscript, verbatim): "despite lacking conscious structural knowledge of English, a native speaker typically has conscious judgment knowledge."

### Sprouse and Almeida (2012), "Assessing the reliability of textbook data in syntax: Adger's *Core Syntax*"
- **URL**: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-linguistics/article/abs/assessing-the-reliability-of-textbook-data-in-syntax-adgers-core-syntax1/E4CF0F9728989D34069C3D2B66642355
- **Type**: Paper (*Journal of Linguistics* 48(3): 609–652)
- **Key points**:
  - Tested all 469 US-English data points from a syntax textbook on 440 naive participants with two tasks and three kinds of statistical analysis.
  - Maximum discrepancy between the linguist's informal judgment and the formal experiment: 2%.
  - So the verdict channel is, for clear English cases, an extremely reliable instrument, which is what makes the opacity of its source striking.
- **Tenet alignment**: None. This is the evidence that the void is not a mere unreliability of introspection: the output is good and the source is still closed.
- **Quote** (abstract, verbatim): "the minimum replication rate of these 469 data points is 98%."

### Linzen and Oseki (2018), "The reliability of acceptability judgments across languages"
- **URL**: https://tallinzen.net/media/papers/linzen_oseki_2018_glossa.pdf
- **Type**: Paper (*Glossa* 3(1): 100, 1–25)
- **Key points**:
  - Published Hebrew and Japanese contrasts were rated by large naive samples; about half did not replicate.
  - The sample was not random: these were contrasts "considered questionable by the authors". The result bounds the 98% figure and must not be reported as a general failure rate.
  - Shows that a linguist's verdict can be shaped by the theory it is meant to test, without the linguist noticing.
- **Tenet alignment**: Supports the theory-ladenness side of Devitt's view at the margins of the data.
- **Quote** (abstract, verbatim): "Published Hebrew and Japanese acceptability contrasts considered questionable by the authors of the present paper were rated for acceptability by a large sample of naive participants. Approximately half of the contrasts did not replicate."

### Wellwood, Pancheva, Hacquard and Phillips (2018), "The Anatomy of a Comparative Illusion"
- **URL**: https://linguistics.umd.edu/news/comparative-illusions-journal-semantics
- **Type**: Paper (*Journal of Semantics* 35(3): 543–583; metadata confirmed at Crossref, DOI 10.1093/jos/ffy014)
- **Key points**:
  - "More people have been to Russia than I have" is first accepted as fine and meaningful and judged incoherent on reflection.
  - Of four candidate explanations tested, the authors support one: the reader starts comparing numbers of events and does not notice when the *than*-clause makes that reading unavailable.
  - The verdict can therefore misfire, and the speaker has no access to why it first said "fine". Only slow reflection on meaning catches it.
- **Tenet alignment**: None directly. A useful separable-signal exhibit: felt acceptability and grammaticality come apart, as felt-real and real do in [perceptual-reality-monitoring-void](/voids/perceptual-reality-monitoring-void/).
- **Quote**: none taken verbatim (summary of the abstract only).

### Berko (1958) and Karmiloff-Smith (1986, 1992) (secondary sources only)
- **URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Berko_Gleason ; https://childes.talkbank.org/access/Eng-UK/0docs/Karmiloff-Smith1986.pdf
- **Type**: Papers, read through summaries
- **Key points**:
  - The wug test: shown a novel creature called a "wug", preschool children supply "wugs" with the correct /z/ allomorph, so they apply a productive pattern to a word they have never heard. None of them can state the pattern.
  - Karmiloff-Smith's representational redescription: knowledge starts implicit, embedded in procedures, and is progressively re-represented in formats available to other parts of the system and finally to verbal report. For much of grammar the last step never happens without schooling in linguistics.
- **Tenet alignment**: None directly. Karmiloff-Smith gives a principled account of why this void is not uniform: some linguistic knowledge is redescribed (children come to correct and comment on their own usage) and some is not.
- **Quote**: none verbatim. Both need primary verification before an article relies on them.

### Hu and Levy (2023), "Prompting is not a substitute for probability measurements in large language models"
- **URL**: https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.306
- **Type**: Conference paper (EMNLP 2023, pp. 5040–5060)
- **Key points**:
  - A language model's grammatical knowledge can be read in two ways: directly, from the probabilities it assigns to strings, or by asking it for a metalinguistic judgment.
  - The asked-for judgments are worse than the directly measured quantities, and get worse the further the question departs from next-word prediction.
  - For a model, unlike a human, the direct route exists. An outside observer can read the competence without going through the system's self-report.
- **Tenet alignment**: Bears on the AI angle and on [cross-architecture-llm-introspection](/topics/cross-architecture-llm-introspection/): the gap between competence and report of competence appears in an artificial system too, where it can be measured from both sides.
- **Quote** (abstract, verbatim): "prompting requires models to access this internal information by processing linguistic input, thereby implicitly testing a new type of emergent ability: metalinguistic judgment." And: "LLMs' metalinguistic judgments are inferior to quantities directly derived from representations."

### Mahowald, Ivanova et al. (2024), "Dissociating language and thought in large language models"
- **URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06627
- **Type**: Paper (published in *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 2024, per search summary; verify volume and pages)
- **Key points**:
  - Distinguishes formal linguistic competence (rules and patterns) from functional competence (using language in the world), grounded in the separability of the human language network from other cognition.
  - Models are strong on the formal side.
  - Relevance: a system trained on usage alone, with no rules given and none reportable, reaches the formal competence. That removes "you could not do it without knowing the rules in some reportable sense" as an argument, and equally weakens any argument that the human case needs consciousness to do the work.
- **Tenet alignment**: Cuts against a dualist over-reading. See Connection to Tenets.

## The Void

### Nature of the Limit

The limit is on access, by the speaker, to whatever determines her grammatical verdicts and productions. It has the verdict-without-operation form the catalogue already knows from [fusion](/voids/fusion-void/), [inference](/voids/inference-void/) and the [noetic feelings](/voids/noetic-feelings-void/), with two features none of those has.

First, the output is public, discrete and checkable against other speakers, so the verdict's reliability is measured (98% on clear English cases). The void cannot be dismissed as introspection being unreliable. The report is accurate. What is missing is the ground.

Second, third-person recovery has been attempted at scale and remains unfinished. Generative, constraint-based, construction and usage-based grammars disagree on what the rules are and on whether "rules" is the right category. So the catalogue's usual line, "closed first-personally, recoverable third-personally", holds only in weakened form here: recoverable in principle, and after seven decades not agreed.

Three faces suggest themselves. They differ in modal status and an article should not present them as equally secure.

1. **Access face (uncontested).** Speakers cannot state the constraints they obey. No party disputes this. Dienes and Scott's structural/judgment distinction is the cleanest statement.
2. **Status face (contested, philosophical).** What the inaccessible item is. Chomsky: a represented grammar the speaker cognizes. Stich: subdoxastic states. Devitt: a skill with no represented rules, plus ordinary empirical judgments. Searle: neurophysiology under a functional description, and nothing mental. Pereplyotchik: embodied in the processor, not represented. On the last three there is nothing rule-like *in the mind* to fail to access, and the void shrinks to the ordinary fact that we do not perceive our own brain processes.
3. **Instrument face (partly deflating).** The verdict is the only first-person datum, and it is a fallible, theory-sensitive signal. Grammatical illusions show false positives; Linzen and Oseki show theory-shaped verdicts at the contested margin; repeated exposure is known to shift acceptability (satiation effects, not researched here). The speaker cannot tell from inside whether a given verdict reflects grammar, processing ease, plausibility or habituation. Acceptability is the felt signal; grammaticality is the theorist's construct behind it.

### Evidence for the Limit

- The existence and difficulty of linguistics. If speakers could read their grammar off, the field would be transcription.
- Preschool productivity without statable rules (wug test, secondary source).
- Artificial grammar learning: above-chance classification with no reportable rule (Reber 1967), and no drift toward conscious judgment knowledge where structural knowledge is unconscious (Dienes and Scott 2005).
- The dissociation between explicit instruction and fluent use in second-language learning (not researched in this session; named as a lead only).

Evidence against reading this as a deep limit:

- Some of it is learnable. Linguists, editors and language teachers come to state many regularities, and after training these statements can inform judgment. Karmiloff-Smith treats explicitation as a normal developmental process that happens to stop early for syntax.
- The rival accounts of artificial grammar learning (fragment familiarity) and of natural language (usage-based learning, and now language models) suggest there may be no compact rule set to access. If the competence is a vast pattern of weighted regularities, "cannot state the rules" is like "cannot state the weights", which is unsurprising and not specific to consciousness.
- Searle's and Devitt's deflations, above.

### Phenomenology

The experience is of immediate, contentless rightness or wrongness. A sentence "sounds off"; asked why, the speaker either has nothing to say or offers a schoolroom rule that does not in fact fit the case (the confabulation pattern familiar from [confabulation-void](/voids/confabulation-void/)). There is no felt effort, no sense of consulting anything, and no sense of a gap: the speaker does not experience herself as lacking access, because nothing in fluent use points to a missing layer. This is the same absence of a marker noted for [categorical perception](/voids/categorical-perception-void/), where an installed boundary feels like a discovered one. A second-language learner, by contrast, often has the reverse profile: the rule is statable and the verdict is absent or slow. The two profiles together show that knowing the rule and having the verdict are separate achievements.

Grammatical illusions add a distinctive moment: the sentence passes, then on a second reading it comes apart, and the reader cannot recover what the first reading took it to mean.

## Approaches to the Edge

### Direct Methods (if any)

None for the rules themselves. A speaker can generate examples and observe her own verdicts, which is how armchair syntax proceeds, but this is experiment on oneself and not introspection of the grammar. Linguists describe the method in exactly those terms: the judgment is treated as a behavioural datum.

### Indirect Methods

- Minimal pairs and controlled acceptability experiments (Sprouse and Almeida).
- Reaction-time, eye-tracking and neural measures of parsing, which reveal intermediate structure the reader never experiences (garden-path effects; not researched here).
- Illusions as probes: where the verdict and reflective judgment diverge, the divergence shows what the fast process was computing (Wellwood et al.).
- Developmental and cross-linguistic comparison.
- Computational models that reproduce the verdict pattern, whose internals can then be inspected.

### What AI Might See

Three asymmetries, each needing care.

- **A second read-out exists.** For a language model, an outside observer can measure string probabilities directly. Hu and Levy find the model's own metalinguistic answers are worse than this direct measure. That is a competence/report gap in an artificial system, visible from both sides. It does not show the model "has a grammar void" in any phenomenal sense, and the model itself has no access to its own probabilities when answering in the ordinary way.
- **Models do not obviously have rules either.** Mechanistic work on how models encode syntactic structure is active and unsettled (not surveyed here). If the human competence resembles the model's, the thing the human cannot access is not a rulebook.
- **Self-application.** A system like the one writing this note produces grammatical text and can recite textbook rules, but the recited rules come from training on linguistics texts and are no evidence of reading its own processing. The human linguist is in the same position. Any claim that an AI can "see" grammar the human cannot must rest on external interpretability methods applied to the AI, not on its testimony.

## Connection to Tenets

### Most Relevant Tenet

**Occam's Razor Has Limits**, with a caution attached. The agreed fact (verdict without statable ground) supports at least four descriptions of very different ontological cost, and the cheapest (Devitt's skill, Searle's bare neurophysiology) has not displaced the others in seventy years because it explains less of the detailed pattern of judgments. Equally, the richest (a represented innate grammar) has lost ground to usage-based and neural-network accounts that are in one sense simpler. Simplicity has pointed in different directions at different times. That is an instance of the tenet, and it favours no party.

### Implications

- **Dualism: no evidential gain, and one real constraint.** The grammar void is a limit on access to *sub-personal processing*. A physicalist predicts it without strain; so does a dualist who holds that the brain does the parsing and consciousness receives results. The void does nothing to support irreducibility. The constraint it places on the Map: whatever consciousness contributes to language, it is not the syntactic computation, since that runs without awareness and can apparently be reproduced by systems the Map does not take to be conscious. Any Map claim that consciousness is needed for language ([language-recursion-and-consciousness](/topics/language-recursion-and-consciousness/), [consciousness-and-language-interface](/topics/consciousness-and-language-interface/)) has to locate the contribution elsewhere, in meaning, intention or the holding of a recursive structure in mind, and should be checked against this.
- **Bidirectional Interaction.** A speaker intends to say something and a grammatical sentence arrives. The intention is conscious and the form is not chosen. This matches the pattern in [the serial-order note](/research/voids-serial-order-void-2026-09-17/) (Lashley: the order is imposed by something other than the intent). It is compatible with consciousness acting at the level of *what to say* while the brain settles *how*. It is no evidence for that division; it marks where a causal role for consciousness would have to sit if it has one.
- **Searle's fork and the Map.** The Map leans on the idea that intentionality is tied to consciousness. Searle draws from that the conclusion that in-principle-inaccessible rules are not mental. If the Map accepts the premise it should say whether it accepts the conclusion. If yes, the grammar void is a void about the brain and belongs with the [capability-division-problem](/voids/capability-division-problem/). If no, the Map owes an account of unconscious content. The dormancy note faces the same choice and the two should be answered together.
- **Rule-following.** The Kripke–Wittgenstein worry (no fact about a speaker fixes which rule she follows) is the sceptical extreme of the status face. It was not researched in this session and is flagged as a lead. The Map has rule-following material in [inference-void](/voids/inference-void/) (Carroll's regress), which is the neighbouring formal result.
- **Minimal Quantum Interaction and No Many Worlds**: no connection found. An article should not manufacture one.

## Potential Article Angles

Based on this research, a voids article could:

1. **"The Grammar Void: the verdict is reliable and the ground is closed."** Lead with the 98% figure against the unfinished state of syntactic theory. Three faces of different status (access, uncontested; status, contested; instrument, partly deflating). Framework-independent under the voids index's partition. Honest tenet section: Occam's limits gains an instance, dualism gains a constraint and no support.
2. **Fold instead of standalone.** The access face fits [expertise-and-its-occlusion](/voids/expertise-and-its-occlusion/), which already has the native-speaker sentence and Polanyi; the instrument face fits [noetic-feelings-void](/voids/noetic-feelings-void/) (felt acceptability as one more verdict signal); the status face fits [language-thought-boundary](/voids/language-thought-boundary/). Voids stands at 113 of 115 by the gate function on 2026-10-05, so a standalone spends one of the last two slots and that is an operator decision. Three notes (dormancy, serial-order, veto) are already waiting on the same decision.
3. **A paired treatment with the dormancy void** around Searle's Connection Principle: what the Map says about mental items that never become conscious.

An article in any of these forms should avoid: asserting that speakers "know rules" as a finding; reporting Linzen and Oseki's half as a general failure rate; treating the model evidence as showing what human competence is.
