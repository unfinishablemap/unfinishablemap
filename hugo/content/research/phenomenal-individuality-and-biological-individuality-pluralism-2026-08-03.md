# Research: Phenomenal Individuality and Biological-Individuality Pluralism

**Date**: 2026-08-03
**Method**: WebSearch budget was exhausted for the session (200/200). All verification was done by targeted retrieval against publishers and metadata authorities: Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, Springer (dc.description), Frontiers, the Stanford Encyclopedia, and two author-hosted primary PDFs. ## Executive Summary

**The subject is real, but the premise it was minted on is false, and that is the most valuable thing this research found.** The commissioning review — and the sentence in [consciousness-and-the-major-evolutionary-transitions](/topics/consciousness-and-the-major-evolutionary-transitions/) that it quotes — holds that whether a subject-of-experience is a further, non-coinciding kind of individual is "a question the framework does not ask." The framework asks it. Peter Godfrey-Smith's "Individuality, Subjectivity, and Minimal Cognition" appears in *the same special issue of* Biology & Philosophy *that Pradeu's "The many faces of biological individuality" introduces* — 31(6), 775–796 and 761–773 respectively — and it is devoted entirely to connecting kinds of biological individuality to the evolution of subjective experience. The Stanford Encyclopedia's survey entry on biological individuality cites it for exactly that.

An article built on "nobody asks this" would therefore publish a claim refutable by one citation from the issue the Map already cites. **The host article's existing sentence is a defect and needs correcting regardless of what else is written.**

The genuine gap survives in a sharper and more defensible form, in three parts. First, Godfrey-Smith asks the question and forecloses the dualist answer *by stipulation* in his second paragraph — "Setting aside dualist and panpsychist views, it seems that the special features of subjects are organizational." The organizational answer is reached after the alternative has been set aside, which is the Map's phenomenal-blindness thesis operating one level down from where the article currently states it. Second, the *criterion lists* really do stop short: the SEP entry contains zero occurrences of "conscious", "phenomenal", "sentience", "experience", or "person", and the most recent general criterion on offer (Fulda 2023) is agential rather than phenomenal. Third — the most useful result for the Map — **the census problem already has a name in the consciousness literature that the Map does not use: the boundary problem.** It has a small, self-consciously under-attended literature that has already decomposed it into sub-problems and already shown that the physicalist theory which *does* deliver a census (IIT, via its exclusion postulate) buys it at the price of proliferating minds across subsystems.

## The Premise Check

The commissioning task treats this as an uncovered subject. The relevant sentence in the host article reads:

> The existing pluralism enumerates evolutionary, physiological, and immunological individuals and stops. Whether a subject-of-experience is a further, non-coinciding kind of individual is a question the framework does not ask.

The second sentence is false, and the first is misleading about the shape of the field. What is true is narrower: the *enumerations of individuality criteria* do not include a phenomenal criterion. What is false is that the field never puts the two topics together.

Three pieces of evidence, each verified at source:

**1. Godfrey-Smith 2016 is in the same special issue.** Pradeu's paper at 31(6): 761–773 is the *introduction* to the issue; Godfrey-Smith's at 31(6): 775–796 is the paper immediately following it. The published abstract (retrieved from Springer's `dc.description` metadata):

> The paper links discussions of two topics: biological individuality and the simplest forms of mentality. […] The last part of the paper links these biological transitions to the evolutionary history of subjective experience.

**2. Pradeu's own introduction names the omission as a defect to be repaired.** Among the five objections to traditional accounts that the issue is organised around, he lists:

> disciplinary isolationism (the tendency to isolate biological individuality from other scientific and philosophical domains that have investigated individuality)

So the issue the Map cites for the pluralism is explicitly an attempt to stop the pluralism from stopping where the Map says it stops.

**3. The SEP survey records the connection.** The entry *The Biological Notion of Individual*, §6.3, closes its discussion of biological agency with:

> Finally, a fourth view is that appeals to the nature of subjectivity and point of view are key here (Godfrey-Smith 2019; see also Godfrey-Smith 2016c). Godfrey-Smith takes understanding the evolution of subjectivity to be central to advancing responses to "explanatory gap" arguments in the philosophy of mind, implying the graduated nature of cognition itself.

This is the reference work a hostile reviewer would reach for first.

## What Godfrey-Smith Actually Argues

Read from the author-hosted preprint of the published paper (pagination and DOI stated on its own title page as *Biology and Philosophy* 31 (2016): 775–796). Its structure is: Self-Production → Multicellularity → Bodies → Unitary and Modular → Nerves → The Prehistory of Subjectivity.

The framing paragraph is the one that matters most to the Map:

> With or without a biological framing, the right approach to the most elusive issues about the mind is by way of the notion of a subject, and subjectivity. If so, part of what we have to understand is a certain kind of unit, a certain kind of constituent of the world: systems that are subjects. Setting aside dualist and panpsychist views, it seems that the special features of subjects are organizational, and this organization will be the product of some sort of evolutionary process.

Two things follow. Godfrey-Smith poses precisely the Map's question — subjects as *a kind of unit*, a constituent of the world to be individuated. And he answers it organizationally only after explicitly setting aside the position the Map holds. The exclusion is a stipulation stated in one clause, not an argument.

He also leaves the coincidence question open in terms the Map can use directly:

> This does not make it clear how organisms and subjects are related – whether all organisms are subjects, whether subjects are a subset of organisms, whether subjectivity and organismality are matters of degree with some association between them

That is the non-coincidence question, asked. His candidate individuating machinery is worth recording because it is the best existing rival to a phenomenal criterion: an interior milieu that makes a body an environment for its own parts; the unitary/modular divide (modular organisms such as bryozoans have per-module nervous systems, so between-module and within-module traffic differ in kind); nervous systems as a *new topology* of cell-to-cell influence, where "Proximity is not destiny, as far as influence goes"; and reafference — compensation for the sensory effects of one's own action — as giving "the organism the beginning of an internal registration of the self/other divide."

On whether subjecthood is graded or on/off, he declines to settle:

> it is easy to think of cognition in a graded way, fading off into marginal forms seen in bacteria and the like, but tempting to think of subjective experience with an on/off model, as a yes-or-no matter. Many philosophical habits of discussion have that feature, but it may be quite misleading. The right model to use is unclear; does subjectivity fade out, with the same easing into marginal forms seen on the cognitive side? I don't know

The companion paper, "Varieties of Subjectivity" (*Philosophy of Science* 87(5): 1150–1159, 2020; presented at the PSA 2018 animal-sentience symposium organised by Jonathan Birch), separates sensory from evaluative aspects of experience and asks which comes first. It is the same programme pushed into the phenomenal register.

## The Criterion Lists Really Do Stop Short

The narrower version of the Map's claim survives scrutiny.

- **SEP, *The Biological Notion of Individual***: retrieved full text contains 0 occurrences of "conscious", "phenomenal", "sentien-", "experience", "subject of experience", or "person". Its section headings run: physiological vs evolutionary, microbialism/eliminativism/holobionts, groups as evolutionary individuals, living agents, reproduction and life cycles, autonomous agency. Subjectivity appears only in the single citation quoted above.
- **DiFrisco 2018/2019** enumerates, per his own section headings, evolutionary, physiological, ecological and developmental individuality, opening: "Individuality is an important concept in biology, yet there are many non-equivalent criteria of individuality expressed in different kinds of biological individuals." No phenomenal criterion.
- **Fulda 2023** offers the most recent bid for a *general* criterion — agential autonomy, under which paradigm individuals show strong autonomous agency and problem cases show weak agential dependence. Agency, not experience.
- **Clarke 2010** argues "there is a real problem of biological individuality, and an urgent need to arbitrate among the current plethora of solutions to it"; **Clarke 2013** resolves it by treating rival organism definitions as "alternative realisers of a common functional role". A functional-role account is structurally incapable of yielding a phenomenal criterion, which is a cleaner statement of the Map's point than "the question is not asked".
- **Gilbert, Sapp & Tauber 2012** run the pluralism in the opposite direction — holobionts dissolve anatomical, physiological, developmental, immunological and genetic individuality at once — without any phenomenal axis to dissolve.

So: the *list* has no phenomenal entry; the *literature* does discuss subjectivity, adjacent to the list, in organizational terms.

## The Finding the Map Should Actually Take: the Boundary Problem

[subject-census](/positions/subject-census/) [P-SC2](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc2) books, as a debt, that the Map has "no worked account of *subject pairing*". That debt has a name outside the Map, and a literature.

**Gómez-Emilsson & Percy (2023)**, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 17: 1233119:

> The boundary problem is related to the binding problem, part of a family of puzzles and phenomenal experiences that theories of consciousness (ToC) must either explain or eliminate. By comparison with the phenomenal binding problem, the boundary problem has received very little scholarly attention since first framed in detail by Rosengard in 1998, despite discussion by Chalmers in his widely cited 2016 work on the combination problem. However, any ToC that addresses the binding problem must also address the boundary problem.

and:

> To a first approximation, the boundary problem asks why we experience hard boundaries around those unified 1PPs and why the boundaries operate at their apparent spatiotemporal scale.

They report setting out "five specific boundary problems to aid precision in future efforts" — i.e. the decomposition work [P-SC2](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc2) says the Map owes has been partly done by someone else, under a name the Map does not currently use anywhere.

**Fekete, van Leeuwen & Edelman (2016)**, *Frontiers in Psychology* 7: 1041, states the census problem as a formal constraint on any quantitative measure of consciousness:

> an MoC that labels a system as conscious will do so for some-perhaps most-of its subsystems, as well as for irrelevantly extended systems (e.g., the original system augmented with physical appendages that contribute nothing to the properties supposedly supporting consciousness), and for aggregates of individually conscious systems (e.g., groups of people). This problem suggests that the properties that are being measured are epiphenomenal to consciousness, or else it implies a bizarre proliferation of minds.

This bears directly on the Map's dialectical position. IIT is the one live physicalist theory that *does* discharge [P-SC2](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc2) — its exclusion postulate picks out maxima of integrated information and thereby yields a determinate count and pairing. Fekete et al. argue the price is subsystem and aggregate proliferation unless the measure is shown to be intrinsic or systemic. The Map can therefore say something stronger than "we owe a pairing law": *the only extant theory that pays this debt pays it in a currency that generates a proliferation problem, and the Map's own indistinguishability result ([P-SC1](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc1), resting on exact Born preservation) means the Map cannot buy its way out empirically either.* That is a symmetric cost statement of exactly the kind Tenet 5 licenses.

**Michael Levin (2019)**, *Frontiers in Psychology* 10: 2688, supplies the closest thing in the biology literature to a non-coinciding individuality criterion of the right shape:

> Any Self is demarcated by a computational surface – the spatio-temporal boundary of events that it can measure, model, and try to affect. This surface sets a functional boundary - a cognitive "light cone" which defines the scale and limits of its cognition.

A cognitive light cone need not coincide with the organism, so this is a genuine additional axis — but it is defined by measurement-and-influence capacity, which makes it agential rather than phenomenal. It is a rival to a phenomenal criterion, not an instance of one.

## The Bridging Literature the Map Has Missed Entirely

Separately from the pluralism papers, there is an edited volume dedicated to joining biological individuality to personal identity: **Meincke & Dupré (eds.), *Biological Identity*, Routledge 2020**. Chapter list verified individually at Crossref. Directly relevant chapters:

| Ch. | Pages | Chapter | Author |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 63–85 | What is the problem of biological individuality? | Eric T. Olson |
| 11 | 200–213 | Pregnancy and biological identity | Elselijn Kingma |
| 13 | 233–250 | The nature of persons and the nature of animals | Paul F. Snowdon |
| 14 | 251–278 | Processual animalism | Anne Sophie Meincke |

Olson and Snowdon are animalists: on their view the subject of experience *is* a biological individual of a specific kind (an organism), which is a direct, developed answer to the commissioning question and one that runs against the Map. Kingma's pregnancy chapter is a census case the Map does not treat at all — during gestation, how many subjects does one organism-shaped region contain, and does the answer track any of the biological criteria? Given [P-SC3](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc3)'s explicit refusal to date channel onset, this is the sharpest available test case for the Map's own position, and it is currently absent from the corpus.

The Map's individuation cluster cites none of this volume.

## Relation to Site Tenets

- **Tenet 1 (Dualism)** is what makes a phenomenal individuality criterion possible at all. If subject boundaries were organizational, the criterion would reduce to one of the existing four and the pluralism would already be complete.
- **Tenet 5 (Occam's Razor Has Limits)** is where the real argument lives, and Godfrey-Smith's bracketing clause is the exhibit. The organizational answer is not simpler-because-truer; it is simpler because the alternative was set aside in a subordinate clause before the analysis began. This is the same move the host article already makes against Michod's fitness criterion, applied to a target that states its own exclusion in writing.
- **Tenet 3 (Bidirectional Interaction)** supplies the reason the Map cannot treat this as decoration. [P-SC1](/positions/subject-census/#p-sc1) makes the census part of the state description; a phenomenal individuality criterion would be the boundary condition the selection law needs.
- **Conflict, stated honestly**: animalism (Olson, Snowdon) holds the subject to be a biological individual outright, and Godfrey-Smith's gradualism is compatible with there being no determinate count — which contradicts [P-I1](/positions/individuation-and-subjecthood/#p-i1)'s determinate-boundaries commitment. Neither is refuted by anything the Map holds; both are framework-boundary disagreements and should be registered as such rather than dispatched.

## Gaps in This Research

Stated plainly so that any article consuming this note inherits the limits rather than laundering them.

- **WebSearch was unavailable** (budget exhausted at 200/200 before this task began). The literature sweep was therefore hypothesis-driven retrieval against known venues, not open discovery. **A phenomenal-individuality criterion may have been proposed somewhere I did not think to look.** The absence claim in this note is scoped to: the SEP survey entry, the Pradeu-edited *Biology & Philosophy* 31(6) special issue, DiFrisco 2018, Fulda 2023, and Clarke 2010/2013. It is not a claim about the whole field.
- **"Rosengard in 1998" is unverified.** It is quoted as it appears in the Gómez-Emilsson & Percy abstract. I could not retrieve the reference list to resolve it, and the name may be a rendering of a different author. **Do not reproduce this attribution in an article without checking it first.**
- **Clarke 2010 and Pradeu 2016 were verified by metadata and publisher abstract only** — full text not retrieved. Claims about their internal enumerations rest on the abstracts and on the SEP's characterisation, not on the papers' own section structure. Clarke 2013 and DiFrisco 2018 abstracts were retrieved in full; Godfrey-Smith 2016 and 2020 were read as author-hosted primary PDFs.
- **The Meincke & Dupré chapters were verified by Crossref metadata only.** Titles, authors and pagination are confirmed; no chapter content was read. The characterisation of Olson and Snowdon as animalists is background knowledge, not a reading of these chapters.
- **IIT's exclusion postulate is characterised from IIT 4.0's abstract and from Fekete et al.'s critique**, not from the postulate's formal statement. The claim that IIT "delivers a census" is a fair summary but has not been checked against the formalism.
