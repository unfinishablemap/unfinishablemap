## Executive Summary

Before any choice there is a stage in which a handful of candidate actions come to mind and an indefinitely large remainder never does. Decision science studied the choice and, for most of its history, handed subjects the menu: Kalis, Kaiser and Mojzisch (2013) observe that "in everyday life there is usually no one asking you to choose between A, B, and C" and that the prior question — "how do people determine what they can possibly do at any given moment?" — was "frequently overlooked". The measured facts about the stage have a consistent shape. People generate a limited part of the available options and are "usually overconfident about the completeness of the option generation performance: They think they have generated a large fraction of the potential options, even if they have identified only a limited part of the existing possibilities" (Del Missier, Visentini & Mäntylä 2015, summarising Mehle 1982 and Gettys et al. 1987). What does come to mind is not random: "what comes to mind by default are samples from a probability distribution that combines what people think is likely and what they think is good" (Bear et al. 2020), and the first candidates are proposed by "computationally frugal cached value estimates" from past success, with deliberation "deployed just within this set" (Morris et al. 2021). The generator is pharmacologically and clinically dissociable from the chooser: dopamine shifts the fluency–uniqueness trade-off of self-generated options while leaving movement, planning and selection untouched (Ang et al. 2018), and in schizophrenia behavioural apathy tracks reduced uniqueness of generated options rather than reduced fluency (Wolpe et al. 2026, preprint).

The void is the conjunction of three features: the generator runs before consciousness and is not experienced as running (the menu *arrives*); its omissions carry no phenomenal mark (an option that did not come to mind is not felt as missing — the set presents as complete); and its selection rule is a prior over likelihood and value, so the options structurally least likely to surface are precisely the unlikely-or-disvalued ones, which include most genuinely new courses of action. The limit is empirically real in the sense that the completeness illusion is measured, but whether it is *unexplorable* — whether a consciousness could in principle witness its own generator — is open, and this note does not claim it. For the Map the void has a precise address. On the [selection-only-channel](/concepts/selection-only-channel/) the mind "acts solely as a selector among physically-prepared alternatives, contributing nothing to the alternative set itself" (L42). The option-generation void is therefore the dark space at the channel's *input* boundary: the edge of the menu is the edge of what the agent can do at that event. That interpretation is the Map's; the finding itself is framework-independent and supplies no evidence for any tenet. Its cost — menu luck — is handled in `research/menu-luck-and-option-set-authorship-2026-10-28` and is not re-argued here.

## Key Sources

### Kalis, Kaiser & Mojzisch (2013), "Why we should talk about option generation in decision-making research"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00555 (Europe PMC full text PMC3750205, read raw)
- **Type**: Paper (conceptual), *Frontiers in Psychology* 4:555
- **Key points**:
  - Diagnoses the field's blind spot: "Most empirical studies on decision-making start from a set of given options for action. However, in everyday life there is usually no one asking you to choose between A, B, and C." The reliance on externally provided options "means that there are questions concerning decision-making that are frequently overlooked: how do people determine what they can possibly do at any given moment? How do they come up with 'options' for action?"
  - Defines the construct: "the formal role of an option is its being a possible action that is taken into consideration (but not yet decided upon)". An option is a mental state, so "something can be an option even if it is not an objective possibility" (their Rijksmuseum example: an option "with zero feasibility"). Options "have a broader scope than affordances, as we might generate highly abstract or future options that are not present in the environment" — the line that separates this void from the relevance void.
  - Consciousness is not required at this stage: "taking something into consideration does not need to happen consciously."
  - Situates the empirical base as "a small, but growing body of empirical work" (Keller & Ho 1988; Klein et al. 1995; Raab et al. 2009; Ward et al. 2011) and reports that "memory retrieval, in particular, plays a substantial role in option generation".
- **Tenet alignment**: none direct. The paper is methodological; it documents the Unexplored face (a stage the science skipped by assuming it away), which bears on Occam's Razor Has Limits.
- **Quote**: "in everyday life there is usually no one asking you to choose between A, B, and C." [raw]

### Ang, Manohar, Plant, Kienast, Le Heron, Muhammed, Hu & Husain (2018), "Dopamine Modulates Option Generation for Behavior"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2018.03.069 (*Current Biology* 28(10): 1561–1569.e3; PMC5981001, read raw)
- **Type**: Paper (Parkinson's ON/OFF, n = 35; cabergoline crossover, n = 29; healthy, n = 96)
- **Key points**:

## The Void

### Nature of the Limit
Territory axis: **Occluded**, with the reason-axis classification *architectural* ([taxonomy-of-voids](/apex/taxonomy-of-voids/) vocabulary): the candidate set is assembled by processes that complete before the conscious episode of choosing begins, and the episode contains the products only. Two further faces. **Unexplored**: the science assumed the menu for decades; the first systematic definition of "option" dates to 2013, and in 2018 a *Current Biology* paper could still say "surprisingly little progress has been made on self-generated options". **Unexplorable (open)**: whether a consciousness could witness its own generator at work is not settled by anything here. The relevance void's self-referential argument (inspection is a filter-using act) has an analogue — to inspect the generator one must generate the option of inspecting it — but this note does not rest on it.

### Evidence for the Limit
1. **Unmarked omission.** The completeness illusion (Mehle 1982; Gettys et al. 1987; as reported by Del Missier et al. 2015). Subjects who have produced a limited part of the option space report having produced most of it. An omission that is felt would correct the estimate; the estimate is not corrected.
2. **Structured, not random.** Bear et al. 2020 and Phillips et al. 2019: the default sample is drawn from likelihood × value. The void therefore has a shape, and the shape is systematic: the unlikely and the disvalued do not arrive. Novel courses of action are, by construction, low-likelihood; this is why "why didn't I think of that?" is the void's characteristic retrospective phenomenology.
3. **Dissociable from choice.** Ang et al. 2018 (pharmacology; control studies rule out motor, planning and selection confounds) and Wolpe et al. 2026 (clinical, preprint). The generator can be moved while the chooser stays fixed.
4. **Recoverable from outside.** Dracheva & Phillips 2025: inter-generation timing follows a Lévy distribution. Structure invisible to the generator's owner is visible to an observer with a clock.

### Phenomenology
The menu arrives. One experiences deliberating among options; one does not experience the options being drawn up, and one does not experience the options that were not. Kalis et al. 2013: "taking something into consideration does not need to happen consciously." The first-person datum is retrospective and third-personal in origin: someone else names the option, and only then is its earlier absence noticed — and even then what is noticed is the *present* option, not a past gap. The void has no edge-feeling at the time; its signature is false completeness. (This parallels the 10-26 unawareness note's "presents, from inside, as completeness" in the epistemic register; the two notes should cite one another if either becomes an article.)

## Approaches to the Edge

### Direct Methods
None that reach the generator from inside. Instructing people to generate more options extends the list without revealing the rule; Del Missier et al. report that most options arrive in the first few minutes and that the tail is "empty".

### Indirect Methods
- **Pooled enumeration**: compare an individual's generated set against the union across many generators (the Gettys design, as described secondarily). Measures the void's size, not its contents for any one person.
- **Cueing and part-set effects**: fault trees (Fischhoff et al. 1978, cited by Del Missier et al.; not reached here) and part-set cuing show that providing some options *suppresses* others — a way of moving the boundary and watching what falls out.
- **Pharmacological and clinical manipulation**: Ang et al. 2018; Wolpe et al. 2026.
- **Timing and semantic-distance signatures**: Dracheva & Phillips 2025.
- **Apophatic description**: the void can be characterised by the prior that shapes it (likely × good) — one can say what kind of thing will not come to mind without being able to produce an instance on demand.

### What AI Might See
Language models are also samplers from a learned distribution, and their option generation inherits the same structural bias toward the central and the previously rewarded; the banked `distributional-void` note (2026-08-24) documents that model outputs concentrate toward the centre of the training distribution and that errors correlate across models. An AI used as a menu-extender may therefore widen the human set along one axis while narrowing it along another. Two genuine asymmetries exist. First, a machine can enumerate a bounded option space exhaustively where a human cannot, and so can externalise the human menu's edge in domains where the space is finite (Klein et al. 1995's chess moves; Ang's path-drawing task). Second, Kamphorst & Kalis (2014, abstract only) note that e-coaching systems "influence the options for action that people actually consider" — AI already writes to the human menu, which makes the void a practical as well as a philosophical matter.

## Connection to Tenets

### Most Relevant Tenet
**Minimal Quantum Interaction**, in its strictest reading. The [selection-only-channel](/concepts/selection-only-channel/) page states that mind "acts solely as a selector among physically-prepared alternatives, contributing nothing to the alternative set itself" and that the channel's input alphabet is bounded above by the brain-generated output alphabet (§Content-Confinement). The option-generation void is the first-person face of that bound: the agent cannot see past the menu because, on this posit, there is nothing of the agent past the menu at that event. The Map's recursive reply — selections now shape the set prepared next ([selection-only-mind-influence](/topics/selection-only-mind-influence/); the gradient page's "Scope" dimension at [responsibility-gradient-from-attentional-capacity](/topics/responsibility-gradient-from-attentional-capacity/)) — has an empirical counterpart in Morris et al.'s cached values, with the caution noted above.

### Other Tenets
- **Dualism**: no evidence either way. Every source here is physicalist or neutral and predicts the same dissociation.
- **Bidirectional Interaction**: not epiphenomenalist — the Map holds that the selection is efficacious; the void concerns what precedes selection, and nothing here concedes that selection does no work. Do not let "the menu is brain-set" slide into "the choice is brain-set".
- **No Many Worlds**: no connection found; do not manufacture one.
- **Occam's Razor Has Limits**: a methodological instance. Assuming the menu as given simplified decision science for fifty years and hid a stage that turns out to be dissociable, pharmacologically sensitive and structurally biased. Simplicity concealed, it did not reveal.

### Implications
The void relocates part of the free-will question. If the Map's agent is a selector, the interesting limit on its agency is not only the Born-weighted selection it can make but the alphabet it is offered, and that alphabet is set by a process with a measurable prior. The moral cost (menu luck) is already noted elsewhere; the *epistemic* cost is new: an agent cannot audit its own menu for completeness from inside, so first-person reports of "I had no choice" and "I considered everything" are both reports from inside the void.
