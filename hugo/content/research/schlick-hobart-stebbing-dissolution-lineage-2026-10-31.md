## Executive Summary

All three texts were reached. Schlick and Hobart were read in full raw text, and Stebbing only as OCR fragments of the 1958 reprint. The originator check changes the task's framing. Freedom as non-compulsion is not Schlick's idea, and Schlick says so. He opens ch. VII by calling free will a "pseudo-problem" that "has long since been settled by the efforts of certain sensible persons", its source disclosed "with exceptional clarity by Hume" (p. 143), and he promises to "say only what others have already said better" (p. 144). The form is Hobbes's (*Leviathan* ch. XXI: liberty is "the absence of Opposition", and "Liberty and Necessity are Consistent") and Hume's (*Treatise* 2.3.2; *Enquiry* §VIII ¶73–74). Hobart calls his own key line, "all compulsion is causation, but not all causation is compulsion", "the answer that has ordinarily been given" (p. 13). Both SEP entries file all of these as classical compatibilism, "developed in the modern era by the empiricists Hobbes and Hume, and reinvigorated in the early part of the twentieth century". The optimistic review's phrase "the earliest statement of its own form (Schlick 1939 p. 150)" is therefore wrong. Within the 1927–1940 window, Schlick 1930 is the first statement, which is all the topics page's table claims ("First statement in the window").

The lineage maps onto the symmetry challenge's three steps. The 1930s writers claimed parity with **no residue**. Hobart means free will "in the fullest, the most absolute sense … implying responsibility, merit and demerit, guilt and desert … that one 'could have done otherwise'" (p. 1). Schlick says the meaning of responsibility is "completely exhausted" by the question of where a motive should be applied, and "behind it there lurks no mysterious connection between transgression and requital" (p. 153). They reached full parity by making punishment forward-looking (Schlick p. 152; Hobart p. 25) and "could" hypothetical (Hobart p. 10). C. A. Campbell (1951), a libertarian, took Schlick's chapter as the probable "locus classicus of the 'pseudo-problem' theory" (p. 442). He answered it with two demands. One is backward-looking responsibility (pp. 447–448), which post-Strawson compatibilism meets. The other is "could have acted otherwise" in its "simple, categorical meaning" (p. 455), which is the host's residue (b). The SEP records the failure of the conditional analysis and the general compatibilist retreat from consequentialism after Strawson 1962. That is why the host measures parity against the four post-1960s families, which are exactly the SEP's "Contemporary Compatibilism" taxonomy (§§4.2–4.5).

Two findings correct live or research material. **Hobart answers Eddington by name.** The section "Material Fate" (pp. 16–17) quotes *Philosophy*, Jan. 1933, p. 41 and says "Prof. Eddington has succumbed" to the fatalist pitfall. That contradicts `topics/first-quantum-free-will-wave-1927-1940` L49 ("contains no physics"). **"A rootless and sourceless event" is Hobart's own wording** (p. 8), not a handout paraphrase as the 10-27 note says.

## Findings by source

### Schlick, *Problems of Ethics* (1939), ch. VII

- **The dissolution, with its debt to Hume.** "With hesitation and reluctance I prepare to add this chapter … [the problem] got into ethics and has become a much discussed problem only because of a misunderstanding. This is the so-called problem of the freedom of the will. Moreover, this pseudo-problem has long since been settled by the efforts of certain sensible persons; and, above all, the state of affairs just described has been often disclosed — with exceptional clarity by Hume. Hence it is really one of the greatest scandals of philosophy that again and again so much paper and printer's ink is devoted to this matter" (p. 143). "I shall, of course, say only what others have already said better" (p. 144). **Subject check**: "with exceptional clarity by Hume" modifies *disclosed*, and the thing disclosed is that the problem arose from misunderstanding. The settling is credited to "certain sensible persons". Do not write "settled with exceptional clarity by Hume" as though it were one quotation.
- **No claim that determinism is true.** "Whether, indeed, the principle of causality holds universally, whether, that is, determinism is true, we do not know; no one knows" (p. 144). "Fortunately, it is not necessary to lay claim to a final solution of the causal problem in order to say what is necessary in ethics concerning responsibility" (p. 145).
- **An allusion to quantum physics.** Settling determinism by reflection is "especially ridiculous when one considers with what enormous expenditure of experimental and logical skill contemporary physics carefully approaches the question of whether causality can be maintained for the most minute intra-atomic events" (p. 145). The words "quantum", "Heisenberg", "Compton" and "Eddington" do not occur, and the 10-27 zero counts are correct as word counts. But this sentence alludes to the quantum causality question (see "Corrections", item 2).
- **Two meanings of "law"** (pp. 146–148): "The natural law is not a prescription as to how something should behave" (p. 147). Civil laws compel, natural laws describe. "Compulsion" occurs "where man is prevented from realizing his natural desires" (p. 148).
- **Freedom.** Schlick says Hume indicated "the inadmissible confusion of the concepts of 'indeterminism' and 'freedom'", calling the genuine kind "freedom of conduct" (p. 150). "Freedom of conduct" is Schlick's rendering. Hume's own terms are "liberty of spontaneity" and "liberty of indifference" (*Treatise* 2.3.2), so do not put "freedom of conduct" in Hume's mouth. "Freedom means the opposite of compulsion; a man is free if he does not act under compulsion, and he is compelled or unfree when he is hindered from without in the realization of his natural desires" (p. 150).
- **Responsibility is forward-looking.** Retaliation "ought no longer to be defended in cultivated society … altogether barbarous"; "Punishment is an educative measure" (p. 152); "The question of who is responsible is the question concerning the correct point of application of the motive. And the important thing is that in this its meaning is completely exhausted; behind it there lurks no mysterious connection between transgression and requital" (p. 153).
- **Could have done otherwise.** The feeling of freedom says "I should have acted differently had I willed something else; but the feeling never says that I could also have willed something else … And it says even less that under exactly the same inner and outer conditions I could also have willed something else" (p. 155).
- **The randomness horn** (already on the topics page): "chance is identical with the absence of a cause; there is no other opposite of causality" (p. 156); "an indetermined will would destroy all responsibility" (p. 158).
- **Dating.** The preface is signed "Vienna, September 1930" (raw). Campbell writes "first published in 1931" (p. 442). The German imprint was not seen, so keep the Map's 1930 dating tied to the preface.

### Hobart (Dickinson S. Miller), *Mind* 1934

- **Identity.** Herbert Feigl, "Miller, Dickinson S." (*Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, 1967, via Encyclopedia.com): Miller "published both under his own name and under the pseudonym R. E. Hobart"; the 1934 article was "published, for obscure reasons, under the name R. E. Hobart", and in it "Miller brought up to date the essentials of the point of view of Hume and J. S. Mill". Feigl also lists "Hume without Scepticism" (*Mind* 39, 1930) under the Hobart name. The driver brief's "R. E. Miller" is a conflation: the pseudonym is R. E. Hobart and the author is Dickinson S. Miller (1868–1963). Note that Feigl's bibliography and the SEP "Compatibilism" bibliography both misprint the title: Feigl gives "Involving Determinism" and the SEP gives "Involving Indeterminism". The raw title reads "Determination".
- **Thesis** (p. 1): "there has never been any ground for the controversy between the doctrine of free will and determinism, that it is based upon a misapprehension, that the two assertions are entirely consistent, that one of them strictly implies the other". He claims the full content: "I mean it as implying responsibility, merit and demerit, guilt and desert. I mean it as implying, after an act has been performed, that one 'could have done otherwise' than one did" (p. 1).
- **Not a determinist thesis.** "I am not maintaining that determinism is true; only that it is true in so far as we have free will … it is not here affirmed that there are no small exceptions, no slight undetermined swervings, no ingredient of absolute chance. All that is here said is that such absence of determination, if and so far as it exists, is no gain to freedom, but sheer loss of it; no advantage to the moral life, but blank subtraction from it" (p. 2).
- **The randomness and disappearing-agent horn.** "Now, in so far as this 'interposition of the self' is undetermined, the act is not *its* act" (p. 6; the scan OCRs the italic word as "is"), "it does not issue from any concrete continuing self; it is born at the moment, of nothing, hence it expresses no quality; it bursts into being from no source" (p. 7); "as if it had been thrown into his mind from without … by a freakish demon" (p. 7). The duplicate-self ("a horse inside") objection: either the inner self decided "because, on the whole, it preferred to decide in that way, or the decision was an underived event, a rootless and sourceless event" (p. 8). "Determinism is free will expressed in the passive voice" (p. 7).
- **Freedom and "can".** Freedom "means the absence of any interference (whether taking the form of restraint or constraint) with his exercise of that power" (p. 8). "It is just because determinism is true, because a law obtains, that one 'could have done otherwise'" (p. 10). Hobart cites Moore, *Ethics* (1912), ch. vi as making "in effect the same remark" (p. 10 n. 1). This is the conditional analysis that the SEP cites as "Hobart 1934".
- **Compulsion** (p. 13): "The answer that has ordinarily been given is surely correct; all compulsion is causation, but not all causation is compulsion." Hobart presents this as received doctrine, not as his own coinage.
- **Material Fate: a reply to Eddington** (pp. 16–17). "We have to notice the pitfall in our subject to which, similarly occupied, Prof. Eddington has succumbed." He quotes Eddington ("What significance is there in my mental struggle to-night whether I shall or shall not give up smoking, if the laws which govern the matter of the physical universe already pre-ordain for the morrow a configuration of matter …", n.: "Philosophy, Jan., 1933, p. 41"). His answer: "Fatalism says that my morrow is determined no matter how I struggle … Determinism says that my morrow is determined through my struggle" (p. 16). The reference matches Eddington, "Physics and Philosophy", *Philosophy* 8 (1933): 30–43, as recorded in the 10-27 note. **What Hobart answers is Eddington's case for why determinism would empty deliberation, not the quantum posit.** He nowhere discusses indeterminacy in physics.
- **His mind–body picture** (p. 17). On Eddington's supposition of complete physical determinism, "the mental process is paralleled in the brain by a physical process … the psychic side of it, the mental struggle proper, a con-cause or side of the cause". The determinist "need not, but may of course, hold this doctrine of automatism". Hobart offers this as an option, not a commitment. Either way it is a parallelist picture the Map rejects, and Hobart cannot be enlisted for interaction.
- **Sourcehood and causa sui.** "The moral self cannot be causa sui. To cause his original self a man must have existed before his original self" (p. 18). The indeterminist's "absolute source" must mean "a source that has in turn no source" (p. 15).
- **Desert.** Hobart himself rejects retributive desert: "owing to reasons for which I have no space here, I cannot hold the theory". The ends of justice are restitution, non-repetition and deterrence (p. 25). He adds that "the idea of desert collapses altogether on the indeterminist's conception of conduct", so the retributive theory "cannot be said to be logically bound up with indeterminism" (p. 25). His account of praise and blame (pp. 4–6) is aretaic: praise is "description, with expressed or implied admiration" of character. That is not purely forward-looking, which distinguishes him from Schlick.

### Hobbes and Hume (the originators of the modern form)

- Hobbes, *Leviathan* ch. XXI: "Liberty, or FREEDOME, signifieth (properly) the absence of Opposition; (by Opposition, I mean externall Impediments of motion;)"; marginal heading and text: "Liberty and Necessity are Consistent". The SEP "Compatibilism" entry calls Hobbes's "no stop" passage "an exemplary expression of classical compatibilism". The SEP "Free Will" entry adds that Hobbes (1654) answered the charge that determinism makes blame unjust "by endorsing broadly consequentialist justifications of blame and punishment … (cf. Schlick 1939; Nowell-Smith 1948; Smart 1961)". So Schlick's forward-looking turn also has a Hobbesian source.
- Hume, *Treatise* 2.3.2: "Few are capable of distinguishing betwixt the liberty of spontaniety [sic, Gutenberg], as it is called in the schools, and the liberty of indifference; betwixt that which is opposed to violence, and that which means a negation of necessity and causes." *Enquiry* §VIII ¶73 calls the section a "reconciling project": "By liberty, then, we can only mean a power of acting or not acting, according to the determinations of the will … this hypothetical liberty is universally allowed to belong to every one who is not a prisoner and in chains". ¶74: "liberty, when opposed to necessity, not to constraint, is the same thing with chance". Hume thus states **both** halves of the 1930s package, freedom as non-constraint and the randomness horn. Part II adds that actions which "proceed not from some *cause* in the character and disposition of the person who performed them … can neither redound to his honour, if good; nor infamy, if evil", which is the ancestor of Hobart's pp. 4–7. Today's sibling note on Franklin 2011 records Franklin naming the first luck formulation "the Hume–Hobart formulation" (pp. 203, 208–209), which matches this lineage.
- Mill (*Examination of Sir William Hamilton's Philosophy*, 1865, ch. XXVI) is named only by Feigl's carrier and was not read. Do not cite him from this note.

### Campbell 1951: the libertarian reply that located the residue

- Campbell treats Schlick ch. VII as the probable source text. He looks for "the locus classicus of the 'pseudo-problem' theory, if locus classicus there be … At least one important 'document in the case' is, I suspect, Chapter VII of Moritz Schlick's Problems of Ethics" (p. 442). Keep the hedge: "if … there be", "I suspect".
- "But it seems a simple matter to show that Schlick's analysis is untenable" (p. 447). (i) A dog's motives can be shaped by punishment, yet we do not hold it responsible, because "we do commonly demand the contra-causal sort of freedom as a condition of moral responsibility" (p. 447). (ii) The dead and the historical are held responsible, which "Schlick's essentially 'forward-looking' interpretation of punishment and responsibility" cannot allow (pp. 447–448).
- On "could have acted otherwise": "I submit that the expression is taken in its simple, categorical meaning, without any suppressed 'if' clause to qualify it … a condition of A's moral responsibility is that he could have chosen otherwise" (p. 455). He engages Moore's and Nowell-Smith's hypothetical readings directly.
- **Place.** Campbell's objection (ii) is backward-looking responsibility. The post-Strawson families supply it without indeterminism (SEP "Free Will": "many, perhaps even most, compatibilists have come to reject this consequentialist approach to moral responsibility in the wake of P. F. Strawson's 1962 landmark essay"). Objection (i) and the categorical reading are the incompatibilist demand that the host books as residue (b). Grant's 1952 reply (*Mind* 61: 381–385, Crossref-verified) was not read.

### SEP framing (why the host's reference class is post-1960s)

- "Compatibilism's place in contemporary philosophy has developed in at least three stages": classical ("developed in the modern era by the empiricists Hobbes and Hume, and reinvigorated in the early part of the twentieth century"); a 1960s transition (the Consequence Argument, Ginet 1966; Frankfurt 1969 against PAP; Strawson 1962); and contemporary forms "that diverge from the classical variety" (McKenna & Coates 2024, §2). The contemporary forms are listed as §4.2 Hierarchical, §4.3 The Reason View, §4.4 Reasons-Responsive and §4.5 Strawsonian. These are the host's four families in the same order of kinds.
- The verdict on the classical analysis: "The classical compatibilist analysis of 'could have done otherwise' thus fails" (§2.2); "In light of the failure of the classical compatibilists' conditional analysis, the burden of proof rests squarely on the compatibilists" (§2.3). SEP "Free Will" speaks of "classical compatibilism's recognized failure to deliver a satisfactory analysis of the freedom to do otherwise", and lists "Moore 1912; Hobart 1934; Schlick 1939; Nowell-Smith 1948, 1954; Campbell 1951; Ayer 1954; Smart 1961" as the early-twentieth-century discussion.

## Relation to the Compatibilist Symmetry Challenge (the Map's reading)

The host's pattern has three steps: parity in moral output, a differentiator outside moral theory, and a residue. The 1930s writers held the first step in an absolute form and the other two not at all. For them there was no rival metaphysics to differ from, since the indeterminist "contradicts himself" (Hobart p. 5) and the problem arose "only because of a misunderstanding" (Schlick p. 143). So there was no residue. They got there by redefinition: desert became forward-looking or was dropped, and "could" became hypothetical. Campbell's 1951 reply is the first place in this lineage where the residue is named against a specific text. The later literature then sorted his two demands. Backward-looking responsibility was taken over by compatibilism. Categorical alternatives stayed with the incompatibilist, and that is where the host locates residue (b).

The host's discipline therefore inherits the dissolution's parity claim and rejects its two strategies. It does not call the libertarian confused, because the challenge concedes "tenet-coherence". It does not define residue away, because step 3 books it. The host's bullet against "Treating compatibilism as pragmatic-only" also gains a historical referent. The pragmatic-only form is real: it is Schlick's (and Hobbes's, per the SEP). The four families are the ones that left it behind.

## Tenet evaluation

- **Dualism.** None of the figures can be enlisted. Hobbes was a materialist. Schlick was a logical empiricist who treats the problem as a confusion. Hobart offers a parallelist "con-cause" picture (p. 17) and a determinist analysis. Stebbing was an analytic realist who criticised the physicists' idealism. Campbell is a libertarian, but this note establishes nothing about his metaphysics of mind, so do not call him a dualist or an agent-causalist.
- **Bidirectional Interaction.** Hobart's p. 17 parallelism treats the mental as a "side of the cause". If the writer mentions it, the Map's interactionism rejects it and concedes nothing to epiphenomenalism. The host fold does not need it.
- **Minimal Quantum Interaction.** Not engaged by the fold. Hobart's reply to Eddington concerns fatalism, not indeterminacy. Schlick's p. 145 allusion says ethics need not wait on intra-atomic causality, which the Map can accept: the Map's case for indeterminism is not a moral argument.
- **Occam's Razor Has Limits.** Hobart's dilemma (p. 8: the self decides from preference, or the decision is "rootless and sourceless") has the same shape as Schlick's partition (p. 156). Answering it in-framework is the job of the open P3 on `topics/first-quantum-free-will-wave-1927-1940` L89 (todo ~L3470). The Map's posit is a settling by the agent, so it is neither uncaused nor caused by a prior preference. Tenet 5 must not be turned on the opponent.
- **No Many Worlds.** Not engaged.

> The parity claim descends from a stronger one, that libertarianism was a confusion. Moritz Schlick (1930, tr. 1939) called free will a "pseudo-problem" whose source Hume had disclosed "with exceptional clarity" (p. 143), and R. E. Hobart (Dickinson S. Miller) held in 1934 that "there has never been any ground for the controversy" (p. 1). Both defined freedom as absence of compulsion or interference, as Hobbes (*Leviathan*, ch. XXI) and Hume (*Enquiry*, §VIII) had; Susan Stebbing (1937) agreed that not all causation compels. Schlick and Hobart left no residue: punishment became "an educative measure" (Schlick, p. 152) and "could have done otherwise" a conditional (Hobart, p. 10). Treating Schlick's chapter as the likeliest "locus classicus of the 'pseudo-problem' theory" (p. 442), C. A. Campbell (1951) pressed two demands: responsibility that looks backward (pp. 447–448), which the families below meet, and "could have acted otherwise" in its "simple, categorical meaning" (p. 455), residue (b) above. The conditional analysis failed (McKenna and Coates 2024, §2.2), so parity is measured against those families. Schlick and Hobart also stated [the randomness objection](/topics/first-quantum-free-will-wave-1927-1940/#randomness-objection).

## Writer guards

1. **Originator.** Never call Schlick (or Schlick 1939 p. 150) the earliest statement of freedom as non-compulsion. He credits Hume and disclaims originality (pp. 143–144). The modern form is Hobbes's and Hume's. "First in the 1927–1940 window" is the most that can be claimed for Schlick 1930.
2. **Hume quotation subject.** "With exceptional clarity" attaches to Hume's *disclosing* the misunderstanding, not to the settling (p. 143). "Freedom of conduct" is Schlick's label, not Hume's.
3. **Hobart's name.** The author is Dickinson S. Miller and the pseudonym is R. E. Hobart; there is no "R. E. Miller". The title is "Determination", never "Determinism" (Feigl's misprint) or "Indeterminism" (the SEP's misprint).
4. **Hobart's desert.** Hobart claims to secure "guilt and desert" (p. 1) and also rejects the retributive theory himself (p. 25). Do not write that he defended retributive desert. Do not write that his whole account is forward-looking either, since his praise and blame are aretaic (pp. 4–6).
5. **Stebbing.** No page is fixed for fragment (A). Cite "1937, ch. X" and paraphrase. The quotable span is "not all causation is a form of being compelled". "Blame entails free will" is **Wisdom's** formula, which Stebbing reports. Do not call Stebbing's chapter a wholesale dissolution: fragment (A) has the problem of free will arising at "responsible to".
6. **Campbell.** Keep his hedge on "locus classicus" ("if … there be", "I suspect"), which the draft does with "likeliest". He is a libertarian. Nothing here licenses calling him a dualist or recruiting him for the Map's tenets. The categorical demand is his position, and the host books it as a residue, not as a result.
7. **The failure verdict is the SEP's.** "The conditional analysis failed" carries the McKenna & Coates citation. Do not raise it to "refuted" or attach it to a named refuter, because this note did not read Lehrer, Chisholm or Austin.
8. **No dualist enlistment, no epiphenomenalism.** Hobart's p. 17 parallelism stays out of the host. If it appears anywhere, it appears as the picture the Map rejects.
9. **Style.** Do not write "This is not X. It is Y." and do not use "load-bearing". Run a pronoun check: the paragraph's "Both" binds to Schlick and Hobart, and the next element is a `##` heading, so nothing downstream is orphaned.
