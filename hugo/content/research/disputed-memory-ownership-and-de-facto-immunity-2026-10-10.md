## Executive Summary

Shoemaker (1970) held that memory-based self-ascriptions are immune to error through misidentification only *de facto*: as things are, our memories derive from our own pasts, but quasi-memory shows a world where they need not. Pryor (1999) sharpened this with *which-object* (*wh*-) misidentification and concluded that memory-based beliefs "have only a de facto immunity, given that cases like Dee's do not in fact occur" (p. 297). Since 2014 a literature on observer memory has split over whether memory is even logically immune. On the "yes" side are Fernández, Lin and Morgan, and Hamilton and Evans before them. On the "no" side are Michaelian and García-Carpintero, with Perrin & McCarroll in between. García-Carpintero (2024) argues that ordinary reconstructive cases, built from testimony or garbled perception, show memory is at most de facto immune, and he names "disputed memories" as the phenomenon.

The empirical record is solid for a small literature. Sheen, Kemp & Rubin (2001) ran three experiments. Most adult same-sex twins had at least one memory whose protagonist they disputed. The twins agreed on nearly everything except who the protagonist was. They rated disputed memories *higher* than undisputed ones on reliving, seeing, hearing, setting, spatial layout and felt emotion, and believed them no less. Close siblings and friends had such memories far less often. A 2006 re-analysis of 77 cases found the disputes self-serving: misfortunes and achievements are claimed and wrongdoing is given away. An independent group replicated the twin effect (Ikier et al. 2003) and found the disputed events unusually mundane (Küntay et al. 2004). Laboratory work on observation inflation, memory conformity and collaborative "memory stealing" reproduces the self/other confusion in non-twins. One 2025 study finds it symmetrical, with other's actions taken as one's own as often as one's own as other's. That suggests a generic source confusion rather than a special pull toward appropriating others' acts.

Whether a disputed memory is an IEM failure is a question about which grounds IEM is relative to, and current evidence cannot answer it. The Map can safely take the narrower result: memory presents ownership without certifying it.

## 1. De Facto Immunity: What Shoemaker Meant and How It Has Been Read {#de-facto}

### 1.1 Shoemaker 1970 [2nd]

*American Philosophical Quarterly* 7(4): 269–285; reprinted in *Identity, Cause, and Mind* (CUP 1984; OUP 2003), pp. 19–48. The APQ text was not reachable. Three independent secondaries quote the same passage in agreement.

- **The memory example** (p. 270, as quoted by García-Carpintero 2024, pp. 1–2): "if I claim on the strength of memory that I saw John yesterday, and have a full and accurate memory of the incident, it cannot be the case that I remember someone seeing John but have misidentified that person as myself; my memory claim 'I saw John' is subject to error through misidentification with respect to the term 'John' (for it could have been John's twin or double that I saw), but not with respect to 'I'".
- **The same passage, longer** (pp. 269–70, as quoted by James 2021, p. 196, with James's bracketed insertion): "My memory report could of course be mistaken, for one can misremember such incidents, but it could not be the case that I have a full and accurate memory of the past incident but am mistaken in thinking that the person I remember [exemplifying the property] was myself". The operative restriction is *full and accurate memory*. Shoemaker's immunity claim concerns accurate memories, and he explicitly allows misremembering.
- **The diagnosis** (p. 270, as quoted by Salje 2025, pp. 3–4): the apparent immunity rests on the fact that "we refuse to call someone's having knowledge of a past experience a case of his remembering it unless the past experience belonged to the rememberer himself."
- **De facto vs logical.** García-Carpintero's reconstruction (2024, p. 2): "If it is conditions in the actual and nearby worlds that rule out the possibility of the specified mistake, then the judgment is de facto IEM relative to evidence E. If the conditions in every logically possible world rule out the mistake, then the judgment is logically IEM relative to E." Salje's gloss (p. 4): de facto IEM holds where "mistakes of the kind ruled out by the status of IEM can't in fact occur, given the world we happen to be in"; logical immunity holds where "these mistakes are impossible in any world, as a matter of conceptual or logical necessity."
- **Terminology guard.** The task brief says "de facto vs de jure". Every secondary consulted (García-Carpintero, Salje, Pryor, SEP) pairs "de facto" with **"logical"**; none uses "de jure". Without the APQ text, absence from Shoemaker is not established. Writers should use "logical".
- **The Map's reprint citation holds.** IEM L44 and quasi-memory L57 cite reprint p. 46 for "de facto". Pryor's notes cite the reprint at "20–22 and 45–46" (n. 38) and "46" (n. 42), and the SEP gives "Shoemaker 1970: 46".

### 1.2 Evans 1982 [2nd]

*The Varieties of Reference*, ch. 7. Salje locates the memory and quasi-memory discussion at "1982:235–48" (n. 4), and the SEP supplement at "§7.5".

- Evans holds that defining quasi-memory "leaves the question of the content of memory states quite untouched; it can still be right to say [. . .] that an apparent memory of φing is necessarily an apparent memory of oneself φ-ing" (p. 248, as quoted by Salje, p. 4; Salje hyphenates "φ-ing" inconsistently between his two quotations of it).
- On Pryor's report (1999, p. 290), Evans holds that a subject with another's apparent memories "merely has a *memory illusion*" and that such apparent memories "could not by themselves offer him knowledge that someone pulled the cat's tail." Pryor's n. 43 cites *Varieties* "244–45" for this.
- **Page discrepancy.** García-Carpintero (2024, p. 12) cites "Evans (1982, pp. 144–145)" for the same argument. Pryor's n. 43 gives 244–45 for it, and Salje's range is 235–48, so "144" is probably a misprint. Do not propagate it.
- Evans is the template for reading (ii): when an apparent memory's subject turns out to be someone else, the right conclusion is "memory illusion", with no knowledge of the existential retained.

### 1.3 Pryor 1999: *de re* and *which-object* misidentification [F, page images]

*Philosophical Topics* 26(1–2): 271–304. Pryor's term is **"which-object-misidentification, or *wh*-misidentification, for short"** (p. 281). The brief's "which-object" is accurate, and the Map's IEM L46 uses the short form.

- ***De re*** (p. 275): "we have a case of *de re* misidentification whenever one's justification for the proposition believed or expressed rests on one's prior justification for believing, among other things, a certain identity assumption—and this identity assumption turns out to be false."
- ***Wh*-misidentification** (p. 282, transcribed): "(i) A subject has some grounds G that offer him knowledge of the existential generalization ∃x Fx. (ii) Partly on the basis of G, the subject is also justified, or takes himself to be justified, in believing of some object *a* that it is F. (iii) But in fact *a* is not F. Some distinct object (or objects) *y* is F, and it's because the grounds G "derive" in the right way from this fact *about y* that they offer the subject knowledge that ∃x Fx." Condition (iii) decides the twin case (§3).
- **Defeaters.** Only an *undercutting* defeater may be used to show vulnerability; an *additive* one, which supplies positive evidence that someone else was F, does not count (p. 296: "So D* is not an additive defeater. It *undercuts* S's justification for believing that he was F."). García-Carpintero (2024, p. 11) gives the source as Pryor p. 284.
- **On memory** (p. 290): "I side with Shoemaker in this debate. It seems plausible to me that Dee's quasi-memories *do* offer him knowledge that someone pulled the cat's tail, and hence I think that Dee *is* a victim of *wh*-misidentification." He concedes that "I don't have much positive argument to present in support of the claim".
- **Conclusion** (p. 297): "So first-person memory-based beliefs are not *immune* to *wh*-misidentification. At best, they have only a de facto immunity, given that cases like Dee's do not in fact occur." This is the hinge. Pryor's de facto clause is a factual claim that Dee-type cases do not occur, so the question becomes whether disputed memories are relevantly like Dee's.
- **Package deal** (p. 296): memory may give "a 'package deal' of justification: it tells me that *someone* performed a certain action, and it also tells me that *I* am the person who performed that action", and the two pieces may be "in principle, separable".

## 2. The Disputed-Memory Record {#record}

### 2.1 Sheen, Kemp & Rubin 2001 [F]

*Memory & Cognition* 29(6): 779–788, doi 10.3758/BF03196407. The authors are at Canterbury (NZ) and Duke.

**Definition** (abstract, p. 779): "disputed memories, in which people dispute ownership of a memory." The phenomenon differs from other memory errors in that "the major detail that is in dispute is who the protagonist in the event is" (p. 779).

**Scope disclaimer** (p. 779): "Our aim in this early stage of the research was not to ascertain the accuracy of the memories, nor to resolve questions of who owned them."

**Experiment 1** (cue words, no mention of disputes; pp. 780–782):
- Sample: 20 same-sex adult twin sets (11 monozygotic, 9 dizygotic; 16 female), interviewed together, 45 cue words, events from ages 8–12.
- 36 disputed memories in all. 14 sets had at least one; 6 had none. The mean was 1.8 (range 0–14). No zygosity or gender difference.
- 21 disputes were *discovered during the session*; 15 were known beforehand. The 2006 commentary adds that the discovery "often seemed to come as a surprise to the participants, and many tried to assert their own right to the memory" (p. 9).
- Table 1 shows disputes in both directions, for example "Both think they got a nail in their foot" and "Both say the other was the one who stole lollies from a shop".

**Experiment 2** (direct elicitation; pp. 782–784):
- Of 40 respondents, 26 (65%) reported at least one dispute: 25 women and 1 man. 20 pairs (19 female, 1 male; 10 MZ, 10 DZ) produced 33 disputed and 167 undisputed shared memories. Both twins had to confirm "a clear recollection of the event" for it to count.
- **Agreement**:
  - "The twins generally agreed on most of the details surrounding the event" (p. 782).
  - On structured questions they agreed on who else was present in 78% of cases, what they were doing in 94%, how they came to be there in 78% and their age in 81%.
  - The authors conclude: "there was good agreement on most aspects of the memory except who the protagonist was" (p. 784).
- **Perspective** (p. 782):
  - 74% of disputed and 64% of undisputed memories were recalled from the field perspective (no significant difference).
  - Of the 33 disputed memories, 16 were recalled by *both* twins from their own field of view; in 10, one twin used the field and the other the observer perspective; in 7, one used the field and the other could not tell.
  - Since one twin per pair is wrong, at least 16 erroneous memories were field memories, presenting the event from the inside.
- **Ratings** (Table 4, p. 784; 7-point scales; disputed vs undisputed; df = 19):

| Scale | Disputed | Undisputed | t |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reliving | 5.5 | 4.7 | 4.31 (p < .001) |
| Actually remember the event | 6.1 | 5.7 | 2.62 (p < .05) |
| See in my mind | 6.0 | 5.3 | 4.81 (p < .001) |
| Hear in my mind | 4.8 | 4.3 | 2.12 (p < .05) |
| Recall the setting | 6.1 | 5.7 | 2.52 (p < .05) |
| I know its spatial layout | 5.1 | 4.6 | 2.66 (p < .05) |

  Narrative, significance and rehearsal scales showed no differences.
- **Phenomenology**:
  - "although the memories must have been partially fabricated for at least half of the sample, the measures of memory quality provide no indication of this" (p. 783).
  - "the disputed memories were no less believed than the nondisputed memories even though both twins realized that one of them was wrong" (p. 783).
  - "neither twin was willing to concede that his or hers was the false memory" (p. 787).
- **Authors' caveat on the ratings** (p. 786). The direction of cause is unclear: disputed memories may get higher imagery ratings because vivid memories are likelier to be disputed, or the disputes may "lead to more imagery, possibly as part of an attempt to assert one's ownership".
- **Other-ascription.** In one Experiment 2 case both twins remember the *other* as the runaway, each recalling sitting in the car while their mother searched. This "was the only one in which both twins recalled their opposite as being the protagonist in the memory as opposed to themselves" (p. 786).

**Experiment 3** (questionnaire, 69 students; pp. 784–785): 6 reported a disputed memory, 3 with siblings and 3 with same-sex friends. Close siblings (within 2 years) came in at 8%, against 65% of twins (p < .001). No man reported one.

**Proposed mechanisms** (p. 786), offered as possibilities and not tested:
- Hyman's three processes: event acceptance, imagery or narrative creation, and source-monitoring error.
- A narrative or image formed "either by both twins having been present and having seen the event occur or by their having heard or told the story to someone else".
- The mistaken twin may have heard the story told by someone who confused the twins' identities.
- "When they do not have their own particular memories, they use their twin's personal memories, unconsciously or otherwise, as a default."

### 2.2 Sheen, Kemp & Rubin 2006 [F]

*Genes, Brain and Behavior* 5(Suppl. 1): 9–13, doi 10.1111/j.1601-183X.2006.00189.x.

- **Not an independent sample.** The corpus is 77 disputed memories: 69 published in 2001, 6 more from the 2001 Experiment 3 records and 2 from Sheen's PhD thesis (Sheen 2002, study 4). 71 came from 42 twin pairs.
- **Direction** (Table 1, p. 11): claimed for oneself, 52; claimed for the other, 14; uncertain or claimed by both, 11. Misfortunes were claimed as one's own in 27 of 34. Wrongdoings were claimed in only 3 of 21. Good events were claimed in 17 of 18 (χ²(3, n = 56) = 30.0, p < .001).
- **Abstract** (p. 9): disputants "claim for themselves memories for achievements and suffered misfortunes but are more likely to give away memories of personal wrongdoing." In the authors' words: "the disputants' memories could be described as self-serving" (p. 11).
- **Detail does not adjudicate.** In a transcribed roller-skate dispute (from Sheen 2002, p. 89), both twins "staked a claim to the memory by producing relevant perceived details" (p. 10). The authors conclude that "the ability to remember detail of this kind does not actually help resolve memory ownerships, because often both disputants can make claims of this kind and one of them must be doing so erroneously" (p. 12).
- **Parents** (Sheen 2002 study 4, p. 10): parents supplied 60 events that had happened to one twin only. The twins disputed 2 of them. For 10, the twins "agreed between themselves but disagreed with their parent as to who had featured in the event". The authors call parents "usually ineffectual referees" (p. 11).
- **Base rate** (p. 11): "it is uncertain whether these distortions are actually rare in other people or whether they are common but rarely detected as such."
- **Novelty claim** (p. 11): there had been "no previous research showing that people might unconsciously appropriate for themselves (or give away) memories that originate in the experience of others."

### 2.3 Independent replication and extension

- **Ikier, Tekcan, Gülgöz & Küntay 2003** [A], *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 17(2): 237–247, doi 10.1002/acp.869, "Whose life is it anyway? Adoption of each other's autobiographical memories by twins". Monozygotic twins, dizygotic twins and siblings were tested. "Disputed memories were more common among MZ twins than in DZ twins and siblings". Disputed memories "were more likely to come from events experienced during preschool years". The authors "consider disputed memories as instances of false memories, at least partly influenced by social interactional processes, and event characteristics." Sample size and nationality are not in the abstract. The brief's "Turkish twins" is **unverified**: the full text was not read, and a search summary's assertion is not evidence.
- **Küntay, Gülgöz & Tekcan 2004** [A], *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 18(4): 405–413, doi 10.1002/acp.976. Independent raters judged disputed events "more mundane than nondisputed personal memories", and "a substantial portion of the disputed memories from the Ikier et al. study were for events that our subjects reported to have also experienced." Ordinary events are easy to reconstruct from schema, and the raw material of a false memory may be one's own similar experiences rather than the twin's.
- Note the zygosity contrast: Ikier et al. found MZ > DZ, while Sheen et al. found no zygosity effect.

### 2.4 Non-twin analogues

- **Vicarious memories**, Pillemer et al. 2015 [A]. Memories of events that happened to others share personal memories' phenomenological profile "at lower levels of intensity". The subject knows they are another's.
- **Borrowed memories**, Brown, Croft Caderao, Fields & Marsh 2015 [A]. Most undergraduates admit telling others' stories as their own. "A substantial percentage of respondents expressed uncertainty as to whether an autobiographical experience actually belonged to them or to someone else, and most respondents have confronted somebody over ownership of a particular story."
- **Memory conformity**, Gabbert, Memon & Allan 2003 [A]. After discussing an event with a co-witness who saw a different video, "A significant proportion (71%) of witnesses who had discussed the event went on to mistakenly recall items acquired during the discussion." This is the testimonial route in the laboratory: what another perceived is recalled as one's own perception.
- **Collaborative "stealing"**, Jalbert, Wulff & Hyman 2021 [A]. After collaborative recall, participants "were more likely to adopt information from partners as their own memories than attribute their contributions to their partners". These biases "only occurred following collaborative remembering and not when individuals received comparable information in a non-social setting."
- **Observation inflation**:
  - Lindner, Echterhoff, Davidson & Brand 2010 [A]: "Action observation robustly produced false memories of self-performance". The effect "persisted despite warnings". The authors favoured interpersonal motor simulation over source monitoring.
  - Lindner, Schain & Echterhoff 2016 [A] support motor simulation.
  - **Neszmélyi & Pfister 2025** [A] ran a full source-monitoring test: "Recognising other's actions as one's own occurred at the same rate as misattributing one's own actions to another agent." This "speaks against a special status for appropriating observed actions."

### 2.5 What subjects claim, with what confidence and phenomenology

- **Claim**: "it happened to me", as an episodic memory and usually from the field perspective. Experiment 2's inclusion rule required a "clear recollection". Most disputes are self-claims (52 of 77). A minority are mutual disownings (14 of 77), concentrated on wrongdoing.
- **Confidence**: no lower than for undisputed shared memories (6.2 vs 5.8, n.s.). Neither twin concedes. Disputes are often discovered only by accident, in conversation.
- **Phenomenology**: richer than baseline on six of seven recollection and imagery scales, with the authors' caveat that the dispute itself may inflate the ratings. Nothing in the rated qualities distinguishes the true memory from the false.
- **Shared content**: high agreement on setting, others present and activity. The disagreement is confined to the protagonist.

## 3. The Core Question {#core-question}

### 3.1 What an IEM failure would require of the mistaken twin

Take twin B, who judges "I was sent home for my skirt" when only twin A was. On Pryor's definition (p. 282), B's judgement is a *wh*-misidentification only if three things hold:
- (a) B's grounds G offer knowledge that someone was sent home.
- (b) G derives in the right way from A's being sent home.
- (c) This would survive an undercutting, non-additive defeater of the self-ascription.

For it to be a failure of *memory's* immunity, G must also be memory grounds in the sense IEM is relative to. And for it to breach *de facto* immunity, it must happen in the actual world, which on Sheen et al.'s evidence it does for at least one twin per pair.

### 3.2 Reading (i): a memory-based self-ascription that misidentifies

The strongest case:
- **Memory by every first-person and functional test.** B's state is a field-perspective, vividly recollected, fully believed episodic seeming (§2.5). On Fernández's functionalist definition, as James applies it to Aunt Gayle (p. 194), such an image "plays the mnemonic role" and so counts as an episodic memory.
- **The existential survives the dispute.** The twins agree that one of them was sent home, and "on most aspects of the memory except who the protagonist was" (p. 784). The error sits in the who-slot alone, which is the signature of misidentification. On condition (c): B's grounds include accurate details of school, skirt and setting that both twins share, so undercutting B's "it was me" plausibly leaves B's grounds supporting "one of us was".
- **Pryor's condition (iii) is plausibly met for co-witnessed events.** The authors list co-presence as one route (p. 786), though how many disputes arose that way was not measured. If B saw A sent home, B's grounds derive from the fact about A. Observation inflation is the laboratory proof that perceiving another's act can turn into a first-person memory of doing it.
- **Philosophers have already classed this case.** James calls the co-witnessed case "an exemplar of a judgment that is subject to error through misidentification". García-Carpintero argues that such reconstructive cases show memory is "at most de facto IEM".
- **The self-serving pattern suggests an identification step.** *(Map reconstruction, not in the sources.)* If the protagonist slot were simply carried over from the original trace, its errors would not track valence. That they do (misfortune 27/34 claimed, wrongdoing 3/21) suggests the slot is filled at reconstruction by a motivated attribution. A judgement whose subject is assigned by a biased attribution is identification-dependent in Evans's sense, which removes the usual explanation of immunity for these cases.

### 3.3 Reading (ii): not memory-based in the relevant sense

The strongest case:
- **Shoemaker's own formulation excludes the case.** His immunity claim concerns "a full and accurate memory of the incident" (p. 270). B has no accurate memory of being sent home, so what B has is misremembering, which Shoemaker explicitly allows. Fernández makes this a definition: IEM fails only on grounds that are "fully accurate". Evans calls such states "memory illusions"; Morgan denies any "memory-like relation" to another's past. On all four, nothing about B touches the immunity.
- **Every mechanism Sheen et al. propose is a source error about the kind of grounds.** Hearing or telling the story, others confusing the twins, the twin's memory used "as a default", and Hyman's source-monitoring step all fit this description. B takes testimony-derived or imagined content for memory. That is a mistake about *which faculty* delivered the content, and it happens before any question of whose past memory reports arises. Perrin & McCarroll classify exactly this case: S "would then misattribute to himself the property of having attended the game, but he would not misidentify himself" (p. 301, via García-Carpintero).
- **The laboratory analogues point to generic source confusion.**
  - Gabbert et al.'s 71% and Jalbert et al.'s partner-adoption are testimonial imports.
  - Neszmélyi & Pfister find self→other and other→self errors at equal rates, which fits a similarity-driven source confusion rather than a failure specific to self-ascription.
  - Küntay et al.'s finding that disputed events are mundane, and that independent raters had "also experienced" many of them, fits schema-built reconstruction. There, B's content may come from B's own similar past. In that case condition (b) fails outright: the grounds do not derive from A's being sent home.
- **García-Carpintero concedes the de facto point.** Even actual instances "wouldn't establish that memory is not even de facto IEM" (n. 22).

### 3.4 Reading (iii): undetermined

The strongest case:
- **The decisive facts were never collected.** The study did not determine which twin was right or how each false memory formed (p. 779), and the authors think most disputes cannot be resolved (2006, pp. 10–11).
- **The classification turns on a theoretical choice the data cannot make**: whether IEM is relative to genuine memory, a factive relation, or to mnemic seemings, a representation that can misrepresent. The philosophical literature is split on exactly this (§1.4).
- **Phenomenology cannot stand in for the missing facts.** The authors report that "the measures of memory quality provide no indication" of which memory is false, so no first-person criterion can sort the cases by route.

### 3.5 Calibrated verdict

Classification by route and by how memory grounds are individuated:

| Route of the false memory | IEM relative to genuine memory | IEM relative to mnemic seemings |
|---|---|---|
| B witnessed A's event and later reconstructed herself as protagonist | Outside IEM: a misremembering, and Shoemaker's de facto claim is untouched | **Candidate *wh*-misidentification**: conditions (a)–(c) plausibly met |
| B heard the story (from A, family, or someone confusing the twins) and absorbed it as memory | Outside | Contested: misattribution (Perrin & McCarroll) vs misidentification (García-Carpintero) |
| B built the event from schema or her own similar experiences | Outside | Outside: condition (b) fails, since the grounds do not derive from A's being F |
| Mutual disowning: B gives her own wrongdoing to A (14 of 77) | Outside on every reading: other-misidentification, as in the thought-insertion reply on IEM L58 | Outside |

**Verdict: (iii).** The data establish an actual-world class of false self-ascriptions grounded in states that present as episodic memory. They do not establish that any member of the class is an IEM failure, because the route is unknown case by case and the right individuation of memory grounds is disputed.

Two conditional claims are firm:
- **Shoemaker's de facto claim, as formulated, survives.** It concerns accurate memory, and the mistaken twin's memory is inaccurate.
- **On the mnemic-seeming reading, the co-witnessed subset would be actual Dee-type cases in Pryor's sense.** Pryor's de facto immunity is expressly conditional on such cases "not in fact" occurring. On that reading, memory's immunity is at best a normal-conditions regularity, which is García-Carpintero's n. 22 fallback.

The genuine-memory reading buys its safety at a price James names: immunity secured by an accuracy condition the subject cannot check from inside is "technically" real but "moot" for her. The disputed-memory data make that price concrete, since nothing in the experience separates B's memory from A's.

**Grades** (evidential-status vocabulary):
- That disputed memories exist among twins, recur, and are confined to the protagonist slot is **strongly supported**: two independent groups and several methods, though samples are small and self-selected and nothing is preregistered.
- That disputed memories are richer than baseline is **supported by one small study**, with an authors' caveat about direction of cause.
- That the dispute pattern is self-serving rests on **one re-analysis** of mostly the same records, which is provisional.
- That a disputed memory is an IEM failure is **not an empirical claim**. It is a *live hypothesis* on the mnemic-seeming reading and excluded by definition on the genuine-memory reading.

## 4. What Follows for "Presentation Is Not Certification" {#certification}

The quasi-memory page (L102) holds that felt ownership can "*present*" a memory as one's own but "cannot *certify*" that it is, on the ground that a quasi-memory with another source "could present in the same way". That is a conditional about possible cases. Disputed memories supply the actual-world instance for ownership:
- in at least one twin per pair, a memory presents an episode as hers, usually from the inside;
- with reliving and imagery ratings above baseline and belief no lower;
- with no phenomenal mark of the error ("the measures of memory quality provide no indication").

Together with R.B., whose accurate memories did not feel like his own on Klein and Nichols's reading (contested by Roache 2016; see quasi-memory L82), the cases give a **double dissociation** between felt ownership and actual provenance. *(Map reconstruction: neither source pairs them.)*

What does **not** follow:
1. **General unreliability.** The rate outside twins is low and unknown: 8% of close siblings in one questionnaire, and "uncertain" by the authors' own account. Disputes cluster on mundane events of intermediate importance. Felt ownership is shown fallible, not untrustworthy as a rule.
2. **Indeterminate ownership facts.** One twin *was* sent home. The case is epistemic: it concerns whether memory certifies ownership, not whether there is a fact of ownership.
3. **Any bearing on introspective IEM, Layer 1 or thought insertion.** All three are present-tense cases. Disputed memories bear only on the diachronic, memory-based case.
4. **"Richer than true memories" as a general law.** The comparison is to *undisputed* shared memories in one study, and the authors flag that the dispute may itself raise the ratings.
5. **An IEM failure.** See §3.5. The certification point holds on both readings, which is why it is the safe thing for the Map to cite.

**Fit with the queued `evidential-status-discipline` paragraph** (P3, "presentation is not certification"): consistent. That paragraph says each listed presentation "leaves the presented fact open" and gives quasi-memory's open fact as whose past. It deliberately phrases IEM separately: "the immunity secures the step from grounds to subject". This note supports that split. For memory, any immunity holds only relative to genuine memory, which the subject cannot identify from inside, so memory-based self-ascription belongs with the presentation-without-certification items. No change to the queued paragraph is needed.

## 5. Relation to the Map's Tenets {#tenets}

- **Dualism** (Tenet 1): **orthogonal.** Reconstructive memory and source monitoring explain disputed memories fully, together with social retelling and motivated attribution. A dualist reading adds no empirical purchase. Felt ownership is a phenomenological datum at its own tier. Its fallibility neither supports nor undermines irreducibility ("Phenomenological Datum ≠ Irreducible Metaphysical Datum"). The verdict is *compatible, no support*.
- **Minimal Quantum Interaction** (Tenet 2): no bearing.
- **Bidirectional Interaction** (Tenet 3): no bearing. The self-serving pattern should **not** be recruited as a signature of conscious selection in reconsolidation (cf. episodic-memory L174's speculative proposal). The common-cause null is motivated cognition, which the authors themselves invoke (self-serving attribution bias, Mezulis et al. 2004, p. 11). It explains the pattern without remainder. If used at all, it is *speculative integration* and is not recommended.
- **No Many Worlds** (Tenet 4): the indexical objection needs a determinate fact of whose past is whose. Disputed memories presuppose such a fact, since one twin was sent home, and show that memory cannot certify it. That repeats quasi-memory's point with an actual instance. It adds **no new concession** beyond [P-I2](/positions/individuation-and-subjecthood/#p-i2) ("stated unconditionally but argued conditionally").
- **Occam's Razor Has Limits** (Tenet 5): the simplest reading of memory, that it reports whose past it is, fails in actual cases. That cautions against simple first-person-certification arguments, which the Map already avoids. It gives no support to any posit.
- **The posited subject** ([P-I4](/positions/individuation-and-subjecthood/#p-i4), [first exposure](/topics/kants-paralogisms-and-the-maps-subject/)): unchanged. The Map posits rather than infers its subject, so an actual case of memory misassigning ownership removes no premise the Map uses.
