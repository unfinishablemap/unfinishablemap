## Executive Summary

"Constructivism" in the study of mysticism holds that a contemplative's tradition shapes the experience itself, and does not merely colour the later report. Steven Katz (1978) is its standard source. Robert Forman (1990, 1999) answered that one class of experience, the "pure consciousness event" (PCE), has no content for a tradition to shape. Robert Sharf (1995, 2000) made a different and more radical move: he doubts that contemplative vocabulary refers to determinate inner events at all, and he criticises Katz's constructivists for still assuming that it does. The Map's standing reply, structural convergence across traditions, answers strong Katzian constructivism reasonably well, is compatible with moderate constructivism, and does not engage Sharf. Forman's PCE is a costly ally: an event with no content is hard to remember, hard to distinguish from unconsciousness, and hard to use as evidence for anything.

## Key Finding: Two Challenges, Not One

**Katz's challenge concerns what experiences are like.** Experiences are real and differ by tradition because concepts enter into them. The prediction is divergence across traditions. Cross-tradition convergence is therefore relevant counter-evidence, which is why the Map's convergence reply has purchase.

**Sharf's challenge concerns what the words do.** His evidence is disagreement *inside* a single tradition among its most accomplished teachers about which state is which. If masters trained in the same lineage, using the same canonical vocabulary, cannot agree on whether a given attainment is *jhāna* or stream-entry, then the vocabulary is not functioning as a set of labels for recognisable inner states. Cross-tradition convergence of reports does nothing against this, because Sharf's claim is that report-level agreement and disagreement track public criteria (lineage, technique, conduct, certification) and not phenomenology.

Sharf distinguishes himself from Katz explicitly. Of "Steven Katz and other 'constructivists'" he writes that they "seem to assume that since the historical, social, and linguistic processes that give rise to the narrative representation are identical with those that give rise to the experience, the former, which are amenable to scholarly analysis, provide a transparent window to the latter" (Sharf 2000, p. 285; line-break hyphenation removed). On Sharf's view the Katz–Forman argument is conducted on a shared assumption he rejects.

## Key Sources

### SEP, "Mysticism" (Jones and Gellman; substantive revision 4 September 2026)
- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mysticism/
- **Type**: Encyclopedia. Read in full at source (raw HTML fetched and grepped). Author metadata now lists Richard Jones first, with Jerome Gellman; the revision is a month old.
- **Key points**:
  - Distinguishes **moderate** from **strong** constructivism. Moderate: no mystical experience lacks cultural structuring, yet "the experiences have substance of their own to contribute to mystical knowledge claims." Strong: culture "completely determines the alleged cognitive content."
  - Katz is quoted for the strong form: the socio-religious milieu "over-determines" mystical experience (Katz 1978, p. 46, as cited by SEP). Gimello's gloss: mystical experiences are "simply the psychosomatic enhancement of religious beliefs and values" (Gimello 1983, p. 85, as cited by SEP).
  - States the structural stalemate: "we only have mystics’ post-experiential accounts, which all parties agree are shaped by a mystic’s culture — we can never get to the experiences themselves to see if they are constructed or not."
  - §7.1 lists constructivist arguments against PCEs; §7.2 and §7.4 list replies (below).
  - **Sharf is not cited anywhere in the entry** (0 hits for "Sharf" in the page text). The philosophy-of-religion literature and the Buddhist-studies literature run largely in parallel.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The entry is a map of the dispute.

### Katz, "Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism" (1978)
- **Type**: Book chapter, in Katz (ed.), *Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis* (Oxford University Press / Sheldon Press), pp. 22–74.
- **Access**: **Primary text not read.** The archive.org scan is lending-only with no searchable text layer. Everything below is via SEP or Sharf.
- **Key points** (secondary):
  - The famous sentence, "There are NO pure (i.e. unmediated) experiences", is reported at p. 26 by several secondary sources. **Unverified at source**, including its capitalisation. `non-temporal-consciousness` L93 prints it with italic "*no*" and "i.e.,".
  - Verbatim as quoted by Sharf (2000, p. 271): "neither mystical experience nor more ordinary forms of experience give any indication, or any grounds for believing, that they are unmediated" (Katz 1978, p. 26).
  - SEP reconstructs the argument: (A) the conceptual scheme completely determines the experience; (B) traditions have pervasively different schemes; so (C) no common experience across traditions.
  - SEP also attributes to Katz the claim that PCEs are impossible because of the "kind of beings" we are (Katz 1978, p. 59).
- **Tenet alignment**: Conflicts with the Map's use of contemplative reports as evidence about consciousness-structure, in its strong form. The moderate form is close to what the Map already says.

### Sharf, "The Rhetoric of Experience and the Study of Religion" (2000)
- **URL**: archived author copy, web.archive.org/web/20100609213335/http://buddhiststudies.berkeley.edu/people/faculty/sharf/documents/Sharf1998,%20Religious%20Experience.pdf
- **Type**: Paper. *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 7(11–12), 267–287. First published as "Experience" in Taylor (ed.), *Critical Terms for Religious Studies* (Chicago, 1998), pp. 94–116 (so stated in the paper's own footnote). **Read in full; quotes grep-verified against extracted PDF text**, with line-break hyphens removed.
- **Key points**:
  - Historical: path treatises (Buddhaghosa, Kamalaśīla, Zhiyi) are routinely read as experience reports, yet their authors' authority lay in scriptural mastery. Sharf does not deny that practitioners had experiences: "This is not to deny that religious practitioners had ‘experiences’ in the course of their training, just that such experiences were not considered the goal of practice, were not deemed doctrinally authoritative, and did not serve as the reference points for their understanding of the path" (p. 272).
  - Ethnographic: among contemporary Theravāda teachers the labels are applied polemically; *samatha* names what rivals teach. "there is simply no public consensus in the contemporary Theravada community as to the application of terms that allegedly refer to discrete experiential states" (p. 279). The same holds for *kenshō* in Sanbōkyōdan Zen, where verification disputes nearly caused schism.
  - Conclusion from that evidence: a meditative state "is identified not on the basis of privileged personal access to its distinctive phenomenology, but rather on the basis of eminently public criteria" (pp. 279–280).
  - The alien-abduction comparison: "is there any reason to assume that the reports of experiences by mystics, shamans, or meditation masters are any more credible as ‘phenomenological descriptions’ than those of the abductees?" (p. 282).
  - The thesis in one sentence: "The more fundamental question is whether we can continue to treat the texts and reports upon which such theories are based as referring, however obliquely, to determinative phenomenal events at all" (p. 282).
  - Sharf leans on Dennett's "Quining Qualia" and Rorty's attack on the "glassy essence". His scepticism is continuous with eliminativism about qualia, which matters for how the Map should answer him.
- **Tenet alignment**: Conflicts with Dualism at the level of method. Sharf's argument, if sound, removes contemplative reports from the evidence base and rests partly on a philosophy of mind the Map rejects.

### Sharf, "Buddhist Modernism and the Rhetoric of Meditative Experience" (1995)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1163/1568527952598549 (metadata confirmed at Crossref: *Numen* 42(3), 228–283)
- **Type**: Paper. **Only pp. 269–270 read**, from page images of the archived author scan (no text layer, so these two quotes are transcribed by eye and are not machine-greppable).
- **Key points**:
  - p. 269: "Buddhist meditation might best be seen as the ritualization of experience: it doesn't engender a specific experiential state so much as it *enacts* it. In this sense Buddhist *mārga* treatises are not so much maps of inner psychic space as they are scripts for the performance of an eminently public religious drama."
  - p. 270: "Whatever ineffable experiences might transpire in the minds of Buddhist meditators, such events do not, and indeed cannot, impinge upon the ideologically charged public discourse concerning experience and enlightenment."
  - The abstract (via the Michigan repository record) locates the privileging of experience in twentieth-century Asian reform movements shaped by Western influence. The detailed case (Burmese *vipassanā* revival, D. T. Suzuki, Nishida) was **not read** and is a lead.
- **Corpus check**: `non-temporal-consciousness` L93 quotes both halves of the p. 269 passage accurately but says Sharf "reads Buddhist meditation reports as" these things. In Sharf the first half is predicated of *meditation* and the second of *mārga treatises*. A subject splice, small, worth fixing when the concept page lands.

### Forman (ed.), *The Problem of Pure Consciousness* (1990); Forman, *Mysticism, Mind, Consciousness* (1999)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780195059809.001.0001 (OUP, 1990; Forman's introduction, "Mysticism, Constructivism, and Forgetting", pp. 3–50, confirmed at Crossref). 1999 monograph: SUNY Press.
- **Access**: **Neither book read.** Sources: the OUP chapter abstract, SEP, and Martin Adam's review of the 1999 book in *Arc* 30 (2002), 255–259, read in full.
- **Key points** (secondary):
  - OUP abstract of the introduction defines the target: constructivism is "the view that mystical experience is significantly shaped and formed by the subject’s beliefs, concepts, and expectations."
  - Adam's summary of the PCE: "a conscious occurrence said to be objectless and therefore non-constructed." Subjects recall nothing except, perhaps, that they were present throughout.
  - Forman's arguments as Adam reports them: (i) Katz models mysticism on sensory experience, which is inappropriate if the experience has no object; (ii) "Constructivists provide no plausible account of how the constructive process is supposed to occur"; (iii) *post hoc ergo propter hoc*: that matching beliefs precede an experience does not show they cause it; (iv) some experiences are unlike anything the subject previously conceived, and belief follows.
  - The "forgetting" model (via SEP and search summaries, **unverified at source**): mystical practice works by dropping conceptual activity, so the result is reached by subtraction, with nothing left to construct.
- **Tenet alignment**: Superficially aligns with Dualism (contentless awareness as consciousness without function). See costs below.

### Gamma and Metzinger, "The Minimal Phenomenal Experience questionnaire (MPE-92M)" (2021)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0253694
- **Type**: Paper, *PLOS ONE* 16(7), e0253694. Abstract and limitations section read from the PLOS XML.
- **Key points**: 1,403 usable responses from meditators; a 12-factor structure for "pure awareness" explaining 44% of variance. The authors flag the constructivist problem in their own data: "The terminology employed by such theories, as well their epistemological and metaphysical background assumptions, may “contaminate” survey responses (and phenomenological reports)." They add that "theory contamination" may also have produced selection bias.
- **Relevance**: the largest empirical study of the phenomenon Forman named concedes it cannot separate report from framework. The sample was 43.9% Vipassanā and 34.9% Zen practitioners, the two modern movements Sharf singles out.

## Major Positions

### Strong constructivism
- **Proponents**: Katz (as usually read), Gimello, Penner.
- **Core claim**: tradition fully determines experiential content; no cross-tradition common core.
- **Standard objections** (SEP §7.4): surprise in untrained subjects (Gellman 1997; Barnard 1997); mystics who become heretics (Stoeber 1992); innovation within traditions; contemplative training aims at loosening conceptual habit (Deikman's "deautomatization"); "thin" versus "thick" description (King 1988). None of these was read at source.
- **Relation to site tenets**: the Map can and does resist this form. Its convergence reply is one of several available.

### Moderate constructivism
- **Core claim**: all contemplative experience is structured by tradition, and it also contributes something of its own.
- **Relation to site tenets**: **this is already the Map's position** under another name. `comparative-phenomenology-of-meditative-traditions` says reports are "shaped by tradition, language, and expectation" yet "not *only* shaped by these factors." A concept page should say plainly that the Map is a moderate constructivist and that its quarrel is with the strong form and with Sharf.

### Decontextualism (Forman)
- **Core claim**: at least one experience-type, the PCE, is unconstructed because contentless.
- **Costs**, all from SEP §4.2 and §7.1 unless noted:
  - *Evidential emptiness* (Bagger 1999): if nothing is present in the event, "Nothing could be retained from the experience to shape a mystic’s beliefs or values. Nor could such occurrence be evidence for any claim."
  - *The unconsciousness problem*: inferring a PCE from a remembered before and after "would fail to distinguish a PCE from plain unconsciousness."
  - *Idealisation* (Jones 2025): "Mystics thereby remember an experience with content as pure consciousness only because of a “confirmation bias” of what was expected." This is constructivism re-entering at the memory stage.
  - *Emptying for other content*: Eckhart's "unknowing" and Kabbalistic *ayin* may describe removal of ordinary content to admit extraordinary content.
  - *Cessation* (Adam 2002, citing Griffiths 1990/1993): Forman's Buddhist exemplar, the attainment of cessation, is standardly understood in Buddhist sources as the cessation of consciousness itself. Adam also asks in what sense a PCE is "consciousness" if nothing about it can be recalled.
- **Relation to site tenets**: the PCE looks like a gift to Dualism and is not a safe one. An event that carries no content and leaves no trace beyond "I was there" supports a claim about consciousness only through the later, concept-laden judgement that it was awareness and not a gap.

### Referential scepticism (Sharf)
- **Core claim**: contemplative terms do not function ostensively; their use is governed by public, institutional criteria.
- **Relation to site tenets**: see "What this costs the Map".

## Key Debates

### Katz versus Forman
- **Core disagreement**: whether all experience is concept-mediated, or one limiting case escapes.
- **Current state**: unresolved and widely regarded as stalled, for the reason SEP gives: only post-experiential reports exist. Sharf (2000, p. 276) already called it an argument "with no resolution in sight."

### Sharf and his critics
- **Leads, unverified**: Janet Gyatso, "Healing Burns with Fire: The Facilitations of Experience in Tibetan Buddhism", *JAAR* 67(1), 113–147 (1999) is often cited as the principal reply, arguing that Tibetan sources do thematise experience (*nyams*). Metadata confirmed at Crossref; **content not read**, and its section titles (including "Constructing the Natural") suggest a more qualified reply than "Sharf is wrong". A 2023 Bristol thesis on the visionary nature of meditative experience also engages Sharf; not read. Evan Thompson, *Why I Am Not a Buddhist* (Yale, 2020), already cited on the Map, is a third.

## What This Costs the Map

Stated without softening:

1. **Convergence is the wrong tool against Sharf.** `contemplative-practice-as-philosophical-evidence` L137 argues that theory-laden artefacts should diverge, so convergence indicates discovery. That reasoning addresses Katz. Sharf's evidence is intra-lineage disagreement about term application, and the Map's own `comparative-phenomenology` page documents a cousin of it (the unresolved witness-status divergence). The Map currently has no reply to the claim that state-labels track public criteria.
2. **The traditions the Map leans on hardest are the modern ones.** Much of the empirical contemplative literature the Map cites samples Western practitioners of post-reform Vipassanā and Zen. If Sharf's history is right, the "phenomenological" framing of these practices is roughly a century old, so their agreement with each other and with Western phenomenology is partly shared ancestry. This bears on the independence condition in `epistemology-of-convergence-arguments`.
3. **The telescope analogy is contested at exactly this point.** `contemplative-epistemology` L91 compares meditation to an instrument that "does not create Jupiter's moons." Instruments are calibrated against independent access to their targets. Sharf's claim is that contemplative calibration is done by teachers applying public criteria, which is what a constructed practice would also look like. The page's existing hedge ("remains open") is accurate; the concept page should say why it cannot currently be closed.
4. **Forman is not a free ally.** Any Map page citing PCEs as evidence for consciousness without content inherits Bagger's dilemma and the cessation problem.

What the Map retains:

- **Sharf's argument is partly underwritten by eliminativism about qualia.** He invokes Dennett to dissolve the question of what the reports refer to. A reader who rejects "Quining Qualia" is not obliged to follow him from "labels are applied by public criteria" to "there are no determinate phenomenal events to label." The first is an ethnographic finding; the second is a philosophical step the Map has independent grounds to refuse. Sharf himself grants that practitioners had experiences. What survives for the Map is a narrower claim: something determinate occurs, and current vocabulary does not reliably individuate it.
- **Disagreement about labels presupposes something to mislabel.** Teachers who accuse rivals of "mistaking jhanic absorption for sotapatti" (Sharf's own description, p. 279) are treating the two as distinct states. This is a point to argue with care; Sharf would answer that the accusation is itself rhetoric.
- **Moderate constructivism leaves structural claims standing**, provided the Map keeps the evidential weight where `non-temporal-consciousness` already puts it: one recurring pattern, suggestive, not decisive.
- **The measured response is methodological.** Microphenomenological interviewing, naive-subject designs, and pre-registration of expected reports are the ways to reduce contamination that Gamma and Metzinger say they could not exclude. Whether any such study exists with tradition-naive subjects was **not searched**; it is the most useful follow-up.
