## Executive Summary

Bernard Williams (1973) argued that a premature death can be a misfortune, because categorical desires give reasons to go on living, but that an endless life could not be made desirable: a fixed character runs out of categorical desires and ends in boredom, and a replaced character fails one of his two conditions for a future worth wanting (that it be clearly *me*, and that it stay tied to the aims I now have in wanting to survive). Thaddeus Metz's SEP entry calls this "first and foremost" among the arguments that having a soul would reduce life's meaning, so it meets the Map where the Map's dualism keeps survival open. The literature since 1994 is large and mostly replies that immortality need not be boring (Fischer; Bortolotti & Nagasawa; Thompson; Neptune on heaven), with a deflationary strand holding that the debate has no coherent footing (Burley) and a weakening strand holding that the argument shows a risk, not a necessity (Gorman; Hauskeller). Applied to the survival the Map leaves open, the argument changes shape in four ways explained below: it bites only on *endless* survival, which the Map never claims; the Map's [haecceity](/concepts/haecceity/) account satisfies Williams's first condition automatically but leaves the second standing (Kagan's "mantra" point); memory and the lack of an exit could make it bite harder for a non-physical survivor than for a bodily immortal; and the [boredom void](/voids/boredom-void/) limits what any felt or forecast tedium can testify. **Verdict: `fold`** into `topics/death-and-consciousness` (a 164-word subsection plus two references, 208 words total, taking the page from 3,769 to 3,977 against the 4,000 gate), with a short cross-link from `voids/boredom-void`. ### Luper, S., "Death", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (substantive revision 28 July 2026) [read]
- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/death/ (§7, "Never Dying")
- **Key points**:
  - Categorical desires are desires not conditional on remaining alive. Luper's example: a father's desire that his daughter have a good start "is not conditional on his remaining alive. In this sense, it is, Williams says, categorical." Such desires give reason to live.
  - Summary of Williams: "Williams thinks that our categorical desires are not only what motivate us to live on, they give meaning to our lives, and are important elements of our characters."
  - Williams's two conditions, quoted by Luper: (1) "it should clearly be me who lives forever," and (2) "the state in which I survive should be one that, to me looking forward, will be adequately related, in the life it presents, to those aims I now have in wanting to survive at all." Luper cites "(1973, p. 83)", and his bibliography says "Page references are to Fischer", that is, the 1993 reprint in *The Metaphysics of Death* (pp. 71–92 per Crossref). **The page is the reprint's, not *Problems of the Self*'s.** Do not attach p. 83 to a *Problems of the Self* citation.
  - On replaced character, Williams says "there is nothing left by which he can judge" whether the future life is desirable. Luper prints this as "(1093, p. 85)", a typo for 1973.
  - Luper's scoping: "Williams's view is that it is not possible to make eternal life desirable (which claim is not identical to the claim that eternal life would inevitably be bad for us)."
  - On identity, Williams "defends a bodily continuity criterion for identity in 'The Self and the Future,' pp. 46–63, so he presumably thinks that a person does survive changes of desires and character." This matters for the Map (see the Map-specific section, below).
  - Luper's own reply: "If we could live endlessly, the stages of our lives would display reduced connectedness, yet remain continuous", and gradual transformation is "distinct from, and preferable to, annihilation."
  - Nagel's opposing view, quoted by Luper from "Death" (1970, p. 60): "life is worth living even when the bad elements of experience are plentiful and the good ones too meager to outweigh the bad ones on their own. The additional positive weight is supplied by experience itself, rather than by any of its contents." [via SEP]
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. It concerns desirability, not the metaphysics of mind.

### Metz, T., "The Meaning of Life", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (substantive revision 26 June 2026) [read]
- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/life-meaning/
- **Key points**:
  - Places Williams in the "anti-theist" or anti-soul literature: "Turn, now, to major arguments for thinking that having a soul would reduce life's meaning, so that if one wants a maximally meaningful life, one should prefer a purely physical world, or at least one in which people are mortal. First and foremost, there has been the argument that an immortal life could not avoid becoming boring".
  - "The literature on this topic has become enormous, with the central reply being that immortality need not get boring".
  - Two further anti-immortality arguments: repetition (Scarre, May, Smuts) and narrative shapelessness (May 2009). Weaker ones concern lost preciousness and urgency (Nussbaum 1989; Kass 2002; Hägglund 2019).

- **Tenet alignment**: Bears on the Map's dualism only as a claim about what survival would be worth, never about whether it is possible.

- **URL**: https://www3.nd.edu/~jspeaks/courses/2006-7/20208/makropulos.html

### Kagan, S. (2007), PHIL 176 *Death*, Lectures 18 and 19, Open Yale Courses (recorded Spring 2007; CC BY-NC-SA transcript) [read]

- **Key points**:
  - Applies Williams to heaven explicitly: "It's a striking fact that even those religions that promise us an eternity in heaven are rather shy on the details." His examples are psalm-singing forever and a colleague's beatific "conversation" with an infinitely rich friend; he finds neither imaginable as desirable forever.
  - Takes Williams's side: "I'm inclined to agree with Williams." "forever is literally forever."
  - **The point that matters most for the Map** (Lecture 19, ch. 1, the Methuselah case): "It's of no interest to me to survive, and merely saying the mantra, "Oh, but it's me" doesn't make it more desirable to me. What I want isn't merely for somebody to be me. I want them to be sufficiently like me." The test is "a question to you, here, now."
  - He states the dilemma explicitly: keep me similar and boredom sets in; avoid boredom by progressive memory loss and personality change and it is "not anything that I especially want for myself."
  - Kagan's book *Death* (Yale UP, 2012) develops the course. **LEAD**: not read.

### Burley, M. (2015), "'The End of Immortality!' Eternal Life and the Makropulos Debate", *Journal of Ethics* 19: 305–321, doi:10.1007/s10892-015-9205-6 (CC BY 4.0) [read, White Rose repository PDF]
- **URL**: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/92191/
- **Key points**:
  - The debate is "a battle of speculative fantasies", "Lacking a firm conceptual grounding in the religious contexts from which terms such as "immortality" and "eternal life" gain much of their sense".
  - Four problems: the dilemma of fantastical thought experiments; the inconceivability of completed infinities ("it makes no sense to speak of an endless life "in its entirety.""); the lack of distinctions between kinds of immortality; and the "mortal values objection" (Nussbaum).
  - Kinds of immortality. Steele's (1976) contingent body-bound immortal "would always have the option of ending his own potentially eternal existence if he decided he had had enough" [via Burley]. Cave (2013) separates medical from true immortals, and Fischer & Mitchell-Yellin (2014) add "robust immortality" [via Burley].
  - His list of the replies' resources: "our ability to enjoy "repeatable pleasures" such as those of consuming good food and drink, and making love (Fischer 1994)"; desire revival (Wisnewski 2005, whom Burley spells "Wisniewski"); "the impermanence of human memory, enabling us to forget earlier experiences and hence savor undergoing them time after time"; and inventing new activities (Bruckner 2012).
  - Religious alternative: Johannine "eternal life as a present possession". Jantzen (1984) [via Burley]: "it is a glaring confusion to equate eternal life with endless survival."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. Its conceivability point parallels the [death void](/voids/death-void/) in reverse (see Key Debates).

## Major Positions

### Williams: endless life cannot be made desirable
- **Proponents**: Williams 1973; Kagan 2007/2012 (who agrees); Smuts 2011 (conclusion, not argument); Hauskeller 2021 and Gorman 2016 (weakened versions).
- **Core claim**: Categorical desires make a premature death bad, but no endless life meets both conditions. Fixed character leads to boredom; replaced character means the future is not adequately related to my present aims, or is not clearly mine.
- **Relation to site tenets**: It concerns no tenet directly. It challenges the *value* of what Dualism permits, which Metz frames as a reason to prefer a world without souls. See Relation to Site Perspective, below.

### Optimists: immortality need not be boring
- **Proponents**: Fischer 1994 (repeatable pleasures, LEAD for detail); Wisnewski 2005; Bortolotti & Nagasawa 2009 (habitual versus situational boredom); Bruckner 2012; Chappell 2009 (LEAD); Thompson 2019; Neptune 2019 (heaven specifically); Chastain 2021 (spiritual experience; "desirable even if not actively desired"); Luper (SEP; continuity without connectedness); Nagel (experience itself as positive weight).
- **Core claim**: The necessity of boredom is unproven. Boredom is contingent on psychology, some goods do not exhaust themselves, and character can change gradually while staying continuous.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral. The Nagel strand, which places value in experience itself rather than its contents, sits structurally close to the Map's meaning-of-life claim that meaning lies "in the intrinsic character of the conscious engagement, not the agent's stance toward it." That is an angle to consider, not an established alliance.

### Deflationists: the debate lacks a coherent footing
- **Proponents**: Burley 2009, 2015.
- **Core claim**: Endless life cannot be conceived as a whole, kinds of immortality go undistinguished, and mortal values are constitutive. Fruitful talk of eternal life lives in religious contexts. One example is Johannine eternal life as a present quality rather than an endless quantity.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Compatible with Occam's Razor Has Limits only loosely. Burley's point concerns what we can evaluate, not what is real.

## Key Debates

### Is boredom necessary or contingent?
- **Sides**: Williams and Kagan (necessary for anything recognisably me) against Bortolotti & Nagasawa, Thompson and Fischer (contingent).
- **Core disagreement**: Whether a fixed human character must exhaust its categorical desires over infinite time.
- **Current state**: Ongoing. Gorman and Hauskeller move to a risk thesis: no one has good reason to bet on being immune, and running out would mean having died too late.

### Does character change preserve what matters?
- **Sides**: Williams and Kagan (identity without similarity is worthless, so the "mantra" point) against Luper and Thompson (gradual change preserving continuity is preferable to annihilation, and core projects can be retained).
- **Core disagreement**: Whether what makes survival worth wanting is numerical identity, psychological similarity, or continuity.
- **Current state**: Open. This is where the Map's account of personal identity enters (below).

### Is the question even well-posed?
- **Sides**: Burley (no) against Fischer (finite stages can be evaluated, as Burley reports him); Rosati 2013 is cited by Burley as holding "We cannot clearly imagine immortality".
- **Map note**: Burley's "inconceivability of completed infinities" is a structural mirror of the death void. That void says one cannot occupy one's own nonexistence from inside. Burley says one cannot survey one's own endless existence as a whole. Both are limits on evaluating one's life *from within* its own perspective. The parallel is the Map's own observation; Burley does not make it.

## The Argument Applied to the Survival Dualism Leaves Open {#map-application}

The literature mostly addresses *body-bound* immortality (Burley 2009's abstract defines its subject as "endless bodily life as a human or humanoid being"; Steele's and Gorman's titles say so). Extension to non-bodily survival appears chiefly in the religious strand: Kagan on heaven, Neptune, Hallett (LEAD), Chastain, and Burley 2015. What follows is **the Map's own analysis**. None of the cited authors applies the argument to a dualist survivor in these terms.

1. **Survival is not yet immortality: the argument bites less.** The Map's death page claims only that survival is "a *coherent possibility*" (`topics/death-and-consciousness`, "What the Map Does Not Claim"). Survival of bodily death neither entails nor requires endless continuation. Williams's argument targets only the further claim of *endless* survival, which the Map does not make. The Map's quantum-immortality page draws the same line for a different route: "finite survival is not yet immortality." Since the Map rejects Many-Worlds, it is also not pushed toward forced endless survival by the quantum-immortality corollary.

2. **The identity horn dissolves and the what-matters horn stays: the argument bites differently.** On the Map's account (`topics/personal-identity`, "What Personal Identity Requires"), identity is "the continuation of *this* conscious perspective", and memories and traits are "evidence of something deeper", "not what your identity consists in." So Williams's first condition ("it should clearly be me who lives forever") is met automatically under any change of character. That does not answer him. The second condition and Kagan's version of it ("merely saying the mantra, "Oh, but it's me" doesn't make it more desirable to me") concern what makes survival *worth wanting*, and haecceity is silent there. The Map's argument against Parfit is that identity is *necessary* for what matters, since a replica's continuity does not suffice. Williams and Kagan claim identity is not *sufficient*. The two claims are compatible, so the Map can accept both. The dilemma then survives on the Map's account as a dilemma about prudential concern alone. **Guard**: do not write that haecceity answers Williams. Note also that Williams himself held a bodily-continuity criterion (SEP), so the identity reading of his first horn is partly the secondary literature's (Thompson's "loss of one's distinctive character"). What the Map's account settles is only a *numerical*-identity reading of it. The Map has not said whether bare continuation of this subject, with no psychological similarity, is worth wanting. This research exposes that gap; it does not fill it.

3. **Memory and exit: the argument may bite harder for a non-physical survivor (speculative).**
   - *Memory.* EM's tedium rests on accumulated experience (IEP: everything that "could happen and make sense to one particular human being had already happened to her"). Replies lean on forgetting ("the impermanence of human memory", in Burley's list; Kagan's progressive amnesia). The death page's [filter-theory](/concepts/filter-theory/) reading of terminal lucidity holds that "the memories were never stored in decaying neurons but accessed through them". If that is right, a disembodied survivor might keep its whole past and lose the forgetting a bodily immortal could rely on. This is conditional on filter theory and is the Map's inference only. It is also partly self-limiting: Kagan argues the amnesia route fails the second condition anyway.
   - *Exit.* EM ends her life by refusing the elixir, and Steele's contingent immortal "would always have the option of ending" it. A subject that survives bodily death has, by hypothesis, lost the bodily exits. Whether such a subject could cease is a question the Map does not appear to address (a grep for "indestructib" over topics, concepts, apex, positions and voids returns 0; other phrasings were not searched). If it could not, Hauskeller's "died too late" would have no remedy. Raise this as an open question, not a claim.

4. **The boredom void: the argument's evidence is weaker than it looks.**
   - *Face 2 (projection).* Williams treats EM's boredom as fitting (IEP). The [boredom void](/voids/boredom-void/) holds that the felt verdict "does not carry on its face what would distinguish the fitting case from the projected one." So EM's weariness cannot certify from inside that life's possibilities were exhausted rather than her engagement failing. Her case is fiction in any event.
   - *Face 3 (forecasting).* Williams's and Kagan's verdict is a forecast made from a finite vantage, "a question to you, here, now." The boredom void reports Hatano et al. (2022): people underpredict how enjoyable waiting is, and the low forecast keeps itself in place. Together with Bortolotti & Nagasawa's "psychologically realistic" boredom and Burley's inconceivability point, a forecast of *eternal* tedium is the least checkable forecast there is. That is the Map's extrapolation; Hatano et al. studied waiting tasks.
   - *Self-supply.* A disembodied survival that is mind alone with itself meets the condition of Wilson et al.'s thinking studies on an endless scale. The boredom void confines that limit to "the untrained default", and the death page's contemplative material (witness consciousness, cessation states) is the obvious counter-resource. Do not overstate either side.

5. **Value theory (an angle, not a finding).** Nagel puts positive weight in "experience itself, rather than ... any of its contents" (via SEP). Williams grounds the worth of going on in categorical desires. The Map's meaning-of-life page grounds meaning in "the intrinsic character of the conscious engagement, not the agent's stance toward it", which is structurally closer to Nagel's view. That gives the Map a principled reason to doubt that tedium would drain all value. But the same page demands "contact with anything that matters", and meaning-deficit boredom is the loss of exactly that, so the angle cuts both ways.

## Relation to Site Perspective

- **Dualism**: The argument is about desirability, not truth. It neither supports nor threatens Dualism. At most it tells against *hoping* for endless survival, which Metz frames as a reason to prefer a world without souls. The Map should keep both directions of wishful thinking out: survival's undesirability is no evidence against it, and its desirability is no evidence for it.
- **Bidirectional Interaction, Minimal Quantum Interaction**: No bearing.
- **No Many Worlds**: Indirect only. The quantum-immortality page's torment reductio (Lewis) concerns degraded endless branches; Williams supplies the best-case objection, that even a healthy endless life would bore. The Map's rejection of Many-Worlds means it is never committed to endless survival by its physics.
- **Occam's Razor Has Limits**: No direct bearing.
