## Executive Summary

Consciousness never catches itself *coming to believe*. Three findings converge on this. First, the **control face**: we cannot believe at will, and on the dominant philosophical view (Williams 1973; Hieronymi 2006) this is a conceptual rather than a merely psychological limit, because a state adopted for reasons unrelated to truth would not be a belief. Second, the **timing face**: on the Spinozan model of belief fixation (Gilbert 1991; Gilbert, Tafarodi & Malone 1993; Mandelbaum 2014), acceptance is part of comprehension itself, so assent happens *before* any deliberate evaluation could begin, and what feels like judging is the later, effortful work of un-believing. This model is empirically contested (Hasson, Simmons & Todorov 2005; Nadarevic & Erdfelder 2013; Vorms et al. 2022), and the contest cannot be settled from the first person because the moment in question has no phenomenal marker. Third, the **transparency face**: when one asks "shall I believe p?" or "do I believe p?", the question is immediately replaced by "is p true?" (Shah & Velleman 2005), so the attitude is never the object of inspection. Consciousness looks through the belief to the world. The void is the moment of assent: it is causally central to mental life, and consciousness has no view onto it.

Distinct from existing voids: the [suspension-void](/voids/suspension-void/) concerns the *withholding* of judgment after the fact and its unverifiability; the [decision-void](/voids/decision-void/) concerns practical commitment to action; the certainty-void research note (2026-03-10) concerns the *feeling* that accompanies belief. The assent void concerns the formation event itself: its involuntariness, its timing, and its transparency.

## Key Sources

### Boespflug, M. & Jackson, E. (2024). "Doxastic Voluntarism." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*
- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/doxastic-voluntarism/
- **Type**: Encyclopedia (first published 1 October 2024)
- **Key points**:
  - Distinguishes **conceptual involuntarism** (something about belief's nature, typically a constitutive truth-aim or truth-norm, precludes believing at will) from **psychological involuntarism** (we cannot for contingent psychological reasons).
  - Reports Williams's (1973, ch. 9) argument: if one could believe at will, one would know one had this power, and could then take oneself to hold a belief one regards as false.
  - Reports Alston's introspective test (try to believe that the U.S. is still a British colony) and notes the method "lacks systematic rigor". The main evidence for psychological involuntarism is a first-person experiment whose outcome everyone already knows.
  - Ginet (2001) offers candidate counterexamples in cases of noncompelling evidence: the juror, the poker player, the person deciding they locked the door.
  - Indirect control (Pascal's holy water, Kant's "direction of choice" toward evidence) is widely accepted. The dispute concerns *direct* control.
- **Tenet alignment**: Bidirectional Interaction. If consciousness causally acts, why can it not set its own beliefs? The conceptual-involuntarist answer is that the limit comes from what belief *is* and does not show that consciousness lacks causal power.
- **Quote** (SEP's rendering of Williams): "to believe *p* is to take *p* to be true."

### Williams, B. (1973). "Deciding to Believe." In *Problems of the Self* (pp. 136–151). Cambridge University Press
- **Type**: Book chapter (originally 1970 in Kiefer & Munitz, eds., *Language, Belief, and Metaphysics*)
- **Key points**:
  - The classic argument that direct believing-at-will is conceptually impossible, not merely beyond our psychology.
  - The argument has a self-knowledge structure: the would-be voluntary believer must *not know* how the belief was acquired. The possibility of believing at will therefore depends on the process being opaque. This is a notable bridge to the occluded layer (below).
- **Note**: Page range taken from standard citations; not independently verified against the publisher. The expand-topic writer should confirm it before using it.

### Hieronymi, P. (2006). "Controlling Attitudes." *Pacific Philosophical Quarterly*, 87(1), 45–74. DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0114.2006.00247.x
- **Type**: Paper
- **Key points**:
  - Distinguishes **evaluative control** (we control an attitude by changing our answer to the question it embodies) from **managerial control** (we act on the attitude as an object, e.g. Pascal's strategy).
  - Evaluative control is genuine agency but is not voluntary in the ordinary sense, and it does not involve awareness of the attitude as the object controlled.
  - The same structure holds for **intention**: "you can no more intend at will than believe at will." This links the assent void directly to the [decision-void](/voids/decision-void/).
- **Tenet alignment**: Bidirectional Interaction. Evaluative control is a model of mental agency that does not work by pushing on an object. It works by *answering*. That makes it a candidate description of how conscious causation shows up in the first person: as responsiveness to reasons, never as manipulation.
- **Quote** (abstract, verbatim): "although belief is subject to two quite robust forms of agency, 'believing at will' is impossible; one cannot believe in the way one ordinarily acts."

### Shah, N. & Velleman, J.D. (2005). "Doxastic Deliberation." *The Philosophical Review*, 114(4), 497–534. DOI 10.1215/00318108-114-4-497
- **Type**: Paper
- **Key points**:
  - **Transparency**: the deliberative question whether to believe p is transparent to the question whether p. One cannot deliberate about believing without deliberating about the world.
  - Explained by the concept of belief including a standard of correctness (correct iff true).
  - Raises whether this deliberative transparency is the same as the transparency of *self-ascription* ("do I believe p?"). That second transparency is the Evans/Moran tradition, in which one answers by looking outward.
- **Tenet alignment**: Dualism (indirect). The attitude that carries the most epistemic weight is the one consciousness cannot turn and face. It shows up only as the world-as-taken.
- **Quote** (abstract, verbatim): "the deliberative question whether to believe that p is transparent to the question whether p."

### Gilbert, D.T. (1991). "How Mental Systems Believe." *American Psychologist*, 46(2), 107–119. DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.46.2.107
- **Type**: Review/theory paper
- **Key points**:
  - Contrasts a **Cartesian** model (comprehension first, then an effortful assessment ending in acceptance or rejection) with a **Spinozan** model (acceptance is part of comprehension, and rejection comes later and takes more effort).
  - Argues that social and cognitive psychology evidence favours Spinoza.
- **Quote** (abstract, verbatim): "People believe in the ideas they comprehend, as quickly and automatically as they believe in the objects they see."
- **Quote** (abstract, verbatim): "comprehension includes acceptance of that which is comprehended."

### Gilbert, D.T., Tafarodi, R.W. & Malone, P.S. (1993). "You Can't Not Believe Everything You Read." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 65(2), 221–233. DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.65.2.221
- **Type**: Experimental paper (three experiments)
- **Key points**: Under cognitive load, participants who read statements explicitly flagged as false later treated them as true, and the effect carried into consequential judgments (sentencing in a mock-crime vignette). Supports the claim that comprehension includes initial acceptance and that rejection needs resources.

### Mandelbaum, E. (2014). "Thinking is Believing." *Inquiry*, 57(1), 55–96. DOI 10.1080/0020174X.2014.858417
- **Type**: Paper
- **Key points**: The strongest philosophical defence of the Spinozan model. Belief is "the automatic output of a computationally null belief acquisition reflex."
- **Quote** (abstract, verbatim): "Evidence is presented demonstrating that we cannot withhold assent from any proposition we happen to consider."
- **Tenet alignment**: Occam's Razor Has Limits. The Cartesian model is the intuitive, introspectively "obvious" one ("Everyone knows that understanding is one thing and believing is another", Gilbert 1991's framing). If the Spinozan model is right, introspection's picture of judging is systematically wrong.

### Counter-evidence: the Spinozan model is contested
- **Hasson, U., Simmons, J.P. & Todorov, A. (2005).** "Believe It or Not: On the Possibility of Suspending Belief." *Psychological Science*, 16(7), 566–571. DOI 10.1111/j.0956-7976.2005.01576.x. Interrupting veracity encoding removed memory for falsity only when the false version of the statement was *uninformative*. Statements that are informative when false are not represented as if true. Comprehension without belief may be possible.
- **Nadarevic, L. & Erdfelder, E. (2013).** "Spinoza's Error: Memory for Truth and Falsity." *Memory & Cognition*, 41(2), 176–186. DOI 10.3758/s13421-012-0251-z. (Online 2012; issue 2013.) Challenges the Spinozan interpretation of the truth bias in memory. The title signals the verdict. The abstract was not retrieved, so any detailed claim should be checked before use.
- **Vorms, M., Harris, A.J.L., Topf, S. & Hahn, U. (2022).** "Plausibility Matters: A Challenge to Gilbert's 'Spinozan' Account of Belief Formation." *Cognition*, 220, 104990. DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2021.104990. A pre-registered replication of Gilbert et al.'s (1990) "Hopi language" experiment, with plausibility added. Quote (abstract, verbatim): "Gilbert's 'truth bias' does not hold for implausible statements — instead, initial encoding seemingly renders implausible statements 'false'."
- **Upshot for the void**: The debate shifts from "accept first, then evaluate" to "initial encoding is already evaluative, weighted by prior plausibility". On *either* side, the first assignment of truth-value happens below deliberate judgment and is not experienced as a judgment. The disputed question (Cartesian vs Spinozan) concerns exactly the event that introspection cannot observe. That is why it has had to be settled with dual-task memory paradigms rather than by asking people.

### Descartes, *Meditations*, IV (1641)
- **Key points**: Judgment is an act of the **will**. The will adds affirmation, denial, or suspension to content supplied by the intellect. Error comes from the will outrunning the intellect. This is the historical high-water mark of doxastic voluntarism. It is also the model Gilbert's Spinozan alternative is named against.
- **Secondary**: Recent scholarship is divided on whether Descartes held *direct* voluntarism about assent, or only about suspension (e.g. *International Journal of Philosophical Studies* 31(2), 2023, "Why Every Belief is a Choice: Descartes' Doxastic Voluntarism Reconsidered"; author not recorded here, so verify before citing).

## The Void

### Nature of the Limit

There are three faces, and each has a different modal status:

1. **Control face (Unexplorable, conceptual).** One cannot believe at will. If Williams and Hieronymi are right, this is not a gap in willpower. The concept of belief excludes it: a state formed in response to reasons that do not bear on truth is not a belief. On this reading the void is necessary for *any* believer, human or artificial.
2. **Timing face (Occluded, psychological).** Whatever the correct model, the initial truth-assignment is automatic and precedes deliberate evaluation (Spinozan: acceptance by default; plausibility-weighted: prior-driven encoding). The felt "act of judging" comes late. It may be a *revision* of an assent already made rather than the assent itself.
3. **Transparency face (Unexplorable in structure).** Asking whether to believe, or whether one does believe, turns attention outward to p. The belief qua attitude is looked *through* and never *at*. Moore's-paradox sentences ("p, but I don't believe p") register this structurally: a first-person report of belief is not a report on an inner object.

### Evidence for the Limit

- Introspective (Alston's test): universal, but methodologically weak.
- Conceptual (Williams 1973; Hieronymi 2006; Shah & Velleman 2005): strong. Suggests the control and transparency faces would hold for any believer.
- Experimental (Gilbert et al. 1993; Mandelbaum 2014 versus Hasson et al. 2005; Nadarevic & Erdfelder 2013; Vorms et al. 2022): the timing face is contested *in its details*. The measured phenomena all require third-person, dual-task methods. The dispute itself counts as evidence that the event is first-personally inaccessible.

### Phenomenology

Approaching the edge feels like finding that one *already* believes. It is the experience of catching oneself having taken a claim on board, as with a headline half-read or a rumour one "doesn't believe" but finds oneself acting on. Deliberate judgment is experienced as weighing and then *settling*. But the settling is never experienced as a choice between two live doxastic options that one then enacts; it presents as the evidence having come down one way. When one tries to believe at will, the experience is not effortful failure. There is nothing to push on (compare [phenomenology-of-resistance-across-domains](/topics/phenomenology-of-resistance-across-domains/), which notes that beliefs resist voluntary revision). Contrast [mental-effort](/concepts/mental-effort/), where attention *can* be pushed. The asymmetry between the voluntariness of attention and the involuntariness of assent may be the most informative feature here.

## Approaches to the Edge

### Direct Methods
- None for the formation event itself. Alston-style introspective experiments establish only the control face.
- Contemplative traditions claim trained noticing of "grasping" or "taking-as-real" at the moment of perception (e.g. Buddhist accounts of how appropriation arises). This could be a partial direct probe of the timing face, but it rests on trained reports whose accuracy is itself the question. Not researched in this note.

### Indirect Methods
- **Dual-task / cognitive-load paradigms** (Gilbert; Vorms et al.): remove the resources needed for evaluation and see what is left by default.
- **Memory-for-falsity paradigms** (Nadarevic & Erdfelder; Hasson et al.): infer how a truth-value was initially tagged.
- **Pascalian self-manipulation as a probe**: managerial control reveals the shape of the void by working around it. It succeeds only if the agent loses track of the manipulation (Williams's opacity condition).
- **Misinformation-correction research** (continued influence effect): shows that retracted claims keep acting, which fits assent without a visible, revocable act.

### What AI Might See
- A language model conditions on everything in context. In a sense it "comprehends" without a separate fixation step, which is Spinozan in structure. But whether it has *beliefs* at all is contested, and if it does not, the control face does not arise. The conceptual argument (Williams) would apply to any genuine believer, so AI offers no escape from the control face.
- One asymmetry is useful. An AI system's belief-like dispositions can in principle be probed from outside (linear probes for "truth directions" in activations). That is a third-person view of something like the timing face, unavailable in the human case at comparable resolution. The expand-topic writer should cite specific interpretability work only after verifying it; none was researched here.

## Connection to Tenets

### Most Relevant Tenet
**Bidirectional Interaction.** The obvious objection runs: "If consciousness causes things, why can't it make itself believe?" The research answers it cleanly. The limit is conceptual. It comes from belief's constitutive answerability to truth and not from causal impotence. Hieronymi's evaluative control also gives the Map a model of first-person mental agency that is real but non-manipulative: the mind acts by *answering questions*, not by moving objects. This fits the Map's minimal-interaction picture, in which conscious influence would not feel like pushing. The Map should not overclaim here. Evaluative control is compatible with physicalism, and this research does not make it evidence *for* interactionism. It removes an objection. It is not an argument.

### Other Tenets
- **Occam's Razor Has Limits.** The Cartesian model is the introspectively simple one ("I understand, then I decide whether to accept"). The experimental record shows the real process is either Spinozan or plausibility-weighted, and more complex than it seems from inside. Simplicity as judged by introspection is a poor guide to the architecture of assent.
- **Dualism.** The transparency face is a structural feature of how belief shows up in experience: always as world, never as state. It connects to the Map's account of phenomenal transparency and to [self-opacity](/voids/self-opacity/).
- **No Many Worlds.** Weak link. Belief fixation assigns one truth-value to *this* believer's world. Nothing further is warranted.
- **Minimal Quantum Interaction.** No direct connection found. It would be speculative to place conscious influence at the assent event, and the note does not recommend it.

### Implications
- The event most central to rational life, taking something to be true, is one consciousness participates in (evaluative control) without witnessing (transparency, timing). That is a sharp instance of the Map's recurring pattern: agency without observational access (compare [decision-void](/voids/decision-void/), [agency-void](/voids/agency-void/)).
- The Spinozan evidence also bears on the [suspension-void](/voids/suspension-void/). If withholding is effortful un-believing, then "suspension" may always be a retraction of an assent already made. That would strengthen the suspension-void's unverifiability face. The expand-topic writer should add a reciprocal link rather than duplicate content.

## Potential Article Angles

1. **"The Assent Void"**: three faces (control, timing, transparency), each with a different modal status. It would argue that the moment of taking-as-true is causally central and phenomenally absent. Open by distinguishing it from suspension, decision, and certainty.
2. **Evaluative control as the shape of conscious agency**: a shorter angle focused on how Hieronymi's distinction answers the "why can't consciousness make itself believe?" objection. This may belong as a section of the voids article rather than as a separate piece.
