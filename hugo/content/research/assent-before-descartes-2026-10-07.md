## Executive Summary

"Assent" (Greek *synkatathesis*, Latin *adsensio*) enters philosophy as a Stoic technical term: the act by which the ruling part of the soul accepts an impression, and the hinge on which the Stoics hung both knowledge and responsibility. From the start the doctrine carries a double claim that the modern literature has had to rediscover. Assent is "wholly in our own power" (Cicero, *Acad.* 2.37 [raw]) and the thing for which we are answerable, *and* "the mind, too, must yield to what is evident" (*Acad.* 2.38 [raw]), so that "he who perceives anything, assents immediately". The Academics turned the second half against the first: if assent to anything non-evident is opinion and the sage never opines, the sage suspends on everything; and Sextus's "forced" and "involuntary" assent (*PH* 1.13, 1.19) is, from the Stoic side, "a contradiction in terms" [SEP]. The Christian line runs from Clement's faith as "an act of consenting" [SEP] through Augustine's "belief itself is nothing else than to think with assent" (*De Praed. Sanct.* 2.5 [raw]), with "this consent is certainly voluntary" (*De Spir. et Litt.* 31.54 [raw]), to Aquinas, for whom believing is "an act of the intellect assenting to the Divine truth at the command of the will" (*ST* II-II q.2 a.9 [raw]). The fourteenth century already contains the modern dispute: Ockham makes an act of will "the very root" of belief (Piché 2017 [snippet]), while Robert Holcot gives both Alston's introspective test and Williams's contradiction argument six centuries early (SEP, Boespflug's translation [SEP]). Descartes is the famous voluntarist because the Fourth Meditation makes error a misuse of a will "of much wider range than the understanding" [raw]; Hobbes objected at once that "we believe it whether we want to or not" [SEP], and the recent scholarship is split on whether Descartes meant every belief to be chosen (Boespflug 2023 [abstract]). Spinoza's *Ethics* IIP49 denies the separate faculty: "Will and understanding are one and the same", and "Suspension of judgment is, therefore, strictly speaking, a perception, and not free will" [raw]. Newman's *Grammar of Assent* (1870) makes assent unconditional and distinct from inference, something that can be "withheld" or "withdrawn" while the reasons remain [raw]; James's "passional nature … must, decide an option" (1896 [raw]) is the voluntarist terminus before the twentieth century. Husserl's doxic modality, already documented in [voids-reality-feeling-void-2026-02-14](/research/voids-reality-feeling-void-2026-02-14/), is the phenomenological bridge and is cross-referenced, not repeated. For the Map, the yield is a lineage: the Stoic double claim is the ancestor of Hieronymi's evaluative control; the Academic *epochē* is the ancestor of the [suspension-void](/voids/suspension-void/); Holcot is the uncredited precursor of the control face's two routes; Spinoza's scholium, not his corollary, is the textual anchor of Gilbert's "Spinozan" model; and none of it bears on the tenets except as the same reply the [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/) already gives to the objection from Bidirectional Interaction.

## Key Sources

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Doxastic Voluntarism" §2 History (Boespflug & Jackson 2024)
- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/doxastic-voluntarism/
- **Type**: Encyclopedia [raw]. §2 has four parts: Antiquity, Middle Ages, Early Modern, Modern. This is the entry both outer reviewers cited; the reviewer's "Epictetus treats assent as subject to voluntary control (3.12.14)" is verbatim.
- **Key points**:
  - "Endorsements of doxastic voluntarism reach back at least to the Roman Stoics." Epictetus in the *Enchiridion* counts "opinion" (ὑπόληψις) among what is "in our power"; his summary of such things as "whatever are our own acts" "suggests his conceiving of opinion as a species of action". "'Opinion' among the Stoics is a species of 'assent' (συγκατάθεσις) which may be given (or not) to impressions … (Vogt 2012: 651). In his *Discourses*, Epictetus treats assent as subject to voluntary control (3.12.14). Though this aspect of Epictetus' thought is largely undeveloped, it suggests an expansive conception of direct doxastic control".
  - Aristotle is set against him: "[I]magining lies within our own power whenever we wish…but in forming opinions we are not free: we cannot escape the alternative of falsehood or truth. (III.427b20)".
  - The Christian strand: faith "would be thought of as a voluntary action as early as Clement of Alexandria (150–215): 'Faith…is in fact preconception by the will, an act of consenting to religion…' (*Stromateis* II.2)". Augustine's "we must believe before we understand" (*De Trinitate* VIII.v.8) and Anselm's "I believe so that I may understand".
  - Middle Ages: Aquinas's fullest discussions "appear in his treatments of faith (*Summa Theologiae* IIaIIae.1–7; *De Veritate* 14.1–2), as do William of Ockham's (1285–1347) (e.g., *Quaestiones Variae* q.5 p.186 in *Opera Theologica* vol. 8)". For Aquinas the will "commands (*coactus*) the intellect to assent" to the articles of faith (IIaIIae 5.2). *De Veritate* 14.1 "even appears to maintain that belief in general is voluntary": "our understanding is determined by the will, which chooses to assent to one side definitely and precisely because of something which is enough to move the will, though not enough to move the understanding". But knowledge (*scientia*) "compels assent—depriving the will of a choice in assenting—it also thereby excludes belief (IIaIIae.1.5; IIaIIae.2.5)", so *credere* "may be better translated 'trust'" (Boespflug 2021b).
  - **Robert Holcot (1290–1349)**, English Dominican, "would give both psychological and conceptual arguments against the possibility of voluntary belief": "every man, who does not want to obstinately deny sense, experiences in himself (*experitur in seipso*) that he cannot by command of the will determine himself to assent to one side of a contradiction, when both are open to question for him. (*Sex Articuli* ii.91; translated by author)"; and "there is a contradiction: I believe that this proposition is true, and that proposition does not appear to me to be true. (*Quaestiones super Sententiarum* I.1)". Yet Holcot "presciently maintains that we may exercise some voluntary control over belief 'indirectly' (*indirecte*) (*Sex Articuli* ii.92; see also Boespflug 2018)".
  - Early modern: "History's most famous doxastic voluntarist, René Descartes (1596–1650), would, like the Stoics he admired, treat assent as a species of action." His voluntarism "is the keystone of the Fourth Meditation's free will response to the so-called epistemological problem of evil." *Comments on a Certain Broadsheet*: "I assigned the act of judging itself, which consists simply in assenting (i.e., in affirmation or denial) to the determination of the will rather than to the perception of the intellect. (AT VIIIa:363; CSM I:307)". Hobbes, Third Objections: "it is not only knowing something to be true that is independent of the will, but also believing it or giving assent to it. If something is proved by valid arguments, or is reported as credible, we believe it whether we want to or not. (AT VII:192; CSM II:134–5)". The entry's own verdict: "Though scholars have been reluctant to take Descartes' apparently extreme voluntarism at face value (Cottingham 2008; Newman 2015; Vitz 2010; Schüssler 2013; Davies 2001), it appears far less extreme when viewed against the backdrop of the conception of free will that Descartes seems to have favored—i.e., compatibilism." Mary Astell "would likewise assign believing to the will".
  - "Beyond Descartes and Astell, doxastic voluntarism would face considerable resistance in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Benedict Spinoza (1632–1677) would offer what appears to be a conceptual argument against voluntarism in his *Ethics* (IIP49)." Locke: doxastic states "are not under the direct control of the will (*Essay* IV.xiii.2, p. 651; IV.xvi.8–9, p. 663)", with the toleration argument from the *First Tract on Government* (1660), indirect control via attention, and "a narrow space for some measure of direct voluntary control in situations where the evidence is ambiguous (IV.xx.15, p. 716)". Hume, *Enquiry* V.ii: "it is not in our power to believe, that such an animal has ever really existed". Reid, *Inquiry* VI.xx: "My belief is carried along by perception, as irresistibly as my body by the earth."
  - Modern: Kant, *Jäsche Logic* Ak 9:73–74: "The will does not have any influence immediately on holding-to-be-true (*fürwahrhalten*); this would be quite absurd", with indirect control retained (*Blomberg Logic*). Kierkegaard, *Philosophical Fragments*: "belief is not a knowledge but an act of freedom, an expression of will…. The conclusion of belief is no conclusion (*slutning*) but a resolution (*beslutning*)" (1844 [1985: 83–84]), with the extent of his voluntarism "a matter of dispute".
- **Absent from §2**: Newman, James, Brentano, Husserl, Scotus, and the Academic sceptics. Newman appears only as Lex Newman (2015) on Descartes. The nineteenth–twentieth-century bridge therefore has to be built from the primary texts below.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The history is a map of positions on belief and will; nothing in it bears on dualism or interaction.
- **Quote**: "History's most famous doxastic voluntarist, René Descartes (1596–1650), would, like the Stoics he admired, treat assent as a species of action."

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Stoicism" (Durand, Shogry & Baltzly 2023)
- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/ (first published 20 January 2023)
- **Type**: Encyclopedia [raw]. Uses Long & Sedley (LS) section numbers for sources.
- **Key points**:
  - The cylinder: "'Hence', he says, 'just as the person who pushed the cylinder gave it its beginning of motion but not its capacity for rolling, likewise, although the impression encountered will print and, as it were, emblazon its appearance on the mind, assent will be in our power.' (Cicero, 62C; see also Gellius, 62D)". The agent's "individual nature or psychological profile" is the decisive antecedent cause, "with the result that our actions are 'up to us' or 'in our power' in a way that is relevant for the notion of responsibility." Bobzien 1998 is the reference work.
  - The attitude set: "Rational creatures are morally and epistemically responsible for our acts of assent, but not for the kinds of impressions we form: assent is 'in our power' or 'up to us' in a way that receiving an impression is not". "('Dissenting', or taking *p* to be false, seems to be more precisely analyzed by the Stoics as suspending assent on *p* and then giving assent to not-*p*.)" "For the Stoics, there is no exaggeration in the claim that what kind of person we are – whether we are virtuous or not, or knowledgeable or not – depends entirely on our acts of assent."
  - Cognition (*katalêpsis*) is "an assent to a special kind of impression, one that functions as a criterion of truth and thus guarantees its own veridicality: such an impression they call 'cognitive' (lit. 'cognition-prompting', *katalêptikos*)"; cognitive impressions "have a peculiar power of revealing their objects" (Cicero, 40B). "the non-Sage will assent to an impression that fails to be cognitive and thus infect their mind with 'opinion' (*doxa*) (Plutarch, 41E). By contrast, the Sage is entirely free of opinion, since she withholds assent on any impression that is non-cognitive (Stobaeus, 41G)."
  - The outcome of assent is "either a doxastic commitment of a certain kind (knowledge, cognition, or the opinion that *p* …) or a 'rational impulse'"; "Since assent is necessary and sufficient for any rational impulse to be created", the one act grounds both belief and action.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The Stoic agent is a corporeal *pneuma*; the doctrine is a physicalist one and the Map should not borrow its authority.
- **Quote**: "assent is 'in our power' or 'up to us' in a way that receiving an impression is not".

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Ancient Skepticism" (Vogt 2022)
- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/skepticism-ancient/ (first published 2010; substantive revision 21 September 2022)
- **Type**: Encyclopedia [raw].
- **Key points**:
  - "Hellenistic discussions envisage three attitudes that cognizers take to impressions (how things seem to them): assent, rejection, and suspension of judgment (*epochê*)." The Apraxia Charge: "A person who believes nothing is like a plant because they cannot act" (Aristotle, *Met.* 1008b).
  - Zeno "was for a time a student at the Academy. He was still in the Academy when he formulated key Stoic doctrines." Arcesilaus and Zeno both "claim Socratic ancestry"; from Arcesilaus's side "Zeno seems to be too optimistic about our cognitive powers to be following Socrates (Frede 1983)".
  - On Sextus: "Sextus characterizes skeptical assent in three ways. He speaks of forced assent (*PH* 1.23–24), involuntary assent (*PH* 1.19), and *adoxastôs* assent (*PH* 2.102)." *PH* 1.13 (Burnyeat's translation with changes): "the skeptic does assent to the experiences forced upon him in virtue of this-or-that impression: for example, he would not say, when warmed or cooled, 'I seem not to be warmed or cooled'". Vogt's own gloss: "Non-doxastic assent is, from the point of view of the Stoics, a contradiction in terms, just like forced and involuntary assent. Assent is defined as in our power, and as that by which beliefs are formed."
  - On doubt: "The ancient skeptics do not describe themselves as making an active effort at doubting what ordinarily they would believe, as some philosophers in the Cartesian tradition" do. Suspension "brings its own peace of mind (*PH* 1.25–30)".
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. Directly relevant to [suspension-void](/voids/suspension-void/): the ancient *epochē* is suspension that arrives rather than suspension that is performed, which is the suspension void's verification face stated two thousand years early.
- **Quote**: "Non-doxastic assent is, from the point of view of the Stoics, a contradiction in terms, just like forced and involuntary assent."

### Cicero, *Academica* II (*Lucullus*), trans. C. D. Yonge (1853)
- **URL**: https://archive.org/details/academicquestion00ciceuoft (OCR full text; Yonge's "Academic Questions" pp. 40–74 for the passages used)
- **Type**: Primary (Latin, 45 BCE) in a public-domain translation [raw]. The OCR has scattered errors ("wldch" for "which"; "forbeai-s to exj)ress" for "forbears to express"), corrected silently in the two quotations that contain them; every other quotation below is verbatim in the OCR text. Rackham's Loeb (1933) and Brittain's Hackett (2006) are the standard modern translations and were not fetched.
- **Key points**:
  - **2.37–38, the Stoic double claim** (Lucullus speaking for Antiochus): "we will proceed to say a little on the subject of assent and approbation, which the Greeks call συγκατάθεσις"; "either sense must be taken from it, or else assent (which is wholly in our own power) must be given"; and immediately: "For as it is inevitable that one scale of a balance must be depressed when a weight is put in it, so the mind, too, must yield to what is evident; for just as it is impossible for any animal to forbear discerning what is manifestly suited to its nature …, so it is equally impossible for it to withhold its assent to a manifest fact which is brought under its notice." Then: "For he who perceives anything, assents immediately." And the stake: "although some things may be in our power, yet they will not be in the power of that man who assents to nothing."
  - **2.145, Zeno's hand**: "when he stretched out his fingers, and showed the palm of his hand, 'Perception,' said he, 'is a thing like this.' Then, when he had a little closed his fingers, 'Assent is like this.' Afterwards, when he had completely closed his hand, and held forth his fist, that, he said, was comprehension." The left hand gripping the fist is knowledge, "what none but a wise man possessed".
  - **2.104, Carneades' two senses** (Cicero speaking): the sage "approves of the one sense, so as never to assent to anything; and adheres to the other, so as to be able to answer yes, or no, following probability whenever it either occurs or is wanting"; one who "in every matter withholds himself from expressing his assent" is "nevertheless agitated and excited to action" by impressions "by which we are excited to action … as long as we avoid expressing a deliberate assent".
  - **2.107, Panaetius**: Cicero's reply to the charge that no one can withhold assent from everything: Panaetius, "almost the greatest man, in my opinion, of all the Stoics, says that he is in doubt" about divination, oracles and dreams "and forbears to express any assent respecting them"; "why may not a wise man do so too in all other cases?"
  - The Stoic reply to Academic trembling-and-paleness cases: "you yourselves say that the wise man when enraged withholds himself from all assent, because there is no distinction between his perceptions which is visible to him" (Yonge p. 45–46; standard numbering not fixed here).
- **Stance check**: Cicero reports both sides in his own voice and Lucullus's; the Stoic claims above are attributed to Antiochus's exposition, the Academic ones to Cicero. Neither "assent is up to us" nor "the mind must yield" is Cicero's personal view.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. But the pairing at 2.37–38 is the historical seed of the distinction the Map's [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/) draws from Hieronymi: assent is the one thing answerable to the agent, and yet it cannot be withheld from the evident. The Stoic sage's freedom over assent is freedom to withhold from the *non*-evident, never to assent against the evident or withhold from it.
- **Quote**: "so the mind, too, must yield to what is evident".

### Aulus Gellius, *Noctes Atticae* 7.2, trans. J. C. Rolfe (Loeb, 1927)
- **URL**: https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Gellius/7*.html
- **Type**: Primary (2nd c. CE, quoting Chrysippus's *On Providence* bk 4) [raw]. The chapter heading: "How Chrysippus also maintained the power and inevitable nature of fate, but at the same time declared that we had control over our plans and decisions."
- **Key points**:
  - Chrysippus's definition: fate is "an eternal and unalterable series of circumstances, and a chain rolling and entangling itself through an unbroken series of consequences".
  - 7.2.7–10, the reply to the responsibility objection: "the peculiar properties of our minds are subject to fate only according to their individuality and quality"; badly formed characters "through their own perversity and voluntary impulse … plunge into continual faults and sin".
  - 7.2.11, the cylinder: "if you roll a cylindrical stone over a sloping, steep piece of ground, you do indeed furnish the beginning and cause of its rapid descent, yet soon it speeds onward, not because you make it do so, but because of its peculiar form and natural tendency to roll; just so the order, the law, and the inevitable quality of fate set in motion the various classes of things and the beginnings of causes, but the carrying out of our designs and thoughts, and even our actions, are regulated by each individual's own will and the characteristics of his mind."
  - 7.2.15, Gellius's closing report that Cicero (*De Fato*) said Chrysippus "is perplexed how to explain that everything is ruled by fate, but that we nevertheless have some control over our conduct."
- **Note**: Gellius's version of the cylinder is about *action and character*; the explicit application to *assent* is in Cicero *De Fato* 42–43 (LS 62C), quoted by the SEP Stoicism entry above and not fetched in the primary this run.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. A compatibilist text; the Map's [free-will](/topics/free-will/) pages lean libertarian and should cite it as the earliest surviving compatibilism about assent, not as support.
- **Quote**: "the carrying out of our designs and thoughts, and even our actions, are regulated by each individual's own will and the characteristics of his mind."

### Epictetus, *Discourses* 3.12.14–15, 1.17, 4.1; *Encheiridion* 1
- **URLs**: *Discourses* 3.12 in T. W. Higginson's translation (1890) at Perseus, http://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0237:text=disc:book=3:chapter=12 ; the George Long *Selection* with *Encheiridion* at https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10661
- **Type**: Primary (c. 108 CE, Arrian's transcription) [raw] in two public-domain translations. Neither prints section numbers; the loci below are matched by content to the standard numbering and should be checked against Oldfather's Loeb (1925–28) or Hard's Oxford (2014) before an article prints section numbers.
- **Key points**:
  - **3.12.14–15** (Higginson), the passage the SEP cites: "The third class relates to the faculty of assent and to what is plausible and persuasive. As Socrates said that we are not to lead a life which is not tested, so neither are we to admit an untested semblance; but to say, 'Stop, let me see what you are and whence you come,' just as the police say, 'Show me your pass.'" The three training topics (desire/aversion; pursuit/avoidance; assent) are Epictetus's standing scheme; the Higginson text prints "sem-blance" across a page break.
  - **Ench. 1** (Long): "Of things some are in our power, and others are not. In our power are opinion ([Greek: hupolaepsis]), movement towards a thing ([Greek: hormae]), desire, aversion ([Greek: echchlisis]), turning from a thing; and in a word, whatever are our acts."
  - **Disc. 1.17** (Long): "And who is able to compel you to assent to that which appears false? No man. And who can compel you not to assent to that which appears true? No man. By this then you see that there is something in you naturally free."
  - **Disc. 4.1** (Long): "Is any man able to make you assent to that which is false? No man. In the matter of assent then you are free from hindrance and obstruction."
- **Stance check**: What Epictetus asserts in these passages is that assent cannot be *compelled from outside*, and that one can *test* an impression before admitting it. That is weaker than "assent is at will", and the two compulsion passages explicitly say one cannot be made to withhold from what appears true. The SEP's "expansive conception of direct doxastic control" is its reading, hedged by "largely undeveloped".
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. For the Map, Epictetus's "Stop, let me see what you are" is managerial control over the *approach* to assent (deferral, examination), which is what the [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/) already concedes everyone has; it is not the control face's missing act.
- **Quote**: "Stop, let me see what you are and whence you come".

### Blackson, T. A. (2025). "Epictetus, the Early Stoics, and Frede's Argument for the First Notion of a Will." *Rhizomata*, 13(1), 83–101
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1515/rhiz-2025-0004
- **Type**: Paper [abstract, verbatim at OpenAlex]; Crossref confirms volume, issue, pages, published 5 August 2025.
- **Key points**: "Michael Frede has argued that there is a minimum belief for a Stoic to have a notion of a will and that Epictetus and the late Stoics were the first to have this belief. Against Frede's interpretation, I argue for a new understanding of what the Stoics believed. Epictetus did not believe, as Frede argues, that adult human beings choose to assent to impulsive impressions. This gets the object of choice wrong in the minimum belief. He believed that the choice adult human beings make is to exercise their ability to use their impressions, and I argue that the early Stoics probably believed this too."
- **Relevance**: the one live 2020s dispute directly on the SEP's Epictetus claim. If Blackson is right, the choice is over the *use* of impressions (examination, deferral), not over assent itself, which brings Epictetus closer to the involuntarist side than the SEP's history places him. Frede's book is *A Free Will: Origins of the Notion in Ancient Thought* (2011) [not reached].
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral.

- **Key points** [abstract, verbatim; HAL hyphenation artifacts removed]: assent "which Zeno introduced into his theory of sensation, founded both Stoic epistemology and Stoic ethics". On *eixis* ("submission") and *synkatathesis*: "they in fact express one and the same act, envisaged according to two different modalities, one of which translates the passivity of the hegemonic part which lets itself be swept along by a representation, and the other of which manifests that this same act, once subject to the control of reason, is a voluntary choice that depends on us." Weak assent: "When a εἶξις/συγκατάθεσις is granted out of weakness, and therefore illegitimately, to a representation that lacks self-evidence or is false, the assent is said to be precipitous or false." The Academics used "the definition of assent as a εἶξις ὑπ᾽ ἀσθενείας, which implies that man can lose all control of his representations" to argue for suppressing assent. Also argues "that all the assents of the ordinary man, including his comprehensions … are opinions and weak beliefs, pertaining to ignorance" (with "a new interpretation of *Lucullus* 41").
- **Relevance**: the Stoics themselves had a *passive* description of the same act they called voluntary, which is the structure the Map's [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/) describes as "agency without a handle". The Academic exploitation of *eixis* is the ancestor of Gilbert's load paradigms: a mind "swept along by a representation" is one that accepts on comprehension.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral.

### Augustine, *De Praedestinatione Sanctorum* 2.5 and *De Spiritu et Littera* 31.54–32.55 (Holmes & Wallis, NPNF1 vol. 5, 1887)
- **URLs**: https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/15121.htm ; https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1502.htm
- **Type**: Primary (428/9 and 412 CE) in a public-domain translation [raw]. Translated by Peter Holmes and Robert Ernest Wallis, revised by B. B. Warfield; chapter numbering is New Advent's (the bracketed Roman numerals are the Benedictine chapters).
- **Key points**:
  - *De Praed. Sanct.* 2.5, chapter headed "To Believe is to Think with Assent": "For who cannot see that thinking is prior to believing? For no one believes anything unless he has first thought that it is to be believed. For however suddenly, however rapidly, some thoughts fly before the will to believe, and this presently follows in such wise as to attend them, as it were, in closest conjunction, it is yet necessary that everything which is believed should be believed after thought has preceded; although even belief itself is nothing else than to think with assent." The Latin is *credere nihil aliud est quam cum assensione cogitare* (not fetched; standard).
  - *De Spir. et Litt.* 31.54, chapter headed "Whether Faith Be in a Man's Own Power": "Consider now whether anybody believes, if he be unwilling; or whether he believes not, if he shall have willed it. Such a position, indeed, is absurd (for what is believing but consenting to the truth of what is said? And this consent is certainly voluntary): faith, therefore, is in our own power." 32.55 opens: "Since faith, then, is in our power, inasmuch as every one believes when he likes, and, when he believes, believes voluntarily". The same chapter then subordinates the will to grace: "it is God who gave us even to believe", while "Nowhere, however, in Holy Scripture do we find such an assertion as, There is no volition but comes from God."
- **Stance check**: Augustine's "voluntary" is anti-Pelagian and theological: the point is that consent is an act for which one is answerable and which grace can give, not that one can believe arbitrary propositions on demand. The 428 text adds the ordering claim (thought before assent) that the Cartesian model inherits.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The *cum assensione cogitare* formula is the definition Aquinas takes over and is the direct ancestor of the modern "belief = acceptance of a proposition" that the [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/) presupposes.
- **Quote**: "belief itself is nothing else than to think with assent".

### Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* II-II q.2 a.1 and a.9 (Fathers of the English Dominican Province, 1920)
- **URL**: https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3002.htm
- **Type**: Primary (c. 1271) in a public-domain translation [raw].
- **Key points**:
  - a.1, "Whether to believe is to think with assent?" *Sed contra*: "This is how 'to believe' is defined by Augustine (De Praedest. Sanct. ii)." Body: taking "to think" as "the movement of the mind while yet deliberating, and not yet perfected by the clear sight of truth", the formula "expresses completely the nature of the act of believing": "some acts of the intellect have unformed thought devoid of a firm assent, whether they incline to neither side, as in one who 'doubts'; or incline to one side rather than the other, but on account of some slight motive, as in one who 'suspects'; or incline to one side yet with fear of the other, as in one who 'opines.' But this act 'to believe,' cleaves firmly to one side, in which respect belief has something in common with science and understanding; yet its knowledge does not attain the perfection of clear sight". "Hence it is proper to the believer to think with assent: so that the act of believing is distinguished from all the other acts of the intellect, which are about the true or the false."
  - a.1 ad 3, the will's role: "The intellect of the believer is determined to one object, not by the reason, but by the will, wherefore assent is taken here for an act of the intellect as determined to one object by the will."
  - a.9: "the act of believing is an act of the intellect assenting to the Divine truth at the command of the will moved by the grace of God, so that it is subject to the free-will in relation to God; and consequently the act of faith can be meritorious."
- **Stance check**: Aquinas's scheme is a *taxonomy of assent-states* (doubt, suspicion, opinion, belief, science) ordered by firmness and evidence, with the will supplying firmness only where evidence does not compel. It is the medieval intellectualist position: the will commands assent where the intellect is not determined, and *scientia* leaves it nothing to do (SEP: IIaIIae.1.5; 2.5).
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The doubt/suspect/opine/believe ladder is a graded-attitude scheme and a useful historical foil for the [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/)'s discrete-transition assumption.
- **Quote**: "an act of the intellect assenting to the Divine truth at the command of the will".

### Descartes, *Meditation IV: Of Truth and Error* (1641), trans. John Veitch
- **URL**: https://pressbooks.library.torontomu.ca/meditationsonfirstphilosophy/chapter/4/ (public-domain Veitch text, paragraph-numbered; the Wikisource copy returned a Wikimedia error this run)
- **Type**: Primary [raw]. AT VII:56–62; CSM II:39–43. The SEP quotes the Cottingham–Stoothoff–Murdoch rendering; the Veitch wording below differs and is what was verified.
- **Key points**:
  - §8, the two faculties: errors "depend on the concurrence of two causes, viz, the faculty of cognition, which I possess, and that of election or the power of free choice,—in other words, the understanding and the will. For by the understanding alone, I [neither affirm nor deny anything but] merely apprehend (*percipio*) the ideas regarding which I may form a judgment". "the power of will consists only in this, that we are able to do or not to do the same thing (that is, to affirm or deny, to pursue or shun it)". And freedom is not indifference: "the more I am inclined toward the one, whether because I clearly know that in it there is the reason of truth and goodness, or because God thus internally disposes my thought, the more freely do I choose and embrace it"; indifference "is the lowest grade of liberty".
  - §9, the diagnosis: errors "arise from this cause alone, that I do not restrain the will, which is of much wider range than the understanding, within the same limits, but extend it even to things I do not understand".
  - §10, the *cogito* as the model: "I could not but judge that what I so clearly conceived was true, not that I was forced to this judgment by any external cause, but simply because great clearness of the understanding was succeeded by strong inclination in the will".
  - §12, the ordering rule: "it is a dictate of the natural light, that the knowledge of the understanding ought always to precede the determination of the will."
  - §15: God has "accorded to me the power of giving or withholding my assent from certain things of which he has not put a clear and distinct knowledge in my understanding".
- **Stance check**: Two claims are packed together and the literature separates them. (1) *Sequence*: understanding first, will second (§12) — this is what Gilbert (1991) names the "Cartesian" model. (2) *Freedom*: assent is an act of will even when clarity makes it inevitable (§8, §10), which is why the SEP reads Descartes as a compatibilist about assent. Boespflug (2023) [abstract]: "Descartes appears to hold that everything we believe is the product of a voluntary choice. Scholars have been reluctant to take this particularly radical version of doxastic voluntarism as Descartes' considered position. I argue that once Descartes' compatibilist conception of free will as well as his position on the 'freedom of indifference' are taken into account, the primary motivations for the rejection of the aforementioned radical version of doxastic voluntarism lose their force. Consequently, we may take Descartes at his word when he maintains that everything we believe, we believe freely – even if we cannot believe anything we wish." (*IJPS* 31(2), 158–178; this closes the "author not recorded" gap in [voids-assent-void-2026-09-28](/research/voids-assent-void-2026-09-28/).)
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral on the tenets. The [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/) ref 5 should become: Descartes, R. (1641/1901). *Meditations on First Philosophy*, Meditation IV, trans. J. Veitch; AT VII:56–62, CSM II:39–43 — the "bare citation" ChatGPT flagged.
- **Quote**: "the will, which is of much wider range than the understanding".

### Spinoza, *Ethics* Part II, Propositions 48–49 with Corollary and Scholium (1677), trans. R. H. M. Elwes (1883)
- **URL**: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800
- **Type**: Primary [raw]. Curley's translation (Penguin 1996) is the SEP's reference and was not fetched.
- **Key points**:
  - IIP48: "In the mind there is no absolute or free will".
  - IIP49: "There is in the mind no volition or affirmation and negation, save that which an idea, inasmuch as it is an idea, involves." Proof via the triangle: the affirmation that its angles equal two right angles "belongs to the essence of the idea of a triangle, and is nothing besides"; so any volition "is nothing but an idea".
  - Corollary: "Will and understanding are one and the same."
  - Scholium, the objection he answers: "experience seems to teach us especially clearly, that we are able to suspend our judgment before assenting to things which we perceive; this is confirmed by the fact that no one is said to be deceived, in so far as he perceives anything, but only in so far as he assents or dissents." The Cartesian premise is named: "The will is thus distinguished from the intellect, the latter being finite and the former infinite."
  - The reply: "I reply by denying, that we have a free power of suspending our judgment: for, when we say that anyone suspends his judgment, we merely mean that he sees, that he does not perceive the matter in question adequately. Suspension of judgment is, therefore, strictly speaking, a perception, and not free will." The boy and the horse: "Inasmuch as this imagination involves the existence of the horse (II. xvii. Coroll.), and the boy does not perceive anything which would exclude the existence of the horse, he will necessarily regard the horse as present: he will not be able to doubt of its existence, although he be not certain thereof. We have daily experience of such a state of things in dreams".
  - Also: "a false idea, inasmuch as it is false, does not involve certainty"; acquiescing in the false is "only that he does not doubt".
- **Stance check for Gilbert's label**: the corollary (will = understanding) is the *conceptual* anti-voluntarism the SEP names. The *psychological* claim Gilbert (1991) stylises as "comprehension includes acceptance" is in the scholium: an idea is affirmed unless something in the mind excludes its object, and suspension is a further perception, not a prior act. Which passage Gilbert himself cites was not verified (his PDF was not reached, see Gaps); the scholium is where the "Spinozan model" lives in the text.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral; Spinoza's monism is not the Map's. Note for [suspension-void](/voids/suspension-void/): Spinoza's "Suspension of judgment is … a perception, and not free will" is a seventeenth-century statement of its verification face, and his dream example is the "we suspend our judgment, namely, when we dream that we are dreaming" case.
- **Quote**: "Suspension of judgment is, therefore, strictly speaking, a perception, and not free will."

### Newman, J. H. (1870). *An Essay in Aid of a Grammar of Assent*
- **URL**: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/34022 (the text is the later revised edition; the Preface is dated 21 February 1870)
- **Type**: Primary [raw].
- **Key points**:
  - Ch. I: "Assent is unconditional; else, it is not really represented by assertion. Inference is conditional, because a conclusion at least implies the assumption of premisses". Ch. II opens: "an act of Assent, first, that it is in itself the absolute acceptance of a proposition without any condition".
  - Ch. IV, notional and real assent: "An act of assent, it seems, is the most perfect and highest of its kind, when it is exercised on propositions, which are apprehended as experiences and images, that is, which stand for things; and, on the other hand, an act of inference is the most perfect and highest of its kind, when it is exercised on propositions which are apprehended as notions".
  - Ch. VI, "Assent Considered as Unconditional", the paradox stated: "how it is that a conditional acceptance of a proposition,—such as is an act of inference,—is able to lead, as it does, to an unconditional acceptance of it,—such as is assent". Against Locke's degrees of assent: on that view "assent becomes a sort of necessary shadow, following upon inference, which is the substance; and is never without some alloy of doubt". Newman's reply: "I have been showing that inference and assent are distinct acts of the mind, and that they may be made apart from each other." Assent "may not be withheld in cases when there are good reasons for giving it to a proposition, or may not be withdrawn after it has been given, the reasons remaining" — he denies the "not": assent *can* be withheld and withdrawn with the reasons unchanged, "and this substantiveness, as I may call it, of the act of assent is the very point which I have wished to establish." The folk evidence: "obstinate men give their own will as their very reason for assenting, if they can think of nothing better; 'stat pro ratione voluntas.'"
  - Ch. IX, the illative sense, is the faculty by which the mind passes from probable inference to unconditional assent in concrete matters.
- **Stance check**: Newman is neither a Cartesian voluntarist nor an involuntarist. Assent is a distinct, substantive act that outruns inference and can be withheld while reasons stand, but "I doubt whether assent is ever given without some preliminary, which stands for a reason". He is the nineteenth-century source for the claim that assent is *all-or-nothing* (unconditional), which is the discrete-transition premise the [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/) L41 flags as an assumption.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral.
- **Quote**: "Assent is unconditional; else, it is not really represented by assertion."

## Major Positions

### Stoic assent: up to us, yet yielded to the evident
- **Proponents**: Zeno, Chrysippus (via Cicero *Acad.* 2.37–38, 145; Gellius 7.2), Epictetus (*Disc.* 1.17, 3.12, 4.1; *Ench.* 1)
- **Core claim**: assent is the act by which the rational soul accepts an impression; it is "wholly in our own power" and the locus of responsibility; yet a cognitive impression compels it "as it is inevitable that one scale of a balance must be depressed when a weight is put in it".
- **Key arguments**: the cylinder (our character, not the push, fixes the roll); Zeno's hand (perception, assent, comprehension, knowledge as four grips); the apraxia reply (no assent, no action).
- **Scholarly dispute**: Frede (2011) finds the first notion of a will in Epictetus's choice to assent; Blackson (2025) [abstract] replies that the object of choice is the *use* of impressions, not assent; Goulet-Cazé (2011) [abstract] shows the Stoics had a passive description (*eixis*) of the same act.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral. The double claim is the historical form of what [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/) L107 says in reply to the Bidirectional Interaction objection: conscious agency over belief is responsiveness to the evident, not manipulation of a state.

### Academic and Pyrrhonian *epochē*
- **Proponents**: Arcesilaus, Carneades (via Cicero *Acad.* 2.104, 2.107), Sextus (*PH* 1.13, 1.19, 1.23–24, 2.102)
- **Core claim**: since assent to the non-cognitive is opinion and nothing is cognitive, withhold; act on what is "probable" or on "forced" and "involuntary" assent that is not opinion.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral. For the Map this is the first sustained claim that suspension is something that *happens to* a mind that has examined impressions rather than something it does, which is the [suspension-void](/voids/suspension-void/)'s verification face; Vogt's point that forced assent is "a contradiction in terms" for the Stoics is the ancient form of the Map's question whether assent can be at once involuntary and one's own.

### Christian voluntarism: faith as thinking with assent, consent as voluntary
- **Proponents**: Clement, Augustine (*De Praed. Sanct.* 2.5; *De Spir. et Litt.* 31.54), Aquinas (*ST* II-II q.2 a.1, a.9; *De Veritate* 14.1), Ockham (per Piché 2017 [snippet]), Kierkegaard (SEP)
- **Core claim**: believing is "to think with assent"; where evidence does not compel, the will determines the intellect to one side; this is why faith can be commanded and meritorious.
- **Key arguments**: Augustine's "whether anybody believes, if he be unwilling"; Aquinas's ladder (doubt, suspicion, opinion, belief, science) with the will firming assent only below the threshold of sight.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral. Note that Aquinas's scheme already confines voluntariness to the *underdetermined* case, which is the permissivist shape the modern voluntarists take.

### Medieval involuntarism: Holcot
- **Proponents**: Robert Holcot (*Sex Articuli* ii.91–92; *Quaestiones super Sententiarum* I.1) [SEP, Boespflug's translations]
- **Core claim**: one "experiences in himself" that he "cannot by command of the will determine himself to assent to one side of a contradiction, when both are open to question"; and voluntary belief "yields a contradiction: I believe that this proposition is true, and that proposition does not appear to me to be true"; indirect control remains.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral. This is the single most useful historical find for the Map's purposes: the SEP's psychological route (Alston) and conceptual route (Williams) are both stated by one fourteenth-century author, so the [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/)'s "two routes" framing has a precedent the void page does not name. Any article should credit Boespflug (2018, 2024) for the recovery and must not call Williams the originator.

### Cartesian voluntarism: will outstrips intellect
- **Proponents**: Descartes (*Med.* IV; *Principles* I; *Comments on a Certain Broadsheet*), Astell (SEP)
- **Core claim**: judgment is the will's affirmation or denial of what the intellect presents; error is the will's going beyond clear and distinct perception; even compelled assent is free.
- **Objections then and now**: Hobbes (Third Objections) [SEP]; Spinoza IIP49; the "reluctant" scholars (Cottingham, Vitz, Schüssler, Davies, L. Newman) versus Boespflug 2023 [abstract].
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral. The Map's existing use ([assent-void](/voids/assent-void/) L67, L95) is as the name of a *sequence* model and as a picture that "has no special claim on introspection"; both uses survive the scholarship, but the article should state that "Cartesian" in Gilbert's sense (understand, then judge) and "voluntarist" in the SEP's sense (every judgment is free) are separable, and that Descartes held both.

### Spinozan identity of will and intellect
- **Proponents**: Spinoza (IIP48–49); Locke, Hume, Reid, Kant as the empiricist and critical involuntarists (SEP)
- **Core claim**: there is no faculty of will over and above particular ideas; an idea affirms its object unless excluded; suspension is a perception, not an act.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral. The textual anchor for Gilbert's "Spinozan" model is the scholium (boy and horse), and the [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/) should cite IIP49 scholium rather than IIP49 bare.

### Nineteenth-century bridge: Newman and James
- **Proponents**: Newman (1870), James (1896)
- **Core claim**: Newman: assent is unconditional, distinct from inference, substantive enough to be withheld or withdrawn; James: where evidence cannot decide a genuine option, the passional nature must.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral. Newman supplies the all-or-nothing premise the void's timing face needs; James supplies the modern form of permissivism.

## Key Debates

### Was Stoic assent voluntary in the modern sense?
- **Sides**: SEP (Boespflug & Jackson 2024) reads Epictetus as "an expansive conception of direct doxastic control"; Frede (2011) locates the first notion of a will there; Blackson (2025) says the choice is over the use of impressions; Goulet-Cazé (2011) shows the Stoic act had a passive description too; Cicero's balance (2.38) says the evident compels.
- **Current state**: open. The safe article claim is the double thesis (up to us; yielded to the evident), with the voluntarist reading flagged as the SEP's and Frede's, not the texts' plain sense.

### Is Descartes a direct voluntarist about every belief?
- **Sides**: Boespflug (2023) yes, given compatibilism; Cottingham, Vitz, Schüssler, Davies, L. Newman reluctant [SEP].
- **Current state**: open; ChatGPT's outer-review point that this "is an interpretive position rather than an uncontested historical datum" stands.

### Does Gilbert's Cartesian/Spinozan contrast match the texts?
- **Sides**: Gilbert (1991) uses the names for a sequence model versus an acceptance-on-comprehension model; Descartes §12 supplies the sequence rule; Spinoza's scholium supplies acceptance-unless-excluded. Which lines Gilbert cites [not reached].
- **Current state**: the labels are well grounded as *stylisations*; neither philosopher was making a claim about processing stages under cognitive load, and the article should say so (the psychology is in [voids-assent-void-2026-09-28](/research/voids-assent-void-2026-09-28/)).

### Who first stated the modern involuntarist arguments?
- **Sides**: the contemporary literature credits Williams (1973) for the contradiction argument and Alston (1988) for the introspective test; the SEP's Holcot passages state both c. 1332–34 (Boespflug 2018 is the recovery).
- **Current state**: not a dispute in print that this run found, but a credit the Map should carry correctly.

## What the Map Needs From This History

Read before writing: [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/) L41–43 (discrete-transition assumption; "the dominant view"), L59–63 (control face), L67 (Gilbert's labels), L95 (Descartes "has no special claim on introspection"), L107 (reply to Bidirectional Interaction), ref 5 (bare Descartes); [doxastic-voluntarism-2026-10-07](/research/doxastic-voluntarism-2026-10-07/) §"What the Map's Interface Account Needs" (seven points; do not repeat them); [suspension-void](/voids/suspension-void/); [introspection-architecture-independence-scoring](/topics/introspection-architecture-independence-scoring/) §Stoic *propatheia* (the Map's only live Stoic content, on sub-assent affect, not on assent).

1. **Name the lineage once and link, rather than extend the void.** The void page is near its gate (2,451 body words by `analyze_length` on 10-07 against a voids soft 2,000 / hard 3,000, per the ChatGPT review's processing note; `wc -w` reads 3,090 because it counts frontmatter). The synthesis tasked "one Stoic/Spinoza anchor sentence" there. The history belongs in the new topics article, which the void then links to from L59 or L67 with a piped wikilink at near-zero cost.
2. **The Stoic double claim is the ancestor of evaluative control.** Cicero 2.37–38 puts "wholly in our own power" and "the mind … must yield to what is evident" in consecutive sentences. That is the structure Hieronymi (2006) calls evaluative control and the [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/) L89–91 calls "agency without a handle". The article should make the lineage explicit and credit Goulet-Cazé's *eixis* finding as the Stoics' own passive description.
3. **Holcot before Williams and Alston.** The two routes to involuntarism the SEP distinguishes are both in Holcot (per Boespflug). The Map's pages credit Williams and Alston as if originators; a one-clause correction ("as Robert Holcot had argued c. 1333") is cheap and the kind of coiner/populariser fix the Map's citation discipline requires.
4. **Spinoza IIP49 scholium, not IIP49 bare, for the "Spinozan" model.** The corollary is the anti-faculty claim; the boy-and-horse passage is the acceptance-unless-excluded claim. Cite the scholium and say Gilbert's label is a stylisation of it. Also install the dream sentence on [suspension-void](/voids/suspension-void/) if that page ever takes a historical line.
5. **Descartes: separate sequence from freedom.** Give ref 5 an edition (Veitch; AT VII:56–62; CSM II:39–43). State that Descartes holds *both* the ordering rule (§12) and the freedom of even compelled assent (§8, §10); the first is what Gilbert borrows, the second is what the voluntarism debate contests (Boespflug 2023 vs the "reluctant" scholars).
6. **Epictetus 3.12.14 is about testing impressions, not believing at will.** Quote it; report the SEP's reading and Blackson's dissent; do not let the article inherit "expansive direct doxastic control" as the texts' plain sense.
7. **Suspension has a longer history than assent.** The *epochē* line (Arcesilaus → Sextus → Descartes's doubt → Husserl) is the [suspension-void](/voids/suspension-void/)'s genealogy, and Spinoza's "a perception, and not free will" is its sharpest pre-modern statement. The new article should give the suspension void one paragraph and a link rather than absorb it.
8. **Tenets.** None bears. The only tenet-relevant content is the reply the [assent-void](/voids/assent-void/) L107 already gives to the objection from Bidirectional Interaction ("if consciousness causes things, why can it not make itself believe?"): the history shows the limit was recognised by physicalist Stoics, Augustinian theists and Spinozist monists alike, which is evidence the limit is about belief's answerability to the evident, not about any metaphysics of mind. Occam's Razor Has Limits gets nothing new from the history; Dualism gets nothing. Keep the register of the sibling note's point 2.
9. **Scope the word "assent".** The Stoic term covers impulse as well as belief (all impulses are assents, per Arius Didymus via Goulet-Cazé); Aquinas's covers a ladder of attitudes; Newman's is all-or-nothing. An article titled on "assent" must say in its first paragraph which of these the Map's void means (Newman's and the modern "acceptance of a proposition as true"), or the title will be read against the Stoic sense.
