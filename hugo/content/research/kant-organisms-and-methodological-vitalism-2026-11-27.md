## Executive Summary

The middle path between vitalism and mechanism had two eighteenth-century forms. Both bear on [vitalism](/concepts/vitalism/)'s central thesis that the vital force was a *schematic placeholder*. The test was run in both directions, and both came back positive.

**The witness direction holds for the vital principle's *nature*.** Barthez defined the vital principle as "la cause qui produit tous les phénomènes de la vie dans le corps humain" and added that "Le nom de cette cause est assez indifférent". He professed a "Scepticisme invincible" about its nature and said "nous ignorons même si ce Principe est une substance, ou seulement un mode du corps humain vivant" (1806 edition, verified). Blumenbach said his *Bildungstrieb*, like "Attraction" and "Schwere", names a force whose constant effect experience shows but whose cause is "für uns qualitas occulta" (1789, verified). Kant made the organism a "natural purpose" while denying that the idea introduces any causal agent: the teleological word "is to introduce no particular ground of causality" (§68, AA 5:383, Bernard trans., verified). These are first-person statements by vitalists and by their most careful critic that the vital term names an unknown cause. The placeholder thesis gains real historical witnesses.

**The scoping direction holds for the vital principle's *profile*.** The same authors gave it determinate features:
- Barthez's principle is "douée de forces motrices et sensitives". He gave it a named "force de situation fixe" in muscle fibre, and its action "n'est point mécanique".
- Blumenbach's drive builds, keeps and restores a species' determinate form, and he sets it apart from contractility, irritability and sensibility.
- Every one of these features was specified by its *effects*. Mechanisms that supplied the effects discharged them, so the thesis survives as "specified only by effects" rather than "no determinate feature".
- **One exception matters.** Barthez gave severed muscle an unreflected *feeling*. He defended it with an argument of explanatory-gap form: "L'idée du sentiment n'a rien de commun avec l'idée du mouvement". That attribution was not reduced. Physiology simply dropped it.

This is the history's closest approach to the consciousness case, and it cuts both ways, as explained below in [The test, run both directions](#the-test-run-both-directions).

**Kant's "no Newton of a blade of grass" (§75, AA 5:400) is the track-record proponent's best exhibit.** Haeckel announced in 1868 that Darwin was "dieser unmögliche Newton". The Map should face this squarely. Kant's claim was an in-principle limit on human explanation, which is the same logical form as the Map's claims about the phenomenal residue. On the same page Kant also refuses to judge that nature contains *no* mechanical ground for organisms ("denn woher wollen wir das wissen?").

## The Test, Run Both Directions {#the-test-run-both-directions}

The thesis under test (`concepts/vitalism` L76): "'Vital force' was a **schematic placeholder**. It named a role — *whatever makes the organism alive* — without committing to any determinate feature a reduction had to preserve." Its origin is `topics/the-convergence-argument-for-dualism` L163 ("a placeholder for unspecified mechanism, picking out no particular feature a reduction had to deliver"), which `concepts/type-specificity` L55 quotes verbatim.

### Direction 1 — witness: the vital term named an unknown cause

| Author | Verified wording (source) | What it witnesses |
|---|---|---|
| Barthez | "Je donne le nom de Principes aux causes générales des phénomènes du mouvement et de la vie, qui ne sont connues que par leurs lois que manifeste l'observation. Ainsi j'appelle Principe Vital de l'Homme, la cause qui produit tous les phénomènes de la vie dans le corps humain. Le nom de cette cause est assez indifférent, et peut être pris à volonté." (1806, vol. 1, ch. I §I, pp. 47–48) | The principle is defined by its effects, and its name is a free label |
| Barthez | "Je prouverai qu'on doit se réduire à un Scepticisme invincible sur la nature du Principe de la Vie dans l'homme." (1806, Discours préliminaire, p. 27) | Its nature is declared unknowable |
| Barthez | "nous ignorons même si ce Principe est une substance, ou seulement un mode du corps humain vivant" (1806, ch. I §XI, p. 61) | Substance status is explicitly suspended, so this is not substantival vitalism |
| Barthez | "je personnifie le Principe Vital de l'Homme, pour pouvoir en parler d'une manière plus commode … Il ne m'importe qu'on attribue, ou qu'on refuse une existence particulière et propre à cet Etre que j'appelle Principe Vital." (1806, ch. III §XLIII, pp. 107–108) | Personification is declared a convenience |
| Barthez | "On peut donner à ces causes générales, que j'appelle expérimentales, ou qui ne sont connues que par leurs lois que donne l'expérience ; les noms synonymes, et pareillement indéterminés, de principe, de puissance, de force, de faculté, etc." followed by the gravitation model: "Newton a dit que leur cause commune est la gravitation." (1806, Discours, pp. 7–8) | The explicit Newtonian template: an "experimental cause" known only by its laws |
| Blumenbach | "…daß das Wort Bildungstrieb, so gut wie die Worte Attraction, Schwere etc. zu nichts mehr und nichts weniger dienen soll, als eine Kraft zu bezeichnen, deren constante Wirkung aus der Erfahrung anerkannt worden, deren Ursache aber so gut wie die Ursache der genannten, noch so allgemein anerkannten Naturkräfte, für uns qualitas occulta ist." He adds Ovid's "causa latet, vis est notissima", and that the only merit in studying such forces is to determine their effects more closely and bring them under more general laws. (*Über den Bildungstrieb*, 1789, c. pp. 25–26; Fraktur OCR normalised) | The same Newtonian template, made explicit |
| Blumenbach (Eng. 1792) | "the expression Formative Nisus, like that of Attraction, … serves only to denote a power, whose constant operation is known from experience, but whose cause, like the causes of most of the qualities of matter, is a qualitas occulta to us" (*An Essay on Generation*, London: Cadell, 1792; translator unnamed on the title page) | Usable English for a quotation |
| Kant | "…this word here only expresses a principle of the reflective not of the determinant Judgement, and so is to introduce no particular ground of causality" (§68, AA 5:383; Bernard 1914). German: "keinen besondern Grund der Causalität einführen solle" | The teleological concept explicitly posits no cause |
| Kant | On the animist option: positing a soul either presupposes organised matter as the soul's instrument, "which does not make the soul a whit more comprehensible", or makes the soul the artificer and removes the product from nature (§65, AA 5:374–375; Bernard pp. 279–280) | A contemporary argument that the substantival posit does no explanatory work |

Two secondary sources frame the pattern:
- **Wolfe (2011)** reads the Montpellier school (Bordeu, Ménuret, Fouquet) as *functional* rather than *substantival* vitalists. He writes that "the vita-list will make no ontological claims about the nature of this vital principle" (hyphenation as published in the HTML). He also notes that the word "vitalism" was itself a Montpellier coinage. Citing Williams 2003, p. 276, he credits Charles-Louis Dumas with first using it.
- **Canguilhem**, as Wolfe quotes him in translation: "Eighteenth-century vitalists are [...] not impenitent metaphysicians but rather prudent positivists, which is to say, in that period, Newtonians." This is reached via Wolfe only; the primary was not consulted.

**Verdict on Direction 1:** confirmed. The Map's thesis has first-person historical witnesses: the vitalists themselves said their term named an unknown cause known only by its effects. This strengthens L76 beyond what the page claims, because the page currently asserts the placeholder structure without citing anyone who held it.

### Direction 2 — scoping: the vital principle carried determinate features

| Author | Verified wording (source) | What it scopes |
|---|---|---|
| Barthez | the principle may be read as "une simple faculté vitale du corps humain, qui nous est inconnue dans son essence, mais qui est douée de forces motrices et sensitives" (1806, ch. III §XLIII, p. 108) | In one sentence: unknown in essence, determinate in its forces |
| Barthez | the "force de situation fixe des molécules de la fibre musculeuse", "entièrement distincte de celle de la contraction musculaire" (1806, ch. IV, pp. 134–135) | A named force with a specific functional signature |
| Barthez | "on doit rapporter les divers mouvemens qui s'opèrent dans le corps humain vivant, à deux Principes différens, dont l'action n'est point mécanique, et dont la nature est occulte. L'un est l'Ame pensante, et l'autre est le Principe de la Vie." (1806, Discours, p. 20) | A determinate *negative* commitment (non-mechanical action), plus a tripartite scheme in which the life-principle is not the mind |
| Barthez (retrospect) | his 1773 oration had established "que ce Principe existe indépendamment de la mécanique du corps humain et des affections de l'Ame pensante" (1806, ch. II, p. 81) | An independence claim |
| Barthez | "L'idée du sentiment n'a rien de commun avec l'idée du mouvement ; et par conséquent il est impossible de concevoir comment des ébranlemens quelconques, communiqués à un organe quelconque par des corps qui lui sont extérieurs, peuvent donner à cet organe la faculté de sentir." (1806, ch. V §LXXXVII, pp. 177–178) | **An explanatory-gap-form argument about feeling, made by a vitalist** |
| Barthez | severed muscles "ont une sensibilité locale, indépendante de l'intégrité de leurs nerfs ; et qu'ils sentent le stimulus qui leur est appliqué ; quoique ce sentiment ne puisse vraisemblablement être réfléchi, comme il le seroit dans un Principe analogue à l'Ame pensante" (1806, ch. V §LXXXIX, p. 181; punctuation normalised from OCR) | **A feeling attributed on behavioural evidence** (contraction of severed muscle) |
| Bordeu | glandular selectivity as "une autre sensibilité, ou, si nous osons l'avancer, un autre goût", hedged at once: "en suivant toujours notre façon de parler métaphorique, quelques nuances de sentiment, qu'il ne faut pas sans doute confondre avec le sentiment proprement dit" (*Recherches anatomiques sur la position des glandes*, 1751, §§CIX–CX, in *Œuvres complètes* 1818, vol. 1) | Determinate organ-specific sensibility that the author himself marks as *not* feeling |
| Bordeu | the bee swarm: "Nous comparons le corps vivant, pour bien sentir l'action particulière de chaque partie, à un essaim d'abeilles …"; each part is "non pas sans doute un animal, mais une espèce de machine à part qui concourt, à sa façon, à la vie générale du corps" (1751, §CXXV; Wolfe 2011 n. 8 gives *Œuvres* vol. 1, p. 187) | A determinate *organisational* model. Bordeu calls each part a "machine" |
| Blumenbach | the drive is a "Trieb …, ihre bestimmte Gestalt anfangs anzunehmen, dann lebenslang zu erhalten, und wenn sie ja etwa verstümmelt worden, wo möglich wieder herzustellen"; it is "eben so deutlich von den übrigen Arten der Lebenskraft der organisirten Körper (der Contractilität, Irritabilität, Sensilität etc.) als von den allgemeinen physischen Kräften der Körper überhaupt verschieden" (1789, c. pp. 24–25; OCR normalised) | Determinate effects (form-specific generation, maintenance and repair) and a determinate place in a taxonomy of forces |

Three further points complicate the picture:
- **The 1778 first edition may differ.** Wolfe (2011, n. 2) says "Barthez had initially asserted the existence of an independent vital force, but withdrew this and added a chapter to the second edition of his book entitled 'Skeptical considerations on the nature of the vital principle'." The 1806 edition does contain "CHAPITRE III. Considérations sceptiques sur la nature du Principe Vital de l'Homme" (p. 82). Barthez's own 1806 notes, however, claim he "constamment témoigné, et même en finissant mon Ouvrage (à la page 347), mon scepticisme absolu et invincible". The 1778 edition has xxvii + 348 pp. per its catalogue record, so p. 347 is its last page. This disagreement cannot be adjudicated without the 1778 text (see [What could not be reached](#what-could-not-be-reached)). **Until it is, attribute the "indifferent name" and "invincible scepticism" wording to the 1806 edition, not to 1778.**
- **Later Montpellier hardened.** Wolfe says the school was "extended into the early nineteenth century by figures such as Jacques Lordat, but on increasingly dogmatic, non-clinical bases". Lordat's "double dynamism" is a lead only and was not verified here.
- **Kant's reading of Blumenbach is contested.** Lenoir (1980) read the *Bildungstrieb* as "an organic version of a Newtonian force" shaped by Kant's regulative framework. Richards (2000) calls the claimed agreement "a historical misunderstanding": Blumenbach, he argues, treated the drive as a real force and ignored Kant's regulative/constitutive distinction. Richards's thesis was reached via secondary summaries only, not at source. Ginsborg (SEP 2022) writes that "Kant himself follows Blumenbach in assuming a distinctive kind of living matter which can be understood only in teleological terms."

**Verdict on Direction 2:** confirmed, in two grades.
1. **Mild scoping, applying to the whole functional catalogue.** "Without committing to any determinate feature a reduction had to preserve" is too strong. The Montpellier and Göttingen vitalists did commit to determinate features: named forces, laws, a taxonomy of life-forces, and organ-specific selectivities. But each feature was individuated by its effects: tone, contraction, secretion, regeneration, form-restoration. A reduction "had to preserve" them only in the sense that any explanation must preserve its explananda. Once mechanisms produced the effects, the features were discharged. The thesis therefore survives with one change of wording: from *no* determinate feature to features *specified only by their effects*. The "mechanism-shaped hole" image (L47, L79) is untouched.
2. **A real exception, which fair engagement must report.** Barthez's sensitive forces carried a *feeling* attribution: severed muscle "feels the stimulus", unreflectively. He defended the attribution with an argument structurally like the hard problem: feeling has nothing in common with motion, so no motion can be conceived to yield it. Physiology did not reduce this feeling to motion. It dropped the attribution, explaining contraction as irritability or excitability with no feeling required.
   - **Against the Map:** a vitalist ran a gap-form argument about feeling, and the phenomenon it protected vanished from science. That is a better exhibit for the track-record induction than Wöhler's urea.
   - **For the Map, framework-relative:** what was retired was a third-person *posit*, a feeling inferred from muscle behaviour to explain that behaviour. It went once the behaviour was explained without it. This is exactly Chalmers's distinction, which the vitalism page already quotes at L83: "Experience is not an explanatory posit but an explanandum in its own right". The gap argument's *conclusion* was never refuted. Its *premise*, that the tissue feels, was withdrawn. Nor was Barthez's attribution type-specific. He ascribed a generic capacity to feel a stimulus, not a determinate phenomenal quality. Bordeu, who did use a quality word ("goût"), disowned its literal reading in the same passage. The type-specificity contrast at L77 therefore survives.
   - **Grade:** this reply works for anyone who grants that human experience is a datum and not a posit. Against illusionism and heterophenomenology, it moves the dispute to that premise rather than winning it. That matches the open P3's planned narrowing of L79, as Dennett's parity demand bites exactly here. **The two edits should agree.**

## Kant's Middle Path (KU §§64–68, 75, 80–81)

All passages below were checked in the Akademie text (korpora.org, AA 5). English is quoted from Bernard (1914, public domain, archive.org full text). For why Guyer/Matthews and Pluhar are not used, see [What could not be reached](#what-could-not-be-reached).

1. **The criterion (§64, 5:370).** "ein Ding existirt als Naturzweck, wenn es von sich selbst (obgleich in zwiefachem Sinne) Ursache und Wirkung ist". The tree illustrates it three times over: it produces its species, it produces itself as an individual by growth, and its parts produce one another (5:371–372).
2. **Organisms are not machines (§65, 5:374).** "An organised being is then not a mere machine, for that has merely moving power, but it possesses in itself formative power" (Bernard pp. 278–279). German: "Ein organisirtes Wesen ist also nicht bloß Maschine: denn die hat lediglich bewegende Kraft; sondern es besitzt in sich bildende Kraft".
3. **Both vitalist options rejected (§65, 5:374–375).** Calling it an analogue of life ("Analogon des Lebens") forces a choice: "we must either endow matter, as mere matter, with a property which contradicts its very being (hylozoism), or associate therewith an alien principle standing in communion with it (a soul)". The soul option fails, as Direction 1 records. Kant concludes: "Genau zu reden, hat also die Organisation der Natur nichts Analogisches mit irgend einer Causalität, die wir kennen" (5:375).
4. **Regulative, not constitutive (§65, 5:375).** "The concept of a thing as in itself a natural purpose is therefore no constitutive concept of Understanding or of Reason, but it can serve as a regulative concept for the reflective Judgement, to guide our investigation about objects of this kind by a distant analogy with our own causality according to purposes generally" (Bernard p. 280).
5. **The maxim (§66, 5:376).** "ein organisirtes Product der Natur ist das, in welchem alles Zweck und wechselseitig auch Mittel ist. Nichts in ihm ist umsonst, zwecklos, oder einem blinden Naturmechanism zuzuschreiben." This is a maxim of judgment, "wenn es gleich bloß regulativ wäre".
6. **No special causality (§68, 5:383).** Quoted in Direction 1. The same section explains why mechanism remains the standard of insight (5:384): "denn nur soviel sieht man vollständig ein, als man nach Begriffen selbst machen und zu Stande bringen kann". This maker's-knowledge criterion is the ground of Kant's pessimism about organisms.
7. **The blade of grass (§75, 5:400).** "…it is absurd for men to make any such attempt or to hope that another Newton will arise in the future, who shall make comprehensible by us the production of a blade of grass according to natural laws which no design has ordered. We must absolutely deny this insight to men." Kant continues at once: "But then how do we know that in nature, if we could penetrate to the principle by which it specifies the universal laws known to us, there cannot lie hidden (in its mere mechanism) a sufficient ground of the possibility of organised beings without supposing any design in their production? would it not be judged by us presumptuous to say this?" (Bernard pp. 312–313). German: "denn woher wollen wir das wissen?" **The limit is on human comprehension. Kant expressly withholds the metaphysical verdict.**
8. **Mechanism's unlimited licence (§80, 5:417–418; not §78).** "The privilege of aiming at a merely mechanical method of explanation of all natural products is in itself quite unlimited; but the faculty of attaining thereto is by the constitution of our Understanding, so far as it has to do with things as natural purposes, not only very much limited but also clearly bounded." He continues: "It is therefore rational, even meritorious, to pursue natural mechanism … so far as can be done with probability; and if we give up the attempt it is not because it is impossible in itself to meet in this path with the purposiveness of nature, but only because it is impossible for us as men" (Bernard pp. 336–337).
9. **Blumenbach (§81, 5:424).** "As regards this theory of Epigenesis, no one has contributed more either to its proof or to the establishment of the legitimate principles of its application,—partly by the limitation of a too presumptuous employment of it,—than Herr Hofr. Blumenbach." Kant credits Blumenbach with leaving natural mechanism "einen unbestimmbaren, zugleich doch auch unverkennbaren Antheil" under the "uns unerforschlichen Princip einer ursprünglichen Organisation", and with naming the formative capacity "Bildungstrieb".

**The Haeckel verdict.** "Nun ist aber dieser unmögliche Newton siebenzig Jahre später in Darwin wirklich erschienen, und seine Selectionstheorie hat die Aufgabe thatsächlich gelöst, deren Lösung Kant für absolut undenkbar erklärt hatte!" (Haeckel 1868, p. 85, DTA).
- **Citation hazard:** Haeckel cites the passage as "§. 74" and the §80 heading as "§. 79", one behind the Akademie numbering. Cite AA 5:400, §75.
- **The verdict is contested.** Darwin explained the origin of adapted forms by selection, not the ontogenetic "production" of an individual blade of grass. Ginsborg (SEP 2022, §3.7) reports "reasons to think that contemporary biological theory is no less committed to teleology than its eighteenth-century counterpart" through function talk.
- **Fair reading:** Kant's in-principle limit has at least a partial falsifier on record, and Haeckel read it as wholly falsified. The Map should treat this as a caution about its *own* in-principle claims (Tenet 5 binds both ways, `tenets.md` L145). It should not explain it away.

**Later reception.** Lenoir's "teleomechanist" programme (1982) and "vital materialism" (1980) named the Göttingen school's attempt to combine Kantian regulative teleology with Newtonian-style vital forces. SEP "Teleological Notions in Biology" (Allen & Neal, rev. 2026-02-06) summarises it as a "teleo-mechanist strategy". Zammito reportedly argued in 2012 that the "Lenoir thesis" can no longer be the starting point (search summary only, not verified).

## Relation to Site Perspective

Grade every link **compatible at most**. Nothing in this history supports dualism, and the note must not be read as if it did.

- **Tenet 1 (Dualism).** Barthez's tripartite scheme kept "l'Ame pensante" apart from "le Principe de la Vie". So the Montpellier vital principle was never a theory of mind, and its retirement does not, even by its own authors' lights, touch the mind-principle. The point is historical, and it is *not* evidence for a non-physical mind: Barthez himself left open (ch. III §XLIV, p. 109) that future facts might show the two "essentiellement réunis dans un troisième Principe plus général". What it does show is that the vitalism-to-consciousness induction runs across a line the vitalists themselves drew. **Compatible.**
- **Tenet 5 (Occam's razor has limits) — binds both sides.** Kant's §75 limit and Haeckel's reply are the track-record induction in miniature. An in-principle explanatory limit, stated about *our* comprehension, was overtaken, or was read as overtaken, by a new *kind* of explanation that reframed the explanandum. The Map's type-specificity claim has the same logical form. The honest lesson is symmetric. Kant's own refusal to rule on nature ("woher wollen wir das wissen?") is the posture the Map already keeps at `concepts/vitalism` L87, where the disanalogy "names a feature of the explanandum, not a verdict on reducibility". Haeckel shows what happens to a limit claim stated without that restraint. **Compatible**; it supports the page's existing boundary clause, and it supplies the self-binding clause the optimistic review found missing at L93 (its Issue (b)).
- **Tenets 2–3.** No contact. Kant's teleology "is to introduce no particular ground of causality", so it offers nothing to a bidirectional-interaction or quantum-selection view. Do not recruit it. **Neutral.**
- **An open challenge to record, not resolve.** Kant's model is a principled middle path: anti-reductive about *explanation*, agnostic about *ontology*. The 07-13 note suggested it as "a useful analogue for how the Map treats consciousness-talk". A critic can ask why the Map does not take the regulative route for consciousness too. The Map's answer runs through the same premise as the Barthez exception: an organism's purposiveness is *judged* by an observer as if designed, whereas experience is *had*. That answer is framework-internal. The article should say so.
- **`topics/biological-teleology-and-the-interface-framework`.** Its two-tier claim (L102) is that teleology is "heuristic for most of biology but literal for conscious selection". This has a Kantian precedent the page does not mention. Kant judges natural purposes "by a distant analogy with our own causality according to purposes" (5:375), so our own purposive agency is the one case known from within, and organisms are judged by analogy with it. The page's "This dissolves the all-or-nothing framing" therefore overstates the novelty. The Map's departure from Kant is genuine but lies elsewhere: it treats the conscious case as a real causal contribution at the selection interface, and that rests on its dualism, not on Kant. **Compatible, as a structural parallel only.**
