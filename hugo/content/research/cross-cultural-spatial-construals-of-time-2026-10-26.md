## Executive Summary

Three well-known results show that speakers with the same body build different spatial construals of time. Aymara speakers place the past in front of the body and the future behind, in both speech and co-speech gesture (Núñez & Sweetser 2006). Yupno speakers of Papua New Guinea gesture the past downhill and the future uphill, an allocentric topographic axis that is not anchored to the body at all (Núñez, Cooperrider, Doan & Wassmann 2012). Mandarin speakers are reported to think of time vertically more readily than English speakers (Boroditsky 2001), but that result failed ten replication attempts across two 2007 papers before being reasserted with new measures in 2011, so it is contested rather than settled. For the Map the point is one of calibration on the cognition side of the embodiment page's cognition/consciousness partition: the body supplies the source domains for temporal metaphor (space, motion, vision) but underdetermines which mapping a community adopts, so the page's inference "different bodies produce different abstract concepts" cannot be read in reverse as "same body, same concepts". None of this bears on dualism, and the note enlists none of these authors for it.

## Fidelity answers to the driver's questions

### (a) Núñez & Sweetser 2006

- **Verified at**: Crossref (DOI 10.1207/s15516709cog0000_62; *Cognitive Science* 30(3): 401–450, 2006) and PubMed (PMID 21702821), both carrying the full abstract. Author affiliations in the PubMed record: UC San Diego (Núñez), UC Berkeley (Sweetser).
- **Both linguistic and gestural**: confirmed. Abstract: "The Aymara language instead has a major static model of time wherein FUTURE IS BEHIND EGO and PAST IS IN FRONT OF EGO; linguistic and gestural data give strong confirmation of this unusual culture-specific cognitive pattern." The abstract also states that "Gestural data provide crucial information unavailable to purely linguistic analysis".
- **The default it reverses**: the abstract calls the ego-reference-point generalisation "FUTURE IS IN FRONT OF EGO and PAST IS IN BACK OF EGO" and describes dynamic models as "quasi-universal cross-culturally". The Aymara model is explicitly a *static* model; the paper's contrast is between ego-reference-point and temporal-reference-point models, which the fold does not need.
- **Population**: Aymara speakers of the Andes. The abstract does not name the country; the paper's fieldwork locale (northern Chile) is from the body text, which I could not reach today. The fold should say "Aymara speakers" or "Aymara speakers of the Andes" and nothing more specific.
- **Unique?** No, and the Map must not say so. Press coverage of the paper (Newswise, 2006) called it the first *documented* case, but a past-in-front construal was already described for Malagasy by Dahl (1995, *International Journal of Intercultural Relations* 19(2): 197–209, DOI 10.1016/0147-1767(95)00004-u, Crossref-verified; title "When the future comes from behind"). Later, de la Fuente, Santiago, Román, Dumitrache & Casasanto (2014, *Psychological Science* 25(9): 1682–1690, DOI 10.1177/0956797614534695, Crossref abstract) found that "Arabic speakers tend to conceptualize the future as behind and the past as ahead of them, despite using spoken metaphors that suggest the opposite". The 2014 result matters for the Map's framing: the mapping can vary independently of the language's own metaphors, which keeps the fold from collapsing into linguistic relativity.
- **"Known past / unknown future" gloss**: the abstract does not use "known" or "unknown". The visual-field motivation (Aymara *nayra* = eye, front, and past) is from the paper's body and the press summaries. The review's NEW text uses "the known past in front and the unknown future behind"; see the amendment below.

### (b) Boroditsky 2001 and its replication record

- **Verified at**: Crossref (DOI 10.1006/cogp.2001.0748; *Cognitive Psychology* 43(1): 1–22), PubMed (PMID 11487292), and the author-hosted full text. The abstract's claim, verbatim from the extracted PDF: "In one study, Mandarin speakers tended to think about time vertically even when they were thinking for English (Mandarin speakers were faster to confirm that March comes earlier than April if they had just seen a vertical array of objects than if they had just seen a horizontal array, and the reverse was true for English speakers)." The paper's own conclusion is hedged against strong Whorfianism: one's native language "plays an important role in shaping habitual thought (e.g., how one tends to think about time) but does not entirely determine one's thinking in the strong Whorfian sense."
- **Chen 2007** (*Cognition* 104(2): 427–436, DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2006.09.012, PMID 17070793): "We were also unable to replicate her experiments in four different attempts." Chen also reports a corpus finding that Chinese speakers use horizontal temporal metaphors more often than vertical ones.
- **January & Kako 2007** (*Cognition* 104(2): 417–426, DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2006.07.008, PMID 16914131): "Six unsuccessful attempts at replicating a key finding in the linguistic relativity literature ... are reported." Their conclusion: "Boroditsky (2001) provides no support for the Whorfian hypothesis."
- **Boroditsky, Fuhrman & McCormick 2011** (*Cognition* 118(1): 123–129, DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2010.09.010, PMID 21030013): "We review all of the available evidence both for and against this hypothesis, and report new data that further support and refine it. ... As predicted by patterns in language, Mandarin speakers are more likely than English speakers to think about time vertically (with earlier time-points above and later time-points below)." The abstract does not name the tasks; the paper's body (not reached today) uses non-linguistic spatial-priming and arrangement measures rather than the 2001 sentence-verification design.
- **Status for the Map**: ten failed replications of the original paradigm (4 + 6) against a 2011 reassertion by different measures. The Map must present the vertical effect as contested, and the fold should not carry it at all. If any page cites it, cite the 2011 paper for the claim and the two 2007 papers for the failures in the same sentence.

### (c) Núñez, Cooperrider, Doan & Wassmann 2012 (Yupno)

- **Verified at**: Crossref (DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2012.03.007; *Cognition* 124(1): 25–35) and PubMed (PMID 22542697, full abstract).
- **Detail confirmed**: "We measured the pointing direction of Yupno speakers' gestures—produced naturally and without prompting—as they explained common expressions related to the past, present, and future. Results show that the Yupno spontaneously construe deictic time spatially in terms of allocentric topography: the past is construed as downhill, the present as co-located with the speaker, and the future as uphill. Moreover, the Yupno construal is not linear, but exhibits a particular geometry that appears to reflect the local terrain."
- **The sentence the fold is built on**: "The findings shed light on how, our universal human embodiment notwithstanding, linguistic, cultural, and environmental pressures come to shape abstract concepts." This is the authors' own statement that a shared body underdetermines the construal; the fold may paraphrase it or quote the phrase "our universal human embodiment notwithstanding".
- **Indoor/turning detail**: not in the abstract; I did not reach the body text, so the fold should not claim that the axis rotates with the speaker's heading or that an indoor variant exists.
- **Follow-up**: Cooperrider, Slotta & Núñez 2022 (*Language and Cognition* 14(1): 131–159, DOI 10.1017/langcog.2021.25, Crossref abstract): "some communities instead understand time using environment-based contrasts ... the topographic system used by the Yupno of Papua New Guinea, in which the past is construed as downhill and the future as uphill." The abstract contrasts "body-based contrasts like front/back and left/right" with "environment-based contrasts", which is exactly the distinction the fold needs: the Yupno case varies the *anchor* of the axis, where Aymara varies only its *polarity*.

### (d) Synthesis sources

- **Núñez & Cooperrider 2013** (*Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 17(5): 220–229, DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2013.03.008, PMID 23608363). Abstract verified: "In culture after culture, converging evidence from language, co-speech gesture, and behavioral tasks suggests that humans handle these elusive yet indispensable notions by construing them spatially." and "Time is not a monolith, but rather a mosaic of construals with distinct properties and origins." What is universal on their account is construing time spatially at all; what varies is the particular construal (axis, polarity, anchor). The abstract does not itself say what the body fixes; the Yupno abstract sentence above is the cleaner source for that.
- **Bender & Beller 2014** (*Cognition* 132(3): 342–382, DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2014.03.016, PMID 24873738). Abstract verified: reviews "eight such accounts" of temporal frames of reference against "data from some thirty empirical studies, conducted with speakers of sixteen different languages", and notes that a taxonomy "is far from reaching consensus". Use it only to establish the breadth of attested variation; its specific case discussions were not reached.
- **Casasanto & Boroditsky 2008** (*Cognition* 106(2): 579–593, DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2007.03.004, PMID 17509553). Abstract verified: "people are unable to ignore irrelevant spatial information when making judgments about duration, but not the converse", in "tasks that do not involve any linguistic stimuli or responses". This supports the *source-domain* half of the Map's claim (time is built on spatial representation, pre-linguistically) and does not bear on which mapping is adopted. It is worth one clause only if the fold wants to name why the body still matters; the recommended fold does without it.

## Major positions

### Conceptual metaphor theory (Lakoff & Johnson 1980, 1999; Lakoff & Núñez 2000)
- **Core claim**: abstract domains are structured by mappings from bodily source domains; time is structured by space and motion.
- **Relation to the cross-cultural data**: the data are usually presented by Núñez himself as *refining* embodiment, not refuting it: the source domain (space) is universal, the mapping is cultural. The Map's current L85 reads the theory in one direction only.
- **Relation to tenets**: the research sits on the cognition side of the page's partition. It says nothing about whether phenomenal experience is embodied and should not be made to.

### Linguistic relativity readings (Boroditsky 2001, 2011)
- **Core claim**: the language's habitual metaphors shape habitual thought about time.
- **State**: contested for the Mandarin case (see (b)). De la Fuente et al. 2014 is the strongest evidence that the mapping can come apart from the language's metaphors, which cuts against a purely linguistic account and toward cultural attitude (temporal focus).
- **Relation to the Map**: this is the angle [language-thought-boundary](/voids/language-thought-boundary/) would carry, and that page is at its hard gate (see "Where it goes"). The embodiment page should keep the framing on the body's underdetermination and should not state a Whorfian thesis.

### Environment-anchored construals (Núñez et al. 2012; Cooperrider, Slotta & Núñez 2022)
- **Core claim**: some communities construe time on an allocentric, terrain-based axis rather than any body axis.
- **Relation to the Map**: strongest single datum for underdetermination, since it varies the anchor, not just the polarity. The handedness research note already recorded the analogous spatial point (left/right frames are not universal): [voids-handedness-void-2026-09-28](/research/voids-handedness-void-2026-09-28/) L134.

## Key debate

### Does a shared body fix the mapping?
- **Sides**: a strong embodiment reading predicts convergence from shared anatomy (front-facing eyes, forward locomotion); the cross-cultural record shows polarity reversal (Aymara, Malagasy, Moroccan Arabic speakers in de la Fuente et al.), anchor replacement (Yupno), and possibly axis addition (Mandarin, contested).
- **Current state**: the field's own synthesis (Núñez & Cooperrider 2013) treats spatialisation of time as near-universal and the specific construal as a mosaic. Nobody in this literature argues that the body fixes the mapping; the Map's L85, read strictly, implied that it did.
