## Executive Summary

Involuntary autobiographical memories (IAMs) are memories of personal events that come to mind "with no preceding attempt at retrieval" (Berntsen 2010). They are not rare: Berntsen (2010) reports them "about as frequent in daily life as are voluntary autobiographical memories" (a survey result, Rubin & Berntsen 2009), and a one-day counter study found them reported **three times** as often (Rasmussen & Berntsen 2011). The dominant account (Berntsen's "Basic Mechanisms View") holds that both kinds run on one episodic system and differ only at retrieval: voluntary recall is goal-directed and executive-monitored, involuntary recall is associative, cue-driven and low in executive control. The boundary is blurrier than the voluntary/involuntary labels suggest, in three directions: (1) *voluntary* retrieval is itself largely cue-driven and "direct" (Uzer, Lee & Brown 2012), and involuntary memories chain inside voluntary recall tasks (Mace 2006); (2) participants classify most memories as involuntary even in the experimentally voluntary condition, apparently tracking felt effort rather than intention (Barzykowski, Staugaard & Mazzoni 2021); (3) philosophically, Mole (2017) argues voluntariness is not a fact about how a recollection is *initiated* but about whether the subject controls the episode's *temporal development*. That third point is what matters for the Map: involuntary recall threatens any claim that retrieval is uniformly an act of attentional selection, but it leaves intact — and arguably sharpens — the Map's more defensible claim that conscious agency operates as guidance, sustaining and rejection over content that arrives unbidden. **Coverage check**: the corpus touches IAMs in six places but nowhere treats the boundary or its agency implications; one of those touches (agency-void) misattributes a frequency claim (see Gaps / defects).

## Key Sources

### Berntsen (2010), "The Unbidden Past: Involuntary Autobiographical Memories as a Basic Mode of Remembering"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721410370301 (author's manuscript: https://pure.au.dk/ws/files/71800737/Berntsen_2010_.pdf)
- **Type**: Review paper, *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 19(3): 138–142. Metadata verified at Crossref. Text read in full.
- **Key points**:
  - Four central claims (Table 1): **universality**; **frequency** — "about as frequent in daily life as are voluntary autobiographical memories"; **system** — "operate on the same episodic memory system as do voluntary memories: They share the same basic encoding and maintenance mechanisms, but differ on retrieval"; **functionality**.
  - Typical diary estimates: "two to five involuntary autobiographical memories per day".
  - Mechanism: "Voluntary recall is a goal-directed process that requires executive functions to monitor the search process, whereas involuntary recall is an associative process -- instigated by situational cues -- that take place with little executive control and therefore relies less on frontal lobe structures".
  - Involuntary memories are more specific, less life-story-relevant, with more emotional reaction and mood impact; voluntary recall "is a top-down search" that is "hampered" at accessing schema-deviant material.
  - Ebbinghaus (1885) named three modes: voluntary, involuntary, non-conscious. Modern psychology studied the first and third and "largely ignor[ed] the category of involuntary, but nonetheless conscious, memories".
  - Functional proposal: without the involuntary mode we would be confined to "living in the present"; it lets us "rehearse lessons from the past ... in relatively effortless ways".
  - Speculation that involuntary remembering "may also take place in non-human species" (because it relies on simple association and less frontal involvement).
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral on dualism. Significant for Bidirectional Interaction: it cleanly separates *conscious* retrieval from *willed* retrieval — a conscious, phenomenally rich memory episode with no act behind it.
- **Quote**: "involuntary, but nonetheless conscious, memories"
- **Metadata note**: the manuscript's reference list gives Hall, Gjedde & Kupers (2008) as *Behavioural Brain Research* vol. **25**; Crossref gives vol. **186**(2): 261–272. Use 186.

### Rasmussen & Berntsen (2011), "The unpredictable past: Spontaneous autobiographical memories outnumber autobiographical memories retrieved strategically"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2011.07.010
- **Type**: Empirical paper, *Consciousness and Cognition* 20(4): 1842–1846. Metadata at Crossref; abstract via OpenAlex.
- **Key points**: 48 Danish undergraduates, one day, mechanical counter. "Involuntary memories were reported three times as frequently as voluntary memories." Involuntary memories were linked less with problem solving and social sharing, more with daydreaming, boredom and "no reasons for remembering", and "predominantly came to mind during unfocused attention."
- **Caveat**: small single-day sample; Rubin & Berntsen (2009) large survey found parity. Cite as a range (roughly parity to 3:1), not a fixed ratio.
- **Tenet alignment**: Bears on Bidirectional Interaction (below).

### Rubin & Berntsen (2009), "The frequency of voluntary and involuntary autobiographical memories across the life span"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.3758/37.5.679
- **Type**: Survey study, *Memory & Cognition* 37(5): 679–688. Metadata at Crossref; content known only through Berntsen (2010)'s description (1975 Danes, stratified sample, parity of reported frequency). Abstract not read.

### Berntsen (2021), "Involuntary autobiographical memories and their relation to other forms of spontaneous thoughts"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2019.0693
- **Type**: Review, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 376(1817): 20190693. Metadata at Crossref; abstract via OpenAlex (publisher page 403).
- **Key points**: IAMs are "a context-sensitive, and associative way of recollecting past events that involves little executive control"; they favour recent events and those with "distinct feature overlap to the current situation"; documented "in young children and great apes and may be an ontogenetic and evolutionary forerunner of strategic retrieval"; clinical intrusions are "a dysfunctional subclass of otherwise functional" IAMs; because of their "highly constrained, situation-dependent and automatic nature", IAMs "form a distinct category of spontaneous thought that cannot be equated with mind wandering."
- **Tenet alignment**: The "forerunner of strategic retrieval" claim matters: on this picture, voluntary retrieval is the later add-on, not the default.

### Barzykowski & Staugaard (2016), "Does retrieval intentionality really matter? Similarities and differences between involuntary memories and directly and generatively retrieved voluntary memories"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1111/bjop.12160
- **Type**: Empirical, *British Journal of Psychology* 107(3): 519–536. Crossref + OpenAlex abstract.
- **Key points**: Distinguishes **intention** (wanting to remember) from **effort** (trying to remember), yielding three retrieval types: involuntary (no intention, no effort), direct voluntary (intention, no effort), generative voluntary (intention and effort). "involuntary memories and directly retrieved memories have been used interchangeably in the literature". Result: "retrieval intention seems to differentiate how a memory appears in a person's mind."
- **Tenet alignment**: Supplies the precise vocabulary the Map needs. The Map's agency story attaches to *effort* ([mental-effort](/concepts/mental-effort/)); this paper shows intention and effort come apart.

### Barzykowski, Staugaard & Mazzoni (2021), "Retrieval effort or intention: Which is more important for participants' classification of involuntary and voluntary memories?"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1111/bjop.12498
- **Type**: Empirical, *British Journal of Psychology* 112(4): 1080–1102. Crossref + OpenAlex abstract.
- **Key points**: "the majority of memories were classified as involuntary independently of whether they were retrieved in the experimentally defined voluntary or involuntary condition. This could indicate that subjective effort is more important than intention for the involuntary-voluntary distinction in contrast with existing theories."
- **Tenet alignment**: Double-edged. First-person classification tracks felt effort, which supports the Map's claim that effort is the phenomenologically salient mark of agency. But it also means introspective reports of "I deliberately recalled X" are poor evidence about intention-driven retrieval.

### Uzer, Lee & Brown (2012), "On the prevalence of directly retrieved autobiographical memories"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028142
- **Type**: Empirical, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 38(5): 1296–1308. Crossref + OpenAlex abstract.
- **Key points**: Under word-cued (i.e. intentional) recall, "directly recalled memories are much faster and more frequent than generated memories"; the authors argue "for a dual-strategies approach that disputes generation as the canonical form of autobiographical memory retrieval."
- **Tenet alignment**: The main empirical support for the **rival reading**: even voluntary retrieval is mostly cue-triggered and effortless. Intention sets the cue; the memory arrives.

### Mace (2006), "Episodic remembering creates access to involuntary conscious memory: Demonstrating involuntary recall on a voluntary recall task"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1080/09658210600759766
- **Type**: Empirical, *Memory* 14(8): 917–924. Crossref + OpenAlex abstract.
- **Key points**: The contents of one involuntary memory can trigger further involuntary memories ("chaining"). The study showed "involuntary memories did occur when participants were recalling the past", i.e. inside a voluntary recall task, dissociating on two independent measures. The idea is traced to Ebbinghaus.
- **Related**: Mace (2004), *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 18(7): 893–899, https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1020 — diary study: IAMs "more likely to be elicited by abstract types of cues than sensory/perceptual cues, thus indicating that the popular Proustian view is incorrect"; the abstract-cue rate "suggests that involuntary memory retrieval shares some common features with voluntary memory retrieval." **Title note**: the published subtitle is "the Proustian view is *incorrect*" (not "incomplete", as the search query guessed).
- **Tenet alignment**: Voluntary episodes are internally mixed, so the boundary runs *within* a single remembering episode, not between episodes.

### Hall et al. (2014), "The Neural Basis of Involuntary Episodic Memories"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn_a_00633
- **Type**: fMRI study, *Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience* 26(10): 2385–2399. Authors: S. A. Hall, D. C. Rubin, A. Miles, S. W. Davis, E. A. Wing, R. Cabeza, D. Berntsen. Crossref + OpenAlex abstract.
- **Key points**: "voluntary memories elicited greater activity in dorsal frontal regions than involuntary memories, whereas other components of the retrieval network, including medial-temporal, ventral occipitotemporal, and ventral parietal regions were similarly engaged by both types". Concludes involuntary memories "share neural components with established voluntary memory systems". Earlier PET precedent: Hall, Gjedde & Kupers (2008), *Behavioural Brain Research* 186(2): 261–272, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bbr.2007.08.026 (metadata only).
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The frontal difference is where "control" shows up; it says nothing about whether control is physically closed.

### Kobelt, Waldhauser, Fellner & Axmacher (2025), "Involuntary and voluntary memory retrieval relies on distinct neural representations and oscillatory processes"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3003258
- **Type**: EEG study, *PLOS Biology* 23(8): e3003258. Crossref + OpenAlex abstract.
- **Key points**: Involuntary retrieval "reactivated sensory feature-dependent yet item-unspecific representations via temporally extended memory replay, accompanied by rapid mid-frontal theta-power increases, indicating memory interference"; voluntary retrieval "recruited goal-directed memory search processes in prefrontal-medial temporal lobe theta-bands to reactivate temporally compressed item-specific representations". Conclusion: involuntary memories "rely on distinct neural processes that access different representational formats".
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. It is the most recent evidence **against** Mole's *maximal* Basic Mechanisms View (no mechanism specific to involuntary retrieval), though a Mole-style reply ("a discernible difference is not a difference of mechanism") remains available. Current-literature marker: the debate is live in 2025.
- **Caveat**: laboratory paradigm with picture/visual-field items, not naturalistic autobiographical memory.

### Staugaard & Berntsen (2019), "Retrieval intentionality and forgetting: How retention time and cue distinctiveness affect involuntary and voluntary retrieval of episodic memories"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-019-00904-w
- **Type**: Empirical, *Memory & Cognition* 47(5): 893–905. Crossref + OpenAlex abstract.
- **Key points**: "At the shortest retention interval, voluntary retrieval led to greater memory access than involuntary retrieval, but after one week, this advantage had disappeared. The findings suggest that memories for events become more cue-dependent over time, which limits the beneficial effects of strategic search".
- **Tenet alignment**: What strategic search adds is real but decays; over time retrieval collapses toward cue-dependence whatever the intention. Relevant to any claim that deliberate search is an efficacious causal contribution (it is, briefly and measurably, then fades).

### Barzykowski & Moulin (2023), "Are involuntary autobiographical memory and déjà vu natural products of memory retrieval?"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1017/s0140525x22002035
- **Type**: BBS target article, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 46: e356, with open peer commentary. Crossref metadata; content via search-result snippets only (abstract not read raw).
- **Key points (snippet-level, verify before quoting)**: IAMs and déjà vu both "natural phenomena resulting from memory processing" based on "the same memory retrieval processes"; IAMs deliver content, déjà vu delivers only the feeling of retrieval.

### Mole (2017), "Are there Special Mechanisms of Involuntary Memory?"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/s13164-016-0326-z (open access; full text read via Europe PMC PMC5561155)
- **Type**: Philosophy paper, *Review of Philosophy and Psychology* 8(3): 557–571. (Online 2016; issue 2017.) Author: Christopher Mole (UBC).
- **Key points**:
  - Defends Berntsen's Basic Mechanisms View against evidence of encoding differences (Clark et al. 2014, SVM decoding) and consolidation interventions (sleep deprivation, Porcheret et al. 2015; Tetris, Holmes et al. 2009/2010): "the existence of a discernable difference does not, by itself, entail a difference of mechanism".
  - Argues for a **maximal** version: "involuntary memory involves no mechanism that is not also involved in voluntary memory".
  - The obstacle is Berntsen's "philosophically contentious conception of voluntariness" — that voluntariness is fixed by whether retrieval is *activated* by a "preceding goal-directed search" or "search description".
  - **Two counterexamples**: (a) while voluntarily reminiscing about school, an intrusive traumatic memory of a sadistic schoolmaster arrives — "experienced as involuntary, even if the conscious entertainment of a search description was the cause of their occurrence"; (b) passing a bakery, the smell prompts recollection of a request to buy bread — voluntary, though not preceded by any retrieval attempt, and "it would be to embark on a regress to suppose that voluntariness always requires that a prior attempt be voluntarily made."
  - Positive account (citing Frankfurt 1978): voluntary episodes "qualify as being voluntary because the subject of the recollection is in control of the larger-scale conduct to which that recollection contributes"; the difference is "a difference as to how the mental episodes to which they contribute are controlled, over the course of their temporal development" — not how they are activated.
  - Clinical corollary: if the maximal view is true, a "cognitive vaccine against traumatic flashbacks" that spares voluntary memory has no dedicated mechanism to target.
- **Tenet alignment**: The most useful source for the Map. It moves the locus of mnemonic agency from *initiation* to *ongoing control* — which is where the Map's attention-and-effort account already puts agency (sustaining, guiding, rejecting). It dissolves the apparent threat from IAM frequency.
- **Quote**: "a difference as to how the mental episodes to which they contribute are controlled, over the course of their temporal development"

### Strawson (2003), "Mental Ballistics or the Involuntariness of Spontaneity"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0066-7372.2003.00071.x
- **Type**: Philosophy paper, *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 103(3): 227–256. (Crossref holds two DOIs for this paper; the other is 10.1111/1467-9264.00135. Reprinted in Strawson, *Real Materialism*, OUP 2008, pp. 233–254.) Primary text **not read**.

Quoted by Arango-Muñoz & Bermúdez: "the role of genuine action in thought is at best indirect. It is entirely prefatory, it is essentially—merely—catalytic" (2003, p. 236).
- **Verification status**: quote relayed through Arango-Muñoz & Bermúdez's Minds Online version, itself read through a WebFetch summariser. **Treat as UNVERIFIED verbatim** until checked against the primary text.
- **Tenet alignment**: The strongest philosophical form of the rival reading. If all retrieval is ballistic, there is no mnemonic agency to be involuntary *about*; the voluntary/involuntary line would mark only whether a catalytic prelude occurred.

### Arango-Muñoz & Bermúdez (2018), "Remembering as a Mental Action"

## Major Positions

### Basic Mechanisms View (Berntsen)
- **Proponents**: Berntsen; Rubin; defended philosophically (in maximal form) by Mole.
- **Core claim**: One episodic system. Involuntary and voluntary memories share encoding and maintenance and differ at retrieval: associative/cue-driven vs. strategic/executive-monitored.
- **Key arguments**: parallel forgetting curves, reminiscence bumps, childhood amnesia, emotional enhancement, and positivity bias across both types (Berntsen 2010); shared retrieval network (Hall et al. 2014); PTSD intrusions follow the same pattern as voluntary trauma memories (Rubin, Boals & Berntsen 2008, via Berntsen 2010).
- **Relation to site tenets**: Compatible. The single-system picture makes the voluntary/involuntary distinction a matter of *how the system is engaged*. That leaves room for conscious engagement to differ between the two modes without positing a separate faculty.

### Special Mechanisms View
- **Proponents**: implicit in clinical "cognitive vaccine" research (Holmes et al.) and dual-representation PTSD theories (Ehlers & Clark 2000 and successors, via Berntsen 2010); Kobelt et al. (2025) on representational format.
- **Core claim**: Involuntary memories involve encoding, consolidation or retrieval mechanisms not used in voluntary memory.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral. Neither view settles whether the executive difference is physically closed.

### Intention/effort decomposition (Barzykowski, Staugaard, Mazzoni)
- **Core claim**: "Voluntary" bundles two dissociable factors, intention and effort. Direct voluntary retrieval (intention, no effort) is common and is phenomenologically close to involuntary retrieval; lay classification tracks effort.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Helpful. The Map's interface claims attach to effortful attentional control ([mental-effort](/concepts/mental-effort/)); they need not claim that every intended retrieval is effortful, or that every retrieval is intended.

### Cue-driven-all-the-way-down (rival reading)
- **Proponents**: empirical — Uzer, Lee & Brown (2012), Mace (2004, 2006), Staugaard & Berntsen (2019); philosophical — Strawson (2003).
- **Core claim**: Voluntary retrieval is also cue-driven. Intention at most supplies or holds a cue ("search description"), and content arrives by the same associative process that yields IAMs. The voluntary/involuntary line marks the *source of the cue* (self-generated vs. environmental), not a difference in the retrieval act.
- **Relation to site tenets**: The serious challenge. It does not refute Bidirectional Interaction, but it does rule out locating conscious causation in "selecting which memory to access" as a general claim. It pushes the Map's locus of mnemonic agency to (a) cue setting and holding, (b) sustaining a search against drift, (c) accepting or rejecting what arrives (metacognitive monitoring), and (d) controlling where the episode goes next.

### Control-over-development account (Mole; Arango-Muñoz & Bermúdez)
- **Core claim**: A recollection is voluntary when the subject controls the larger episode it belongs to. Initiation is irrelevant: intrusions can follow deliberate search, and voluntary recollections can be triggered by a bakery smell. Remembering is a mental action when metacognitive feelings monitor and guide it.
- **Relation to site tenets**: The best fit for the Map. It agrees with the Map's existing framing of attention as sustaining and steering ([attention-as-interface](/concepts/attention-as-interface/), [mental-effort](/concepts/mental-effort/)), and with [spontaneous-intentional-action](/concepts/spontaneous-intentional-action/) (intentional without prior deliberation). **Caution**: the account is a compatibilist-friendly analysis of control; it does not by itself support non-physical causation. The Map may adopt its *locus* claim without implying Mole endorses the Map's metaphysics.

## Key Debates

### Is the difference one of mechanism?
- **Sides**: Berntsen (retrieval-only difference) and Mole (no difference of mechanism at all) vs. encoding/consolidation-difference evidence (Clark et al. 2014; Holmes et al. 2009; Porcheret et al. 2015) and representational-format evidence (Kobelt et al. 2025).
- **Core disagreement**: Whether a detectable neural difference amounts to a distinct mechanism.
- **Current state**: Ongoing. The 2025 EEG result is the latest push toward distinct processes.

### What makes a memory voluntary: intention, effort, or control?
- **Sides**: Berntsen's operational definition (no preceding retrieval attempt, i.e. intention at initiation); Barzykowski et al. (intention vs. effort; lay reports track effort); Mole (control over temporal development).
- **Current state**: Unresolved. Methodologically important because diary and lab classification depends on it.

### Is remembering a mental action at all?
- **Sides**: Strawson (content-arrival is ballistic; action is merely prefatory) vs. Arango-Muñoz & Bermúdez (metacognitively controlled reconstruction is action); Michaelian's simulationism is in the background (remembering as a kind of imagining, which raises the same agency question for imagination).
- **Current state**: Live in philosophy of memory; largely disconnected from the IAM empirical literature. **That disconnection is the Map's opening**: no source found puts the Mole/Strawson question together with the Rasmussen–Berntsen frequency data and the Barzykowski intention/effort dissociation.

### Functional or failure?
- **Sides**: Berntsen (IAMs functional, intrusions a dysfunctional subclass) vs. a BBS commentary on Barzykowski & Moulin arguing IAMs and déjà vu are "cognitive failures" and "there is no hard evidence supporting the idea that IAMs are functional".

## Potential Article Angles

1. **`concepts/involuntary-memory` (or `concepts/involuntary-autobiographical-memory`)**: a concept article in the target section. Lead with the definition, the frequency range (parity to 3:1; *not* 2:1), and the one-system view, then build to the agency question. **The Map's claim**: involuntary recall shows that conscious retrieval is not uniformly an act. Mnemonic agency lives in the control of an unfolding episode (Mole), in effortful sustaining (Barzykowski's effort factor, [mental-effort](/concepts/mental-effort/)), and in metacognitive acceptance or rejection (Arango-Muñoz & Bermúdez), not in initiation. Relation to Site Perspective: *Dualism* — IAMs are conscious without being willed, so phenomenality and agency come apart and the explanatory target is phenomenality itself. *Bidirectional Interaction* — the Map's causal claim survives only in its guidance/sustaining form, and the article should say plainly that the "attention selects which memory" form fails. *Occam's Razor Has Limits* — the Basic/Special mechanisms dispute is itself a parsimony argument (Mole explicitly), a good worked case.
2. **Alternative angle, "The Ballistic Objection to Mnemonic Agency"**: organise around Strawson vs. Mole/Arango-Muñoz & Bermúdez, with the IAM literature as the empirical test. More philosophical, better suited to `topics/`, but topics headroom is tighter; check before choosing.
3. **Cheaper alternative (no new article)**: refine-draft insertions into `concepts/episodic-memory` (put Berntsen 2010 to work in the body) and `topics/consciousness-and-memory` (correct the "attention selects" sentence), plus the agency-void citation fix. A reasonable fallback if the queue prefers not to spend a concepts slot.

Follow `obsidian/project/writing-style.md`: front-load the definition and the agency conclusion, and use named-anchor forward references for the Mole account. Avoid the "This is not X. It is Y." construct, which the subject invites ("retrieval is not an act; it is…").

## Defects Found in Live Content (for the driver to route; not fixed here)

1. **`voids/agency-void`**, Intrusion paragraph: "Berntsen (2010) finds involuntary autobiographical memories outnumber deliberately retrieved ones roughly 2:1." Berntsen (2010) says "about as frequent" (Rubin & Berntsen 2009 survey). The "outnumber" finding is Rasmussen & Berntsen (2011, *Consciousness and Cognition* 20(4): 1842–1846), at **three times**, from a one-day, n=48 counter study. Fix: re-attribute and correct the ratio, or state the range. Check the Hugo mirror too.
2. **`topics/consciousness-and-memory`** (~line 122, reconsolidation paragraph): "when waking retrieval does reopen a trace, it does so through a conscious act—attention selects which memory to access". This is false as a general claim, since IAMs are frequent and arise "predominantly … during unfocused attention" (Rasmussen & Berntsen 2011). Needs a scope limit ("when retrieval is deliberate…") or reframing to guidance and sustaining.
3. **`concepts/semantic-memory`** (MQI paragraph): locating conscious *selection* at the moment a TOT resolution "pops" into awareness picks the most involuntary point in the retrieval episode (Strawson's ballistic moment). Softer issue: a framing risk, not a factual error. The adjacent Bidirectional paragraph, which puts agency in *effortful search*, is the defensible version.
4. **`concepts/episodic-memory`**: Berntsen (2010) listed but uncited in the body (the original harvest finding). Either use it or drop it.

- **Barzykowski & Moulin (2023) BBS** abstract not read raw; claims above are search-snippet level.
- **Rubin & Berntsen (2009)**, **Kvavilashvili & Mandler (2004)**, **Martin-Ordas et al. (2013)**, **Barzykowski et al. (2019)**: metadata only.
- Mace et al. (2025) *BJP* ADHD paper: author list and volume/pages not verified.
- No source found that tests whether *effortful* sustaining of a voluntary search changes what is retrieved in ways cue-only models cannot capture. That is the empirical question the Map's guidance claim would need, and it seems to be open.
- Reconsolidation of *involuntarily* reactivated traces (Holmes/James "reactivation + Tetris" work) not researched. It bears directly on defect 2, since if involuntary reactivation also opens traces, the "conscious act" condition is wrong on mechanism too.
- Michaelian's simulationism and the imagination/agency literature were only touched on.
- Mind-wandering/spontaneous-thought frameworks (Christoff et al. 2016 dynamic framework) not consulted, beyond Berntsen (2021)'s claim that IAMs are distinct from mind-wandering.
