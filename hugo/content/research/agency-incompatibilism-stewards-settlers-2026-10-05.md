# Research: Agency Incompatibilism and Steward's Settlers

**Date**: 2026-10-05
**Intended slug**: `agency-incompatibilism` (target `topics/`)
**Search queries used**: Steward "A Metaphysics for Freedom" Inquiry symposium 2013 Responses; Steward "A Metaphysics for Freedom" review settling animals luck objection; Crossref listings for *Inquiry* 56 (2013) and *Res Philosophica* 91(3) (2014); OpenAlex abstracts by DOI; Google Books search-within on volume `ifPSU80vRQ4C` (about 40 phrase queries).

## Executive Summary

Helen Steward's agency incompatibilism holds that agency as such, the power many animals have to settle matters at the time of action, cannot exist in a deterministic world. Her settler is a whole physical animal acting top-down on its own parts, and she says so in terms that leave no room for the Map's posit. She agrees with the Map about who settles and that microphysical indeterminism is needed but insufficient. She disagrees about what the settler is, about whether consciousness is what makes an act the agent's, and about how far down the animal kingdom settling goes. On luck she concedes more than the Map does, then relocates the agent's contribution to timing, manner and (in a 2013 revision) the deployment of care and attention. Two journal symposia exist, and in both she concedes that her anti-compatibilist argument reaches only the causal theory of action. On sourcehood and luck the Map's non-physical settler adds nothing she lacks. What it may add lies elsewhere: a cheaper route to non-reduction and an answer to the subjectivity condition she builds into agency and leaves to biology.

## Key Sources

### Steward, *A Metaphysics for Freedom* (OUP 2012)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199552054.001.0001
- **Type**: Book (snippets and abstracts)
- **Key points**:
  - OUP abstract: she "offers a non-dualistic version of libertarianism, rooted in a conception of what biological forms of organisation might make possible in the way of freedom."
  - Chapter 6 abstract: on Mele's luck problem "the libertarian should make an important concession to the compatibilist".
  - Chapter 8 abstract: there is "a very difficult issue" about how causation by an animal "could fail to reduce to causation of effects by the parts of that same animal", and "the key to the solution of this problem may lie with the idea of coincidence."
  - p. 16 (the index lists it as "16 n. 37", so probably a footnote): the "dualism on which I rely here needs to be sharply distinguished from Cartesian dualism"; that animals are not identical with their bodies "has no tendency whatever to imply that animals consist of an immaterial soul together with a material body."
  - p. 11: "quantum indeterminacy, by itself, is inadequate to make the problem disappear", with the parenthesis "(though it will, I think, still prove to be necessary)".
  - p. 91: it is "quite likely, as John Searle has argued, that consciousness will turn out to be essentially a biological phenomenon, as will agency".
  - p. 47: "there is nothing in the Libet experiments to trouble my view of agency".
  - p. 131: the libertarian "ought not to fear the conclusion that, on this understanding, it can indeed be said to be a matter of luck that Joe decides to move in with his girlfriend rather than not".
  - p. 141 states the residual worry: luck "in the much more troubling sense that it does not seem to be entirely up to Joe, at the time of the decision, which decision is made". The snippet does not show whose voice this is or how she answers it.
- **Tenet alignment**: conflicts with Dualism; agrees with Bidirectional Interaction in form only (see Major Positions).

### Steward, "Précis of *A Metaphysics for Freedom*" (2014)
- **URL**: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/84724/ (accepted version); https://doi.org/10.11612/resphil.2014.91.3.13
- **Type**: Paper, read in full
- **Key points**:
  - Agency incompatibilism is "the idea that agency itself is inconsistent with determinism".
  - Leeway and sourcehood are one condition: "one could only be a true source if one had leeway."
  - Prior causes act by "influence, not determination"; "It remains the agent herself who has to convert this influence into activity".
  - The modal demand is weak: "All that is required for Agency Incompatibilism is that no individual action be a necessitated event." It need not have been possible for the agent to choose B instead of A.
  - Two determinisms threaten agency, antecedent and "bottom-up", and microphysical indeterminism "addresses only the first".
  - Mental causation is the wrong criterion: "Causation by the mental, then, cannot be the key to understanding what is agential about agency, because so much agency proceeds without it."
  - The aim is "a truly naturalistic libertarianism", with freedom emerging "from the deep and still largely mysterious foundations of biology".
- **Quote**: "Nothing is a truly ultimate source of events which produces those events only because it is wholly caused to produce them by something else".

### Steward, "Responses" (2013)
- **URL**: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/84719/ (eight accepted-version PDFs); https://doi.org/10.1080/0020174x.2013.841055
- **Type**: Paper; four of eight replies read in full
- **Key points**:
  - *To Garnett* (rational-cost problem, "Crazy Singer" case): she had conceded that where reasons fix what to do and when, no freedom-relevant alternative remains, leaving only fine detail to settle. Reflecting on a fully specified version of the case she revises this: "it is in the deployment of the necessary care and attention that we see the agent's settling of what occurs", and so "perhaps contra my original position, there actually is a metaphysical possibility of not pressing the button at t which I should care about".
  - *To Boxer*: she denies that "settling begins where reasons end". "Settling only begins where reasons fail to dictate a unique course of action – but that, in my view, is nearly everywhere." Reasons "quite vastly underdetermine what one ought (at this very moment) to do or be doing".
  - *To Clancy*: an agent whose properties fix everything has no role. "What settles the outcome is not Rufus, but his properties". Her agent causation "can only exist at all in an indeterministic universe".
  - *To Broadie and Boxer*: she concedes the book's attack reaches only reductive, causal-theory compatibilism. To Broadie she also insists that sub-intentional doings ("scratching one's ear, crossing and uncrossing one's legs") "feel agentive" and must be covered.
- **Tenet alignment**: the Clancy reply parallels the Map's sourcehood reasoning; the Garnett reply touches the Map's attention and effort material.

### Steward, "Replies to Randolph Clarke, John Bishop, and Helen Beebee" (2014)
- **URL**: http://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/84726/ ; https://doi.org/10.11612/resphil.2014.91.3.17
- **Type**: Paper, read in full
- **Key points**:
  - *To Bishop*, the clearest statement of her non-dualism: "Agent causation is not a strange input from some other ethereal realm into the physical world; agent causation is simply the influence of a whole animal on its own parts. And since animals are physical, their influence on things is also physical". She accepts that she is "no less of a realisationist" than Bishop.
  - Top-down influence is synchronic: "The important relations between animal and parts have to be synchronous, not diachronic."
  - She admits the account is programmatic and that the prospect seems hopeless "in my darker moments".
  - What agency needs is "causal pluralism, plus top-down causation, plus indeterminism", the last because top-down influence must be "an indeterministic power, such that it remains an open possibility at each moment whether or not it will be exercised."
  - *To Clarke*: the claim that our concept of agency is incompatibilist is "an empirical question", argued from developmental psychology in Chapter 4. One feature of the concept she lists there: "An agent is a centre of some form of subjectivity".
  - *To Beebee*: "my central commitment is actually to indeterminism, rather than to incompatibilism." She defines this as denying "DPD", an abbreviation taken from Beebee's paper and not expanded in the reply.

### Buckareff, review in *Philosophy in Review* 33(6) (2013), 493–495
- **URL**: https://journals.uvic.ca/index.php/pir/article/view/12836/3943
- **Type**: Review, read in full
- **Key points**:
  - "A better title for Steward's book would have been A Metaphysics for Agency."
  - He reports her view that "actions are themselves exercises of agent-causal power and not products of such exercises (200)", and that agent causation is "found among all agents, not just human agents".
  - He quotes her p. 6, n. 15: animal agency is no proof of indeterminism but "it is a very good reason for believing in it" (the phrase is confirmed by a Google Books snippet at p. 6).
  - He quotes her p. 195 on alternatives: the action need only be "a non-compulsory exercise of power on his part". This quotation was reached through Buckareff only.
  - Verdict: "I agree with few of Steward's assumptions and conclusions", though he recommends the book.

### Levy, "Are We Agents at All? Helen Steward's Agency Incompatibilism" (2013)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1080/0020174x.2013.776482
- **Type**: Paper, **abstract only**
- **Key points** (Levy's description, not checked against her text):
  - Steward "concedes that the luck objection is persuasive with regard to existing versions of libertarianism".
  - On her view agents settle "very fine-grained aspects of their movements: when and how they move, even when and how their neurons fire."
  - Objection one: we control those aspects only indirectly, by engaging in broadly individuated action types.
  - Objection two: "these aspects of our movements are lucky for us and, since this is true, they cannot play the role of grounding our agency."
- **Note**: Levy is the same author the-divided-will cites for the luck objection. His paper sits in *Inquiry* 56(4), outside the special issue, and Steward's "Responses" does not list him among those answered.

## Major Positions

### Agency incompatibilism (Steward)
- **Core claim**: every action is a settling of some matter by its agent, and settling requires that the action not be necessitated. Animals act, so determinism is false.
- **Key arguments**: sourcehood requires leeway; the causal theory of action loses the agent; the concept of agency children acquire includes settling; bottom-up determination is answered by top-down causation that resolves otherwise unexplained coincidences among parts.
- **Relation to site tenets**: rejects Dualism. Agrees with Bidirectional Interaction only in form: the whole affects its parts, but both are physical and nothing crosses an ontological boundary. On Minimal Quantum Interaction she grants that quantum indeterminacy is probably necessary and denies it is sufficient, and the sources read here name no physical locus for the openness. No Many Worlds is not discussed in anything read, though settling plainly presupposes that unactualised alternatives stay unactual.

### Non-reductive or differently reductive compatibilism (Broadie, Boxer, Clancy, Clarke, Bishop)
- **Core claim**: one can reject the causal theory of action and still hold that agency is compatible with determinism (Broadie's intentional-item account, Boxer's willing-as-trying, Clancy's counterfactual "strong compatibilist settling", Bishop's realisationism). All as reported by Steward.
- **Relation to site tenets**: these press the gap the Map also has to cross. Steward's concession that her argument reaches only the causal theory applies equally to any Map text that argues from the disappearance of the agent straight to incompatibilism.

### Luck scepticism (Levy; Garnett in part)
- **Core claim**: what Steward leaves open is exactly what the agent does not control, so it cannot ground agency.
- **Relation to site tenets**: the same pressure the-divided-will records as unanswered.

## Key Debates

### What does the Map's settler add over the animal settler?
- **On sourcehood and luck: nothing found.** Both views end in a primitive exercise of power by the agent. Steward reaches that terminus without a second ontological category. Her Clancy reply is the Map's own argument that properties and states cannot substitute for the agent, made by a physicalist about animals.
- **On reduction: possibly something, at a price.** Steward must hold that the animal's efficacy is fully realised in its parts and yet irreducible to theirs, and she calls her solution programmatic. A non-physical settler is not composed of the parts it influences, so it avoids the constitution problem. It pays instead with the pairing and interface problems the Map already records as owed. This is a trade, and the note should not present it as a win.
- **On subjectivity: possibly something.** Steward writes subjectivity into the concept of an agent and bets, with Searle, that consciousness is biological (p. 91, snippet). The Map's reason for dualism is the hard problem. If that argument holds, her settler inherits it. That is the Map's leverage, and it comes from the philosophy of mind, since nothing in the free-will debate supplies it.
- **Against the Map: scope.** For Steward, habitual, unreflective and sub-intentional movements are settlings, and so are the actions of many non-human animals. [agent-causation](/concepts/agent-causation/) says agent causation is attenuated in scripted behaviour and ties selection to conscious effort. A keyword check found no mention of animals in [agent-causation](/concepts/agent-causation/) or [free-will](/topics/free-will/). Either the Map extends its settler to animals and to unreflective action, or it owes an account of what those doings are.
- **Current state**: open. No published exchange between Steward and an interactionist dualist was found.

### Does relocating what is settled answer luck?
- **Sides**: Steward grants that reasons may determine *what* is decided and holds that the agent still settles when and how, and usually what as well, since reasons rarely rank options uniquely (reply to Boxer). Levy replies that the fine-grained residue is lucky for the agent. Garnett replies that the rational-cost half of the problem survives.
- **Bearing on the-divided-will**: that article's reply (b) confines selection to choices with no settled ranking. Steward's claim that such choices are "nearly everywhere" would make (b) less of a retreat than it reads. It still leaves the akratic case untouched, since akrasia is defined by a ranking the agent acts against. Her pp. 147–161 treatment explains the capacity for akrasia, as the-divided-will already says.
- **Bearing on attention**: her Garnett reply places settling in care, concentration and effort. That is near the Map's attention-as-interface material in wording. She means an animal's physical top-level control, so it should not be cited as support for the interface model.
- **Current state**: ongoing; no reply by Steward to Levy's *Inquiry* paper was located.

## Potential Article Angles

1. **The rival that shares the Map's question.** Present Steward as the strongest non-dualist answer to "who settles?", then state plainly that on sourcehood and luck the Map has no advantage, and locate the disagreement in the reduction trade and the subjectivity condition.
2. **Two determinisms.** Use her antecedent/bottom-up distinction to test Minimal Quantum Interaction: quantum openness answers the first, and the article should say what in the Map answers the second.
3. **Scope as the test case.** Animals and sub-intentional action force the Map to say whether its settler is at work wherever something acts.
