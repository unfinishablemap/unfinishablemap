## Executive Summary

**Verdict: the subject merits a standalone concepts page, `concepts/dissociative-subtype-of-ptsd`, and the page's verdict should run against the Map's protective-withdrawal reading of trauma-linked dissociation.** No section host is available. `concepts/depersonalisation` is at 3,498 of 3,500 body words (1 word of headroom). The flagship is at 4,694 against 4,000 and is excluded as a host. The corpus has no PTSD page at all, yet three Map pages lean on the PTSD literature without naming it (details under "Recommended next step").

**What the dissociative subtype is.** DSM-5 (2013) added a specifier, "with dissociative symptoms", for people who meet full PTSD criteria and also have persistent or recurrent depersonalisation or derealisation. ICD-11 has no such subtype. Lanius et al. (2010) gave it a mechanism. On their model, re-experiencing PTSD is emotional *under*modulation, "mediated by failure of prefrontal inhibition of limbic regions". The dissociative subtype is *over*modulation, "mediated by midline prefrontal inhibition of the same limbic regions". Later work tied the overmodulated state to the passive end of the evolved defence cascade (tonic immobility, shutdown, opioid-mediated analgesia).

**Current status, 16 years on: the subtype has held, but the mechanism is weaker than in 2010.**
- *Held*: the class itself. About 20 latent class and profile studies find a dissociative group, and in one large veteran sample a class model beat dimensional ones (Wolf et al. 2023).
- *Held*: a prefrontal signal. A prospective study of 145 recent trauma survivors linked derealisation to raised ventromedial prefrontal activation (Lebois et al. 2022).
- *Not held*: the psychophysiological signature. A 2022 systematic review of 28 studies found "No clear trend" for hypoarousal.
- *Not held*: specific circuitry. The implicated regions "have varied greatly across studies" (Brewin et al. 2025), and the largest whole-brain resting-state study found widespread *hyper*connectivity rather than localised inhibition (Shaw et al. 2023).
- *Not held*: the treatment claim. A meta-analysis of 21 trials found no evidence that dissociation moderates psychotherapy outcome (Hoeboer et al. 2020).

**Against the Map's depersonalisation models (item 2), the subtype adds little new mechanism.** It is the Sierra–Berrios corticolimbic-inhibition reading moved into a trauma setting; Lanius et al. 2010 cite Sierra & Berrios 1998 and a depersonalisation-disorder imaging study. What it adds:
- a *control group*: dissociative and non-dissociative PTSD compared under the same trauma provocation;
- *state provocation* in the scanner;
- a *functional rationale*: evolved passive defence;
- a *single-dial symmetry*: one prefrontal–limbic balance yields both hyperarousal and detachment, and patients alternate between them.

It also changes two things. Its own psychometrics separate emotional numbing from depersonalisation/derealisation, the pressure Knappik already turns on the affective reading through a PTSD-subtype paper. And its neural picture overlaps DID more than it overlaps depersonalisation disorder, so "depersonalisation" is probably not one mechanism across conditions.

**Trauma confound (item 3).** The group-level hippocampal difference in DID that the Map cites is real. It has not been shown to be specific to DID. In Dimitrova et al. 2023, every DID participant had current or remitted PTSD, and the healthy controls were screened to exclude any trauma history. PTSD and adverse childhood experience each predict smaller hippocampi, the latter "in those with and without psychiatric diagnosis". In the only trauma-exposed comparisons:
- PTSD-only patients also had smaller hippocampi (Chalavi et al. 2015b).
- DID and dissociative-amnesia patients *without* PTSD had normal ones (Weniger et al. 2008, n = 13).

The DID-specific residue is a within-group correlation between CA1 volume and *self-reported* amnesia. That correlation was challenged on multiple-testing grounds, and its authors conceded the point and re-ran it on an a priori region (Reinders et al. 2022). The rung sentence on `metacognitive-access-versus-retention` and the flagship stays true; what it lacks is the PTSD qualifier.

**Relevance to the Map (item 4).** The overmodulation model does make the brain-only story more complete, and on the one point where the flagship gives the interface reading a role. The flagship's "Trauma Connection" credits the interface reading with an account of dissociation's apparent purposiveness ("consciousness withdrawing from specific channels to protect itself"). It answers it only with a generic "affective-salience physicalism". The subtype literature supplies that physicalism in worked form: an evolved, opioid-mediated passive defence, implemented as prefrontal over-regulation of limbic and periaqueductal-grey output, with a mirror-image partner state. Selectivity and protectiveness are therefore predicted by the rival and no longer favour the interface reading even weakly.

The gain is at the mechanism level (level 3 of the dissociation chain). Level 4 is untouched, so bare Dualism is unaffected. The verdict for the Map is *constrains, does not establish*.

## 1. The Specifier and the Model

### DSM-5 "with dissociative symptoms"
- **Wording.** Two secondary transcriptions agree on the depersonalisation clause: "persistent or recurrent experiences of feeling detached from, and as if one were an outside observer of, one's mental processes or body". The first is Eder-Moreau et al. 2022, quoting APA 2013. The second is Muehlhausen 2021, citing p. 272, which adds "(e.g., feeling as though one were in a dream; feeling a sense of unreality of self or body or of time moving slowly)". For derealisation, Eder-Moreau et al. give "persistent or recurrent experiences of unreality of surroundings". The rest of the specifier text (the full-criteria requirement, "in response to the stressor", and the substance/medical-condition exclusion) was seen only in a search-engine summary of the NCBI TIP 57 box, which blocked direct fetches. Treat that part as a lead.
- **A small finding for `concepts/depersonalisation` L37.** That page says the "as if" preface "belongs to the phenomenological tradition and the scale, not to the diagnostic text". That is right for the DPDR criteria it is discussing. The same manual's PTSD specifier does use "as if one were an outside observer". The page's wording is defensible in context, and no task is proposed; it is recorded so a future pass does not "correct" it the wrong way.
- **ICD-11.** Brewin et al. 2025 (full text): "In contrast to the DSM‐5, the ICD‐11 does not include a dissociative subtype of PTSD or complex PTSD." They add that ICD-11 represents dissociation through flashbacks and emotional numbing within the criteria.
- **DSM-5-TR status**: not verified this pass (PubMed `DSM-5-TR AND dissociative subtype AND PTSD` returned 0).

### Prevalence: the spread is mostly a matter of method
- Community: Stein et al. 2013 (WHO World Mental Health Surveys, 25,018 respondents, 16 countries) found dissociative symptoms in 14.4% of respondents with 12-month PTSD.
- Meta-analysis: White et al. 2022 (49 studies, N = 8,214) found 38.1% (95% CI 31.5–45.0) of PTSD cases overall, 22.8% in latent class/profile samples, and 48.1% under strict DSM-5 criteria, "characterised by high levels of heterogeneity".
- Latent classes: 12% of current PTSD (Wolf, Miller et al. 2012, n = 492); 25% (Steuwe et al. 2012, n = 134, childhood-abuse PTSD); 7.5% of a young veteran sample (Wolf et al. 2023, n = 374). Brewin et al. 2025 summarise "from 10 to 30%".

### The overmodulation/undermodulation model (Lanius et al. 2010, *Am J Psychiatry* 167(6):640–647)
- **Abstract (raw)**: "The dissociative subtype is characterized by overmodulation of affect, while the more common undermodulated type involves the predominance of reexperiencing and hyperarousal symptoms." "reexperiencing/hyperarousal reactivity is viewed as a form of emotion dysregulation that involves emotional undermodulation, mediated by failure of prefrontal inhibition of limbic regions. In contrast, the dissociative subtype of PTSD is described as a form of emotion dysregulation that involves emotional overmodulation mediated by midline prefrontal inhibition of the same limbic regions. Both types of modulation are involved in a dynamic interplay and lead to alternating symptom profiles in PTSD."
- **Note the last sentence.** The original model already allows alternation within one patient. The "two fixed types" reading is a simplification that later critics attack and that the model's authors did not hold.
- **Reference list (OpenAlex, metadata level)**: cites Sierra & Berrios 1998 ("Depersonalization: neurobiological perspectives"), Lemche et al. 2007 (limbic and prefrontal responses to emotional faces in depersonalisation disorder), Röder et al. 2007 (pain response in hypnotic depersonalisation), and Nijenhuis et al. 1998 (animal defensive reactions as a model for trauma-induced dissociation). The model was built partly *from* the depersonalisation-disorder corticolimbic literature.
- **Reverse cross-citation (OpenAlex `cites:` filter, 18 works with "depersonali[sz]ation" in the title)**: Lemche et al. 2016 (CNS Spectr), Sierra et al. 2014 (cortical thickness in DPD), Daniels et al. 2015 (VBM in DPD), Sierk et al. 2018 (white matter in DPDR), Salami et al. 2020 (EEG review), Murphy 2023 (critical review). This confirms, at metadata level, that the depersonalisation-disorder imaging literature cross-cites the model.

### The evidence the model rests on

| Study | Source type | n | Finding (raw abstract) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Griffin, Resick & Mechanic 1997, *AJP* 154(8):1081–1088 | experiment | 85 rape survivors, ≤2 weeks post-assault | high peritraumatic dissociation → "suppression of autonomic physiological responses"; "discrepancy between self-reports of distress and objective physiological indicators" |
| Lanius et al. 2002, *Biol Psychiatry* 52(4):305–311 | experiment (fMRI, script-driven imagery) | 7 PTSD in a dissociative state, 10 controls | pilot work: "Approximately 70% of patients relived their traumatic experience and showed an increase in heart rate … The other 30% of patients had a dissociative response with no concomitant increase in heart rate"; dissociated patients showed more medial frontal (BA 9/10) and anterior cingulate (BA 24/32) activation |
| Hopper, Frewen, van der Kolk & Lanius 2007, *J Trauma Stress* 20(5):713–725 | experiment (dimensional fMRI) | — | "dissociation correlated positively with activity in the left medial prefrontal and right superior temporal cortices" |
| Felmingham et al. 2008, *Psychol Med* 38(12):1771–1780 | experiment (fMRI) | 12 dissociative vs 11 non-dissociative PTSD | "enhanced activation in the ventral prefrontal cortex for conscious fear, and in the bilateral amygdala, insula and left thalamus for non-conscious fear"; the regulatory strategy "appears to function only during conscious processing of threat" |
| Nicholson, Friston, Zeidman … Lanius 2017, *Hum Brain Mapp* 38(11):5551–5561 | experiment (resting-state stochastic DCM) | 62 PTSD, 41 PTSD + DS, 52 trauma-unexposed controls | PTSD: "predominant bottom-up connectivity from the amygdala to the vmPFC"; PTSD + DS: "predominant top-down connectivity between all node pairs" and "the strongest intrinsic inhibitory connections within the vmPFC" |
| Lebois et al. 2022, *Am J Psychiatry* 179(9):661–672 (AURORA) | prospective cohort | 1,464 self-report; 145 fMRI at 2 weeks | "Derealization was associated with increased ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) activation in the emotion reactivity task"; predicted worse 3-month PTSD "distinct from the effects of childhood maltreatment history" |

The 2002 and 2008 studies are small. The DCM and AURORA studies are the strongest support for the prefrontal half of the model. AURORA is the only prospective test.

### The defence-cascade rationale and the predictive-processing version
- **Lanius, Boyd, McKinnon et al. 2018, *Curr Psychiatry Rep* 20(12):118** (narrative review, abstract): reviews "the defense cascade model of dissociation and Mobb's threat detection model". It proposes that "kappa-opioid receptor antagonists may serve as a pharmacological vehicle for the selective targeting of dissociative symptoms and associated emotion overmodulation".
- **Terpou et al. 2019, *J Neurosci Res* 97(9):1110–1140** (narrative review, abstract): PTSD and its dissociative subtype "are thought to differ in their proclivity to elicit active (i.e., fight, flight) versus passive (i.e., tonic immobility, emotional shutdown) defensive responses". It credits the midbrain periaqueductal grey with a unifying role.
- **Kozlowska, Walker, McLean & Carrive 2015, *Harv Rev Psychiatry* 23(4):263–287** (narrative review, abstract): the defence cascade runs arousal → fight/flight → freezing → "tonic immobility and collapsed immobility are responses of last resort to inescapable threat". "Each of these defense reactions has a distinctive neural pattern mediated by a common neural pathway".
- **Laurin et al. 2026, *Neurosci Biobehav Rev* 182:106546** (theoretical model, abstract): the predictive-processing form. In non-dissociative PTSD the ranking is "[Prior > Interoception > Exteroception]". In the dissociative subtype, "emotional over-inhibition and anterior insula hypoactivity weaken priors and interoception, while exteroceptive inputs dominate". This is the subtype's counterpart to the Ciaunica/Seth active-inference account the Map already engages for depersonalisation disorder.

## 2. What Has Held and What Has Not

### Held
- **The class.** Hansen, Ross & Armour 2017 (systematic review, 11 LCA/LPA studies): "The majority of the studies were supportive of the D-PTSD subtype". Misitano et al. 2024 (systematic review, 13 LPA studies): "All identified a dissociative subtype of PTSD". Brewin et al. 2025 (full text): "Close to 20 studies have used latent class or profile analysis … and provided support for this unique presentation". They report that a model comparison in trauma-exposed veterans found "the subtype structure provided the best fit to the data" (Wolf et al. 2023). Brewin et al. also say depersonalisation and derealisation "are far less common and are not strongly correlated with PTSD severity", unlike forms such as reduced awareness, which "appear to covary linearly with PTSD symptom severity".
- **The prefrontal signal.** Raised medial/ventromedial prefrontal activity or top-down coupling during dissociative responding recurs across Lanius 2002, Hopper 2007, Felmingham 2008, Nicholson 2017 and AURORA (Lebois 2022). A cross-condition voxel meta-analysis (Cavicchioli et al. 2023, 44 studies, 1,384 individuals; abstract) found "increased activity of dorsal anterior cingulate cortex as a common neurological signature" of the dissociative spectrum. For some conditions it also found "attempts to overcontrol of affective states".

### Not held, or contested
- **Psychophysiology.** Beutler et al. 2022, *Eur J Psychotraumatol* 13(2):2132599 (systematic review, 28 studies, N = 1,300): "No clear trend exists across all measured physiological markers in trauma-related dissociation … The current review is unable to provide robust evidence that peri- and post-traumatic dissociation are associated with hypoarousal, questioning the validity of distinct psychophysiological profiles in PTSD." That is the 1997/2002 heart-rate signature, failing to generalise.
- **Specific circuitry.** Brewin et al. 2025 (full text): "the implicated brain regions and the nature of these alterations have varied greatly across studies … these studies have generally focused on trait dissociation (as opposed to a state of active dissociation), so that these alterations may reflect a vulnerability or proclivity to dissociative experiences rather than the neurobiology of an active dissociative experience."
- **Hyperconnectivity, not localised inhibition.** Shaw, Terpou, Densmore, Théberge, Frewen, McKinnon & Lanius 2023, *Nature Mental Health* 1(10):711–721, studied 197 participants, 134 with PTSD. Only the preprint abstract was read (PsyArXiv 10.31234/osf.io/b6kdm, via OpenAlex); the published abstract was not retrievable. It found that "participants with the dissociative subtype demonstrated widespread small-scale and large-scale functional hyperconnectivity, compared to controls", and stated that "the field has yet to arrive at a neurobiological framework able to account for the disparate findings across these various scales". This comes from the model's home laboratory.
- **Limbic response depends on awareness.** Felmingham 2008 (above) and Klimova et al. 2013, *Eur J Psychotraumatol* 4:20430 (ERP, 16 high- vs 23 low-dissociative PTSD), found high-dissociative patients' early "preconscious" cortical responses to emotional faces *increased*, with reductions only in later conscious components. The overmodulation holds at the level of conscious processing. It is not a global limbic dampening.
- **Treatment response.** Lanius et al. 2012 reported "a differential response to current cognitive behavioral treatment" as a validator. Later evidence:
  - Hoeboer et al. 2020, *BJPsych Open* 6(3):e53 (meta-analysis, 21 trials, 1,714 patients): "Pre-treatment dissociation was not related to treatment effectiveness … (Pearson's correlation coefficient 0.04, 95% CI -0.04 to 0.13)".
  - Wolf, Lunney & Schnurr 2016, *J Consult Clin Psychol* 84(1):95–100: a flatter slope, but "this effect was small in magnitude". The results "raise doubt about the common clinical perception that exposure therapy is not effective".
  - Burton et al. 2018, *J Consult Clin Psychol* 86(5):439–451: "These findings are not in line with the dissociative-subtype theoretical literature".
  - Zoet et al. 2018 (title only): "Presence of the dissociative subtype of PTSD does not moderate the outcome of intensive trauma-focused treatment".
  - Brewin et al. 2025 still write that the subtype "may respond less well" (citing Wolf 2016 and Burton 2018). The honest summary is a small or null moderation effect.
- **Mirror image or add-on.** If dissociation were the overmodulated opposite of re-experiencing, the dissociative class should have *less* re-experiencing. It has more. Stein et al. 2013: dissociation "associated with high counts of re-experiencing symptoms". Wolf, Miller et al. 2012: the dissociative class had "severe PTSD symptoms combined with marked elevations on items assessing flashbacks". Burton 2018: the dissociative class "was also marked by higher reexperiencing symptoms". Lanius 2010's "alternating symptom profiles" absorbs this. Chiba et al. 2021, *Mol Psychiatry* 26(9):5023–5039 (behavioural meta-analysis N = 491; neuroimaging meta-analysis N = 316), formalise it as amygdala–vmPFC reciprocal inhibition with alternation *within* patients. On that reading the "subtype" is a disposition to spend more time in one state of a single bistable circuit.
- **Overlap with neighbouring diagnoses.** Ross, Ridgway & Myron 2024, *Psychol Trauma* 16(4):686–691 (62 inpatients at a specialist trauma programme): "Almost every participant met International Classification of Diseases, 11th Edition criteria for complex PTSD, DSM-5 criteria for the dissociative subtype of PTSD, and DSM-5 criteria for dissociative identity disorder or other specified dissociative disorder; these four diagnoses seem to identify largely the same patients and may not be separate disorders." This is a specialist sample, not a population.
- **Structural replication.** Daniels et al. 2016 (VBM, 15 PTSD-D vs 44 PTSD) reported grey-matter differences in the precentral, fusiform and inferior temporal gyri. Daniels et al. 2019, *Front Psychol* 10:738, attempted "a direct replication of brain volume alterations" (angular gyrus) for dissociation across trauma-spectrum disorders, and it "failed".

### Current status (one paragraph for the article)
As a psychometric class, the dissociative subtype is reasonably robust, though its prevalence depends on method. As a neurobiological type, it has a recurring prefrontal-overregulation signal (strongest in the DCM study and the AURORA prospective cohort). It has no stable regional signature, no reliable autonomic signature, and a resting-state profile of widespread hyperconnectivity. As a clinical type, its predicted treatment difference has largely not appeared. The 2010 model survives best in its own dynamic form: one prefrontal–limbic balance whose states patients alternate between. The "two fixed subtypes" reading has not survived.

## 3. Comparison with the Production Models the Map Already Discusses

The Map engages two production families for depersonalisation (`concepts/depersonalisation` §"Four Readings"; flagship L84–92):
- (A) the **affective/corticolimbic-inhibition** reading: Sierra & Berrios 1998; Sierra & David 2011; Medford 2012; Gerrans 2019;
- (B) the **active-inference/interoceptive-precision** reading: Seth, Suzuki & Critchley 2012; Ciaunica et al. 2022; Deane et al. 2020.

Billon, Zahavi and Knappik are readings of *what* comes apart, not production mechanisms.

**Is the dissociative subtype a "third production model"?** Mechanistically, no. It belongs to family A. Sierra & Berrios 1998 has "a left-sided prefrontal mechanism would inhibit the amygdala"; Lanius 2010 has "midline prefrontal inhibition of the same limbic regions". The nodes differ but the architecture is the same, and Lanius 2010 cites Sierra & Berrios. Its predictive-processing form (Laurin et al. 2026) is family B transposed. Precisely, the subtype adds four things and changes two.

**Adds:**
1. **A within-trauma control.** DPDR imaging compares patients with healthy controls (Phillips 2001 n = 6; Daniels 2015 25 vs 23; Zheng et al. 2025 47 vs 49). Subtype designs hold trauma exposure and PTSD diagnosis constant and vary only dissociation (Felmingham 2008; Nicholson 2017; Daniels 2016). That is the confound control the depersonalisation literature lacks.
2. **State provocation.** Script-driven imagery evokes the depersonalised state on demand in the scanner (Lanius 2002, Hopper 2007). DPDR imaging studies the chronic condition, not an episode evoked and contrasted within the same session.
3. **A functional rationale.** The defence cascade explains *why* a prefrontal–limbic system would have a setting that produces detachment, numbing and analgesia under inescapable threat. This is an evolutionary-functional story. As the Map presents family A (`concepts/depersonalisation` L55–57), it is a mechanism without a reason; whether Sierra and colleagues themselves proposed an adaptive function was not checked this pass. A depersonalisation-disorder patient's own image of an "experiential airbag" that inflates "as soon as there is a risk of being crushed by an extreme danger" (quoted in Ciaunica, Charlton & Farmer 2021, per the `depersonalisation` research note of 2026-09-30) is the first-person face of the same idea.
4. **Single-dial symmetry.** One balance, tipped either way, produces hyperarousal or detachment, with alternation within a patient (Lanius 2010; Chiba et al. 2021). Under the [evidential-status-discipline](/project/evidential-status-discipline/)'s single-mechanism rival gate, this is the deflation that would apply to any reading counting trauma-linked hyperarousal and trauma-linked detachment as two separate channels.

**Changes:**
5. **Psychometric pressure on the affective reading from inside the subtype literature.** Frewen, Brown, Steuwe & Lanius 2015, *Eur J Psychotraumatol* 6:26406 (557 online participants), extracted six factors, "2) Emotional Numbing/Anhedonia, 3) Dissociation" among them. Steuwe et al. 2012 found "dissociative symptoms are distinct from but correlate significantly with the core PTSD symptom clusters". This is the paper Knappik 2022 cites for "a breakdown of affective feelings can leave the other factors intact"; Knappik's Crossref reference list confirms "Frewen, P. A., Brown, M. F., Steuwe, C., & Lanius, R. A. (2015). Latent profile analysis and principal axis factoring of the DSM-5 dissociative subtype". So `concepts/depersonalisation` L57 already rests on a PTSD-subtype finding without naming it. The overmodulation model is affect-first, yet its own field's factor analyses separate numbing from depersonalisation/derealisation. The tension the Map presses on Sierra–Berrios presses on Lanius too.
6. **No neural unity across conditions defined by the same symptoms.** Lotfinia, Soorgi, Mertens & Daniels 2020, *J Psychiatr Res* 128:5–15 (systematic review, 33 MRI studies): "Patients with dissociative identity disorder and with dissociative PTSD showed more overlap in brain activation than each group showed with depersonalization/derealization disorder … dissociative processing cannot be localized to a few distinctive brain regions but rather corresponds to differential neural signatures depending on the symptom constellation." The two largest recent resting-state studies point in opposite directions. Shaw et al. 2023 found hyperconnectivity in PTSD-D. Zheng et al. 2025, *BMC Psychiatry* 25:59, found "significantly decreased FCs" in 47 drug-naïve DPDR patients. Methods differ, so treat this as a lead, not a result. Either way, a single "self-model ascending channel" individuated by one neural failure is not what the data show. This favours Knappik's localist reading at the architecture tier. It does not refute the interface vocabulary, which can say different upstream causes inhibit one channel, but it removes any support that vocabulary drew from a shared neural signature.

**What it does not change.** It says nothing new about *what* comes apart (mineness, affect, transparency, type-specific relations). The Billon/Zahavi/Knappik standoff on `concepts/depersonalisation` is untouched.

## 4. The Trauma-Exposure Confound behind the DID Hippocampal Finding

**What the Map currently says.**
- Flagship L64: "DID patients show smaller hippocampal and CA1 volumes at group level (Dimitrova et al., 2023)".
- `metacognitive-access-versus-retention` L131: "a group-level structural difference still exists: hippocampal volumes are smaller, and the CA1 reduction correlates with self-reported amnesia (Dimitrova et al. 2023)".
- `clinical-phenomenology-and-altered-experience` L125: "smaller hippocampal and amygdalar volumes (Vermetten et al. 2006)".

None of these is contradicted below. What follows is the attribution question: is the difference a DID effect, a PTSD effect, or a trauma-exposure effect?

**The Dimitrova sample (full text, PMC9975991).** The sample was 32 women with DID and 43 matched healthy controls. "Twenty-nine volunteers with DID had a co-morbid diagnosis of PTSD and three had PTSD in remission." The authors explain: "PTSD was not included as a co-morbidity because all individuals with DID also had a diagnosis of PTSD (in remission). Adding PTSD as a covariate would therefore invalidate the analyses." Control exclusion criteria included "the presence of dissociative symptoms and a history of trauma". So the group contrast is DID + PTSD + trauma against no-DID, no-PTSD and no-trauma; the three cannot be separated. The authors' own limitations section concedes the gap: "smaller hippocampal volume has been found in various mental disorders with and without dissociative symptoms". Their abstract: "Dissociative amnesia was the only dissociative symptom that correlated uniquely and significantly with reduced bilateral hippocampal CA1 subfield volumes" (the depersonalisation/derealisation factor did not); "only emotional neglect correlated negatively with bilateral global hippocampus, bilateral CA1, CA4 and GC-ML-DG".

**The published exchange.** The Map holds neither side of it.
- Huntjens, Otgaar, Pijnenborg & Wessel 2022, *Psychol Med* 52(13):2835–2836 (commentary, full text):
  - "More importantly, all DID patients had comorbid PTSD. Thus, the results equally favor the interpretation that PTSD and hippocampal CA1 volume reduction are linked, refuting the authors' conclusion of a specific dissociative disorder effect."
  - On multiple testing: "Dimitrova et al., explored associations between 4 dissociation scores and 20 hippocampal indices, yielding 80 correlations. Three were statistically significant (0.030 p ⩽ 0.049)".
  - On the index: "as an index of dissociative amnesia, they used the subjective amnesia scale scores of the DES … An objective cognitive measure of dissociative amnesia was not included."
- Reinders, Dimitrova et al. 2022, *Psychol Med* 52(13):2837–2845 (reply, full text):
  - On the PTSD point: "we do not agree that our 'results equally favour the interpretation that PTSD and CA1 volume reduction are linked' … a study in PTSD with and without dissociative features is recommended to investigate if the association between CA1 volume and dissociative amnesia still holds."
  - On multiple testing: "Here, we agree." A Bonferroni-corrected a priori re-analysis of three regions gave "decreased volume of the CA1 (t(72) = 2.320, p = 0.035)" and a CA1–amnesia correlation of "r = −0.398, p = 0.015".
  - The reply also restates the proposal as "might be a biomarker".
  - Both items carry corrigenda (PMIDs 35924602 and 36722033; not read).

**Trauma-exposed comparison groups.** These are the comparisons that decide the attribution question.
- **Chalavi et al. 2015b**, *Hum Brain Mapp* 36(5):1692–1704 (abstract): 17 DID, all with PTSD; 16 PTSD-only; 28 controls; same laboratory, overlapping cohort. "PTSD-DID (left: 10.19%; right: 11.37%) and PTSD-only with a history of childhood traumatization (left: 7.11%; right: 7.31%) had significantly smaller global hippocampal volume relative to HC … These findings support a childhood trauma-related etiology for abnormal hippocampal morphology in both PTSD and DID."
- **Chalavi et al. 2015a**, *Psychiatry Res Neuroimaging* 231(3):308–319 (abstract): DID-PTSD had smaller hippocampal volume than healthy controls, and larger putamen and pallidum than PTSD-only. No hippocampal difference from PTSD-only is reported. "Severity of lifetime traumatizing events and volume of the hippocampus were negatively correlated."
- **Weniger, Lange, Sachsse & Irle 2008**, *Acta Psychiatr Scand* 118(4):281–290 (abstract): severe childhood-abuse survivors. The PTSD group (n = 10) "displayed significantly reduced amygdala and hippocampal size"; the dissociative amnesia/DID group *without* comorbid PTSD (n = 13) "displayed normal amygdala and hippocampal size". This is the only DID/DA group without PTSD in the literature found. It is very small. A follow-up letter from the same group (Irle et al. 2009, title only) is titled "Further evidence that post-traumatic stress disorder but not dissociative disorders are related to amygdala and hippocampal size reduction in trauma-exposed individuals".
- **Wolf et al. 2023**, *J Psychopathol Clin Sci* 132(4):409–427 (abstract): in PTSD, "Covarying for age, sex, and PTSD severity", depersonalisation/derealisation severity was associated with "increased bilateral whole hippocampal, hippocampal head, and molecular layer head volume (p = .010-.034; padj = .032-.053)". This is marginal after correction but runs the opposite way. Together with Dimitrova's null for the depersonalisation/derealisation factor, the hippocampal signal, where there is one, tracks amnesia/trauma, not depersonalisation.

**PTSD and trauma hippocampal literature.**
- Logue et al. 2018, *Biol Psychiatry* 83(3):244–253 (ENIGMA-PGC, 1,868 subjects, 794 with PTSD; abstract): "significantly smaller hippocampi in subjects with current PTSD compared with trauma-exposed control subjects (Cohen's d = -0.17, p = .00054)". This is a small effect even against trauma-exposed controls. Dimitrova's controls were trauma-free, which inflates the expected contrast.
- Gilbertson et al. 2002, *Nat Neurosci* 5(11):1242–1247 (twin study; abstract): "smaller hippocampi indeed constitute a risk factor for the development of stress-related psychopathology". The unexposed co-twins of severe-PTSD patients also had smaller hippocampi.
- Ben-Zion et al. 2023, *Mol Psychiatry* 28(2):657–667 (longitudinal cohort, 100 survivors; abstract): "smaller volume in the subiculum and right CA1 hippocampal subregions" in non-remitting PTSD, with "No time-dependent volumetric changes". CA1 is implicated in PTSD itself, as a vulnerability trait.
- Hakamata et al. 2022, *Front Neuroendocrinol* 65:100994 (systematic review of 27 meta-analyses; abstract): adverse childhood experiences are associated with "diminished hippocampal gray matter volume. Importantly, these alterations were consistently observed in those with and without psychiatric diagnosis."

**Assessment.** The group-level difference (DID smaller than trauma-free controls) is replicated: Vermetten et al. 2006 found 19.2% smaller hippocampi (n = 15 vs 23), Chalavi 2015b, and Dimitrova 2023. It sits correctly on the "group-level structural difference" rung. What is *not* established is that the difference belongs to DID or to dissociative amnesia rather than to PTSD or early adversity:
- every DID sample in the main series had PTSD;
- the controls were trauma-free;
- the PTSD-only group also shows reduced volume;
- the one DID/DA-without-PTSD group shows none;
- the early-adversity association holds without any diagnosis.

The DID-specific residue is the within-DID correlation between CA1 volume and self-reported amnesia, r ≈ −0.40 after an a priori re-analysis, in one sample of 32. Its measure is self-report, which the Map's own four-level page treats as level-3 evidence. The test both sides call for, PTSD with and without dissociative amnesia, has not been run (none found).

**A secondary finding (out of scope; grep-validated live).** `clinical-phenomenology-and-altered-experience` L125 cites Vermetten et al. 2006 for "smaller hippocampal and amygdalar volumes" in DID. The larger later study from the Reinders cohort found "Analyses of covariance did not show volumetric differences between the DID and control groups" for the amygdala (Reinders et al. 2022, *BJPsych Open* 8(2):e70, n = 32 vs 42; abstract). The amygdala half of the L125 clause has a larger null against it. The hippocampal half stands. That page has 288 words of headroom.

## 5. Calibrated Relevance to the Map

**The four levels** (the [evidential-status-discipline](/project/evidential-status-discipline/)'s dissociation chain):
- *Level 1 (separability)*: trauma-provoked depersonalisation is another case of felt reality and ownership coming apart from intact perception and cognition. No new information.
- *Level 2 (phenomenological reality)*: no new information.
- *Level 3 (mechanistic independence)*: this is where the subtype acts. It supplies an intra-cerebral mechanism (prefrontal over-regulation of limbic and PAG output), a state-provocation paradigm, a within-trauma control, and an evolutionary function.
- *Level 4 (irreducibility)*: not reached. Nothing in the model explains why inhibited limbic output should *feel* unreal rather than merely be less vigorous.

**Does it make a brain-only production story more complete?** Yes, at the point where the Map had reserved a role for the interface reading. The flagship's "Trauma Connection" (L143–147) names the apparent purposiveness of dissociation as what "stands in need of explanation". It offers "consciousness withdrawing from specific channels to protect itself" against "an affective-salience physicalism" and concludes that "which framework earns it the clinical data do not settle". The subtype literature fills in the second option. The protectiveness is the passive end of an evolved defence cascade (Kozlowska 2015; Terpou 2019), and the selectivity is what prefrontal regulation of threat circuitry does when threat is inescapable (Lanius 2010; Nicholson 2017). The purposiveness is inherited from evolution, not chosen by a withdrawing subject. Under the compatibility-vs-support rule, the selectivity datum is now predicted by the rival from its core, without auxiliaries. Where the flagship left it at "the data do not settle", the honest register is that the rival explains the trauma-linked selectivity at least as well, and with a mechanism. That counts against the interface reading's comparative standing on this point. It does not establish physicalism.

**Three co-optation traps the article must avoid.**
1. *"Top-down" is a neural direction, not mind-to-brain causation.* Prefrontal-to-limbic regulation is one brain region modulating another. Citing it for [Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction) would be the access-to-phenomenal slide that the discipline's "Access/Functional Mental Causation ≠ Phenomenal Mental Causation" rule forbids.
2. *"Only during conscious processing" is an access-level finding.* Felmingham 2008's result, that the regulatory strategy engages for consciously perceived but not masked threat, is tempting as evidence that consciousness does the regulating. The manipulation is masking, which changes global availability. Every workspace theory predicts that a regulatory loop engages on globally available content. It is evidence about access, not phenomenal causation.
3. *Inhibition is not disconnection.* Like conversion disorder (flagship L104–112, with Voon et al. 2010 at L110), trauma-related depersonalisation now looks like *increased* coupling (top-down overregulation; widespread hyperconnectivity) rather than a channel falling silent. The flagship's DPDR heading ("The Self-Model Channel Is Inhibited") already uses the right verb. Its table column "Down-weighted interoceptive precision" (L121) and the lead's "selective disconnections between intact processing regions" (L56) fit trauma-related depersonalisation less well than they fit the DPDR resting-state data. Record, do not edit (the flagship is OVER).

**Tenet 5.** The overmodulation/undermodulation dichotomy was itself a simplicity claim: one dial, two settings. The data have complicated it (co-elevated re-experiencing, alternation, hyperconnectivity). The tenet's warning applies to the rival's tidiness as much as to the Map's single-channel phrasing.

### Draft "Relation to Site Perspective" paragraph (for the expand-topic article)

> **[Dualism (Tenet 1)](/tenets/#dualism)**: compatible with, not evidence for. On one point the subtype moves the balance away from the Map's interface reading. It is the best-specified production account of trauma-provoked depersonalisation: a prefrontal–limbic balance that, tipped one way, yields re-experiencing and hyperarousal and, tipped the other, yields detachment and numbing, with a functional rationale in the passive end of the evolved defence cascade. That account answers the question [clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence](/topics/clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence/) left open: why trauma-linked dissociation looks selective and protective. Its answer is a mechanism whose purposiveness is inherited from evolution rather than chosen by a withdrawing subject, so the selectivity no longer favours the interface reading even weakly. The rival predicts it. The account does not reach the question the Dualism tenet turns on. It says when, and through what circuitry, the sense of unreality arises, not why inhibited limbic output should feel unreal, so the bare thesis is untouched. The account is also weaker than its 2010 statement: its autonomic signature has not held up across studies, its implicated regions vary, and its resting-state profile is widespread hyperconnectivity rather than localised inhibition. What the Map may say is that trauma-related depersonalisation *constrains* the interface reading's protective-withdrawal gloss without *establishing* physicalism. The rival's gain is in completeness at the mechanism level, where it was already strongest. **[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)**: not engaged. The model's "top-down" is prefrontal-to-limbic, a direction within the brain, and supports at most the access-level reading of mental causation. **[Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction)** and **[No Many Worlds](/tenets/#no-many-worlds)**: not engaged. **[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)**: the one-dial, two-setting model is the rival's own simplicity bet, and the alternation and co-elevation data have already complicated it.

## Key Debates

### Subtype or severity?
- **Sides**: subtype (Wolf, Miller et al. 2012; Steuwe 2012; Hansen 2017; Wolf 2023's model comparison; Brewin 2025's "PTSD severity alone did not predict who would manifest these symptoms") versus severity/add-on (Stein 2013's re-experiencing association; Burton 2018; Ross 2024's diagnostic overlap).
- **Current state**: class models fit best in the best-powered comparison. The class is co-elevated on re-experiencing, which favours the dynamic-alternation reading over a mirror-image typology.

### Overmodulation as localised inhibition or global reconfiguration?
- **Sides**: vmPFC-to-amygdala/PAG top-down inhibition (Nicholson 2017; Lebois 2022) versus widespread hyperconnectivity (Shaw 2023), pre-conscious limbic enhancement (Felmingham 2008; Klimova 2013), and Brewin 2025's "varied greatly".
- **Current state**: open. Both camps are in the same laboratory network.

### Is the DID hippocampal difference DID's?
- **Sides**: Dimitrova/Reinders (CA1 as a candidate amnesia biomarker in DID) versus Huntjens/Otgaar (PTSD and self-report confounds; multiple testing).
- **Current state**: group difference replicated; specificity untested; both sides call for a PTSD with/without dissociative amnesia study.
