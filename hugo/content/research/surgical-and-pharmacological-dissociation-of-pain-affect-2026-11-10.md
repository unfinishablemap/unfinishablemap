## Executive Summary

The surgical and pharmacological record is large, old, and real. Prefrontal lobotomy (1940s–50s), cingulotomy (1962 on; one 2024 review pools 420 cingulotomy and cingulate-stimulation patients), low-dose opioids and hypnotic suggestion all reduce how much pain *matters* to a patient more than they reduce whether a pain is felt. Melzack and Wall (1965) put the canonical sentence into *Science*: lobotomized patients "report that they still have pain but it does not bother them" [raw]. On a single stroke patient, Ploner et al. (1999) showed the converse, a "clearly unpleasant" feeling with no identifiable pain sensation [raw].

The record cuts three ways, and the grades differ.
1. **For the generic claim that pain's sensory and affective dimensions separate**, it is strong. It is convergent across lesion, drug and suggestion, and it includes a double dissociation.
2. **For asymbolia's own profile**, it adds almost nothing. Aydede's SEP entry draws the distinction [raw]. Lobotomy and morphine patients "do show the usual affective reactions and symptoms when they are stimulated momentarily by normally painful stimuli"; they "Probably … still feel the negative affect but don't mind it", whereas asymbolics show no affect even to momentary pains. The relief is graded, dose-dependent, and largely located at what Price (2000) calls "secondary affect" [abstract]. The "six Berthier patients, no replication" objection to asymbolia therefore stands.
3. **For the metaphysics**, it is the physicalist's own result. Pain science built the sensory/affective decomposition partly from these very patients (Melzack & Wall 1965). It maps the components to separate pathways and titrates the affective one with a drug. That makes the concept page's lede ("a phenomenal layer that physicalist accounts struggle to accommodate") an over-reach. The record is compatible with the Map's interface reading and leaves the explanatory gap where it was, but it favours neither side.

## Key Sources

### SEP "Pain" (Aydede, Spring 2019 edition; first published 30 Sep 2005, substantive revision 4 Mar 2019) [raw]
- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2019/entries/pain/ (fetched live at https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pain/)
- **Key points** (§6.1 "The argument from reactive dissociation for eliminating pain", and §7):
  - "It has been well known that certain surgical procedures, some drugs and certain pathological conditions reduce or remove the unpleasantness of pain while preserving its sensory-discriminative aspect." The data come from prefrontal lobotomy (Freeman et al. 1942; Freeman and Watts 1946, 1950; Hardy et al. 1952; Barber 1959; Bouckoms 1994), cingulotomy (Foltz and White 1962a, 1962b; White and Sweet 1969), hypnotic suggestion (Barber 1964; Rainville et al. 1997, 1999), nitrous oxide and "some opium derivatives like morphine (Barber 1959)".
  - These patients "can recognize and identify their pain as such, but do not feel or seem bothered or distressed in ways characteristic to having pain experiences."
  - "Although it is usually not recognized in the literature, there are important differences among the phenomena afflicting these patients". Asymbolia's dissociation is "sometimes similar to cingulotomy patients' but interestingly different from lobotomy patients'". "In fact, there is evidence that pain asymbolia may be the only form of genuine dissociation (Grahek 2007)."
  - "The lobotomy and morphine patients, on the other hand, do show the usual affective reactions and symptoms when they are stimulated momentarily by normally painful stimuli. But they don't seem to care or be bothered by their standing persistent or chronic pains. Probably, they still feel the negative affect but don't mind it, whereas the pain asymbolia patients don't even seem to feel the momentary negative affect."
  - On Dennett: "Dennett primarily relies on the kind of dissociation involved in lobotomy and morphine cases and treats them as if they involved the same kind of strong dissociation involved in pain asymbolia."
  - §7: since Melzack and Wall (1965) and Melzack and Casey (1968), pain science has conceived pain more like an emotion, "by first drawing the sensory/affective distinction and then emphasizing more and more its affective aspect."
- **Bibliography slips** (do not copy): SEP gives Melzack & Casey 1968 as pp. "223–43" (citing sources give 423 as first page) and Freeman & Watts 1946 as "*Proceedings of the Royal Academy of Medicine*, 39: 44–447".
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral survey. Its lobotomy/asymbolia distinction is the single most important finding for this note.

### Melzack, R., & Wall, P. D. (1965). "Pain Mechanisms: A New Theory." *Science* 150(3699):971–979 [raw]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.150.3699.971 (Crossref-confirmed)
- **Key points** (p. 971, "Psychological evidence"): the Anzio soldiers who "entirely denied pain" are contrasted with lobotomy patients. "If the men had felt pain, even pain sensation devoid of negative affect, they would, it is reasonable to assume, have reported it, just as lobotomized patients (14) report that they still have pain but it does not bother them." Note 14 is "W. Freeman and J. W. Watts, Psychosurgery in the Treatment of Mental Disorders and Intractable Pain (Thomas, Springfield, Ill., 1950)". The same paragraph attributes "nonpainful pain (17)" to Nafe (1934).
- **Significance**: this is the earliest verbatim form located of the circulating line, and it already uses the lobotomy data as evidence for "pain sensation devoid of negative affect". The gate-control paper, three years before Melzack & Casey's multidimensional model, treats the lobotomy dissociation as a premise. That is the documentary basis for the reviewer's "decomposition is our result".
- **Tenet alignment**: Physicalist research programme; neutral on the bridge.

### Dennett, D. C. (1978). "Why You Can't Make a Computer That Feels Pain." *Synthese* 38(3):415–456 [raw]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00486638
- **Key points**: aspirin "is also unique among analgesics in lacking the 'reactive disassociation' effect" (p. 431; scare quotes in the original, so the term is presented as borrowed). Of the morphine group and sub-anaesthetic nitrous oxide: "After receiving the analgesic subjects commonly report not that the pain has disappeared or diminished (as with aspirin) but that the pain is as intense as ever though they no longer mind it" (p. 431). "Lobotomized subjects similarly report feeling intense pain but not minding it" (p. 432). He cites Keats & Beecher (1950) for "reversible pharmacological leucotomy" (note 23) and names no patient series for the morphine phenomenology. A note credits Trigg (1970) with the view that "it would be abnormal but not conceptually impossible to have a very intense pain but not dislike it."
- **Tenet alignment**: Conflicts (eliminativism about the folk concept of pain). It is the philosophical populariser of the lobotomy/morphine reports, and SEP faults it for conflating them with asymbolia.

### Ploner, M., Freund, H.-J., & Schnitzler, A. (1999). "Pain affect without pain sensation in a patient with a postcentral lesion." *Pain* 81(1–2):211–214 [raw]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0304-3959(99)00012-3 (Crossref-confirmed; Clinical Note)
- **Key points**: a 57-year-old man with a right postcentral stroke confined to SI hand area and SII. On the affected hand no laser stimulus up to 600 mJ evoked pain, "However, at intensities of 350 mJ and more, the patient spontaneously described a 'clearly unpleasant' intensity dependent feeling emerging from an ill-localized and extended area 'somewhere between fingertips and shoulder', that he wanted to avoid." He was "completely unable to further describe quality, localization and intensity of the perceived stimulus" (p. 213). The authors claim "for the first time in humans, a loss of pain sensation with preserved pain affect" (p. 211). They concede that "damage to SI and SII produced hypalgesia in our patient, suggesting interaction between medial and lateral pain system in normal pain experience" (p. 213).
- **Framing check**: Ploner et al. frame the case with the lateral/medial pain-system division (Albe-Fessard et al. 1985; Vogt et al. 1993) and do not cite Melzack & Casey. The reviewer's "predicted … from functional neuroanatomy" is fair in substance, but the Melzack-Casey lineage is the reviewer's gloss.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. One patient. It completes a double dissociation, the standard neuropsychological signature of separable processing streams.

### Williams, G. H., Jr. (1949). "Prefrontal Lobotomy for the Relief of Intractable Pain; Value and Limitations: Report of Five Cases." *Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine* 16(2):91–97 [raw]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.3949/ccjm.16.2.91 (Crossref-confirmed; PDF at ccjm.org/content/ccjom/16/2/91.full.pdf)
- **Key points**:
  - Case 1, the day after bilateral lobotomy: the patient "was apathetic, incontinent, manifested no anxiety or concern over her condition and although she admitted awareness of pain when questioned directly, did not ask for sedatives." At six weeks, "when questioned directly concerning pain, acknowledged none."
  - Another case: "She admitted that pain persisted but did not trouble her."
  - A third patient's wife wrote that after three pain-free weeks "he became aware of pain" and "I believe that it does bother him."
  - Conclusions (p. 96) [raw, despaced]: "Lobotomy will not eliminate pain but it will alter the individual's reaction to discomfort." Williams quotes Watts & Freeman (1946): "pain may be present but when divorced from its implications —the threat to the patient's security and fear of disability and death — it then becomes bearable and may be accepted with fortitude." The Watts & Freeman words are [secondary] via Williams.
- **Significance**: five cases show what the large record looks like up close. The relief comes with global apathy, it can wear off, and in case 1 the pain report itself vanishes. The authors' own gloss locates the effect at the pain's "implications", which is Price's secondary affect avant la lettre.

### Howarth, J. C., & Gardner, W. J. (1952). "Transorbital Leukotomy for the Pain of Malignant Disease." *Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine* 19(3):140–143 [raw]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.3949/ccjm.19.3.140
- **Key points**: the pain indication arose because observers saw "a definite change in the attitude of many patients with severe pain, as shown by the lessened emotional response to their suffering." The series has 25 patients. "Good" results are graded on diminished complaint and sedation, not on separate sensory and affective measures. "Freeman reported in his first 100 cases that the effects of the operation on pain of long duration were not dramatic."

### Hudson, A. J. (2000). "Pain Perception and Response: Central Nervous System Mechanisms." *Can. J. Neurol. Sci.* 27(1):2–16 [raw]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0317167100051908
- **Key points** on Foltz & White, [secondary] for the primary:
  - Foltz & White's rationale is quoted: "that transection of the cingulum might be of benefit in those clinical cases of intractable pain in which marked emotional factors appeared to contribute to the intolerable situation".
  - "They found that the noxious quality of chronic pain was alleviated although pain was still present and, therefore, it remained uncertain whether the emotional reaction to, rather than the pain itself, was relieved."
  - Later, patients "maintained that they still experienced pain but it was no longer unpleasant".
  - Hudson cites Jones et al. for "high levels of opioid receptor binding in the projections of the medial thalamic pain system to both the anterior cingulate region and the prefrontal cortex."

### Kollenburg, L., Arnts, H., Green, A., Strauss, I., Vissers, K., Vinke, S., & Kurt, E. (2024). "The cingulum: a central hotspot for the battle against chronic intractable pain?" *Brain Communications* 6(5): fcae368 [raw]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/braincomms/fcae368
- **Key points**:
  - The systematic review pools "420 patients" across cingulotomy and DBS-ACC series. Foltz & White (1962) is 16 patients (6 neoplastic, 10 non-neoplastic); Hurt & Ballantine (1974) is 68.
  - "Interestingly, patients undergoing cingulotomy described the pain to not 'worry them' and to be 'not particularly bothersome' after surgery." This cites Foltz & White 1962 and 1957, [secondary] for those words.
  - Measurement caveat: "standard pain assessment scores might underestimate its effect as pain intensity but not its emotional component is measured."
  - Responder definitions are ≥30% or ≥50% reductions in VAS/NRS *intensity*.
- **Significance**: the surgical record's sensory-versus-affective claim rests on qualitative patient descriptions. Its quantified outcome is reduced pain *intensity*.

### Lind, V., & Akram, H. (2025). "Cingulotomy for Cancer Pain." *Stereotactic and Functional Neurosurgery* 103(6):543–552 [raw, accepted manuscript]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1159/000548804
- **Key points**: the ACC's "disruption through targeted lesioning may reduce suffering without eliminating nociception". Across 10 observational studies, "72 out of 111 (65%) patients with cancer pain" had "good" relief. Where individual data exist (n = 24), "the median decrease in pain intensity score used was 66.7%". Foltz & White's rationale was to ease "mental suffering". The best responders had comorbid anxiety or depression.
- **Significance**: cingulotomy reduces rated intensity substantially too. The surgical record is not a clean "intensity spared, affect removed" experiment.

### Dynes, J. B. (1949). "Lobotomy for Intractable Pain." *JAMA* 140(1):15 [metadata + secondary]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1949.02900360017006. Crossref lists Dynes as sole author; the Intropsych page cites "Dynes & Poppen (1950)" with a wrong year.
- **Key points** via the Intropsych "Lobotomies" page [secondary]: "The patient [after lobotomy] was unconcerned about pain and in practically all instances he was no longer anxious and fearful as he had been prior to the lobotomy." ### Price, D. D. (2000). "Psychological and neural mechanisms of the affective dimension of pain." *Science* 288(5472):1769–1772 [abstract]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.288.5472.1769
- **Key points**: "The affective dimension of pain is made up of feelings of unpleasantness and emotions associated with future implications, termed secondary affect." There are "serial interactions between pain sensation intensity, pain unpleasantness, and secondary affect". The converging pathways reach ACC structures "whose function may be to establish emotional valence and response priorities."
- **Significance**: the Map's pages work with two layers (sensory-discriminative, affective-motivational). Price's model has three, and the lobotomy record sits mostly at the third.

### Rainville, P., Duncan, G. H., Price, D. D., Carrier, B., & Bushnell, M. C. (1997). *Science* 277(5328):968–971 [abstract]
- **Key point**: hypnotic modulation of unpleasantness changed ACC but not S1 activity, "as originally suggested by early clinical lesion studies". Already on the concept page (L109).

### Opioid psychophysics [abstract, all]
- **Price, Von der Gruen, Miller, Rafii & Price (1985)**, *Pain* 22(3):261–269. Low morphine doses (0.04 and 0.06 mg/kg) "resulted in statistically reliable reductions in affective but not sensory intensity VAS responses"; the highest dose (0.08 mg/kg) "reduced both sensory and affective VAS responses".
- **Gracely, Dubner & McGrath (1979)**, *Science* 203(4386):1261–1263, the **counter-result**: "The fentanyl significantly reduced the sensory intensity without reducing the unpleasantness of the tooth pulp stimuli".
- **Kupers, Konings, Adriaensen & Gybels (1991)**, *Pain* 47(1):5–12. Morphine "reduced the affective but not the sensory dimension of pain sensation in both groups of neurogenic pain patients"; in idiopathic pain "neither the affective nor the sensory dimension of pain sensation were affected."
- **Oertel et al. (2008)**, *Clin. Pharmacol. Ther.* 83(4):577–588 (alfentanil, fMRI). Activation in sensory regions "decreased linearly"; in affective regions (parahippocampal gyrus, amygdala, anterior insula) "pain-related activation disappeared at the lowest alfentanil dose".
- **Zubieta et al. (2001)**, *Science* 293(5528):311–315. Endogenous mu-opioid activation "was associated with reductions in the sensory and affective ratings of the pain experience".
- **Grade**: the harvest's phrase "opioid selectivity for unpleasantness over intensity" holds only at low doses, for some drugs and some pain types. One classic study found the reverse. The dissociation is real but graded. Its strongest form is a dose-response asymmetry with a receptor-level mechanism, which is the physicalist's best single datum here.

### Freeman, W., & Watts, J. W. (1948). "Pain Mechanisms and the Frontal Lobes." *Ann. Intern. Med.* 28(4):747–754 [abstract]

Then "On the other hand, pain is an emotion", with "an uncomfortable preoccupation with the future consequences of the pain."
- **Significance**: the sensation/emotion split, and the future-oriented affect, are on the page twenty years before Melzack & Casey.

## Coiner versus Populariser

- **"Sensory-discriminative" / "motivational-affective" (and "cognitive-evaluative")**. The *terms* and their mapping to separate systems are Melzack & Casey (1968) [metadata; SEP raw]. Casey himself later called "motivational-affective" conceptually problematic (Casey & Lorenz 2000) [abstract]. The *distinction* is older. Freeman & Watts (1948) [abstract] frame pain as both "a sensation" and "an emotion". Hardy, Wolff & Goodell titled their 1952 monograph *Pain Sensations and Reactions* [metadata, cited in Melzack & Wall 1965 and Dennett 1978, both raw]. Strong (1895) is the usual earliest cite [metadata only; content not verified]. **Do not credit Melzack & Casey with discovering the split**, only with naming and anatomising it.
- **"Pain asymbolia"**. Schilder & Stengel (1928; English, *Arch. Neurol. Psychiat.* 25:598, 1931) coined it. Melzack & Wall (1965) cite that 1931 paper as their note 57 [raw]. Rubins & Friedman (1948) gave a later systematic study. This is already owned by todo L5174 (the `concepts/pain-asymbolia` L50 priority fix) and corroborated by the sibling note. No action here.
- **"Reactive dissociation"**. SEP says Dennett "dubbed" it [raw]. Dennett's own text puts 'reactive disassociation' in scare quotes as if borrowed [raw]. Its origin was not found, so do not name Dennett as the coiner in Map prose.
- **"Secondary affect"**. This is Price's term in the 2000 abstract ("termed secondary affect") [abstract]. Earlier Price-group uses are likely (Wade, Dougherty, Archer & Price 1996, cited in Rainville et al. 1999) but were not checked. Attribute it to Price (2000) as its standard statement, not as its coinage.
- **"Reversible pharmacological leucotomy"**. Dennett attributes it to "some researchers", citing Keats & Beecher (1950) [raw / metadata].

## Major Positions

### Physicalist functional decomposition (at full strength)
- **Proponents**: Melzack & Wall (1965); Melzack & Casey (1968); Price (2000); Rainville et al. (1997); Ploner et al. (1999); Hardcastle (1999, via SEP); the predictive-processing accounts already on the concept page.
- **Core claim**: "Pain" names a hierarchy of separable processes. Discriminative coding runs in the lateral system (S1/S2, posterior insula). Unpleasantness runs in the medial system (ACC, anterior insula, medial thalamus). Secondary affect, the evaluation of pain's implications, involves prefrontal cortex. Each can be selectively damaged, spared or pharmacologically tuned.
- **Key arguments**:
  1. **Prediction and mapping.** The split was a premise of the theory before asymbolia became a philosophers' case: Melzack & Wall used the lobotomy patients as evidence for "pain sensation devoid of negative affect" in 1965.
  2. **Double dissociation.** Asymbolia, cingulotomy and hypnosis remove or reduce affect with discrimination spared. Ploner's patient keeps affect with discrimination lost. Each direction tracks lesion or activation site.
  3. **Dose-response with a mechanism.** Low-dose morphine reduces affective ratings first (Price et al. 1985). Alfentanil abolishes affective-region activation at the lowest dose while sensory-region activation declines linearly (Oertel et al. 2008). Opioid receptor binding is dense in the medial system's projections (via Hudson 2000). The felt awfulness has a dose-response curve and a receptor map.
  4. **The varieties fit the model too** (this note's inference, not a sourced claim). Aydede's distinction (lobotomy and morphine patients still react to momentary pains, asymbolics do not) is what a serial model like Price's would lead one to expect. A prefrontal lesion should spare immediate unpleasantness and remove secondary affect. An insular disconnection should remove immediate unpleasantness itself. On that model the physicalist can explain the fine structure of the clinical differences, which a bare "phenomenal layer excised" reading does not.
  5. **Eliminativist extension** (Dennett 1978). If reports of pain "as intense as ever" that patients "no longer mind" are sincere and normal, the folk concept that makes painfulness essential to pain is incoherent, and the "felt awfulness" quale it posits is a confusion.
- **Relation to site tenets**: This is the reading the 2026-11-10 reviewer meant by "Decomposition is our result, not yours". On its own terms it has no gap to explain at the dissociation: the affective component just *is* the medial-system role. It is silent on why medial-system activity is felt, and that is the general explanatory gap, which applies to the sensory component equally. It does not refute the Map. It removes the dissociation as a dualism-specific datum.

### The Map's interface reading
- **Core claim** (current `concepts/pain-asymbolia` L97, L117): the dissociation is a shared explanandum. The Map locates its disagreement at the [constitution-vs-causal-work](/concepts/constitution-vs-causal-work/) bridge from role to felt awfulness.
- **What the record does to it**:
  - It **fits**: every intervention is physical and changes felt quality, which interactionism expects of an interface.
  - It **does not discriminate**: identity theory predicts the same.
  - It **presses one framing**. The lede's "exposing a phenomenal layer that physicalist accounts struggle to accommodate" is contradicted by the record. Physicalist pain science predicted the layer's separability and maps it. The page's own description field ("straining accounts that make unpleasantness intrinsic to nociceptive processing") and L71 ("the disciplined claim is narrower") already say the defensible thing.
- **No tenet upgrade.** Nothing here bears on Minimal Quantum Interaction or No Many Worlds. For Bidirectional Interaction it stays a shared explanandum, as L117 says. For Occam's Razor Has Limits, the physicalist's account is here the *richer* one (it predicts the varieties), so the razor argument at L119 should not be extended to this material.

### Grahek / Aydede: asymbolia as the only genuine dissociation
- **Core claim** (SEP raw; Grahek 2007 via SEP): lobotomy, cingulotomy and morphine patients mostly keep momentary pain affect and lose concern for standing pain. Asymbolics lose the momentary affect. The large record is therefore a *different* dissociation, of minding from feeling, not of feeling from sensing.
- **Relation to site tenets**: it preserves asymbolia's philosophical distinctiveness, at the price of leaving its empirical base at six cases plus scattered reports. The Map cannot have both "a large base" and "a unique dissociation".

## Key Debates

### Does the surgical record answer the "six Berthier patients" objection?
- **Sides**: the harvest framing (yes: hundreds of patients show the same split) versus Aydede/Grahek (no: a different split). Kollenburg et al.'s measurement caveat and Lind & Akram's intensity data add a further point: the modern surgical data do not measure the two dimensions separately.
- **Current state**: it answers the objection **for separability** and **not for asymbolia's profile**. The honest wording is "the dimensions separate, by degree, in hundreds of patients; the all-or-none asymbolic pattern remains a handful of cases."

### Is opioid analgesia selective for affect?
- **Sides**: Price et al. 1985, Kupers et al. 1991 and Oertel et al. 2008 (yes, at low doses, for some pain types) versus Gracely et al. 1979 (fentanyl: intensity down, unpleasantness unchanged) and Zubieta et al. 2001 (endogenous opioids: both).
- **Current state**: dose-, drug- and pain-type-dependent. Use "low-dose morphine can reduce unpleasantness before intensity", not "opioids selectively remove unpleasantness".

### Is the lobotomy effect on pain or on the patient?
- **Evidence**: Williams (1949) case 1 was "apathetic, incontinent", and at six weeks denied pain altogether. Dynes reported global slowing (via Intropsych). Freeman reported the effect of transorbital leukotomy on pain of long duration "not dramatic" in his first 100 cases (via Howarth & Gardner 1952). Watts & Freeman's own gloss puts the effect at pain's "implications".
- **Current state**: the lobotomy series confounds pain-specific change with global affective blunting. It is the weakest strand for any selectivity argument, which is also why Klein's "lost care" reading of asymbolia has a natural home here.
