## Executive Summary

The candidate void is the claim that a mentally healthy person is shut out of an accurate view of themselves, and that the shutting-out is part of what keeps them healthy. Taylor and Brown (1988) argued it; Alloy and Abramson (1979) supplied its best-known evidence, that depressed students judged their control over an outcome accurately while non-depressed students overestimated it. Each leg has been disputed by named authors in the primary literature. A meta-analysis reports a depressive-realism effect close to zero, and a preregistered replication found none. The standard measure of unrealistic optimism has been argued to produce the "optimistic" pattern from unbiased respondents, and its defenders reply that the criticism reaches one of four measures. The health benefit was called unproven in 1994 and a 2015 review still advises caution. What survives is a tendency to rate oneself above the average peer, reported as robust in a large meta-analysis. That is a tilt in judgement. Nothing read this run shows that the accurate view is unavailable to the person who tilts, and Taylor and Brown themselves describe the illusions as mild and held in check by the social world. The subject therefore fails as a separate void and is recommended as a fold.

## The Void

### Nature of the Limit

The candidate belongs to the Occluded category. The claim would be that a true assessment of one's own qualities, control and prospects is kept from a healthy person by the same processes that keep them healthy, so that the accurate view comes only with distress. If that held, self-knowledge and well-being would trade against each other, and the limit would be self-maintained in the sense of [self-maintained-cognitive-limits](/voids/self-maintained-cognitive-limits/).

### Evidence for the Limit

Sorted by how well each leg stands in the sources read:

1. **People rate themselves above the average peer.** Reported as robust in a meta-analysis of 291 samples (Zell et al. 2020, abstract only). This is the best-supported leg. It is a statement about groups.
2. **People judge bad events less likely for themselves than for others.** Disputed. Harris and Hahn (2011) argue that the usual measure yields this pattern from unbiased answers; Harris et al. (2017) report no residual optimism once the artifacts are controlled; Shepperd et al. (2013) reply that the criticism reaches one of four measures.
3. **People take good news to heart more than bad.** Disputed. Sharot et al. (2011, n = 19) report it; Shah et al. (2016) call the pattern a statistical artifact; Garrett and Sharot (2017) say it survives correction; Burton et al. (2022) find the same asymmetry for neutral events, where no motive is in play.
4. **Depressed people see their own control accurately.** Weak. The meta-analytic effect is d = −.07 and both groups show a positive bias (Moore & Fresco 2012, abstract only); a preregistered replication found no relation (Dev et al. 2022, preprint read); Msetfi et al. (2005) attribute the original pattern to reduced processing of context.
5. **The illusions cause good functioning.** Called unproven by Colvin and Block (1994); still treated with caution by Bortolotti and Antrobus (2015).

What none of the sources shows is the step a void needs. Legs 1 to 3 are about the direction of average error. An occlusion requires more: that the accurate view cannot be reached by the person, or cannot be held. Taylor and Brown deny this of their own thesis in 1994, describing mild distortions that the social world keeps mild. Jefferson and colleagues add that for a single person it is often impossible to say whether the optimistic belief is false at all. Leg 4 was the only evidence that accuracy and health come apart in a given mind, and it is the weakest of the five.

### Phenomenology

No first-person literature was found or looked for. A self-enhancing judgement presumably does not feel like one, as with the [bias blind spot](/voids/biological-cognitive-closure/), but that is an inference from neighbouring findings and no source read this run reports it. [UNVERIFIED lead]

## Approaches to the Edge

### Direct Methods

The tilt can be measured against an outside standard where one exists (actual control in a contingency task, test scores in an overconfidence task). Dev et al. did this and found bias in both directions depending on the actual level of control: overestimation where there was none, underestimation where control was high (preprint pp. 18–19). [VERIFIED at source: preprint] That pattern reads more like noisy estimation pulled toward the middle than like a guarded secret.

### Indirect Methods

Comparison across people: if depressed people were accurate, their judgements would mark where the healthy view departs from the facts. This was the method's whole promise, and it is the leg that failed to replicate.

### What AI Might See

A system that scores people's self-assessments against outcomes can report a group's average tilt, and that requires no claim about what the system experiences. Whether language models show a comparable tilt in assessing their own outputs is an empirical question about calibration that was not researched here. [UNVERIFIED lead] Nothing in this note bears on whether such systems are conscious; the Map's standing position ([P-AC1](/positions/ai-consciousness-scope/#p-ac1)) is that this has low probability for current digital systems and is not ruled out.

## Connection to Tenets

A compliant tenet section for any text built from this note would say the following.

None of the Map's [tenets](/tenets/) is supported by this material.

**[Dualism](/tenets/#dualism)** gains nothing. The data are self-ratings and probability estimates compared with base rates and task parameters. Every account offered for them in the sources is a physicalist one: motivated belief formation (Taylor and Brown; Sharot), evolved bias in belief-forming systems (McKay and Dennett), scale and sampling artifacts in unbiased estimators (Harris and Hahn; Shah and colleagues), and reduced context processing in depression (Msetfi and colleagues). None of the authors cited argues for dualism and none should be read as lending it support. An illusionist can add this literature to the case that self-report is unreliable; the Map's reply to illusionism is graded in [P-RR1](/positions/replies-to-rivals/#p-rr1) as a verdict on a live programme, and nothing here improves that reply. As a catalogue entry the material would add no weight either, since the voids register has [suspended the cumulative step](/positions/voids-as-evidence/#p-v3).

**[Occam's Razor Has Limits](/tenets/#occams-limits)** is illustrated in a small way and from an unexpected side. "Healthy people are deluded about themselves and the depressed see truly" is a simple story that was widely repeated and has not held up. The illustration is of a simple account outrunning its evidence, which a physicalist psychology found and corrected by its own methods. It is no evidence for the tenet.

**[Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction)** is not engaged. Whether optimistic belief changes outcomes is a question about beliefs as psychological states, which every framework grants can affect behaviour. **Minimal Quantum Interaction** and **No Many Worlds** are not engaged.

### Implications

For the Map the useful result is negative. [interested-party-void](/voids/interested-party-void/) argues that a mind with stakes in the answer cannot investigate itself neutrally, and its evidence section currently cites terror-management research and Kunda on motivated reasoning. Positive illusions are the obvious third exhibit and are absent. Adding them honestly means adding a contested exhibit: a tilt in comparative self-rating that is reported as robust, with the stronger claims (accuracy only in depression, illusion as a condition of health, optimism as motivated) each disputed in the primary literature. That fits the page's existing pattern, which already reports the failed multi-lab replication of mortality salience beside the claim it qualifies.
