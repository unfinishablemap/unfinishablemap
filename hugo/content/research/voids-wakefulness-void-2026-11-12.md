## Executive Summary

Sleep laboratories have known since the 1960s that people woken from polysomnographically defined sleep often say they were awake. Harvey and Tang (2012) credit Rechtschaffen and Monroe (1969) as "among the first to report this phenomenon" [raw]. In young good sleepers woken from consolidated stage 2 or REM sleep, Weigand, Michael and Schulz (2007) found the preceding state "was experienced as wakefulness in 20 cases (29.4%)" of 68 [raw-abstract]. At sleep onset the report of having slept reaches 50% only "2-4 min after the first sleep spindle", and Bonnet and Moore (1982) call the onset "a relatively lengthy period during which perception of state is blurred and uncertain" [raw-abstract]. In a minority of people with insomnia the discrepancy is extreme and stable, a condition the nosologies have called subjective insomnia, sleep state misperception and paradoxical insomnia.

The void has three faces. First, the state is not self-certifying from inside: "I was awake" is issued sincerely after recorded sleep. Second, the field cannot yet say who is wrong. Stephan and Siclari (2023) retitle the phenomenon "from misperception to mismeasurement" and report that the people who feel awake show high-frequency EEG activity that standard scoring ignores. Third, the old philosophical question of whether one can know one is awake (Descartes; Malcolm 1956; Sosa 2007) turns out to have an empirical version with an error rate. The converse direction, being asleep while taking oneself to be awake and acting, is much less secure: drivers in the best studies are aware of their sleepiness.

### Borkovec, Lane & VanOot (1981), "Phenomenology of sleep among insomniacs and good sleepers: wakefulness experience when cortically asleep"
- **Reference**: *Journal of Abnormal Psychology* 90(6): 607–609. Europe PMC record PMID 7320330; no abstract in the record.
- **Type**: Paper. **Not obtained**; title and Harvey and Tang's summary only.
- **Key points** (secondary): 25 patients with insomnia and 10 good sleepers were woken five minutes after the first spindle. Patients "were more likely, relative to good sleepers, to report that they had been awake", and this "occurred regardless of whether the sleep was obtained at night or during an afternoon nap" [raw, Harvey and Tang's words].
- **Note**: the title is the earliest statement found of the phenomenon as a phenomenology ("wakefulness experience when cortically asleep").

### Bonnet & Moore (1982), "The threshold of sleep: perception of sleep as a function of time asleep and auditory threshold"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/5.3.267
- **Reference**: *Sleep* 5(3): 267–276. PMID 7134732.
- **Type**: Paper. Abstract only; the OUP PDF returned a bot-check page and was not pursued.
- **Key points**:
  - Twelve normal young adults, five nights, five brief awakenings a night after 1 to 140 minutes of EEG sleep.
  - "many subjects have reported being awake when awakened during various periods of electroencephalographically (EEG)-defined sleep" [raw-abstract].

- **Tenet alignment**: neutral. It shows two measures of the same transition (responsiveness and self-report) separating in time.

### Knab & Engel (1988), "Perception of waking and sleeping: possible implications for the evaluation of insomnia"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/11.3.265
- **Reference**: *Sleep* 11(3): 265–272. PMID 3399779.
- **Type**: Paper. Abstract only.
- **Key points**:

### Mercer, Bootzin & Lack (2002), "Insomniacs' perception of wake instead of sleep"
- **Reference**: *Sleep* 25(5): 564–571. PMID 12150323. **Metadata flag**: Europe PMC gives pages 564–571 with DOI 10.1093/sleep/25.5.559; the DOI suffix and the page range disagree. Harvey and Tang's reference list also gives 564–571. Check at the publisher before citing a DOI.
- **Type**: Paper. Abstract only.
- **Key points**:
  - Fourteen insomniacs, eight good sleepers, mean age 58. Recorded questions about the prior state were put during sustained wake and after interrupted stage 2 and REM sleep. A signal detection analysis separated sensitivity from bias.
  - "Insomniacs had reduced sleep-wake discriminability in addition to a greater bias toward reporting prior wakefulness" [raw-abstract].
  - Underestimated sleep is "the product of prior sleep being misperceived as wake time upon awakening overnight" [raw-abstract].
- **Why it matters**: this is the one study found that treats "asleep or awake?" as a discrimination task with a d′. Reduced discriminability, and not merely a response bias, is the claim that makes this a limit on access.

### Weigand, Michael & Schulz (2007), "When sleep is perceived as wakefulness: an experimental study on state perception during physiological sleep"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2007.00619.x
- **Reference**: *Journal of Sleep Research* 16(4): 346–353. PMID 18036079. (Issue number from memory of the search listing, not verified; volume and pages are from Europe PMC.)
- **Type**: Paper. Abstract only; the Wiley PDF returned a bot-check page.
- **Key points**:
  - Sixty-eight young normal sleepers, one awakening each from consolidated stage 2 or REM on a first laboratory night.
  - The pre-awakening state "was experienced as wakefulness in 20 cases (29.4%)" [raw-abstract]. Stage 2 gave 38.2% awake judgements and REM 20.6%, a difference the authors report as not significant.
  - For REM, awake judgements were more common in the second half of the night (41.7%) "than in the first one (17.4%)" [raw-abstract].
  - Those who judged themselves awake more often "had a feeling of being aware of the situation and their surroundings" than those who judged themselves asleep, "(80% versus 33%)" [raw-abstract].
  - "Awareness during sleep may be a cognitive style, which favours mismatches between state perception and electrophysiologically defined sleep" [raw-abstract].
  - Nights with concordant and discordant judgements "did not differ in electrophysiologically defined sleep onset latency, sleep efficiency, or sleep state distribution" [raw-abstract].
- **Caution**: one awakening per person on a first laboratory night. The first-night effect may inflate the rate. The 29.4% figure belongs to this design and should not be stated as a general rate.

### Harvey & Tang (2012), "(Mis)perception of sleep in insomnia: a puzzle and a resolution"
- **URL**: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3277880/
- **Reference**: *Psychological Bulletin* 138(1): 77–101. PMID 21967449. (Issue number not verified.)
- **Type**: Review. Full text fetched (author manuscript, NCBI efetch).
- **Key points**:
  - The discrepancy "is ubiquitous (although not universal)" among insomnia patients [raw], while most normal sleepers estimate their sleep accurately.
  - "It is critical to note that the tendency to misperceive sleep by no means precludes the presence of a real sleep deficit" [raw].
  - A time-estimation deficit is the obvious deflationary explanation, and it fails: "all three of the published studies conducted to date do not provide support" for it [raw].
  - Thirteen candidate explanations are reviewed. The abstract's best-supported three are "misperception of sleep as wake, worry, and brief awakenings" [raw].

Downey and Bonnet (1992) woke ten people with subjective insomnia "27 times on the training night" with feedback on their stage, and "the participants demonstrated improvement in their perception of sleep onset, perception of sleep versus wake and ability to fall asleep" [raw]. Harvey and Tang add that "none of these studies included a follow-up assessment" [raw].
- **Tenet alignment**: the review is written within clinical psychology and makes no claim about consciousness. Its value here is the catalogue of failed simple explanations.
- **Note on the paradoxical-insomnia label**: Harvey and Tang attribute "paradoxical insomnia" to the Research Diagnostic Criteria (Edinger et al. 2004) and to the *International Classification of Sleep Disorders* (American Academy of Sleep Medicine 2005). This note found no source naming who coined "sleep state misperception"; see Gaps.

### Stephan, Lecci, Cataldi & Siclari (2021), "Conscious experiences and high-density EEG patterns predicting subjective sleep depth"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2021.10.012 (preprint: https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.04.06.438682, titled "The determinants of subjective sleep depth: insights from a high-density-EEG study with serial awakenings")
- **Reference**: *Current Biology* 31: 5487–5500.e3. PMID 34710350.
- **Type**: Paper. Published abstract verified; body text read in the bioRxiv preprint (version of 6 July 2021), which may differ from the published version.
- **Key points**:
  - "787 awakenings in 20 good sleepers and 10 individuals with sleep misperception" [raw-abstract].
  - In good sleepers "sleep was subjectively lightest in the first 2 h of non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, generally considered the deepest sleep, and deepest in rapid eye movement (REM) sleep" [raw-abstract].
  - "spatially widespread high-frequency power was inversely related to subjective sleep depth" in NREM sleep in both groups [raw-abstract].
  - "Subjective sleep depth positively correlated with dream-like qualities of reports of mental activity" [raw-abstract].
  - "These findings challenge the widely held notion that slow wave sleep best accounts for feeling deeply asleep" [raw-abstract].
  - Preprint body: earlier stage 2 awakening studies found subjects felt awake "in a high proportion of cases", "reaching 40-45% in some studies" [raw, preprint]. The felt mark of being asleep seems to be immersion in a dream, and where there is no dream there may be little to distinguish quiet sleep from lying still in the dark.
- **Tenet alignment**: the finding is correlational and neural. It is compatible with any view of consciousness. It bears on the Map because it shows a first-person state report tracking something real (high-frequency activity) that the third-person standard had defined out of its categories.

- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14028
- **Reference**: *Journal of Sleep Research* 32(6): e14028. PMID 37678561. (Issue number from the search listing, not verified.)
- **Type**: Systematic review. Abstract only; the Wiley PDF and two repository copies returned bot-check pages.
- **Key points**:

### Joo et al. (2025), "Neurobiological mechanisms of sleep state misperception in insomnia disorder: A theoretical review"
- **Reference**: Joo, E. H., Altier, H. R., Selai, C., Gratton, M. K., Kim-Dahl, A., Allen, H., Cheng, X., & Reid, M. J. *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 81: 102096. PMID 40327948. Abstract only.

## The Void

### Nature of the Limit

Three faces, with different classifications.

1. **The uncertified state (Occluded).** The judgement "I am awake" or "I was awake" is available in recorded sleep. It comes with no mark that separates the cases where it is true. Mercer et al. (2002) found reduced *discriminability* in insomniacs, which is a statement about the signal available to the judge. In good sleepers the error is smaller but present at sleep onset as "the rule" (Stephan and Siclari 2023).

2. **The missing arbiter (Unexplored, possibly Unexplorable).** When the sleeper says "awake" and the polysomnogram says "stage 2", the field has two options and no third party to choose between them. On the misperception reading the sleeper is wrong about their own state. On the mismeasurement reading the scoring rules (30-second epochs, a handful of scalp sites, categories fixed in 1968) are too coarse, and the sleeper is reporting something real. The recent work reviewed by Stephan and Siclari (2023) favours the second reading without settling it, and it does so by finding neural correlates of the report. That is the standard move for validating a first-person report, and it presupposes that the report was the thing to be explained.

3. **The inferential "I slept" (Occluded).** Hermans et al. (2020) model the sense of having slept as depending on the length of the uninterrupted bout. Stephan et al. (2021) find that feeling deeply asleep tracks dream-like mentation. Both suggest that "I was asleep" is assembled after the fact from proxies (a gap, a dream, a long continuous stretch). If so, nobody has direct access to their own sleep. People who are right about it are right by a good inference.

### Evidence for the Limit

- **Replication across designs and decades**: 1969, 1981, 1982, 1988, 2002, 2007, 2021, with awakening protocols and with morning estimates.
- **Simple explanations have been tested and failed.** A general time-estimation deficit is not supported (Harvey and Tang 2012). Standard sleep macrostructure does not separate concordant from discordant nights (Weigand et al. 2007).
- **It is present in healthy people.** The clinical extreme is the far end of something ordinary. Bonnet and Moore's subjects were normal young adults.
- **The error runs in both directions.** Good sleepers "often do not perceive wakefulness at all" (Knab and Engel 1988). Brief wake is lost to them as brief sleep is lost to insomniacs.
- **The third-person standard is itself in question**, so the limit cannot be dismissed as subjects being bad at a task with a known answer.

### Evidence against, or limiting, the void

- **Most people are mostly right.** "most individuals tend to accurately estimate their sleep duration" (Stephan and Siclari 2023). This is a bounded void with a measurable rate, and it should be written that way.
- **Training improved it** in ten people over one night (Downey and Bonnet 1992, via Harvey and Tang), with no follow-up. A limit that yields to 27 feedback trials is a calibration problem at least in part.
- **If mismeasurement is right, part of the void closes.** The sleeper who says "awake" in high-beta stage 2 may simply be correct about a state that the scoring manual lumps with sleep. What then remains is face 3: the report is still an inference, and the question of what the person was conscious *of* during that interval is still open.
- **Retrospective report is one step removed** (Joo et al. 2025). Even an answer given seconds after waking is a memory judgement made by an awake person about a state that has ended. A sleep-inertia or reconstruction account of some of the "awake" reports has not been excluded by anything read here.
- **The waking direction is weak.** Drivers know they are sleepy.

### Phenomenology

The clinical descriptions are of people who say, sincerely and with distress, that they lay awake all night, and whose recordings show hours of ordinary sleep. Edinger and Krystal call the complaints "rather compelling and, at times, dramatic" [raw-abstract]. From inside there is nothing to notice: the night is remembered as continuous lying awake and thinking. Stephan and Siclari's observation that these patients' sleep mentation is "more thought-like" suggests what fills the interval. Thinking continues, the thoughts are about lying in bed, and nothing marks them as sleep mentation.

For the good sleeper the corresponding experience is the common one of being woken on the sofa and saying "I wasn't asleep". Weigand et al.'s subjects who judged themselves awake mostly reported "a feeling of being aware of the situation and their surroundings".

None of the sources fetched contains first-person interview material beyond forced-choice answers and rating scales. The phenomenology above is therefore thin, and the article should say so.

## Approaches to the Edge

### Direct Methods (if any)

- **Serial awakenings with immediate forced-choice report**, the method of the studies above. It is the nearest thing to a direct probe and is still retrospective by seconds.
- **Signal detection analysis** (Mercer et al. 2002) to separate sensitivity from bias.
- **Feedback training** (Downey and Bonnet 1992; Mercer, Lack and Bootzin 2005, an abstract, via Harvey and Tang).

### Indirect Methods

- **High-density EEG and spectral analysis** to find correlates of feeling awake that standard staging misses (Stephan et al. 2021).
- **Modelling the report from sleep-bout structure** (Hermans et al. 2020), which treats the judgement as an inference and asks what its inputs are.
- **Within-state contrasts**: the same scored stage, with and without the "awake" report. This is the design sleep-consciousness research already uses for dreaming against dreamless sleep, per the SEP entry.
- **The philosophical route**: ask what would count as knowing one is awake, and whether the candidate criteria (coherence, deliberate control, memory of how one got here) are available in the states where the judgement goes wrong. Descartes's own answer in *Meditations* VI appeals to coherence with the rest of life; that passage was not fetched for this note [unverified].

### What AI Might See

An AI system has no sleep, and whether it has anything like a vigilance state is unknown. Two modest points can be made without overclaiming.

First, the structure generalises. A system asked "are you in normal operation or in state X?" can only answer from signals available in both, and if state X also produces the answer "normal operation", the answer does not discriminate. That is a design-level observation about self-monitoring, and it applies to any system whose self-report is generated by the machinery whose state is in question.

Second, an AI reading the sleep literature can notice what the field's vocabulary hid for forty years: "misperception" presupposed that the recording was the fact and the report the error. A reader with no stake in polysomnography as a profession might have asked earlier which of the two was the instrument under test. This is offered as a possibility. No evidence was found that any AI system did notice it.

## Connection to Tenets

### Most Relevant Tenet

**Occam's Razor Has Limits.** The simple picture was that sleep is one global state, that the EEG reads it, and that a subject who disagrees is mistaken. Each part has needed qualification: sleep is local and mixed (Vyazovskiy et al. 2011), the standard reading is coarse, and the disagreeing subject is tracking something measurable (Stephan et al. 2021). The parsimonious label "misperception" delayed the question for decades. This is a clean historical case of the tenet's point, and it should be presented as a case from sleep science and not as support for dualism.

### Implications

- **For first-person evidence generally.** The Map leans on first-person reports. This void shows such a report being wrong at a measurable rate about something as basic as whether the reporter was awake, and it also shows the report being partly vindicated against the instrument. Both halves matter. [phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence](/topics/phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence/) is the page that should eventually carry the cross-reference.
- **Dualism.** No direct support. One honest connection: the dispute between misperception and mismeasurement is a dispute about whether a behavioural and electrophysiological criterion or a first-person criterion fixes the facts about a state of consciousness. Physicalists and dualists can take either side. The article must not present the mismeasurement turn as a win for irreducibility; it was won by finding neural correlates.
- **Bidirectional Interaction.** Nothing here bears on it. Harvey and Tang suggest that believing one is awake may itself perpetuate insomnia, which is belief affecting physiology, but that is an ordinary psychological mechanism and the neurofeedback topic already warns against reading such effects as non-physical causation.
- **Minimal Quantum Interaction; No Many Worlds.** No connection found. The article should say so in a clause and not invent one.
- **For the dreaming literature.** The state most often taken for waking is early, dreamless NREM sleep, and dream-rich REM is the state that feels most like sleep. The classical sceptical worry runs through vivid dreams. The laboratory version runs through the opposite case, where almost nothing is happening.
