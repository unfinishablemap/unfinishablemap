## Executive Summary

Doxastic voluntarism is the thesis that at least some beliefs are under direct voluntary control; involuntarism, the universal denial, is the contemporary majority view but is held on two different grounds that behave differently. **Psychological involuntarism** (Alston 1988) rests on an introspective test (try, for $500,000,000, to believe the United States is still a British colony) and leaves the limit contingent; **conceptual involuntarism** (Williams 1973; Hieronymi 2006; Shah & Velleman 2005) derives it from belief's constitutive aim or norm of truth and makes it bind any believer. Williams's argument, the best-known conceptual route, "is widely held to be unsuccessful—at least without modifications" [raw SEP]: Winters (1979) showed one could believe at will without knowing one had the power, and Bennett's (1990) Credamites believe "just like that" and forget how. Hieronymi grants the Credamites "a successful counter to Williams' argument, as given" [raw] and relocates the impossibility in commitment-constitution: believing *is* answering the question whether *p*, so one cannot do it for reasons that do not bear on *p*, and "you can no more intend at will than believe at will" [raw]. Against the whole involuntarist front stand doxastic compatibilism (Steup; Ryan), permissivist voluntarism (Ginet; Roeber), activity views of belief (Boyle; Sosa), and a 2024–26 revival of direct voluntarism (Soter 2024; Singh 2026; Crowe 2026), while Strawson (2003) argues from the other side that judgement "neither [is] nor can be a matter of action" [abstract]. For the Map, the debate matters at a single joint: the live pages ([assent-void](/voids/assent-void/), [decision-void](/voids/decision-void/)) already treat belief as a *state* not adopted at will and judgement as an *act*; what a new concept article can add is the taxonomy of control (direct / indirect / long-range / evaluative / managerial / back-end), the Winters–Bennett–Hieronymi repair chain, the compatibilist and Frankfurt-style dissolutions the reviewers found missing, and a clear statement that none of the tenets bears on whether belief is voluntary.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Doxastic Voluntarism" (Boespflug & Jackson 2024)
- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/doxastic-voluntarism/
- **Type**: Encyclopedia. First published 1 October 2024; copyright line "Mark Boespflug … Elizabeth Jackson" [raw].
- **Verified**: raw HTML fetched; every quotation below grepped in it. Note that the em-dash in "unsuccessful—at least" is a true em-dash and the phrase "widely held to be unsuccessful" is broken by non-breaking spaces in the HTML, so a naive literal grep returns zero; use `\s+` between words.
- **Key points**:
  - Definition: "doxastic voluntarism is the thesis that our beliefs are under our voluntary control, or that we can believe at will … Doxastic voluntarism is an existential claim: the view that at least some of our beliefs are voluntary. Involuntarism, then, is the view that none of our beliefs are voluntary."
  - Alston's four-way taxonomy of control: "basic voluntary control" = **direct control**, "the ability to Φ 'right away' or 'just like that', via an uninterrupted voluntary act (Alston 1988: 263); in our terms, direct control implies temporal immediacy and causal basicality." The entry notes that most authors take voluntarism "to be a thesis about direct control" and that "The possibility of direct doxastic control, however, is controversial (and in fact, widely rejected by contemporary authors)".
  - Rival definitions of "voluntary": parity with action (Steup 2008; Roeber 2019); intentional belief (Scott-Kakures 1994; Steup 2012; Peels 2017); believing "intentionally for a practical reason" (Hieronymi 2006, 2009; Setiya 2008; Buckareff 2014). The entry's own caution: "insofar as these arguments rely on a specific notion of 'voluntary', even if they are sound, it doesn't follow that beliefs are involuntary full-stop."
  - Two routes to involuntarism: §3.1 psychological (Alston; Plantinga; Nottelmann; Peels; Booth 2017) and §3.2 conceptual, "The arguably more popular route". "This view—that belief aims at truth—forms the backbone for many involuntarist arguments."
  - Five defences of voluntarism: epistemic permissivism (§4.1), doxastic compatibilism (§4.2), voluntarism via scepticism (§4.3, Descartes; Weatherson 2008; Frederick 2013), one-off voluntarism (§4.4, Feldman; Peels 2015; Reisner 2013), non-standard views of belief (§4.5, Boyle 2009; Aronowitz; Hedden; Zimmerman).
  - §5 empirical work: Turri, Rose & Buckwalter (2018) found lay participants "predominately agreeing … that individuals, described in a series of vignettes, are able to make choices in believing"; Nottelmann, Booth & Lomholt (2023) reply that laypeople "refer to a belief as chosen, even when it is acquired through obviously indirect means"; Cusimano & Goodwin (2020) and Cusimano, Zorrilla, Danks & Lombrozo (2024) find people "attribute a greater ability to others in voluntarily changing beliefs than they do to themselves".
- **Absent from the entry** (relevant to the reviewers' S8 list): Sosa and Strawson are not mentioned anywhere in the entry (zero hits for both surnames and for "Mental Ballistics"); "Frankfurt" occurs only in a bibliographic place-name. The Frankfurt-style route therefore has to be documented from Peels 2013 / Steup 2023 / Odoj 2025 below, not from the SEP.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The entry is a map of positions; nothing in it bears on dualism or interaction.
- **Quote**: "While many regard Williams' claim that belief involves a truth-aim as a key insight, his argument is widely held to be unsuccessful—at least without modifications."

### Williams, B. (1973). "Deciding to Believe." *Problems of the Self*, ch. 9, pp. 136–151
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511621253.011
- **Type**: Book chapter. Pages 136–151 confirmed at OpenAlex (the 09-28 note's gap on the page range is closed; the 1970 original venue was not checked).
- **Key points**:
  - Scope: "I am not going to take religious and moral beliefs, but cases of more straightforward factual belief" and "I shall be talking about belief as a psychological state" [abstract, verbatim].
  - The argument, as Hieronymi quotes it [raw]: "If I could acquire a belief at will, I could acquire it whether it was true or not. If in full consciousness I could will to acquire a 'belief' irrespective of its truth, it is unclear that before the event I could seriously think of it as a belief, i.e., as something purporting to represent reality. … With regard to no belief could I know … that I had acquired it at will. - **Tenet alignment**: Neutral; the argument is about the concept of belief.

### Hieronymi, P. (2006). "Controlling Attitudes." *Pacific Philosophical Quarterly*, 87(1), 45–74
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0114.2006.00247.x ; open copy https://escholarship.org/uc/item/3ms6v6bz (author's postprint dated 12 October 2004; its page numbers are the preprint's, not PPQ's)
- **Type**: Paper [raw]. 16,524 words extracted; every quotation below grep-verified.
- **Key points**:
  - Thesis (abstract, verbatim): "although belief is subject to two quite robust forms of agency, 'believing at will' is impossible; one cannot believe in the way one ordinarily acts. Further, the same is true of intention … It turns out, perhaps surprisingly, that you can no more intend at will than believe at will."
  - On Williams: "Moreover, this is, as suspected by Bernard Williams, a conceptual matter, traceable in part (but only in part) to the relationship between belief and truth".
  - On Bennett: "Bennett's example seems to me a successful counter to Williams' argument, as given. The Credamites are capable of believing 'just like that,' and yet they can regard the beliefs at which they thus arrive as aiming at truth." And: "I agree with Bennett that Williams' intended point is right. Despite the plausibility of the Credamites, believing in the way you raise your right hand or look left is a conceptual impossibility."
  - The two forms of agency: "we control our beliefs by evaluating (and reevaluating) what is true. Call this 'evaluative' control." Managerial control is the manipulation of an attitude as an object; "the commitment-constitution of belief does impose a constraint on our attempts at belief management—we cannot produce a belief in ourselves without coming to be committed to its content as true—I will argue that this constraint does not impugn the claim that we exercise managerial control over our beliefs."
  - The immediacy claim the Map's assent-void Seam now rests on: "there are no possible cases in which you answer positively the question whether p but are prevented from believing p. In answering the question positively, one has already, therein, believed. The immediacy of evaluative control is thus not temporal or causal but rather a consequence of the constitutive relation between the commitment to p as true and the belief."
  - Indirectness: "while immediate, our control over our beliefs is typically indirect in a way our control over ordinary objects often is not. … you control your belief by answering a question about its content—a question that only rarely concerns your own psychology."
  - Her core impossibility claim, independent of psychology: "Regardless of one's contingent psychological constitution, believing is not an action in its own right, but rather the possible product of a distinct managerial action".
  - She also disputes Shah's disposition requirement: "If Shah were right, it would be impossible to believe at will due to this disposition. … This seems to me both too strong—I don't see why some creature (like a Credamite) couldn't have the concept of belief".
  - Her 2009 follow-up (CJP Suppl. 35, 149–187) [abstract]: "nothing could qualify both as having been done 'at will,' in the relevant sense, and as a belief. Thus, no believer could believe at will. If my arguments are correct, our inability to believe at will reveals no genuine lack in our powers of mind, any more than an inability to draw a Square circle reveals a lack of artistic skill." SEP: Setiya (2008) objects that the argument "proves too much: it entails that you could not voluntarily intend to form a belief"; Hieronymi (2009) accepts the result; Vermaire (2022) replies to both.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral on dualism; relevant to Bidirectional Interaction only as a description of what conscious agency over belief looks like from inside (reasons-responsiveness, not manipulation). Fully compatible with physicalism.
- **Quote**: "you can no more intend at will than believe at will."

### Shah, N. & Velleman, J. D. (2005). "Doxastic Deliberation." *The Philosophical Review*, 114(4), 497–534
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1215/00318108-114-4-497

### Steup, M. — doxastic compatibilism (2000; 2008; 2012; 2017; 2023)
- **URLs**: "Doxastic Voluntarism and Epistemic Deontology", *Acta Analytica* 15(1), 25–56 (2000) [SEP bibliography]; "Doxastic Freedom", *Synthese* 161(3), 375–392, https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-006-9090-4 (print 2008; OpenAlex year 2007); "Belief Control and Intentionality", *Synthese* 188(2), 145–163, https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-011-9919-3 (2012); "Control Over and Responsibility for Belief", Routledge 2023, pp. 114–131, https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003298861-8
- **Type**: Papers/chapter. 2000/2008/2012 [SEP] (Springer redirected to an SSO page); 2023 [abstract, verbatim at OpenAlex].
- **Key points**:
  - SEP: Steup "argues that compatibilism entails that (most of) our actions and our beliefs are free"; "most of our doxastic attitudes fulfill the most popular compatibilist requirements for free action: e.g., they fit with reactive attitudes, fit with our higher-order attitudes, are weakly intentional, and are caused by a reasons-responsive process (2008: 390)"; treating "responsiveness to practical reasons" as grounding freedom while "responsiveness to epistemic reasons does not" is "chauvinism" (2008: 388); deliberation is the central example and non-deliberative belief "can still be implicitly intentional, similar to intentional, automatic actions such as brushing one's teeth (2012: 155)"; he "generally doesn't argue for compatibilism, but makes his arguments conditional on its truth (2000: 55; 2008: 391)".
  - 2023 chapter abstract [verbatim]: "Steup applies John Martin Fischer's semi-compatibilism to the doxastic area … if semi-compatibilism is true, then human agents are epistemically responsible for what they believe even if determinism is true and there is no such thing as free will. … when one applies the toolbox of Fischer's semi-compatibilism to the question of whether human agents have voluntary control over their beliefs, the result is that believing is no less under the control of their will, and thus no less intentional, than acting is." This is the explicitly Frankfurt-descended route (Fischer's semi-compatibilism is built on Frankfurt cases) the reviewers found missing.
  - Objections (SEP): Peels 2017 ("compatibilist control is not sufficient for doxastic responsibility"; influence is), Perrine 2020, Buckareff 2006, Nottelmann 2006, Booth 2009 and 2013 (*Synthese* 191(8), 1867–1880, "On some recent moves in defence of doxastic compatibilism"), Schmitt 2015, Wagner 2017.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral; compatibilism about belief is orthogonal to whether consciousness influences physics. Note that the Map's free-will pages lean libertarian; a concept article should say that Steup's conditional ("if compatibilism is true") is a conditional the Map does not assert, while his parity point (belief and action are reasons-responsive in the same way) stands or falls independently of the Map's position on determinism.

### Singh, K. (2026). "Belief as Commitment to the Truth." In *The Nature of Belief* (OUP), ch. 9, pp. 189–214
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/9780197744208.003.0009 (published 1 April 2026; OUP chapter page not reachable)
- **Type**: Book chapter [abstract, verbatim at Crossref/OpenAlex]. Same volume as Smithies, "Belief as a Feeling of Conviction" (ch. 11, doi 10.1093/9780197744208.003.0011) and Mugg, "Cognitive Architectures, Kinds, and Belief" (ch. 10).
- **Key points**: "belief involves more than just regarding a proposition as true; it requires committing to the truth by subjecting one's representation of it to a normative standard of correctness. … It also addresses three key puzzles related to belief: doxastic voluntarism, the aim of belief, and Moore's paradox. ## Major Positions

### Psychological involuntarism
- **Proponents**: Alston 1988; Plantinga 1993; Nottelmann 2006 ("entirely convincing"); Peels 2017; Booth 2017 (contingent, via suspension)
- **Core claim**: we lack the capacity to believe at will; the limit is a fact about us.
- **Key arguments**: the colony test; the $500,000,000 inducement; absence of any felt "doing".
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral. Note the method is introspective, which the Map elsewhere treats as evidence; SEP §5 shows it is contestable by vignette studies in both directions (Turri et al. 2018 vs Nottelmann et al. 2023; Cusimano & Goodwin 2020 self/other asymmetry).

### Conceptual involuntarism
- **Proponents**: Williams 1973; Winters 1979 (modest form); Scott-Kakures 1994; Hieronymi 2006, 2009; Shah & Velleman 2005; McHugh 2012–17; Buckareff 2014; O'Shaughnessy 1980 ("The Unwillability of Belief", SEP bibliography)
- **Core claim**: belief's constitutive relation to truth excludes forming it for reasons unconnected to truth; so no believer, whatever its make-up, believes at will.
- **Key arguments**: Williams's knowledge-of-the-power argument (repaired by Winters; countered by Credamites); Hieronymi's commitment-constitution (believing *is* answering "is p?"; extrinsic reasons cannot answer it); Scott-Kakures's "cognitive blind spot"; Shah & Velleman's transparency.
- **Status**: the SEP's "arguably more popular route", but with the caveat that each argument "relies on a specific notion of voluntary" so "it doesn't follow that beliefs are involuntary full-stop."
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral; if sound it binds artificial believers too, but the arguments are about human doxastic deliberation (the universal-quantifier scope check the 10-07 synthesis asked for applies).

### Doxastic compatibilism / parity
- **Proponents**: Steup 2000–2023; Ryan 2003; Roeber 2019 (parity); McHugh 2014 and Shah 2002 (reasons-responsiveness without voluntarism)
- **Core claim**: beliefs meet the same compatibilist conditions (reasons-responsiveness, fit with higher-order attitudes) that make actions free; denying it is "chauvinism".
- **Key arguments**: deliberation as doxastic decision; Fischer's guidance control transferred to belief (Steup 2023); Ryan's "cannot just do" analogy.
- **Objections**: Peels 2013/2014/2017; Booth 2009/2013; Perrine 2020.
- **Relation to site tenets**: conditional on compatibilism, which the Map's free-will pages do not assume; the parity claim itself is independent of determinism.

### Permissivist and one-off voluntarism
- **Proponents**: Ginet 2001 (juror, poker, locked door; evidence "noncompelling"); Nickel 2010; Roeber 2019/2020 (equipollent cases); Feldman 2001 (turn the lights off); Peels 2015 and Reisner 2013 (evidence-dependent beliefs); Crowe 2026 (existential reasons)
- **Core claim**: where evidence underdetermines, or where believing makes the belief true, one can choose.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral; the SEP's point that *perceived* permissiveness is what matters is the useful one for a phenomenology-minded article.

### Activity and judgement-as-act views
- **Proponents**: Boyle 2009; Hieronymi (two forms of agency); Shah & Velleman (judgment an act); Sosa 2013/2015 [not reached]; Peacocke 2026 (judgment-wh as intentional action); Ziff 2024; Singh 2026 ("belief is a volitional act")
- **Core claim**: believing or judging is something one does, though not "at will" in the practical-reasons sense.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral; this family is what the assent-void's repaired Seam already adopts (state not chosen; judgement an act).

### Ballistic / passivity view
- **Proponents**: Strawson 2003; (Li 2026 labels the family)
- **Core claim**: thought and judgement "neither are nor can be a matter of action"; freedom there is only "rational necessitation".
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral; supports the passivity reading of the Map's voids and must be named as such so the corpus is not sampled from one survey.

### Direct voluntarism revived (2024–26)
- **Proponents**: Soter 2024 (back-end control); Singh 2026; Crowe 2026; Odoj 2025 (PAP-consistent doxastic freedom); Eben 2025
- **Core claim**: some belief control is direct, or belief is itself volitional.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral; the article should present these as live minority positions with abstract-level verification only.

## Key Debates

### Does Williams's argument survive?
- **Sides**: Williams; Winters (no), Bennett (no, but conclusion right), Hieronymi (no "as given"; conclusion right on other grounds), Buckareff (repairable)
- **Current state**: the SEP's verdict ("widely held to be unsuccessful—at least without modifications") is the citation to use. The Map's assent-void already carries it (L61).

### Is involuntarism conceptual or contingent?
- **Sides**: Hieronymi 2009 ("no believer could believe at will") vs Booth 2017 ("merely contingently true") and Alston (psychological)
- **Current state**: open; Booth's route via suspension has drawn no 2024–26 citations.

### Is judgement an act?
- **Sides**: Shah & Velleman, Boyle, Peacocke 2026, Singh 2026 (yes, in some sense) vs Strawson 2003 (no) vs Ziff 2024 (the activity is voluntary, the conclusion involuntary)
- **Current state**: active; this is the debate the 10-07 reviewers found the Map's Seam had collapsed.

### What grounds doxastic responsibility?
- **Sides**: control (Steup) vs influence (Peels 2017) vs reasons-responsiveness without voluntarism (McHugh; Shah) vs Frankfurt-style detachment (Steup 2023; Odoj 2025)
- **Current state**: active; mostly orthogonal to the Map's concerns but it is where "Frankfurt-style arguments" live.

### Does the folk believe in belief-choice?
- **Sides**: Turri, Rose & Buckwalter 2018 (yes) vs Nottelmann, Booth & Lomholt 2023 (the vignettes conflate direct and indirect); Cusimano & Goodwin 2020 (others more than self)
- **Current state**: unresolved; the self/other asymmetry is the finding "in keeping with the introspective reports of epistemologists" [SEP].

L61 carries the SEP "unsuccessful" verdict, Winters, Booth 2017 and Hieronymi's "as given" concession. A concept article must not re-litigate these; it should be the place the void page links *to* for the taxonomy and the rival camps.
2. **The tenets do not bear on voluntariness.** Assent-void L111: "Neither Minimal Quantum Interaction nor No Many Worlds bears on the void; placing conscious influence at the assent event would be speculation the Map does not make." L107 on Bidirectional Interaction: the involuntarism of belief "says nothing about causal impotence"; evaluative control "is fully compatible with physicalism; it removes an objection and supplies no evidence for interactionism." Keep exactly this stance. The one thing the debate gives the interface account is a *reply*, not a datum: "if consciousness causes things, why can it not make itself believe?" is answered by belief's answerability to truth (Hieronymi; Williams), on any metaphysics.
3. **Shape vs voluntariness asymmetry.** Decision-void L84: assent "lacks the phenomenal form the candidate-site argument requires: assent presents as evidence coming down, not as a crossing among live alternatives … Hieronymi's parity of believing and intending at will concerns voluntariness, which the argument never invoked." So the decision void's candidate-site argument is about the *shape* of the transition, and Hieronymi's parity (intending is no more at-will than believing) does not touch it. A concept article should state the asymmetry once and not import the parity as if it demoted the decision void.
4. **Where resistance phenomenology sits.** [phenomenology-of-resistance-across-domains](/topics/phenomenology-of-resistance-across-domains/) L62 distinguishes felt pushback on failed *revision* from the assent-void's "nothing to push on" at *acquisition*. Soter's front-end/back-end split maps onto this: back-end (guidance-blocking) control is where any felt effort would live.
5. **Where agency-loss sits.** [thought-insertion](/concepts/thought-insertion/) treats loss of felt agency over a *thought*; nothing there is about belief-at-will, and the article should not conflate inserted thoughts with involuntary beliefs (Mullins & Spence's "abnormal belief or experience" question is a different axis).
6. **Universal-quantifier discipline.** The conceptual arguments are about human doxastic deliberation; Hieronymi's "no believer could believe at will" is her conclusion, and Singh/Crowe/Soter are live dissent. Any "whatever the substrate" claim must be scoped to "if the conceptual argument holds".
7. **Named rivals the reviewers required** (S8): Steup, Frankfurt-style (Peels 2013; Steup 2023; Odoj 2025), Boyle, Sosa [not reached; title-only], Strawson. All are now sourced here at least to abstract level except Sosa and Peels 2013.
