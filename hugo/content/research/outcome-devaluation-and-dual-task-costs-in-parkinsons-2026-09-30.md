## Assessment Verdict

**Proceed, with a corrected framing.** The subject is uncovered on the Map (no page discusses outcome devaluation or dual-task costs in Parkinson's; [paradoxical-kinesia](/topics/paradoxical-kinesia/) names them as the unrun tests and leaves the direction "open" with the habitual reading holding "the modest edge"). The literature does not leave the matter as open as that page implies, and it does not move it the way that page expects.

**Verdict on which control system the evidence says fails.** The two tests answer in different domains and point in different directions:

1. **Outcome devaluation and slips of action (instrumental learning with reward outcomes)**: the habitual reading's own prediction, stated explicitly by de Wit et al. (2011), is that if Parkinson's impairs stimulus-response habit formation, patients "should paradoxically outperform control subjects on a subsequent outcome devaluation test." That is what [paradoxical-kinesia](/topics/paradoxical-kinesia/) L82 also predicts for the habitual reading. The prediction failed. de Wit et al. (2011; 30 patients on or off medication, 14 controls) found habit formation "not impaired" and "if anything" a "disease severity-dependent deficit in goal-directed behavior." Mi et al. (2021; 20 patients OFF medication, 20 controls) found the opposite of the habitual prediction in the expression test: patients "responded less for valuable outcomes and more often to stimuli that were associated with devalued outcomes," with worse performance at higher symptom severity, which the authors read as "a relatively excessive reliance on habitual control." Computational two-step work agrees: Sharp, Foerde, Daw and Shohamy (2016) found "no effect of disease or medication on model-free learning" and "a marked impairment in model-based learning" OFF medication that dopamine replacement remediated. Reducing dopamine in healthy volunteers "tipped the balance towards habitual control" (de Wit et al. 2012, females only), and raising it promotes "model-based over model-free choice" (Wunderlich et al. 2012). **On this test, dopamine loss damages goal-directed (outcome-sensitive, model-based) control and spares or over-weights habit.** That is the direction the willed-deficit reading predicted, and the reverse of what the habitual reading predicted.

2. **Dual-task costs (motor performance)**: Parkinson's patients do have trouble doing two things at once, and the motor-automaticity account (Wu, Hallett and Chan 2015) holds that they "have difficulty in performing learned motor skills automatically" and fall back on "attentional control and/or compensatory efforts." Dual tasking reliably degrades gait in Parkinson's (Raffegeau et al. 2019: 19 studies, SMD −0.68). But the specific prediction that patients' cued or automatic actions degrade *more than controls'* under concurrent load is only partly borne out. Kelly, Eusterbrock and Shumway-Cook (2012) report that patients "consistently demonstrate greater dual-task walking deficits than healthy, age-matched individuals," and Yogev et al. (2005) found gait *variability* rose under dual task in patients only. Yet in the largest baseline-controlled comparison (Rochester et al. 2014: 121 incident patients, 189 controls), "PD and controls responded similarly to the dual-task for all gait characteristics except for step width and step width variability," and gait speed fell "in a parallel fashion" in both groups in Yogev et al. Worse for the version of the prediction stated in [paradoxical-kinesia](/topics/paradoxical-kinesia/) ("it should degrade under a concurrent attention-demanding task more in patients than in controls"): external cues *reduce* dual-task interference in patients (Rochester et al. 2005: auditory cues under dual task produced a 19% step-length gain). **On this test, the evidence is compatible with an automaticity deficit but is equally compatible with a general reduction in attentional and executive capacity, and the cueing result undercuts the claim that cued movement runs on an attention-hungry route.**

**Net for the kinesia question.** The outcome-devaluation test, the one [paradoxical-kinesia](/topics/paradoxical-kinesia/) calls "the standard behavioural signature of habit," comes out *against* the habitual reading. The dual-task test is the habitual reading's home ground and it does not win there cleanly. The article's "modest edge" for the habitual reading rests on anatomy and freezing of gait; both discriminating tests it named either reverse that edge or fail to confirm it. The corrected statement is: **in the instrumental-learning domain, goal-directed control is what dopamine loss impairs; in the motor domain, automaticity is what patients lose; neither result transfers cleanly to the other domain, and "willed selection" as the Map uses it is not the same construct as "model-based" or "response-outcome" control.** Redgrave et al. themselves (2010) left the question open in almost these terms, asking whether patients' difficulties initiating goal-directed performance "are due to the primary disease state (that is, the loss of dopamine from goal-directed circuits" or arise "because goal-directed control is overwhelmed by the absence of automatic control routines."

**Tenet 3 standing.** Nothing here moves Bidirectional Interaction from *available* to *actual*. A goal-directed deficit that dopamine replacement remediates is a dopamine-dependent goal-directed system, which non-reductive physicalism with mental causation predicts as readily as the Map's selection-interface reading does. What the evidence does is remove the anatomical rival's claim to the discriminating test, restoring the Map's reading to *compatible* on equal footing with the rival rather than trailing it.

## Major Positions

### The habitual-control (automaticity-deficit) reading
- **Proponents**: Redgrave et al. (2010); Hernandez, Redgrave and Obeso (2015, 2019); Wu, Hallett and Chan (2015); Vandenbossche et al. (2012) for freezing.
- **Core claim**: Dopamine loss falls first on the sensorimotor (posterior) putamen, the habit territory; patients lose automatic control and are "forced into a progressive reliance on the goal-directed mode."
- **Key arguments**: Anatomy of denervation; loss of automatic motor features (arm swing, stride, facial expression); imaging showing extra cortical and cerebellar recruitment during automatic performance; dual-task fragility; freezing as de-automatisation.
- **Relation to site tenets**: A physicalist rival to the Map's willed-deficit reading of the same asymmetry. As [paradoxical-kinesia](/topics/paradoxical-kinesia/) notes, it is not hostile to Tenet 3: on this reading what survives is effortful attended control, which is where the Map locates [felt effort](/concepts/mental-effort/).

### The goal-directed-deficit reading (instrumental-learning evidence)
- **Proponents**: de Wit, Barker, Dickinson and Cools (2011); Sharp, Foerde, Daw and Shohamy (2016); Mi et al. (2021); consistent with de Wit et al. (2012) and Wunderlich et al. (2012) on dopamine manipulation in healthy people.
- **Core claim**: Dopamine loss impairs outcome-sensitive, model-based control; stimulus-response habit formation is preserved, and under competition habits dominate more in patients than controls.
- **Key arguments**: Direct devaluation and slips-of-action tests in patients; two-step task decomposition; medication remediates the model-based deficit; severity tracks the deficit.
- **Relation to site tenets**: This is the direction the Map's willed-deficit reading predicts, but the construct tested is response-outcome knowledge and model-based planning, which is not the Map's self-initiated selection. The result restores parity between the readings; it does not establish that a conscious selector is what dopamine loss disables.

### The resource-capacity reading of dual-task costs
- **Proponents**: Rochester et al. (2014); Wu and Hallett (2008) partly; Kelly et al. (2012) list this among the mechanisms.
- **Core claim**: Dual-task costs in Parkinson's reflect reduced attentional and executive capacity and age-related resource decline, with a specific postural-coordination deficit; not a signature of habit loss as such.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Neutral; it removes the dual-task test from the discriminating list because both readings can absorb it.

## Key Debates

### Is habit *learning* impaired or habit *expression* dominant?
- **Sides**: Knowlton et al. (1996) and the probabilistic-classification tradition find impaired incremental learning in patients; de Wit et al. (2011) and Mi et al. (2021) find intact stimulus-response reliance and (Mi) excess habitual expression.
- **Core disagreement**: Whether slow feedback-driven acquisition and outcome-insensitive expression measure the same "habit system." Redgrave et al. (2010) reply that "conflict-induced habit formation may be different from that induced by extensive training."
- **Current state**: Unresolved, but both direct devaluation studies in patients point the same way.

### Do patients show *disproportionate* dual-task costs?
- **Sides**: Kelly et al. (2012) and O'Shea et al. (2002) say yes for gait speed; Rochester et al. (2014) and Yogev et al. (2005) find parallel speed costs with patient-specific variability or posture effects.
- **Core disagreement**: Baseline control. When single-task demand is equalised, the global cost difference shrinks to specific gait characteristics.
- **Current state**: Mixed; the strongest baseline-controlled sample favours similar costs with a postural exception.

### Does cueing load or unload attention?
- **Sides**: The habitual reading (as extended in [paradoxical-kinesia](/topics/paradoxical-kinesia/)) implies cued walking runs on the attention-demanding route; Rochester et al. (2005, 2010) find cues reduce dual-task interference and improve dual-task walking.
- **Current state**: The cueing evidence favours cues as unloading, which fits either a bypass story or a story on which cues make the goal-directed route cheaper. It does not fit the prediction that cued walking degrades more under load.

## The Analytic Question Mapped onto the Two Readings

| Test | Willed-deficit reading predicts | Habitual reading predicts | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outcome devaluation, well-practised task | Patients *less* outcome-sensitive | Patients *more* outcome-sensitive | No group difference in outcome sensitivity, severity-linked goal-directed deficit (de Wit 2011); patients less goal-directed and more habitual under competition (Mi 2021) |
| Model-based vs model-free learning | Model-based impaired | Model-free impaired | Model-based impaired OFF, remediated ON; model-free spared (Sharp 2016) |
| Dopamine manipulation in healthy people | Less dopamine → more habit | Less dopamine → less habit | Less dopamine → more habit (de Wit 2012, females); more dopamine → more model-based (Wunderlich 2012) |
| Dual-task cost, self-paced motor tasks | No specific prediction | Larger in patients | Larger, in count-of-patients terms (Wu & Hallett 2005, 2008); confounded with executive capacity by the authors' own account |
| Dual-task cost, gait vs controls | No specific prediction | Larger in patients | Mixed: larger (O'Shea 2002 via Kelly 2012), parallel with PD-specific variability (Yogev 2005), similar except posture (Rochester 2014) |
| Cued walking under dual task | Cues bypass; cost should not rise | Cues recruit goal-directed route; cost should rise | Cues *reduce* interference (Rochester 2005) |
| Freezing under dual task | No specific prediction | Predicted | Dual task plus turning is the strongest trigger (Spildooren 2010) |

Reading the table: the three instrumental rows reverse the habitual reading's edge; the freezing row and the self-paced motor rows support it; the gait rows are mixed; the cueing row cuts against the specific extension made in [paradoxical-kinesia](/topics/paradoxical-kinesia/). The structural point for any article is that the two tests belong to different task domains, so *which control system fails* has a domain-indexed answer. A single-system verdict is not licensed by this literature.

**What this does not touch.** Whether goal-directed control is *felt* remains outside all of these data, which is where [dopamine-and-the-unified-interface](/topics/dopamine-and-the-unified-interface/) already places the disagreement between the Map and the physicalist rival. Nothing here bears on Tenet 1, Tenet 2 or Tenet 4.

## Gaps in Research

These propagate into any article and must be carried as stated limits, not as verified claims:

- **Abstract-only sources**: Sharp et al. (2016) (Brain full text not retrieved; the model-based effect sizes, sample sizes and medication-order controls are unread); Knowlton et al. (1996); Wunderlich et al. (2012); Wu and Hallett (2005, 2008); Wu, Hallett and Chan (2015); Wu et al. (2015, Cereb Cortex); Hernandez et al. (2019); Raffegeau et al. (2019) (whether any included study reports a control contrast is unread); Rochester et al. (2005, 2014); Yogev et al. (2005); Spildooren et al. (2010); Vandenbossche et al. (2012); O'Shea et al. (2002).
- **Full texts read selectively, not end to end**: Redgrave et al. (2010) (author manuscript, grep-targeted); de Wit et al. (2011) (repository PDF with two-column extraction artefacts; the Discussion's treatment of medication was only partly read); de Wit et al. (2012); Mi et al. (2021) (the formation-test group statistics were not extracted); Kelly et al. (2012).
- **Unread leads cited through Mi et al. (2021)**: Hadj-Bouziane et al. (2012), Hayes et al. (2015), Olson et al. (2019) on instrumental learning in Parkinson's; Shohamy et al. (2004) on probabilistic stimulus-response learning. None entered the Citations list.
- **Unreachable**: Foerde (2018), "What are habits and do they depend on the striatum?" *Current Opinion in Behavioral Sciences* 20, 17–24 (doi:10.1016/j.cobeha.2017.08.011), metadata verified at Crossref but no abstract or text obtained; it is the likeliest single review of the human-patient habit evidence and should be read before the article is written. Foerde and Shohamy (2011, *Neurobiol Learn Mem*) retrieved as abstract only and not used.
- **Unverified claim to avoid**: that patients show *larger* dual-task costs than controls as a general finding. Kelly et al. (2012) assert it; Rochester et al. (2014) and Yogev et al. (2005) qualify it. No control-contrast meta-analysis was found (control pair: the Raffegeau search resolved at Crossref and Europe PMC, so the search route works; a "dual-task Parkinson controls meta-analysis" search returned nothing usable).
- **Control pair for the absence claim on devaluation studies**: the free-text query `parkinson "slips of action"` returned Mi et al. (2021) plus one unrelated protocol, and the author query returned de Wit et al. (2011). The claim that only two direct devaluation/slips studies exist in patients is therefore a search result, not a literature review; a third may exist under other vocabulary ("instrumental," "response-outcome").
- **Sex-restricted result**: de Wit et al. (2012)'s depletion effect held in females only; any article must say so.
- **Construct gap**: none of the instrumental tasks measures self-initiated *movement*; the willed-deficit reading's *self-initiated* and the learning literature's *goal-directed* are related but distinct constructs. This is the central caveat.
- **Not surveyed**: levodopa and deep-brain-stimulation effects on automaticity; the Ballanger/Distler kinematic lineage (already in [paradoxical-kinesia](/topics/paradoxical-kinesia/)); animal 6-OHDA devaluation work (Redgrave et al. cite Faure et al. 2005 as their ref. 119; unread here).
