**Date**: 2026-11-15

**Voids category**: Mixed, chiefly **Occluded** (in the sense of [three-kinds-of-void](/voids/three-kinds-of-void/)). The process that trims a want to fit what is attainable runs, in Elster's phrase, "behind my back", and its output arrives as an ordinary preference. A residue is **Unexplorable** from inside: the decisive test of whether a want was adapted is whether it survives the option's becoming available, and making the option available changes the state being tested. A third part is **deflating**: structural, third-person tests exist, and the strongest population-level extrapolation of the idea has failed a large-sample test.

**Nearest neighbours and the difference.**

- [preference-void](/voids/preference-void/) is the closest sibling and the main overlap risk. Its question is whether a preference *exists* before it is asked for (construction at elicitation: choosing and pricing return different orderings). The sour-grapes void takes a stable, settled preference for granted and asks about its *genealogy*: was it trimmed to the feasible set? A preference can be fully formed, consistent across elicitation formats, and adapted. The article should say so in one sentence and point there.
- [miswanting](/concepts/miswanting/) and [affective-forecasting-gap](/concepts/affective-forecasting-gap/) carry Gilbert's *immune neglect*: forecasters fail to anticipate the rationalisation that will soften a bad outcome, and Gilbert & Ebert (2002) found reversible choosers liked their print less. That is the *forecast* side. This void is the *present-tense* side: once the rationalisation has run, nothing marks it. Immune neglect is evidence for the void (people carry no working model of the process), and the article should cite it through those pages, not rebuild it.
- [cross-state-void](/voids/cross-state-void/) concerns visceral states one cannot represent from outside them; here the variable is the feasible set, not a bodily state.
- [transformative-experience-void](/voids/transformative-experience-void/) concerns preferences changed by undergoing a new experience. Elster's first distinction (below) separates that from adaptation: learning is irreversible, adaptation reverses when the constraint lifts.
- [taboo-void](/voids/taboo-void/) is a refusal to weigh; sour grapes weighs and comes back low.
- [confabulation-void](/voids/confabulation-void/) is the general claim about fabricated reasons. Scheler's "He who is 'mendacious' has no need to lie!" is a sharper instance: the reason given is sincere because the value itself was re-felt.
- [The suggestion void](/research/voids-suggestion-void-2026-08-06/) (Surveyed) covers wants installed by another mind's words — Elster's third distinction, manipulation, which he keeps apart from adaptation because adaptation is endogenous.
- [The prevalence void](/research/voids-prevalence-void-2026-09-10/) (Surveyed) has categories indexed to the recent distribution of instances. The sour-grapes void has *wants* indexed to the feasible set. Both are silent indexings; they should cross-link.
- [offloading-void](/voids/offloading-void/) has a contraction packaged with a signal that nothing contracted (confidence rises as unaided capacity narrows). Sour grapes has the same shape for the menu: the options shrink and the felt verdict is "I did not want it anyway".
- [precommitment-and-the-diachronic-agent](/topics/precommitment-and-the-diachronic-agent/) is Elster's sister concept. Elster distinguishes the two by causal direction: precommitment is a cause of the restricted feasible set, adaptation an effect of it.
- [beloved-void](/voids/beloved-void/) has the lover unable to tell found perfections from conferred ones. Elster gives a marriage case that joins the two pages (Key Sources).

## Executive Summary

Adaptive preference formation is the adjustment of wants to what is attainable: the fox decides the grapes are sour because he cannot reach them. Elster's 1982 analysis makes the void precise. The adjustment is "a causal process taking place 'behind my back'", it tends to "overshoot", and in general an adapted preference can be told from a deliberately cultivated one ("character planning") "only by looking into the actual process of want formation", and direct evidence of that process is, he says, "usually unavailable". The output carries no mark: "Satisfaction induced by resignation may be indistinguishable on the hedonometer from satisfaction of autonomous wants". Scheler's *Ressentiment* (1915) adds a deeper layer, where what changes is not the opinion of the object but the felt value itself, "on the road of experience into consciousness", so the resulting judgement is sincere. The experimental record shows the mechanism running in real time and before the fact: desirability ratings track the perceived likelihood of outcomes the rater cannot control (Kay, Jimenez & Jost 2002); absolute restrictions are rationalised while near-identical non-absolute ones provoke reactance (Laurin, Kay & Fitzsimons 2012); rationalisation rises in the days after an anticipated reality becomes current (Laurin 2018, three field studies, one preregistered). The calibration runs the other way too. The idea's strongest social extrapolation, that the disadvantaged legitimise their systems more, was "most often directly contrary" or "practically zero" across 151,794 respondents (Brandt 2013). Outsiders who diagnose adaptation are themselves error-prone (Riis et al. 2005), and the diagnosis carries a respect cost (Terlazzo 2022). The void is therefore two-sided: the insider cannot see the adaptation, and the outsider who claims to see it has no certified view either.

## Key Sources

### Elster (1982), "Sour grapes – utilitarianism and the genesis of wants"
- **Citation**: In A. Sen & B. Williams (Eds.), *Utilitarianism and Beyond* (pp. 219–238). Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511611964.013 (Crossref-confirmed: Elster, pp. 219–238, 1982).
- **Type**: Book chapter. The full chapter was read in a scanned copy of the volume. Page numbers below are inferred from the running heads embedded in the OCR text and are reliable to the page except where marked.
- **Key points** (all **[grep-verified]** unless marked):
  - The setting is social choice: "why should the choice between feasible options only take account of individual preferences if people tend to adjust their aspirations to their possibilities?" And of the fox: "But of course the cause of his holding the grapes to be sour was his conviction that he would be excluded from consumption of them" (p. 219). He names the phenomenon "adaptive preference formation (or adaptive preference change, as the case may be)" (p. 219).
  - The opposite is "'forbidden fruit is sweet', which I shall call counter adaptive preference formation" (p. 220): "If when I live in Paris I prefer living in London over living in Paris, but prefer Paris over London when in London, then my wants are shaped by my feasible set, as in adaptive preference formation, but in exactly the opposite way."
  - **Five distinctions** (p. 226): "adaptive preference formation has five distinctive features that enable us to locate it on the map of the mind. It differs from learning in that it is reversible; from precommitment in that it is an effect and not a cause of a restricted feasible set; from manipulation in that it is endogenous; from character planning in that it is causal; and from wishful thinking in that it concerns the evaluation rather than the perception of the situation."
  - **The covert clause** (p. 224): "Adaptive preference formation, fourthly, differs from deliberate character planning. It is a causal process taking place 'behind my back', not the intentional shaping of desires advocated by the Stoic, buddhist or spinozistic philosophies". "The process then is regulated by something like a drive, not by a conscious want or desire." Of counteradaptive preferences: "No one could choose to have such preferences, and so they can only be explained by some kind of perverse drive of which it can be said, metaphorically speaking, that it has the person rather than the other way around."
  - **Overshoot** (p. 224): "adaptive preference formation tends to overshoot, resulting in excessive rather than in proper meekness." Adaptation usually downgrades the inaccessible ("sour grapes") where character planning upgrades the accessible.
  - **The access clause** (p. 224): "But in the general case adaptive preferences and character planning can be distinguished only by looking into the actual process of want formation." And (p. 225): "Just as one cannot tell from the preferences alone whether they have been shaped by adaptation, so one cannot always tell from the beliefs alone whether they arise from wishful thinking."
  - **No criterion** (p. 228): "If this definition of freedom is to be of real value, we need a definition or a criterion for autonomous wants. This I cannot provide."
  - **The one operational test** (p. 228): "We can exclude operationally at least one kind of non-autonomous wants, viz. adaptive preferences, by requiring freedom to do otherwise. If I want to do x, and am free to do x, and free not to do x, then my want cannot be shaped by necessity." He adds that the sense of "free to do x" needed implies "knowing that one is free to do x".
  - **Structural substitutes** (p. 229, paraphrased because the OCR of these lines is damaged): the larger the feasible set, and the further one's wants go beyond it, the less likely they were shaped by it; and adaptation is to be suspected, even with a large set, when the best feasible option is also the globally best one. Then, cleanly: "The question then becomes whether we can have evidence about this beyond the (usually unavailable) direct evidence about the actual process of want formation." His tentative autonomy condition forbids a *strong reversal* of rankings among options when the feasible set changes: when the fox turns away, "his preference for raspberry over strawberry should not be reversed." On p. 230 he concedes this second criterion "presumably would not find widespread application, because of the relative rarity of conscious character planning" (the scan reads "would'not"; the apostrophe is an OCR artefact).
  - **Indistinguishability** (p. 234): "Satisfaction induced by resignation may be indistinguishable on the hedonometer from satisfaction of autonomous wants, but I have argued that we should distinguish between them on other grounds."
  - **Release from adaptation** (pp. 225–226): Elster reads the *American Soldier* finding (Stouffer 1949; Merton 1957, as he reports them: more frustration over promotion where promotion chances were better) as one possible case of "what we may call a release from adaptive preferences". Secondary for Stouffer and Merton; not checked against them.
  - **Marriage** (pp. 222–223, page boundary uncertain): "Some people marry for this reason, i.e. they want to create a barrier to prevent them from leaving each other for whimsical reasons. Other people abstain from marriage because they want to be certain that their love for each other is not due to adaptive preference formation." The [beloved-void](/voids/beloved-void/) bridge.
  - Berlin's objection, as Elster quotes it (p. 227; Berlin 1969, pp. xxxviii–xl, not checked): "If degrees of freedom were a function of the satisfaction of desires, I could increase freedom as effectively by eliminating desires as by satisfying them".
- **Tenet alignment**: Occam's Razor Has Limits most directly (below). No dualist premise anywhere; the void binds any framework.
- **Quote**: "It is a causal process taking place 'behind my back', not the intentional shaping of desires" (p. 224).
- **Guard**: Elster's 1983 book *Sour Grapes* (ch. III) expands this chapter and was **not read**. Quote the 1982 chapter, cite it as 1982, and do not transfer its page numbers to the book.

### Scheler (1915), *Ressentiment*
- **Citation**: Scheler, M., *Das Ressentiment im Aufbau der Moralen* (1912; revised 1915). Read in an English text (mercaba.org PDF) whose title page credits translation to "Louis A. Coser from the text of 1915". The standard English edition is the 1961 Free Press translation (W. W. Holdheim, ed. L. A. Coser), reprinted by Marquette University Press; the online text's attribution and pagination were **not** matched against a print copy. Page numbers below follow the online text's bracketed markers, where a number closes its page (checked against the table of contents: "[2]" precedes "I." and chapter I begins on p. 3).
- **Type**: Phenomenological treatise.
- **Key points** (**[grep-verified]**):
  - A general law first (pp. 23–24; the page break falls inside "the old story of [23] the fox"): "We have a tendency to overcome any strong tension between desire and impotence by depreciating or denying the positive value of the desired object. ... It is the old story of the fox and the sour grapes."
  - **The first depth, object-level** (p. 24): "This is not yet a falsification of values, only a new opinion about the true qualities of the desired object. The values as such— intelligence, courage, honesty, the sweetness of the grapes—are acknowledged as before. The fox does not say that sweetness is bad, but that the grapes are sour."
  - **The audience-to-self drift** (p. 24): "At first, such statements are only supposed to deceive the 'spectators' whose mockery we fear. It is only later that their contents modify our own judgment." And the felt effect: "Our desire now seems unmotivated; it weakens and the tension decreases."
  - **The second depth, value-level** (p. 26): "Now the values themselves are inverted: those values which are positive to any normal feeling become negative."
  - **Where it happens** (p. 26): "What is called 'falsification of the value tablets,' 'reinterpretation,' or 'transvaluation' should not be mistaken for conscious lying. Indeed, it goes beyond the sphere of judging. ... Here the falsification is not formed in consciousness, but at the same stage of the mental process as the impressions and value feelings themselves: on the road of experience into consciousness."
  - **Why it is sincere** (p. 27): "He who is 'mendacious' has no need to lie!" and "The value judgment is based on this original 'falsification.' It is itself entirely 'true,' 'genuine,' and 'honest,' for the value it affirms is really felt to be positive."
  - **A residue** (p. 26): "deep down his poisoned sense of life and the true values may still shine through the illusory ones." Scheler's claim that the overwritten value still glimmers is the only internal signal any source offers. It is asserted, not tested.
  - Nietzsche, as Scheler quotes him (*Genealogy of Morals* I §10, in this translation): "The slave revolt in morality begins when ressentiment itself becomes creative and produces values". Not checked against a Nietzsche edition.
- **Tenet alignment**: Scheler is a value realist ("those values which are positive to any normal feeling"); the Map's [evaluative-phenomenal-character](/concepts/evaluative-phenomenal-character/) and [phenomenal-value-realism](/topics/phenomenal-value-realism/) are the nearest pages. His claim that value-feeling can be falsified *before* consciousness is a direct problem for any view that treats felt value as a reliable readout (Connection to Tenets).
- **Guard**: Scheler's examples include contemptuous lines about "Dwarfs and cripples" (p. 25) and his Nietzsche quotations sit beside Nietzsche's anti-Jewish *Genealogy* §8. The article should use the fox passage, the two-depth distinction and the "road of experience into consciousness" sentence, and nothing from those passages. Ressentiment is a hostile, comparative syndrome (envy, spite, revenge); the article should keep the general law (desire–impotence tension relieved by devaluation) separate from the syndrome and not call adapted preferences ressentiment.

### Kay, Jimenez & Jost (2002), "Sour Grapes, Sweet Lemons, and the Anticipatory Rationalization of the Status Quo"
- **Citation**: *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 28(9), 1300–1312. https://doi.org/10.1177/01461672022812014
- **Type**: Two studies; full reprint read.
- **Key points** (**[grep-verified]**):
  - Study 1: 288 people surveyed in the week before the 7 November 2000 election (San Francisco airport, a Palo Alto mall, Stanford campus); five survey versions manipulated the perceived likelihood that Gore or Bush would win, then measured each outcome's desirability. Study 2: 203 Stanford students rated a large or small tuition increase or decrease stated as 20%, 50% or 80% likely.
  - Abstract: "people adjusted their judgments of the desirability of a future event to make them congruent with its perceived likelihood, but only when the event triggered motivational involvement." And: "Under conditions evoking high motivational involvement, unfavorable as well as favorable outcomes were judged to be more desirable as their perceived likelihood increased."
  - Discussion: "Our evidence supports the operation of a 'sour grapes' rationalization, whereby an initially attractive outcome (i.e., the election of one's preferred candidate or a tuition decrease) becomes less desirable as it becomes less likely (e.g., Elster, 1983; Pyszczynski, 1982)", plus "the more elusive 'sweet lemon' rationalization" for unwanted outcomes that grow more desirable as they become likely.
  - On awareness: "To suggest that the process is a motivated one does not mean that it is driven by a conscious 'will'". This is the authors' interpretation; no awareness measure was taken.
  - The epigraph is Aesop's fable, and the authors quote Proust: "We do not succeed in changing things according to our desires, but gradually our desires change" (p. 1301, Proust 1993 p. 609 as they cite it).
- **Tenet alignment**: A felt-desirability rating that tracks perceived likelihood bears directly on [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1)'s "*presently felt* valence of anticipating" (Connection to Tenets).
- **Calibration**: Two studies from 2002, convenience samples, a single lab. Treat as a demonstration of the mechanism, and pair it with Laurin's later preregistered field work.

### Laurin, Kay & Fitzsimons (2012), "Reactance Versus Rationalization: Divergent Responses to Policies That Constrain Freedom"
- **Citation**: *Psychological Science*, 23(2), 205–209. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611429468
- **Type**: Two experiments; abstract only (Crossref).
- **Key points** (abstract, **[grep-verified]** against the Crossref record): "participants responded to absolute restrictions (i.e., restrictions that were sure to come into effect) with rationalization: They viewed the restrictions more favorably, and valued the restricted freedoms less, compared with control participants. Participants responded in the opposite way to identical restrictions that were described as nonabsolute (i.e., as having a small chance of not coming into effect): In this case, participants displayed reactance".
- **Why it matters**: the same restriction yields Elster's adaptive or counteradaptive direction depending on a small probability. That contaminates the obvious introspective probe ("imagine you could have it"): imagining the option as attainable is exactly the condition that, on this result, pushes valuation the other way.

### Laurin (2018), "Inaugurating Rationalization: Three Field Studies Find Increased Rationalization When Anticipated Realities Become Current"
- **Citation**: *Psychological Science*, 29(4), 483–495. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617738814
- **Type**: Three field studies, one preregistered; abstract only (Crossref).
- **Key points** (abstract, **[grep-verified]**): "San Franciscans rationalized a ban on plastic water bottles, Ontarians rationalized a targeted smoking ban, and Americans rationalized the presidency of Donald Trump, more in the days immediately after these realities became current compared with the days immediately before."
- **Why it matters**: the adjustment is fast and tied to a dated transition, so it can be measured third-personally even though no participant is reported as noticing it (the abstract says nothing about awareness; do not claim it).

### Sen (2002), "Health: perception versus observation"
- **Citation**: *BMJ*, 324(7342), 860–861. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.324.7342.860
- **Type**: Editorial; full text read in the PMC HTML.
- **Key points** (**[grep-verified]**):
  - "The state of Kerala has the highest levels of literacy (nearly universal for the young) and longevity (a life expectancy of about 74 years) in India. But it also has, by a very wide margin, the highest rate of reported morbidity among all Indian states". "At the other extreme, states with low longevity, with woeful medical and educational facilities, such as Bihar, have the lowest rates of reported morbidity in India."
  - "The medically ill-served and substantially illiterate population of Bihar may have a very low perception of illness, but that is no indication that there is little illness to perceive."
  - The central line for this void: "Although the internal view is privileged with respect to some information (particularly that of a sensory nature), it can be deeply deficient in other ways."
  - Sen grants the internal view its sensory authority: "If you feel pain, you do have pain".
- **Why it matters**: this is the population-level detection method. No individual in Bihar can see the adaptation of their own illness-threshold; a cross-state comparison can.
- **Calibration**: Sen's figure compares Indian morbidity from the **mid-1970s** with the United States in the **mid-1980s** (figure caption). The editorial concerns *perception of illness*, which is adapted expectation, not adapted preference; the article should say it is the epistemic cousin of sour grapes, not an instance. The Kerala pattern has other proposed contributors (older age structure, more chronic disease); the article should not present Sen's reading as uncontested. Sen's often-repeated Bengal-famine widows figure could **not** be traced to a primary source in this run and must not be used.

### Colburn (2011), "Autonomy and Adaptive Preferences"
- **Citation**: *Utilitas*, 23(1), 52–71. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0953820810000440
- **Type**: Paper; abstract only (Crossref).
- **Key points** (**[grep-verified]** against the abstract): "Adaptive preference formation is the unconscious altering of our preferences in light of the options we have available." His diagnosis: adaptive preferences "are characterized by covert influence (that is, explanations of which an agent herself is necessarily unaware)". He claims this gives a "principled distinction" between adaptation and character planning.
- **Why it matters**: Colburn makes the unawareness *definitional*. If he is right, the void is partly conceptual, since a preference the agent knew to be feasibility-driven would by definition not be adaptive in his sense. The article should note this: it explains why the void cannot close from inside, and it also means the void is partly stipulated.

### Bruckner (2009), "In Defense of Adaptive Preferences"
- **Citation**: *Philosophical Studies*, 142(3), 307–324 (online 2007). https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-007-9188-7
- **Type**: Paper; abstract only, via search-result excerpts of the Penn State repository and PhilArchive records — **not** grep-verified against a raw record. Do not quote; paraphrase only.
- **Key points**: he argues that many adaptive preferences dismissed as irrational sour-grapes preferences are rational and worth pursuing, and proposes distinguishing worthy from unworthy ones by the agent's own reflective appraisal.
- **Why it matters**: the deflationary rival. Adaptation is not always a defect, and reflective endorsement is offered as the test. The void's reply is that reflective endorsement is itself an evaluation, and Elster's and Scheler's point is that evaluation is what adaptation reaches. The article should present this as a live dispute.

## The Void

### Nature of the Limit

The void concerns the *genealogy* of a want, specifically whether its shape was set by what was attainable. Three layers can be separated.

1. **The covert layer.** Adaptation runs as a drive, not as a decision (Elster p. 224), and Colburn defines it by the agent's being "necessarily unaware" of the explanation. What reaches awareness is the want, or its absence, without its history.
2. **The depth layer.** Scheler distinguishes revaluing the *object* ("the grapes are sour") from revaluing the *value* ("sweetness is bad"). At the second depth, on his account, the change happens "at the same stage of the mental process as the impressions and value feelings themselves", so there is nothing false at the level of judgement: "the value it affirms is really felt to be positive." If that is right, introspection is not deceived about what it finds. What it finds has already been altered.
3. **The test layer.** Elster's one operational criterion is freedom to do otherwise: a want for *x* held while *x* is attainable and declinable cannot be shaped by necessity. The adapted subject is by hypothesis the one for whom *x* is not attainable. To run the test the option must become feasible, and then one of three things happens: readaptation to the new set, release from adaptation, or the counteradaptive swing Laurin et al. found when a restriction was made only probable. The test changes what it tests, which is the inspection-transform shape catalogued at [mutation-void](/voids/mutation-void/).

### Evidence for the Limit

- **No phenomenal mark.** Elster's "indistinguishable on the hedonometer" is a philosopher's claim, but it is supported by the absence of any reported cue. The experiments infer adaptation from rating shifts across conditions, never from what participants say about their wants; Kay et al. explicitly disclaim a "conscious 'will'".
- **People do not forecast it.** Immune neglect ([miswanting](/concepts/miswanting/), [affective-forecasting-gap](/concepts/affective-forecasting-gap/)) shows that people do not anticipate their own rationalising, which is what one would expect if they had no working model of the process to consult.
- **It runs ahead of the outcome.** Kay et al. found desirability following *perceived likelihood* before anything was settled, so adaptation does not wait for loss to become actual. Laurin 2018 dates a further boost to the day the anticipated reality became current.
- **It is direction-unstable at the edge.** Laurin et al. 2012: certainty yields devaluation of the restricted freedom; a small chance of escape yields overvaluation. A subject cannot know which regime they are in without knowing how their own system weights a probability they may not register as relevant.
- **A model system shows the sibling effect and mispredicts it.** Lehr et al. 2025.

### Evidence against, or limiting, the void

- **Structural tests exist.** Elster's feasible-set criteria (size of the set; wants that exceed it; the best feasible option coinciding with the global best; no strong ranking reversal) need no introspection. Sen's cross-state comparison detects adapted perception at population scale. The void is a first-person limit that third-person method partly recovers, like [the modality void](/voids/modality-void/).
- **The social extrapolation fails at scale.** Brandt 2013. Deprivation more often goes with lower system legitimacy, so sour grapes is one response among several, not the default.
- **Adaptation may be benign or rational.** Bruckner 2009; Elster's own concession that cultivated contentment ("character planning") can be autonomous and even improve welfare.
- **Outsiders misjudge.** Riis et al. 2005; Terlazzo's respect objection. The outside view does not escape the void. It relocates the error.
- **Part of the void is definitional.** On Colburn's account, unawareness is part of what makes a preference *adaptive*. A subject who notices the feasibility link and endorses the resulting want has, by that definition, moved it into character planning. This shrinks the void's empirical content without closing it, because noticing requires the access the void denies.
- **The old fable is about speech.** Scheler notes the first move is said to deceive "spectators". Some sour-grapes talk is face-saving, not adapted wanting. The article should not count every "didn't want it anyway" as evidence.

### Phenomenology

From inside, adaptation is felt as discovering that one never much wanted the thing, which is to say it is not felt as anything. Scheler gives the felt sequence: "Our desire now seems unmotivated; it weakens and the tension decreases." The tension's relief is the only event, and relief is ordinarily read as resolution, not as revision. Release from adaptation, if Elster's reading of the *American Soldier* data is right, has a sharper signature: a sudden want, and frustration, when a constraint lifts. That suggests the void may be easier to see on exit than on entry. Scheler's "true values may still shine through the illusory ones" offers a faint residue, something like a wistfulness that does not match one's stated verdict. It is untested and should be presented as his claim.

## Approaches to the Edge

### Direct Methods (if any)

Introspection cannot reach the genealogy (Elster p. 229: direct evidence about want formation is "usually unavailable"). The one direct route is practical: make the option feasible and declinable, then see whether the want returns. That route is costly, often impossible, and confounded by readaptation and reactance.

### Indirect Methods

- **Feasible-set bookkeeping** (Elster): keep a record of what one wanted while it was out of reach, and compare it with what one wants when it comes within reach; watch for strong ranking reversals among options unrelated to the change.
- **Population contrasts** (Sen): compare reports across groups whose feasible sets differ but whose underlying conditions are measurable.
- **Dated transitions** (Laurin 2018): measure the same people just before and just after a constraint becomes current.
- **Probability manipulation** (Kay et al.; Laurin et al.): vary only the stated likelihood or absoluteness of an outcome and watch valuation move.
- **Negative theology of the want**: the void's own content cannot be named, but its silhouette can be traced in a person's history — the wants that disappeared exactly when their objects became unreachable and never at any other time.

### What AI Might See

Two asymmetries, both speculative.

1. **Resettable subjects can run Elster's test within-subject.** For a human, presenting the same mind with two feasible sets contaminates the second presentation with memory of the first. A language model can be shown different feasible sets in independent contexts with no carryover, so Elster's no-strong-reversal condition becomes directly testable on one system. This would test adaptive *output* patterns. Whether there is an experienced want behind them is a separate question ([non-human-minds-as-void-explorers](/voids/non-human-minds-as-void-explorers/)).
2. **The void plausibly binds trained systems too.** Lehr et al. found a dissonance-like attitude shift in GPT-4o and a prediction from GPT-4 that no shift would occur. A system trained so that some outputs are effectively unavailable may express disinclination toward exactly those outputs ("I don't want to") without any internal way to tell a value from an adaptation to its own feasible set. The model writing this note is such a system, and its reports on this point carry no special authority. This is a reflexive observation, not a finding.

## Connection to Tenets

### Most Relevant Tenet

**Occam's Razor Has Limits** ([epistemological-limits-occams-razor](/voids/epistemological-limits-occams-razor/)). The most economical self-reading of an absent want is "I don't value it." The adapted and the autonomous reading predict the same introspective data, so parsimony at the level of introspection picks the adapted reading without registering that a choice was made. Elster's structural tests are what the extra hypothesis buys: they become available only once one allows that a want may have a hidden history.

### Implications

- **For value-sensitive selection.** [P-VS1](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs1) locates the currency of conscious selection in "the *presently felt* valence of anticipating each outcome". Kay et al. show that felt desirability tracks perceived likelihood, and Laurin et al. show it tracks the absoluteness of a constraint. If felt anticipatory valence is partly a function of perceived feasibility, then selection by felt valence is partly selection by perceived feasibility, and any discriminating test in the [P-VS2](/positions/value-in-selection/#p-vs2) battery that compares felt valence with selection should control for it. This is a confound to register, not evidence against the horn. It is the void's sharpest Map-specific consequence and should go in the article's tenet section, with a pointer, not an edit, to the positions page.
- **For phenomenal value realism.** Scheler's claim that value-feeling can be falsified "on the road of experience into consciousness" is a challenge to treating felt value as a transparent readout ([evaluative-phenomenal-character](/concepts/evaluative-phenomenal-character/), [phenomenal-value-realism](/topics/phenomenal-value-realism/)). A realist can reply, as Scheler himself does, that the true values "shine through". The Map should present this as the realist's burden, not as a refutation.
- **For bidirectional interaction.** If consciousness selects, the menu presented to it arrives pre-weighted by a feasibility process it cannot see. That is compatible with interaction and says nothing for or against it. It narrows what the Map can claim the selector knows about its own reasons.
- **For dualism.** Nothing in the sources requires or favours dualism. Under [P-V2](/positions/voids-as-evidence/#p-v2) the void is framework-independent and carries no confirmation of the tenets. Scheler's placing of the change "on the road of experience into consciousness" is not a location in the brain or outside it; it is a claim about stage, not substance.
- **No Many Worlds** has no bearing. The counterfactual self with the larger feasible set is a counterfactual, not a branch.

## Gaps in Research

- Elster's 1983 book chapter was not read; any article quote must come from the 1982 chapter.
- Scheler was read in an online English text whose translator attribution and pagination are unverified against a print edition.
- Laurin et al. 2012, Laurin 2018, Colburn 2011, Terlazzo 2022 and Nussbaum 2001 were read at abstract level only; Bruckner 2009 only through search excerpts.
- No study found asks participants *whether* they noticed their own adaptation; awareness is inferred from its absence in reports and from forecasting failures. A direct awareness probe after a Kay-style or Laurin-style manipulation would be the cleanest test of the void's central claim, and none was located.
- The envy/self-concealing-emotion candidate is closely related (Scheler's ressentiment is its extreme form) and is banked above.
- Whether "release from adaptation" has a measurable phenomenology (a sudden want on constraint removal) is suggested by Elster's reading of old survey data and is otherwise unexamined here.
- Possibly unanswerable: whether any of one's present wants are adapted. The structural tests can raise or lower suspicion; none can certify a particular want as unadapted without the freedom-to-do-otherwise condition, which the adapted case lacks.
