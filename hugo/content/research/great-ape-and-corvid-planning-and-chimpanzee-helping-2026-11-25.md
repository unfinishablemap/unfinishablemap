## Executive Summary

Great apes act now for needs hours away. Bonobos and orangutans saved tools for an apparatus they met an hour, and in a second experiment fourteen hours, later (Mulcahy & Call 2006). Apes also made tools in advance, though only a fraction of what was needed (Bräuer & Call 2015). A zoo chimpanzee cached stones while calm and threw them later in agitated displays (Osvath 2009). Ravens matched the apes' performance on tool and barter tasks with delays of up to 17 hours (Kabadayi & Osvath 2017). Chimpanzees help too. They fetch objects that someone is reaching for (Warneken & Tomasello 2006), and they pick the right tool for a partner's task when they can see that task (Yamamoto, Humle & Tanaka 2012). Every one of these findings has a lean reading in the published literature, stated by named critics at full strength:

- the planning data may be reproduced by associative learning (Lind 2018; Hampton 2019);
- there is "as yet no convincing evidence" of mental time travel in animals (Suddendorf & Corballis 2007);
- some apparent helping is a by-product of task design (Tennie, Jensen & Call 2016).

Those critics have in turn been answered (Osvath & Kabadayi 2018; Melis, Engelmann & Warneken 2018).

The Map's line holds at the grade the evidence supports. The capacities are present, and their limits are real: chimpanzees seldom help without a request, the planning studies covered a narrow range of tasks, and evidence that apes prepare for alternative futures is disputed. What remains open is whether the animal represents the future need, or the partner's goal, *as* such. That moves the amplification thesis from ape *absence* to the *declarative use* of capacities apes have, which is where `concepts/teaching-as-metarepresentation` already places it.

## Major Positions

### Rich reading: planning and helping as genuine, shared capacities
- **Proponents**: Osvath, Kabadayi (Lund); Call, Mulcahy, Bräuer (Leipzig and St Andrews); Warneken, Melis, Engelmann; Yamamoto; Engelmann et al. 2023 on alternative futures.
- **Core claim**: great apes, and independently ravens, plan for needs outside current perception and motivation, and chimpanzees help others with an understanding of their goals.
- **Grade they claim**: varies. Mulcahy & Call claim "precursor skills". Osvath & Osvath 2008 and Osvath & Karvonen 2012 claim pre-experience or episodic simulation, the richest grade. Yamamoto et al. say understanding of goals is present while voluntary helping is limited.
- **Relation to tenets**: neutral. Nothing in the rich reading bears on dualism; it reduces how much of the human–ape gap is available as a contrast.

### Lean reading: associative or by-product mechanisms suffice
- **Proponents**: Suddendorf, Corballis, Redshaw, Taylor (Queensland and Auckland); Hampton (Emory); Lind (Stockholm); Tennie, Jensen, Call (on helping); Silk et al. (on other-regarding preferences).
- **Core claim**: the behaviour is real, but its mechanism has not been shown to involve a represented future or a represented other's goal. Conditioned reinforcement, stimulus attraction or task by-products can produce it.
- **Strongest form**: Lind's simulations reproduce the key patterns of both flagship studies. Hampton's bottle-cap point and Tennie et al.'s GO/NO-GO result show the same thing in each case: the measure used cannot separate the rich mechanism from the lean one.
- **Relation to tenets**: also neutral. A lean mechanism in apes neither helps nor harms the Map. The Map's question sits one level up, at representation *as* such, which neither side's paradigms yet test.

## Key Debates

### Do the raven results show flexible planning? (2017–2019)
- **Sides**: Kabadayi & Osvath 2017 and Osvath & Kabadayi 2018 say yes. Redshaw, Taylor & Suddendorf 2017, Hampton 2019 and Lind 2018 say the study has not shown it.
- **Core disagreement**: whether selecting a reward-associated object before a delay requires representing the delayed use. Hampton's proposed discriminator is a condition where no future use is possible. Bourjade et al. 2014 ran that control in apes, and only three of thirteen individuals responded differentially.
- **State**: unresolved. No raven replication with a no-future-use control was found (not searched exhaustively).

### Can apes prepare for alternative futures? (2016–2024)
- **Sides**: Redshaw & Suddendorf 2016 and 2024 say there is no good evidence. Lambert & Osvath 2018 say the task is invalid. Engelmann et al. 2023 report a positive pre-registered result.
- **State**: live, and the most current exchange on the subject.

### Is chimpanzee helping intended or a by-product? (2016–2018)
- **Sides**: Tennie, Jensen & Call 2016 and Jensen, Tennie & Call 2018 argue by-product. Melis, Engelmann & Warneken 2018 argue the helping is real.
- **Shared ground**: signalled need strongly predicts helping, and proactive helping is rare. Yamamoto et al. 2009 and 2012 and Melis et al. 2018 agree on this, so it is the best-supported *limit* on helping.

```markdown
### Planning and Helping: Present but Limited

Great apes act now for needs hours away. Bonobos and orangutans saved a suitable tool, above baseline rates, for an apparatus they would meet an hour later, and in a second experiment fourteen hours later, without needing to see the apparatus while choosing; the authors read this as "precursor skills for planning" (Mulcahy & Call 2006). Apes also make tools in advance, though when eight were needed they prepared fewer than two per trial (Bräuer & Call 2015). A zoo chimpanzee gathered stones while calm, hours before throwing them in agitated displays at arriving visitors (Osvath 2009): a single observational case of preparing in one motivational state for use in another. [[cetacean-and-corvid-consciousness|Ravens]] chose a tool or a barter token for use up to 17 hours later, a performance their testers read as paralleling apes' (Kabadayi & Osvath 2017). The deflationary reading is strong. Suddendorf and Corballis (2007) found "as yet no convincing evidence" of mental time travel in nonhuman animals, and a later associative-learning model, run on both the ape and the raven studies, tracked their key patterns (Lind 2018).

Helping has the same shape. Three human-raised young chimpanzees fetched objects their caregiver was reaching for, unrewarded, showing what Warneken and Tomasello (2006) call "similar though less robust" skills and motivations to those of 18-month-old infants. Chimpanzees handed a partner the appropriate tool from a set of seven objects, but only when they could see its predicament, and they "will seldom help others without direct request for help" (Yamamoto, Humle & Tanaka 2012). Tennie, Jensen and Call (2016) found no evidence of helping once by-product benefits were separated out; Melis, Engelmann and Warneken (2018) reply that the chimpanzees failed that study's own test of apparatus understanding.

So apes plan and help. What stays open is whether they represent the future need, or the partner's goal, *as* such, and that is where the amplification thesis now rests. The record is compatible with that thesis and with readings on which experience merely accompanies these capacities, and discriminates between neither (see [[consciousness-and-cognitive-distinctiveness#What the Comparative Pattern Can and Cannot Establish|what comparative data can establish]]).
```
