## Executive Summary

Between 1927 and 1940 four writers built the first "quantum free-will" position and at least ten contemporaries answered it. The builders: Ralph Lillie (1927, physiological amplification of quantum indeterminacy), Arthur Eddington (1928, 1932, 1935), Arthur Compton (1931, 1935) and Pascual Jordan (1932, 1934, 1938, the *Verstärkertheorie*). The critics split into three kinds. (1) **The magnitude objection**: the first and sharpest statement is Eddington's own, in the same 1928 chapter that launched the wave: the few-key-atom "switch" is "too desperate a way of escape", and the only remaining route is for mind to "tamper with the odds" of a "crowd of atoms", which "involves a genuine physical difference between inorganic and organic (or, at any rate, conscious) matter" (pp. 312–314, raw). In 1935 he **restated** this, did not retreat, and did not adopt amplification: the controlling unit "contains many billions of atoms" and freedom must come from a "conscious unit" with "a much higher indeterminacy of behaviour—simply because of the unitary nature of that which in reality it represents, namely the Ego" (pp. 88–89, raw). (2) **The randomness-is-not-freedom objection**: Schlick's generic form is in *Fragen der Ethik* (1930; English 1939): "chance is identical with the absence of a cause; there is no other opposite of causality" (p. 156, raw), written without any reference to quantum physics. Its quantum-specific form, the one Popper quotes, is Schlick's 1935 *Erkenntnis* reply to Jordan ("they stop where chance begins ... a higher degree of randomness ... a higher degree of irresponsibility", Popper p. 227 citing *Erkenntnis* 5, p. 183). Schrödinger (1936) gave the physicist's version: if choices rode on quantum indeterminacy, "the laws of Nature are after all at 'my' mercy", and if they did not, "why on earth do I feel responsible for what I do" (carrier text). Hobart (1934) gave the general philosophical form. (3) **Relocation and dissolution**: Planck (1932) kept strict causality "even to the highest achievements of the human soul" and put freedom in the self-observing ego ("no eye can see itself", pp. 155–163, raw); Russell (1931) called Eddington's free-will argument one "based on ignorance" (quoted by Eddington 1935, p. 306, raw); Jeans (1933) held that free will "lies beyond the province of physics" and that even an opened "keyhole is useless unless we have a key of the same nature as the lock" (pp. 280–282, raw); Stebbing (1937) treated freedom as the opposite of compulsion ("not all causation is a form of being compelled", hocr fragment) and charged the physicists with misleading "theologians, preachers, and even philosophers" (Davidson 1942 carrier). Jordan's version drew its own 1935 *Erkenntnis* cluster: Zilsel, Neurath, Schlick, Frank, and the plant physiologist Bünning.

For the Map the finding is that both its selection posit and the strongest objection to it were on the table by 1935, and that Eddington stated the Map's own interface dilemma before anyone else: his p. 314 sentence ("It is one thing to allow the mind to direct an atom between two courses neither of which would be improbable for an inorganic atom; it is another thing to allow it to direct a crowd of atoms into a configuration which the secondary laws of physics would set aside as 'too improbable'") draws exactly the line the Minimal Quantum Interaction tenet draws, and he then chose the **deviation** horn the Map's default reading declines. None of the four builders can be enlisted for the Map's dualism (Eddington's background is "mind-stuff" idealism, Compton's argument is evolutionary, Jordan's is Machian positivism turned organicist), and the critics establish real debts the Map still carries.

## Key Sources

### Eddington, *The Nature of the Physical World* (1928), ch. XIV "Causation"
- **Archive**: `natureofphysical00eddi` (raw).
- **Where the wave starts** (p. 295): "science thereby withdraws its moral opposition to free-will." Followed by the intuition argument (p. 302): "nowhere in the world as yet is there any trace of a deciding factor as to whether I am going to lift my right hand or my left. It depends on an unfettered act of volition not yet made or foreshadowed."
- **Volition defined within indeterminism** (p. 311): "If the laws of physics are not strictly causal the most that can be said is that the behaviour of the conscious brain is one of the possible behaviours of a mechanical brain. Precisely so; and the decision between the possible behaviours is what we call volition."
- **Eddington's own randomness caveat** (pp. 311–312), the earliest in-window statement that atomic indeterminacy is not freedom: "When the decision of an atom is made between its possible quantum jumps, is that also 'volition'? Scarcely; the analogy is altogether too remote." And: "there is no more ground for calling the background of the spontaneous behaviour of the atom 'volition' than for calling the background of its causal behaviour 'reason'."
- **The key-atom hypothesis stated and rejected** (pp. 312–313): "It would be a possible though difficult hypothesis to assume that very few atoms (or possibly only one atom) have this direct contact with the conscious decision, and that these few atoms serve as a switch to deflect the material world from one course to the other. But it is physically improbable that each atom has its duty in the brain so precisely allotted that the control of its behaviour would prevail over all possible irregularities of the other atoms." "I do not think that our decisions are precisely balanced on the conduct of certain key-atoms. Could we pick out one atom in Einstein's brain and say that if it had made the wrong quantum jump there would have been a corresponding flaw in the theory of relativity?"
- **The magnitude conclusion** (p. 313): "It seems that we must attribute to the mind power not only to decide the behaviour of atoms individually but to affect systematically large groups—in fact to tamper with the odds on atomic behaviour. This has always been one of the most dubious points in the theory of the interaction of mind and matter."
- **The statistical-law horn, faced** (p. 313): "Has the mind power to set aside statistical laws which hold in inorganic matter? Unless this is granted its opportunity of interference seems to be too circumscribed to bring about the results which are observed to follow from mental decisions. But the admission involves a genuine physical difference between inorganic and organic (or, at any rate, conscious) matter. I would prefer to avoid this hypothesis, but it is necessary to face the issue squarely. The indeterminacy recognised in modern quantum theory is only a partial step towards freeing our actions from deterministic control." Then the insurance image (pp. 313–314): "we have admitted an uncertainty which may take or spare human lives; but we have yet to find an uncertainty which may upset the expectations of a life-insurance company."
- **The sentence the Map's Tenet 2 echoes** (p. 314): "the hypothesis that the mind operates through two or three key-atoms in the brain is too desperate a way of escape for us, and I reject it for the reasons already stated. It is one thing to allow the mind to direct an atom between two courses neither of which would be improbable for an inorganic atom; it is another thing to allow it to direct a crowd of atoms into a configuration which the secondary laws of physics would set aside as 'too improbable'."
- **His positive posit, the unity of the conscious brain-region** (pp. 314–315): "We must suppose that in the physical part of the brain immediately affected by a mental decision there is some kind of interdependence of behaviour of the atoms which is not present in inorganic matter." And: when considering their "collective behaviour we shall have to take account of the broader unifying trends in the mind-stuff, and not expect the statistical results to agree with those appropriate to structures of haphazard origin." He adds that even a materialist "must surely not neglect to find some kind of physical substitute for the Ego."
- **Tenet alignment**: the p. 314 line is the Minimal Quantum Interaction distinction; but Eddington's posit is a **deviation** from inorganic statistics (odds-tampering), the horn the Map's default Born-preserving reading declines. His metaphysics ("mind-stuff", "the physical world ... is the abstract symbolic representation of something") is structuralist idealism, not the Map's dualism.

### Eddington, *New Pathways in Science* (1935), ch. IV "The Decline of Determinism" and ch. XIV "Criticisms and Controversies"
- **Archive**: `in.ernet.dli.2015.203149` (raw). Ch. IV descends from the 1932 Mathematical Association presidential address (*Mathematical Gazette* 16: 66–80; *Nature* 129: 233–240, both Crossref-verified).
- **Epigraphs** (p. 72): Born ("The determinist should therefore not protest but experiment", *Naturwissenschaften* 1929 p. 117) and **Bohr** in Eddington's translation (*Naturwissenschaften* 1930 p. 77): "Whilst the feeling of free-will dominates the life of the spirit, the regularity of sensory phenomena lays down the demand for causality. But in both domains simultaneously the point in question is an idealisation, whose natural limitations can be more closely investigated, and which determine one another in the sense that the feeling of volition and the demand for causality are equally indispensable in the relation between Subject and Object which is the kernel of the problem of perception." This is Bohr's complementarity line on the subject, carried by a sympathetic reader.
- **Refusal of atomic free will** (p. 86): "The indeterminist is sometimes said to postulate 'something like free-will' in the individual atoms. ... But if it is suggested that we postulate psychological characters in the individual atoms of the kind which appear in our minds as human free-will, I deny this altogether. We do not discard one rash generalisation only to fall into another equally rash."
- **The chink** (p. 87): "Although the door of human freedom is opened, it is not flung wide open; only a chink of daylight appears. But I think this is sufficient to justify a reorientation of our attitude to the problem." (p. 88): "If our new-found freedom is like that of the mass of ·001 mgm., which is only allowed to stray [a fraction of a millimetre; figure garbled in the OCR] in a thousand years, it is not much to boast of. The physical results do not spontaneously suggest any higher degree of freedom than this."
- **Answer to fidelity question (a): the 1928 position is restated, amplification is not adopted** (p. 88): "If we could attribute the large-scale movements of our bodies to the 'trigger action' of the unpredetermined behaviour of a few key atoms in our brain cells the problem would be simple; for individual atoms have wide indeterminacy of behaviour. It is obvious that there is a great deal of trigger action in our bodily mechanism, as when the pent up energy of a muscle is released by a minute physical change in a nerve; but it would be rash to suppose that the physical controlling cause is contained in the configuration of a few dozen atoms. I should conjecture that the smallest unit of structure in which the physical effects of volition have their origin contains many billions of atoms. If such a unit behaved like an inorganic system of similar mass the indeterminacy would be insufficient to allow appreciable freedom. My own tentative view is that this 'conscious unit' does in fact differ from an inorganic system in having a much higher indeterminacy of behaviour—simply because of the unitary nature of that which in reality it represents, namely the Ego." (p. 89): "in the conscious cell the behaviour symbolises a single volition of the spirit and not a conflict of billions of independent impulses." "We conclude then that the activities of consciousness do not violate the laws of physics, since in the present indeterministic scheme there is freedom to operate within them." He then distinguishes his posit from a lucky coincidence (p. 89): "Here we do not suppose that the conspiracy of the atoms in a brain cell to bring about a certain physical result instead of all fighting against one another is due to a chance coincidence."
- **What moved between 1928 and 1935**: the emphasis. Responsibility and "self-understanding" become "the two aspects of human freedom on which I would lay most stress" (p. 89); he admits the self-formation dilemma is one "I am no more able to solve than hundreds who have tried before me" and rejects "the solution sometimes offered, that responsibility is a self-contradictory illusion" (pp. 89–90). The reasoning argument (p. 90): "I attach importance to the physical unpredictability of the motion of my pen, because it leaves it free to respond to the thought evolved in my brain which may or may not have been predetermined by the mental characteristics of my nature."
- **Eddington's own framing of what he claims** (p. 306, replying to Russell): "I have not suggested that either religion or free will can be deduced from modern physics; I have limited myself to showing that certain difficulties in reconciling them with physics have been removed." And (p. 301): "Many writers have said that our researches into the laws of atomic physics have no bearing on the problem of free will and volition. Planck evidently is not of this way of thinking. For him, as for me, the main problem turns on whether physics does or does not assert the principle of causality."
- **Davis 2009 n. 68** (per the sibling note) has Eddington stressing consciousness "amplifying uncertainties". Neither 1928 nor 1935 supports that: both reject the few-atom route by name and posit unitary behaviour of a many-billion-atom unit instead. Any Map page should follow the raw chapters.

### Russell, *The Scientific Outlook* (1931), as quoted by Eddington (1935, pp. 306–307)
- **Status**: the 1931 scans (`scientificoutloo0000bert`, `scientificoutloo0000russ_k8i7`) are lending-restricted; the three passages below are grep-verified **in Eddington's quotation of them**, with Eddington's page references into Russell.
- p. 112: "Sir Arthur Eddington deduces religion from the fact that atoms do not obey the laws of mathematics. Sir James Jeans deduces it from the fact that they do."
- p. 121: "The scientific argument leading to the conclusion which he rejects is much stronger than the argument in favour of free will, since that is based on ignorance, whereas the one we are now considering is based upon knowledge."
- p. 96: "[Eddington's] optimism is based upon the time-honoured principle that anything which cannot be proved untrue may be assumed to be true, a principle whose falsehood is proved by the fortunes of bookmakers."

### Planck, *Where Is Science Going?* (1932), chs. IV–V "Causation and Free Will"
- **Archive**: `whereissciencego00plan_0` (raw).
- **The riddle** (pp. 107–108): "This is one of man's oldest riddles. How can the independence of human volition be harmonized with the fact that we are integral parts of a universe which is subject to the rigid order of nature's laws?" ... "we have our most direct and intimate source of knowledge, which is the human consciousness, telling us that in the last resort our thought and volition are not subject to this causal order."
- **Causality kept universal** (pp. 155–156): "The principle of causality must be held to extend even to the highest achievements of the human soul. We must admit that the mind of each one of our greatest geniuses—Aristotle, Kant or Leonardo, Goethe or Beethoven, Dante or Shakespeare—even at the moment of its highest flights of thought or in the most profound inner workings of the soul, was subject" to the causal law.
- **Freedom relocated to the self-observing ego** (p. 161): "there is a point, one single point in the immeasurable world of mind and matter, where science and therefore every causal method of research is inapplicable, not only on practical grounds but also on logical grounds, and will always remain inapplicable. This point is the individual ego." (p. 163): "in principle there is no reason why we should not discover the causal connections in our own personal conduct, but that in practice we never can do so because this would mean that the observing subject would also be the object of research. And that is impossible; for no eye can see itself."
- **Einstein in the Epilogue** (Murphy's dialogue): "when you mention people who speak of such a thing as free will in nature it is difficult for me to find a suitable reply. The idea is of course preposterous." (raw; Jeans 1933 pp. 279 and Davidson 1942 p. 89 quote the same passage.)
- **Place**: Planck is the determinist critic who grants the wave's framing (the problem turns on causality) and denies its physics; his positive move is epistemic self-reference, not luck. Eddington 1935 ch. XIV answers him at length (pp. 300–301, raw).

### Schlick, *Problems of Ethics* (1939; German *Fragen der Ethik*, 1930), ch. VII "When Is a Man Responsible?"
- **Archive**: `in.ernet.dli.2015.213349` and `problemsofethics029364mbp` (raw). Chapter runs pp. 143–158; §1 "The Pseudo-Problem of Freedom of the Will" (p. 143), §4 "Freedom and Indeterminism" (p. 149), §7 "Causality as the Presupposition of Responsibility" (p. 156).
- **Dating**: preface signed "Vienna, September 1930"; so the generic chance objection **predates Compton's August 1931 note** and was not written against the quantum wave: the whole book has zero occurrences of "quantum", "Heisenberg", "Compton" or "Eddington".
- **The thesis** (p. 150): "the question of whether man is morally free (that is, has that freedom which, as we shall show, is the presupposition of moral responsibility) is altogether different from the problem of determinism." "Freedom means the opposite of compulsion; a man is free if he does not act under compulsion".
- **The chance passage** (p. 156): "if we should conceive of a decision as utterly without any cause (this would in all strictness be the indeterministic presupposition) then the act would be entirely a matter of chance, for chance is identical with the absence of a cause; there is no other opposite of causality. Could we under such conditions make the agent responsible? Certainly not."
- (p. 158): "If one makes clear to himself that a causeless happening is identical with a chance happening, and that, consequently, an indetermined will would destroy all responsibility, then every desire will cease which might be father to an indeterministic thought. No one can prove determinism, but it is certain that we assume its validity in all of our practical life". The chapter closes with the confusion table (p. 158): "Indeterminism (Chance). Freedom. / (No Cause). (No Compulsion)."
- **Note on the phrase** "chance is the only alternative to determinism": it is Popper's gloss, not Schlick's wording. Popper (p. 227): "The idea that the only alternative to determinism is just sheer chance was taken over by Schlick, together with many of his views on the subject, from Hume". Schlick's own words are "there is no other opposite of causality."

### Schlick, "Ergänzende Bemerkungen über P. Jordan's Versuch einer quantentheoretischen Deutung der Lebenserscheinungen", *Erkenntnis* 5 (1935): 181–183
- **Record**: Crossref 10.1007/bf00172305 (1935-12); reprinted in *Die Wiener Zeit* (Schlick Gesamtausgabe, 2008), pp. 611–620. Primary gated (Springer).
- **This is the source of Popper's Schlick quotation**, grep-verified in the Popper OCR: Popper pp. 226–227, "Determinists like Schlick have put it in this way: '... freedom of action, responsibility, and mental sanity, cannot reach beyond the realm of causality: they stop where chance begins. ... a higher degree of randomness ... [simply means] a higher degree of irresponsibility.'" with n. 40: "See M. Schlick, Erkenntnis, 5, p. 183 (extracted from the last eight lines of the first paragraph)." (Popper's translation; the German was not seen.)

- Popper n. 18 adds that Schlick was a physical determinist in *Allgemeine Erkenntnislehre* (2nd edn, 1925, p. 277).

### Bohr, "Light and Life" (lecture August 1932; *Nature* 131 (1933): 421–423, 457–459)
- **Record**: Crossref 10.1038/131421a0 (25 March 1933). Nature PDF behind the publisher's cookie wall; the Dover reprint in *Atomic Physics and Human Knowledge* is lending-restricted.
- **hocr fragments** recovered from the reprint: "...the organism that not only evade a causal mechanical description but resist even a physical..." ; "...whether he can because he will, but it is hardly disputable that we have the feeling of, so-to-speak, being..." ; "...begin and end with a renunciation as to explaining our own conscious activity."
- **Reading**: Bohr's line is that volition belongs to a description complementary to the physiological one, and that an analysis of explanation "would ... begin and end with a renunciation as to explaining our own conscious activity." It neither endorses nor needs the amplifier. The fuller Bohr statement available in verified form is the 1930 passage Eddington translated (above). Whether Bohr ever criticised Jordan's amplifier by name was not established here (Gaps).

## Major Positions

### The wave: indeterminacy as the opening (Lillie 1927; Eddington 1928/1935; Compton 1931/1935; Jordan 1932–1938)
- **Core claim** (Eddington's formulation, p. 311): physics no longer fixes the brain's behaviour; the decision among the physically possible behaviours "is what we call volition".
- **Three mechanisms inside the wave**: Lillie–Compton–Jordan amplification of single events (see the sibling note for Compton's apparatus and caveats); Eddington's unitary "conscious unit" that tampers with the odds across billions of atoms; Jeans's and Weyl's cautious "crevices".
- **Relation to site tenets**: the wave as a whole supplies the Map's Tenet 3 opening and its Tenet 2 vocabulary, but no member of it is a Map dualist: Eddington's background is "mind-stuff"; Compton's efficacy argument is evolutionary and he declared the question beyond physics; Jordan's is positivist organicism with a political career attached.

### Critic type 1: magnitude (Eddington 1928, pp. 312–314; restated 1935, p. 88)
- **Proponent**: Eddington against his own side, before Compton wrote. Popper's grain objection (1965) is the late form.
- **Relation to tenets**: this is Horn 2's magnitude form in [trilemma-of-selection](/topics/trilemma-of-selection/); the Map's answer is Tenet 2 plus neural amplification, which is the Lillie–Compton reply Eddington did not take.

### Critic type 2: randomness is not freedom (Schlick 1930 generic; Hobart 1934; Schlick 1935 against Jordan; Schrödinger 1936; Stebbing 1937)
- **Core claim**: an undetermined act is "entirely a matter of chance" (Schlick 1930, p. 156); "in so far as [it] is undetermined, the act is not its act" (Hobart); "the laws of Nature are ... at 'my' mercy" or else "why ... do I feel responsible" (Schrödinger).
- **Relation to tenets**: this is the Map's [luck objection](/concepts/quantum-indeterminacy-free-will/); the Map's reply (selection tracks reasons) is in-framework only, as that page already says. Schlick's "there is no other opposite of causality" is a partition the Map rejects along with Popper ("clearly absurd", Popper p. 228), which is the Occam's-Razor-Has-Limits point applied to a definitional dichotomy.

### Critic type 3: relocation and dissolution (Russell 1931; Planck 1932; Jeans 1933; Stebbing 1937; Dingle 1931)
- Russell: indeterminacy is ignorance, so nothing follows. Planck: causality universal, freedom an artefact of self-observation ("no eye can see itself"). Jeans: free will outside physics; and even an opening needs a key of the lock's nature. Stebbing: freedom is non-compulsion; the moral problem never needed physical indeterminism, and the physicists misled the public. Dingle: physics contributes nothing.
- **Relation to tenets**: Jeans's keyhole is the interface problem the Map's [quantum-interface position](/positions/quantum-interface/) carries; Stebbing's dissolution is the [compatibilist-symmetry-challenge](/concepts/compatibilist-symmetry-challenge/); Planck's self-reference argument is unused on the Map and is a genuine third thing (an epistemic, not a modal, account of felt freedom).

## Key Debates

### Did Eddington endorse amplification?
- **Sides**: Davis 2009 (yes, "amplifying uncertainties") versus the raw chapters (1928 pp. 312–314 and 1935 p. 88 both reject the few-atom route and posit unity instead).
- **Current state**: settled by the raw text; the Map should not describe Eddington as an amplifier theorist. The "trigger action" phrase at 1935 p. 88 is the hypothesis he names in order to call it "rash".

### Which objection came first, and against whom?
- **Magnitude**: (Eddington, 1928), answering the Lillie 1927 family; Compton 1931 is the reply.
- **Randomness-is-not-freedom**: generic (Schlick, 1930) and (Hobart, 1934), neither aimed at physics; quantum-specific (Schlick, 1935) against Jordan, and (Schrödinger, 1936) as the physicist's dilemma; (Stebbing, 1937) in the ordinary-language register against Eddington. Eddington himself (1928, pp. 311–312) had already said atomic indeterminacy is not volition, which is why his posit is unity rather than a single jump.
- **Interface**: (Jeans, 1933), with Descartes named.

### Deviation or Born-preservation?
- Eddington 1928 chose deviation: mind must "tamper with the odds" and the statistics of a conscious region will not "agree with those appropriate to structures of haphazard origin" (pp. 313–315). Schrödinger 1936 is the first to say that this is the horn on which "the laws of Nature are at my mercy". The Map's default reading takes the other horn and records the resulting observer-indistinguishability as a debt ([popper-three-worlds-interactionism](/concepts/popper-three-worlds-interactionism/#penny-toss) already says so). The historical point for the Map: the dilemma is not a modern discovery; it was stated and chosen on in 1928 and answered in 1936.
