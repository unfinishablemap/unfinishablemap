**Voids category**: Mixed. What emotional tears are *for* is **Unexplored** (in the sense of [three-kinds-of-void](/voids/three-kinds-of-void/)): the science has four live answers and no settled one. What a given episode of crying is *about*, and whether it helped, is **Occluded** for the crier: the tears arrive with a felt reason, the felt reason can be produced by an electrode, and the felt relief can be the ordinary return from a low. Plessner's thesis supplies a possible **Unexplorable** residue: if weeping is the answer a person gives where every articulate answer has run out, a full articulate account of it would be the very response whose absence it marks. That last claim is a philosophical inference and is flagged as such wherever it appears.

**Nearest neighbours and the difference.**

- [mirth-void](/voids/mirth-void/) is the sibling and the main risk. Its four faces are the reason gap (stimulated laughter gets a made-up cause), the dissection gap (analysis kills the joke), the will gap (two motor systems) and the self-surprise gap. Weeping shares the reason gap and the will gap in outline, and the article must say so and point there rather than rebuild them. It does not share the dissection or self-surprise gaps: explaining why a scene is moving does not reliably stop the tears, and one can be moved by one's own writing. It adds three faces mirth lacks: a function nobody has established, a relief whose reality the crier cannot judge, and the valence crossing (the same tears at grief and at joy). Mirth has a felt state with a name. Weeping is a behaviour whose felt state is disputed (sadness, being moved, kama muta, helplessness), which is part of the void.
- [confabulation-void](/voids/confabulation-void/) is the general claim that consciousness cannot detect its own fabricated explanations. The stimulation case below is a specific instance, and the article should cite the general page in a clause.
- [valence-void](/voids/valence-void/) asks why experience is sorted into good and bad at all. The weeping void takes the sorting for granted and notes that one bodily expression straddles it.
- [mattering-void](/voids/mattering-void/) concerns the felt weight of significance. Cova and Deonna's account of being moved (a positive "core value" standing out) is a candidate content for what tears of joy register, so the two pages should cross-link; the subject here is the expression and its opacity, not mattering as such.
- [agency-void](/voids/agency-void/) covers what consciousness cannot control, with Wegner's rebound. Darwin's 1872 observation that one effort to repress tears "seems often to lead to an opposite result" is an early instance and belongs in a sentence with a pointer.
- [interoceptive-void](/voids/interoceptive-void/) covers the body's partial opacity to its owner. Tears are partly visible to their owner (one feels them) and fully visible to others, so the relevant opacity is of meaning, not of sensation.
- [voids-between-minds](/voids/voids-between-minds/) notes that observers can see something about a mind that the mind cannot operate from within. Provine's tear-removal result (tears resolve observers' ambiguity) and the contested chemosignal results give two versions of this, one visual, one possibly below anyone's perception.

## Executive Summary

Emotional tears are, in the field's own words, "a poorly understood behavior that is considered uniquely human" (Gelstein et al. 2011) [raw-abstract]. Darwin called weeping "an incidental result, as purposeless as the secretion of tears from a blow outside the eye" (1872) [raw], and a century and a half later the leading reviewers still describe a debate over its function (Zickfeld & Wróbel 2023, preprint) [raw-crossref]. That is the first face: the **function void**, unexplored and being explored.

The second face concerns the crier. A woman with Parkinson's disease, stimulated through one electrode contact, began to weep within seconds; asked why she was crying, she gave reasons ("I no longer wish to live ... Everything is useless") and added "I'm crying over myself, of course" (Bejjani et al. 1999, as quoted by Schildkrout) [raw-secondary]. The electrode was the cause. In pathological crying the expression occurs without the feeling (Parvizi et al. 2001) [raw-abstract]. Observers read tears by context (Provine et al. 2009; Aragón & Bargh 2018), and Vingerhoets and Bylsma (2016) suggest the crier does something similar: sentimental tears "typically occur quite unexpectedly" and seem "to make the individual aware that there is something important happening" [raw]. This is the **occasion void**: the crier's access to what the tears are about is not privileged and can be manufactured.

The third face is the **relief void**. Over 70% of respondents in a 37-country survey said crying generally makes them feel better, but only 50% of the same respondents reported a mood improvement after their most recent cry (Vingerhoets & Bylsma 2016, reporting Vingerhoets 2013) [raw-secondary]. Laboratory criers feel worse immediately, recover to baseline by 20 minutes and report improvement at 90 (Gračanin et al. 2015) [raw-abstract], and the recovery "may be misperceived as a real mood improvement" (Gračanin, Bylsma & Vingerhoets 2014) [raw]. A fourth face, the **will gap**, is shared with mirth and should be brief. Plessner's reading of laughing and crying as "capitulations" at the limits of behaviour, which are nonetheless meaningful answers, gives the void its philosophical frame and a possibly unexplorable core.

### Darwin (1872), *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals*, ch. VI
- **URL**: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1227/pg1227.txt (Project Gutenberg #1227; the edition behind it was not checked against the 1872 first edition).
- **Type**: Book.
- **Key points**:
  - "we must look at weeping as an incidental result, as purposeless as the secretion of tears from a blow outside the eye, or as a sneeze from the retina being affected by a bright light, yet this does not present any difficulty in our understanding how the secretion of tears serves as a relief to suffering" [raw]. Vingerhoets, van de Ven and van der Velden (2016) give the page as p. 175 [raw-secondary]; the Gutenberg text has no pagination.
  - **Darwin held both halves.** The tears are purposeless *as an adaptation*, and yet they relieve. Quoting only "purposeless" misrepresents him. The article must keep the "yet".
  - On control: "A single effort of repression brought to bear on the lacrymal glands does little, and indeed seems often to lead to an opposite result. An old and experienced physician told me that he had always found that the only means to check the occasional bitter weeping of ladies who consulted him, and who themselves wished to desist, was earnestly to beg them not to try, and to assure them that nothing would relieve them so much as prolonged and copious crying" [raw]. Anecdote, second-hand, and of its period; usable as Darwin's report, not as evidence.
  - On the other direction, Darwin reports the Rev. R. Taylor's claim that New Zealand women "can voluntarily shed tears in abundance" for mourning [raw]. So voluntary weeping is reported as a cultivated skill. Any "tears cannot be willed" sentence is false as stated.
  - On joy: tears "during loud laughter" (chapter contents) and reports of tears in laughter from several peoples [raw].
- **Tenet alignment**: Occam's Razor Has Limits, as the starting point of a long search for a single function.

### Gračanin, Bylsma & Vingerhoets (2018), "Why Only Humans Shed Emotional Tears: Evolutionary and Cultural Perspectives"
- **Reference**: *Human Nature* 29(2): 104–133. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12110-018-9312-8. PMID 29556968.
- **Type**: Review. **Abstract only.** The Tilburg repository PDF and Springer both returned bot-check pages; recorded as blocked and not worked around.
- **Key points**:
  - "Producing emotional tears is a universal and uniquely human behavior. Until recently, tears have received little serious attention from scientists" [raw-abstract].
  - "The main hypothesis that emerges from this overview is that crying evolved as an emotional expression that signals distress and promotes prosocial behaviors in conspecifics" [raw-abstract].
  - Effects on the crier's mood are proposed to follow "primarily as a consequence of fulfillment of the proposed signaling function of tears" [raw-abstract]: on this view the relief, where it occurs, is mostly socially mediated.
  - Ritual weeping is read as fitting the signalling framework [raw-abstract].
- **Tenet alignment**: neutral. The leading evolutionary account, and the one the article should state first.

### Vingerhoets & Bylsma (2016), "The Riddle of Human Emotional Crying: A Challenge for Emotion Researchers"
- **Reference**: *Emotion Review* 8(3): 207–217. https://doi.org/10.1177/1754073915586226. PMC6402489 (full text via NCBI efetch; Europe PMC's own full-text endpoint returned nothing).
- **Type**: Review, full text read.
- **Key points**:
  - In popular media "there are many strong claims about crying (e.g., crying brings relief) of which the scientific basis is not clear" [raw-abstract].
  - Crying "should rather be considered as a behavior that obeys the laws of operant conditioning and is under the influence of biological, psychological, and social factors than as a reflex-like symptom" [raw].
  - Exposure "to an emotional event by itself often does not suffice" to produce tears [raw].
  - The most common trigger is "a feeling of powerlessness or helplessness, often in combination with other emotions"; in positive cases tears go with "being overwhelmed with joy, elation, or gratitude", and "Tears thus may actually reflect feelings that cannot be expressed or consummated in other behaviors" [raw]. This last sentence is an empirical psychologist arriving at Plessner's thesis by another road.
  - **The general/specific gap**: "over 70%" of respondents in a 37-country study agreed that crying generally helps them feel better, but "when being asked specifically about their most recent crying episode, only 50% of these very same respondents reportedly had experienced a positive mood change after crying (see Vingerhoets, 2013)" [raw]. Reported from Vingerhoets 2013, which was not read.
  - **Tears as news to the crier**: "it is also plausible that tears may feed back to the crying individual about his/her psychological state. In particular, sentimental crying ... typically occurs quite unexpectedly, and seems to make the individual aware that there is something important happening and that the situation has a major impact on the crying individual (Cova & Deonna, 2014; Tan & Frijda, 1999; Vingerhoets, 2013)" [raw]. "Plausible" is their word; it is a hypothesis.
  - Frey's blood-clearance hypothesis is called "very popular"; other mechanisms "should be taken more seriously" [raw].
- **Tenet alignment**: the feedback sentence is the empirical hinge of the occasion face.

### Gračanin, Bylsma & Vingerhoets (2014), "Is crying a self-soothing behavior?"
- **Reference**: *Frontiers in Psychology* 5: 502. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00502. PMC4035568, full text read.
- **Type**: Review.
- **Key points**:
  - Tears come at "watching a movie or enjoying the beauty of nature ... just as the passing away of attachment figures or birth of a child", and crying occurs in situations of "being overwhelmed by strong emotion, be it negative or positive" [raw].
  - **Against detoxification**: salivary cortisol fell more in women who cried more at a film (Vingerhoets & Kirschbaum 1997), but "similar effects were observed after separation from the mother in young rhesus monkeys that do not produce emotional tears, but merely emit distress calls", which "seriously challenges the biochemical detoxification" reading [raw].
  - **The recovery artefact**: "Since mood in criers, compared to non-criers, reaches a nadir after a crying episode, the necessary consequence of this change is the return to baseline that must happen sooner or later. This recovery process can also be experienced as very intensive and, as such, it may be misperceived as a real mood improvement" [raw]. Also: retrospective reporters "likely are biased by implicit lay theories, which pose that crying brings mood improvement and relief (Cornelius, 1997)" [raw].
  - Efran and Spangler's (1979) two-factor theory, reported here: crying "first appears during an emotionally arousing event in the phase of recovery after the initial phase of arousal", when a barrier "has disappeared or can be given up" [raw-secondary]. Efran & Spangler not read.
  - The authors' own conclusion is that there is "converging evidence for the direct, self-soothing effects of crying" [raw-abstract]. So the relief void must be stated as uncertainty about reading relief from inside, not as a finding that crying does not help.
- **Tenet alignment**: the recovery-artefact sentence is the core of the relief face.

### Gračanin, Vingerhoets, Kardum, Zupčić, Šantek & Šimić (2015), "Why crying does and sometimes does not seem to alleviate mood: a quasi-experimental study"
- **Reference**: *Motivation and Emotion* 39(6): 953–960. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-015-9507-9. PMC4608976, full text read.
- **Type**: Laboratory study, 28 observed criers and 32 non-criers, films chosen to provoke tears "through a wide spectrum of negative and positive emotions" [raw].
- **Key points**:
  - "Whereas retrospective studies suggest that crying can be beneficial in terms of mood enhancement, results of quasi-experimental laboratory studies consistently demonstrate its negative effects on mood" [raw-abstract].
  - Immediately after the film "negative mood significantly increased in criers, while it did not change in non-criers"; mood returned to baseline at the third measurement (20 minutes) and "Criers subsequently reported mood enhancements at the final measurement" (90 minutes) compared with before the film [raw-abstract].
  - In retrospective designs "participants are generally free to choose which crying episodes they describe", and "it is not possible to precisely define the time interval between the crying episode and the reported feeling" [raw].
  - The authors themselves call for attention to "factors that influence the memory of emotional events and mood" [raw].
- **Caution**: one small sample; the 90-minute improvement is relative to baseline, and the paper does not show that crying, rather than recovering from a low, is what produced it. The 2014 review's recovery-artefact sentence is the counter-reading. Both belong in the article.

### Gross, Fredrickson & Levenson (1994), "The psychophysiology of crying"
- **Reference**: *Psychophysiology* 31(5): 460–468. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8986.1994.tb01049.x. PMID 7972600.
- **Type**: Laboratory study, abstract only.
- **Key points**: 150 women watched a sad film; 33 cried. "Crying is thus associated with an aversive state, including negative emotion and a complex mixture of sympathetic, parasympathetic, and somatic activation" [raw-abstract]. Two prior views are stated: crying "serves homeostasis by facilitating recovery" or "produces an aversive high-arousal state that motivates behavior aimed at ending the tears" [raw-abstract].
- **Tenet alignment**: neutral; the classic laboratory result the relief face rests on.

### Bejjani et al. (1999), "Transient acute depression induced by high-frequency deep-brain stimulation", read through Schildkrout
- **Reference**: Bejjani, B.-P., Damier, P., Arnulf, I., Thivard, L., Bonnet, A.-M., Dormont, D., Cornu, P., Pidoux, B., Samson, Y., & Agid, Y. *New England Journal of Medicine* 340(19): 1476–1480. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199905133401905. PMID 10320386. Europe PMC holds no abstract.
- **Type**: Case report. **Primary not obtained**: a university-hosted PDF returned an HTML page to curl and HTTP 404 to WebFetch. Everything below is from B. Schildkrout, "Depression: An Uncommon Cause", *Psychiatric Times* (posted 7 January 2015, since updated), https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/depression-uncommon-cause, fetched raw.
- **Key points** (all [raw-secondary], Schildkrout's text):
  - A 65-year-old woman with Parkinson's disease and "no psychiatric history"; photographs in the report "show her crying". Schildkrout names her "Ms P"; the NEJM report does not name her.
  - "In response to being asked why she was crying, Ms P is reported to have said, 'I'm falling down in my head, I no longer wish to live, to see anything, hear anything, feel anything . . .'" Asked whether she was in pain: "No, I'm fed up with life, I've had enough ... Everything is useless". Later: "I'm crying over myself, of course."
  - "Within 5 seconds of activating contact 0 on the left" she became "acutely and profoundly depressed"; the depression "abated within 90 seconds" after the stimulation was turned off, followed by about five minutes in which "she laughed and joked with the examiner". She "remembered the entire experience". Stimulation of contact 0 was repeated twice with the same result; sham stimulation produced no change.
- **What the case shows, kept modest**: a physically caused episode of weeping came with sincere, mood-congruent, global reasons ("everything is useless") and a sense of self-evidence ("of course"). It is the weeping analogue of the 1998 laughter-stimulation case in [mirth-void](/voids/mirth-void/), and the article should present it that way. It does **not** show that ordinary weeping is confabulated; it shows that the felt reason is not a certificate of the cause.
- **Caution**: the patient's words are a translation (presumably from French) given in the NEJM report as Schildkrout quotes it. Do not quote them as verified against the NEJM text. The induced state was a depression with tears; whether the tears or the mood came first is not reported in what was read.

### Parvizi, Anderson, Martin, Damasio & Damasio (2001), "Pathological laughter and crying: a link to the cerebellum"
- **Reference**: *Brain* 124(9): 1708–1719. https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/124.9.1708. PMID 11522574.
- **Type**: Case study and theory, abstract only.
- **Key points**: episodes "occur either without an apparent triggering stimulus or following a stimulus that would not have led the subject to laugh or cry prior to the onset of the condition"; "PLC is a disorder of emotional expression rather than a primary disturbance of feelings" [raw-abstract]. The authors propose that cerebellar structures which "automatically adjust the execution of laughter or crying to the cognitive and situational context" then operate "on the basis of incomplete information about that context" [raw-abstract].
- **Use**: the expression and the feeling dissociate, so tears are not a readout of feeling even for neurology. The context-fitting claim is also the mechanism-level version of the occasion face: on Parvizi's account the fitting of tears to context is itself automatic, done below the person.

### Provine, Krosnowski & Brocato (2009), "Tearing: Breakthrough in Human Emotional Signaling"
- **Reference**: *Evolutionary Psychology* 7(1). https://doi.org/10.1177/147470490900700107.
- **Type**: Experiment, abstract from the Crossref record.
- **Key points**: "Tear removal produced faces rated as less sad. Anecdotal findings suggest further that tear-removal often produced faces of uncertain emotional valence, perhaps awe, concern, or puzzlement, not just less sad. Tearing signaled sadness and resolved ambiguity" [raw-crossref]. Emotional tearing "may be unique to humans and does not develop until several months after birth" [raw-crossref].
- **Use**: for an observer, tears disambiguate. That sits oddly beside tears of joy, and the oddity is resolved by context (next source). The article can use the contrast: tears fix the reading *given a context*, and supply none of their own.

### Aragón, Clark, Dyer & Bargh (2015); Aragón & Bargh (2018): dimorphous expressions
- **References**: *Psychological Science* 26(3): 259–273, https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614561044; *Cognition & Emotion* 32(2): 286–302, https://doi.org/10.1080/02699931.2017.1301388.
- **Type**: Experiments, abstracts only.
- **Key points**:
  - Extremely positive experiences "often generate both positive expressions (e.g., smiles) and expressions normatively reserved for negative emotions (e.g., tears)" [raw-abstract, 2015]. The proposed function is emotion regulation; evidence called "preliminary" [raw-abstract, 2015].
  - Happiness "can also be expressed through physical displays that without context, would appear to be sadness (tears, downward turned mouths, and crumpled body postures)" [raw-abstract, 2018]. Observers "interpreted that ... sadness displayed in positive contexts signalled happy-consummatory states" [raw-abstract, 2018].
- **Use**: "without context" is the operative phrase. The article should not claim that criers cannot tell joy from grief. The claim is narrower: the tear does not carry its valence, and whoever reads it, crier included, reads it from the situation.

### Cova & Deonna (2014), "Being moved"
- **Reference**: *Philosophical Studies* 169(3): 447–466 (online 2013). https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-013-0192-9. Published version from the University of Geneva open archive (unige:109384), full text read.
- **Type**: Philosophy paper with survey data.
- **Key points**:
  - "being moved, we claim, is the experience of a positive core value (particular object) perceived by the moved subject as standing out (formal object) in the circumstances triggering the emotion" [raw].
  - Against the idea that tears at good news must be secretly sad: "Do people moved to tears by surprise reunions in their honour really have in mind that life is a trying journey?" [raw].
  - Being moved "appears to involve a sense of being taken by force" [raw]; its phenomenology "may well resemble that of some episodes of sadness (in particular in the presence of tears and of a lump in one's throat)" but it is "far from unpleasant" [raw].
  - In their survey, being-moved recollections drew more reports of "tears" than joy recollections [raw].
- **Use**: a serious philosophical answer to the valence crossing. Tears of joy may mark a distinct emotion, not ambiguity. This partly closes the void and the article must say so. What it does not close is that the crier's ordinary words ("I'm so happy", "I don't know why I'm crying") do not name it, and the theories disagree about what it is.

### Zickfeld et al. (2019), "Kama muta: Conceptualizing and measuring the experience often labelled being moved across 19 nations and 15 languages"
- **Reference**: *Emotion* 19(3): 402–424. https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000450. PMID 29888936.
- **Type**: Multi-site survey study (3,542 participants), abstract only.
- **Key points**: kama muta is "evoked by experiencing or observing a sudden intensification of communal sharing" and "commonly accompanied by a warm feeling in the chest, moist eyes or tears, chills or piloerection, feeling choked up or having a lump in the throat, buoyancy, and exhilaration" [raw-abstract]. Speakers say they are "moved to tears," "emotionally touched," "stirred" [raw-abstract].
- **Use**: a second, rival positive-emotion account of many tears (communal sharing rather than core value). The two should be named as rivals, not merged.

### Gelstein et al. (2011); Gračanin, van Assen, Omrčen, Koraj & Vingerhoets (2017); Agron et al. (2023): the chemosignal dispute
- **References**: *Science* 331(6014): 226–230, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1198331; *Cognition & Emotion* 31(1): 139–150, https://doi.org/10.1080/02699931.2016.1151402; *PLoS Biology* 21(12): e3002442, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3002442.
- **Type**: Experiments, abstracts only.
- **Key points**:
  - Gelstein et al.: "Emotional tearing is a poorly understood behavior that is considered uniquely human"; "merely sniffing negative-emotion-related odorless tears obtained from women donors induced reductions in sexual appeal attributed by men to pictures of women's faces", with reduced arousal and testosterone [raw-abstract].
  - Gračanin et al. 2017: "All three studies, separately or combined in a meta-analysis, failed to replicate the original inhibitory effects of tears"; effects "if any, are very weak at best"; crying "exerts its strong inter-personal effects through the visual and auditory sensory channels" [raw-abstract].
  - Agron et al. 2023 (Sobel's lab, as was Gelstein): "sniffing emotional tears with no odor percept reduced human male aggression by 43.7%", with four olfactory receptors responding in vitro [raw-abstract].
- **Use**: an open empirical dispute about whether tears carry a message that neither the crier nor the recipient perceives. The article must give both sides and must not state the chemosignal as established. If it is real, it is a striking case for [voids-between-minds](/voids/voids-between-minds/): a communication channel opaque to both ends.

- **Type**: Review, abstract from the Crossref record.

### Simons, Bruder, van der Löwe & Parkinson (2012), "Why try (not) to cry: intra- and inter-personal motives for crying regulation"
- **Reference**: *Frontiers in Psychology* 3: 597. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00597. PMC3544119, full text read. (Received 31 July 2012, accepted 18 December 2012; Vingerhoets and Bylsma cite it as 2013.)

- **Key points**: to produce tears, "actors are commonly instructed to think of a sad memory in order to help them cry when required", and "actors also sometimes make their tears flow by rubbing their eyelids with menthol or other irritants" [raw]. That is the will gap's shape, and it should be stated as what this survey reports, with Darwin's cultivated mourners as the counter-case.

### Plessner (1941), *Laughing and Crying*, read through Rasini (2013)
- **Primary**: Plessner, H. *Lachen und Weinen: Eine Untersuchung der* [or *nach den*] *Grenzen menschlichen Verhaltens*. First published in the Netherlands; Rasini gives 1941, Northwestern University Press says "First published in German in 1940". The title preposition and the year are **unresolved**. English: *Laughing and Crying: A Study of the Limits of Human Behavior*, trans. J. S. Churchill and M. Grene, Evanston: Northwestern University Press, 1970; open edition at https://doi.org/10.21985/N2313X (CC BY-NC-ND 4.0). **The English text was not read**: the open repository refused curl (HTTP 403) and the PDF exceeded WebFetch's size limit.
- **Secondary read in full**: Rasini, V. (2013). "L'espressione non verbale: il riso e il pianto in Plessner". *RIFL* 7(2): 123–135. https://doi.org/10.4396/20130709. Rasini cites the Italian edition she edited (Plessner 2000, *Il riso e il pianto*, Milan: Bompiani); page numbers below in "Plessner 2000" are that edition's, as Rasini gives them.
- **Key points** (English is this note's translation; all [raw-it]):
  - Rasini's English abstract: laughter and crying "are opaque: they cannot be connected with a specific mood or a specific cause"; they are "'to the limit' kinds of conduct" that reveal man's "eccentric" nature, "his connection with his body but also his ability to transcend it" [raw, English in the original].
  - Opacity: anger, joy, love and so on give the body a "symbolic imprint" through which the affect shows, but laughing and crying remain "opaque" (Rasini p. 125, citing Plessner 2000: 51–52). Their opacity is "the consequence of a total loss of the mastery that man ordinarily exercises over his own body" (Rasini p. 130).
  - Capitulation: laughing and crying "represent rather 'capitulations', moments of falling: one abandons oneself to laughter; one lets oneself go into crying" (Rasini p. 130, citing Plessner 2000: 110). When a person cannot meet a situation, "his higher unity of control abdicates in favour of pure corporeality" (Rasini p. 131, Rasini's paraphrase).
  - Yet they are answers: laughing and crying are still "meaningful reactions", "an authentic answer: the only one possible for the person in a situation that has become in some way 'impossible'". Rasini quotes Plessner: "the real impossibility of finding a fitting expression and a suitable answer is at the same time the only fitting expression and the only suitable answer" (Rasini p. 131, citing Plessner 2000: 111). The person, Plessner says, "remains a person".
  - Crying specifically: one is moved to tears in situations where "the relativity of subjective existence fails before the power of a term felt as 'absolute'"; Plessner: "With the interruption of the normal relativity of our existence in the world and with the world, which ordinarily hides from us the purity of being, we reach the limit of all conduct" (Rasini p. 132, citing Plessner 2000: 220). This holds "whether the occasion is a pain or a feeling of sublime joy" (Rasini p. 132, Rasini's words). So for Plessner the difference between laughing and crying is not pleasure versus pain but two ways in which conduct reaches its limit: ambiguity (laughter) and the loss of relativity before something overwhelming (crying).
  - Crying isolates where laughter is shared: "crying involves a closing of man in on himself"; the trigger works the more it is "subjectively lived, and indeed the sharing of the motive can inhibit crying" (Rasini p. 132).
  - Neglect: Plessner says that anyone who knows the history of psychology and aesthetics will understand "the asymmetric division of interest, the preferential treatment reserved for laughter and the neglect of crying" (Rasini p. 126, citing Plessner 2000: 27).
  - Footnote 7: if the situation is felt as seriously threatening to life, the person "loses his head" and capitulates entirely, as a person; laughing and crying require that it not be (Rasini p. 131).
  - Anti-metaphysical: "No recourse to metaphysical terms" (Rasini p. 126, citing Plessner 2000: 34). Plessner's anthropology is not dualist. The tension "between being a body and having a body" (Rasini p. 133) is a structure of lived existence, not a two-substance claim.
- **Tenet alignment**: Dualism, carefully. Plessner gives the best phenomenological description of what it is like for a person to yield to the body and still be answering through it. He does not draw a dualist conclusion, and the article must not imply he does.
- **Guard**: if the article quotes Plessner, it must quote the 1970 English translation from a read copy. Until then, paraphrase and attribute to Plessner "as Rasini presents him".

## The Void

### Nature of the Limit

Four faces, plus one possible core.

1. **The function void (Unexplored).** There is no settled answer to what emotional tears are for. The candidates are a distress signal that elicits help (Gračanin et al. 2018; Provine et al. 2009; Zickfeld & Wróbel 2023), self-soothing (Gračanin et al. 2014), a chemical signal (Gelstein et al. 2011; Agron et al. 2023; failed replication Gračanin et al. 2017), and none at all as an adaptation (Darwin 1872). Blood clearance (Frey 1985) is popular and weakly supported. This is ordinary scientific ignorance and is being reduced. It belongs in a voids article for one reason: the people who weep, every one of them, have no access to which of these, if any, their tears are doing.

2. **The occasion void (Occluded).** The crier does not have privileged access to what the tears are about. Three findings support this, in order of strength. An electrode produced weeping with sincere global reasons and the comment "of course" (Bejjani et al. 1999). Pathological crying shows the expression running without the feeling (Parvizi et al. 2001). Tears appear at opposite valences and are read by context; Vingerhoets and Bylsma (2016) suggest the crier, too, learns from the tears "that there is something important happening". On this face the crier's relation to her tears is partly that of an observer: the tears are evidence she interprets, not a report she issued. Plessner's "opacity" is the philosophical statement of the same point.

3. **The relief void (Occluded).** Whether a cry helped cannot be read off from inside. Criers feel worse at once (Gross et al. 1994; Gračanin et al. 2015), the return to baseline "may be misperceived as a real mood improvement" (Gračanin et al. 2014), the general belief in relief outruns the specific episode (over 70% against 50%, Vingerhoets & Bylsma 2016), and recall favours the cries that fit the lay theory (Cornelius 1997, as reported). Against this, Gračanin et al. (2015) find a real improvement over baseline at 90 minutes, and the 2014 review concludes there is converging evidence for self-soothing. So the void is not "crying does not help". It is that the crier's sense that it helped cannot distinguish help from recovery, and the science needs a 90-minute delay and a control group to do so.

4. **The will gap (Occluded; shared with mirth).** The deliberate routes reported go around the act: summon the emotion through memory, or provoke irritant tears with menthol (Simons et al. 2012). Single efforts at suppression often backfire (Darwin 1872, anecdotal). Some people can weep at will as a cultivated mourning skill (Darwin's report from New Zealand; ritual weeping in Gračanin et al. 2018). The article should give this face one paragraph and point to [mirth-void](/voids/mirth-void/) and [agency-void](/voids/agency-void/).

5. **The boundary core (possibly Unexplorable; philosophical inference).** Plessner holds that crying arises where conduct reaches its limit: something felt as absolute removes the ordinary relativity of one's existence, no fitting articulate response remains, and the bodily capitulation is itself the only fitting response. If that is right, the opacity is not a gap in information that more introspection could fill. To give a full articulate account of what the tears said would be to produce the articulate response whose unavailability the tears answer. This is an argument, not a finding. It depends on Plessner being right, and Vingerhoets and Bylsma's "feelings that cannot be expressed or consummated in other behaviors" is the only empirical-side sentence that points the same way.

### Evidence for the Limit

- **Convergence from unrelated methods**: neurosurgery (Bejjani), neurology (Parvizi), social perception experiments (Provine; Aragón & Bargh), survey and laboratory work on relief (Gross; Gračanin), and phenomenological anthropology (Plessner) arrive at an expression that does not carry its own meaning.
- **The function question has survived 150 years** of serious attempts, each of which has had to be qualified.
- **The relief paradox is replicated and explained in a way that implicates self-report**: the explanations on offer (delayed effect, recovery misread as improvement, selective recall, lay theory) all make the crier's sense of benefit an unreliable instrument.
- **Even the theories of tears of joy disagree** (dimorphous regulation; being moved as core value; kama muta as communal sharing; helplessness before the overwhelming). If the experts cannot agree what tears of joy register, the crier's "I'm just so happy" is not a settled report either.

### Evidence against, or limiting, the void

- **Most crying is not mysterious to the crier.** Grief at a death is not opaque to the griever. The article must say plainly that the occasion void bites on a subset (sentimental tears, tears of joy, tears that "come from nowhere", induced and pathological tears), and that the stimulation and pathology cases are abnormal.
- **Observers and criers read tears by context, and context is usually sufficient.** Aragón and Bargh's observers got tears of joy right in context. Ambiguity without context is not ambiguity in life.
- **Being moved may be a distinct, nameable emotion** (Cova & Deonna 2014; Zickfeld et al. 2019). If so, part of the valence crossing dissolves: tears of joy register being moved, not joy-plus-confusion.
- **Self-soothing has support** (Gračanin et al. 2014, 2015). The relief void is about reading relief from inside, not about whether there is any.
- **The chemosignal is disputed.** The article must not build on it.
- **"Uniquely human" is the field's consensus, not a proof.** Darwin reports a weeping macaque, and the reviews hedge ("appears to be", "most likely"). The article should say "on the current evidence".

### Phenomenology

The familiar case is tears that arrive before their reason: at the end of a film, at a stranger's kindness on the news, at a piece of music, at a wedding of people one barely knows. The usual first-person report is surprise, sometimes embarrassment, and a search for an explanation ("I don't know why I'm crying"). Cova and Deonna's informants describe a "sense of being taken by force", warmth in the chest and a lump in the throat. Plessner describes letting oneself go, a falling, and an isolation that sharing the motive can stop. The tears then seem to tell the person something: that this mattered more than she knew.

In the stimulation case the phenomenology is the reverse. Ms P did not experience a puzzle. She experienced reasons, total and self-evident, and remembered the episode afterwards. The absence of any felt gap between cause and reason is what makes the case useful. No source read here contains first-person interview data on the onset of crying gathered by a structured method. "I don't know why I'm crying" is folk testimony and should be labelled so.

## Approaches to the Edge

### Direct Methods (if any)

- **Delayed and controlled mood measurement** (Gračanin et al. 2015): it is the only way found to separate relief from recovery, and it bypasses the crier's judgement.
- **Diary studies** (Bylsma et al. 2011, not read) reduce, but do not remove, recall selection.
- **Stimulation and lesion cases**: they show what the crier says when the cause is known to the experimenter. They are rare and ethically constrained.

### Indirect Methods

- **Tear-removal and context manipulation** (Provine et al. 2009; Aragón & Bargh 2018) to measure what tears add to a reading and what context supplies.
- **Chemosignal studies** with blinded sniffing (Gelstein; Gračanin; Agron), which test a channel neither party can introspect.
- **Cross-cultural measurement** (Zickfeld et al. 2019's 19 nations; the 41-country tear dataset reported by Wróbel et al. 2021, preprint, not used) to see whether the occasions of tears are fixed or learned.
- **Phenomenological description of the limit** (Plessner): describe the situations in which tears come and what the person can and cannot do in them, rather than introspect the tears.
- **A micro-phenomenological study of tear onset** would be the obvious next probe; none was found, and five searches do not show none exists.

### What AI Might See

An AI system does not weep, and nothing in this research suggests it has anything like the state tears are said to register. Three modest points can be made.

First, a text-trained system has read an enormous number of descriptions of what makes people cry, and can often predict which passage of a story will produce tears in readers without being moved by it. That is the observer's position taken to an extreme: it reads the occasion from the context with no tears of its own to interpret. Whether that counts as understanding what tears say is exactly the open question, and the article should leave it open.

Second, the structure of the occasion face generalises. When a system explains an output it did not consciously choose, its explanation is produced by the same machinery whose operation is in question. The stimulation case shows a human version, and AI self-explanation has a similar shape. This is a point about self-report in general, not a claim that AI introspection resembles human weeping.

Third, an outside reader of the crying literature can see something its subject cannot: that "crying brings relief" is reported generally by more people than report it for their last cry. That figure (70% against 50%) is a statistic no single crier can compute from inside. It is offered as an illustration of what aggregation sees, not as a discovery.

## Connection to Tenets

### Most Relevant Tenet

**Occam's Razor Has Limits.** The search for *the* function of tears has run from Darwin's "purposeless" through Frey's detoxification to distress signal, self-soothing and chemosignal. Vingerhoets and Bylsma's verdict that crying "obeys the laws of operant conditioning" and is shaped by "biological, psychological, and social factors" rather than being "a reflex-like symptom" suggests that the single-function question may itself be the simplification. The reframing of the relief question from "does crying help?" to "for whom, and under which specific conditions" (Rottenberg et al. 2008b, as quoted in Gračanin et al. 2015) is the same move. This is a modest, clean case and should be presented as one.

### Implications

- **Bidirectional Interaction.** Weeping is the textbook case of a grasped meaning producing a glandular secretion: one reads a letter, and tears form. The Bejjani case runs the other way, a physical cause producing a mood with reasons. Both directions occur and neither discriminates between physicalism and interactionism; a physicalist reads the letter case as neural semantics. The article may note that tears make the mind–body traffic unusually visible, and must say that they do not adjudicate it. [emotion-and-dualism](/topics/emotion-and-dualism/) is the page for the stronger claims and should not be echoed here.
- **Dualism.** Plessner's description of a person who "abdicates in favour of pure corporeality" and still "remains a person" is phenomenology any theory of the person–body relation has to accommodate. The Map's reading, that this looks like an agent yielding control of an interface while remaining the one who answers, is the Map's reading. Plessner explicitly declines metaphysics. The article should state the reading as the Map's and give Plessner's non-dualist frame alongside it.
- **The occasion face cuts both ways for the Map.** The Map leans on first-person evidence. Here the first-person report of why one is weeping can be wholly produced by an electrode and arrive with "of course" attached. The same caution [phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence](/topics/phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence/) applies elsewhere applies here, and the article should say so rather than treat tears as privileged testimony from consciousness.
- **Minimal Quantum Interaction; No Many Worlds.** No connection found. The article should say so in a clause and not invent one.
