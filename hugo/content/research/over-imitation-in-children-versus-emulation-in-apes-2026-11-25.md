## Executive Summary

Shown a puzzle box with a hidden reward, a human demonstrator performed both causally relevant and irrelevant actions. Chimpanzees copied the whole sequence while the box was opaque. When the box was transparent they "ignored the irrelevant actions in favour of a more efficient, emulative technique". Three- and four-year-old children "employed imitation to solve the task in both conditions, at the expense of efficiency" (Horner & Whiten 2005). This is **over-imitation**: faithful copying of actions that are visibly unnecessary. It is robust in Western preschoolers, it increases into adulthood (McGuigan et al. 2011), and it survives the absence of any intentional demonstration (Whiten et al. 2016). It is absent in bonobos (Clay & Tennie 2018) and, on the best current evidence, in dogs (Johnston et al. 2017; Fisher et al. 2026).

Three rival explanations compete, and each is stated at full strength below:

- **automatic causal encoding**: children encode every intentional adult action as causally meaningful (Lyons et al. 2007, 2011);
- **social affiliation**: copying is driven by the wish to belong and be like the model (Over & Carpenter 2012, 2013);
- **normativity**: children parse the extra action as part of a conventional activity that one ought to perform (Kenward 2012; Keupp et al. 2013).

The field's own appraisal is that all three "capture important parts of the phenomenon and none can explain all instances of OI by itself" (Hoehl et al. 2019). Since then a dual-process model has gained support (Schleihauf & Hoehl 2021). A 2026 pre-registered test did not confirm its prediction (Marsh & Tunçgenç 2026).

**What the subject adds to the Map.** On every one of these accounts, transmission fidelity comes from the *learner*. It occurs with uncommunicative models and with no demonstration intended. So high-fidelity transmission of opaque techniques has a human source that does not run through a teacher representing the learner's ignorance. That qualifies two existing claims:

- `concepts/teaching-as-metarepresentation` (TM) currently reads teaching as the amplifier "above all where a skill is causally opaque";
- `concepts/cumulative-culture` (CC) currently frames ape emulation against human teaching as the whole contrast.

The over-imitation findings are **compatible** with the Map's declarative-use thesis and nowhere **discriminating**. The normative account is at most **suggestive**: its content resembles CC's "representing [a practice] as 'the way we do this'". One finding cuts the other way. Children keep copying actions they have themselves called "silly", so this is a domain where declarative knowledge fails to steer behaviour.

## Key Sources

### Horner & Whiten (2005), "Causal knowledge and imitation/emulation switching in chimpanzees (*Pan troglodytes*) and children (*Homo sapiens*)"
- **URL**: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15549502/ (DOI 10.1007/s10071-004-0239-6); *Animal Cognition* 8(3):164-181.
- **Type**: Empirical paper.
- **Key points** (VERIFIED, abstract):
  - "Young wild-born chimpanzees from an African sanctuary and 3- to 4-year-old children observed a human demonstrator use a tool to retrieve a reward from a puzzle-box."
  - Opaque box: chimpanzees "reproduced both the relevant and irrelevant actions, thus imitating the overall structure of the task". Clear box: they "ignored the irrelevant actions in favour of a more efficient, emulative technique".
  - "In contrast to the chimpanzees, children employed imitation to solve the task in both conditions, at the expense of efficiency."
  - The authors' explanation is hedged: "may be due to a greater susceptibility of children to cultural conventions, perhaps combined with a differential focus on the results, actions and goals of the demonstrator."
  - Sample sizes, from Hoehl et al. Table S1 read in layout view: chimpanzees n = 12 and children n = 16, four per group, between-groups design. These are small samples; say "a small study" if size matters.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. It bears on the comparative premises of the Bidirectional Interaction reading in TM and CC, not on dualism.

### Lyons, Young & Keil (2007), "The hidden structure of overimitation"; Lyons et al. (2011), "The scope and limits of overimitation in the transmission of artefact culture"
- **URLs**: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18056814/ (PNAS 104(50):19751-6); https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21357238/ (*Phil Trans R Soc B* 366(1567):1158-67).
- **Type**: Empirical papers (ACE account).
- **Key points** (VERIFIED, abstracts):
  - 2007 names the phenomenon and rejects the prior assumption that children overimitate "as a purely social exercise".
  - Children have "a strong tendency to encode all of the adult's actions as causally meaningful".
  - This "automatic causal encoding process allows children to rapidly calibrate their causal beliefs about even the most opaque physical systems".
  - The effect is resistant: "Despite countervailing task demands, time pressure, and even direct warnings, children are frequently unable to avoid reproducing the adult's irrelevant actions".
  - 2011: children "persist in overimitating even when doing so is costly (underscoring the involuntary nature of the effect)", but "the effect is constrained by intentionality".
- **Tenet alignment**: Rival. ACE explains the human–ape difference as a learner-side causal-learning bias and posits no metarepresentation.

### Over & Carpenter (2012), "Putting the social into social learning"; Over & Carpenter (2013), "The social side of imitation"
- **URLs**: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21767011/ (*J Comp Psychol* 126(2):182-92); https://doi.org/10.1111/cdep.12006 (*Child Development Perspectives* 7(1):6-11, metadata from Crossref; abstract from the OpenAlex inverted index).
- **Type**: Theoretical papers (affiliation account).
- **Key points** (VERIFIED, abstracts):
  - 2012: "Three critical factors in determining what is copied are children's own (learning and/or social) goals in the situation, children's identification with the model and with the social group in general, and the social pressures which children experience within the imitative situation."
  - 2013: imitation "is often intimately tied to children's need to belong to the group and their drive to affiliate with those around them". Their perspective resolves why children "sometimes copy selectively and sometimes copy faithfully (so-called overimitation)".
- **Tenet alignment**: Rival. The explanation is motivational, not representational.

### Kenward, Karlsson & Persson (2011); Kenward (2012); Keupp, Behne & Rakoczy (2013): the normativity account
- **URLs**: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20943697/ (*Proc R Soc B* 278(1709):1239-46); https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22436894/ (*J Exp Child Psychol* 112(2):195-207); https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23933292/ (*J Exp Child Psychol* 116(2):392-406).
- **Type**: Empirical papers.
- **Key points** (VERIFIED, abstracts):
  - Kenward et al. 2011 is titled "Over-imitation is better explained by norm learning than by distorted causal learning". Its verbal measures "do not support the hypothesis that children over-imitate primarily because they encode unnecessary actions as causing the goal even in causally transparent systems". On opaque systems: "this is not over-imitation, but ordinary causal learning."
  - Kenward 2012 tested 48 three- and five-year-olds. "Failure of the puppet to perform the unnecessary action resulted in spontaneous protest by the majority of the children, with some using normative language", even after the puppet reached the goal. That is "not compatible with the hypothesis that the primary cause of over-imitation is that children believe the unnecessary action causes the goal."
  - Keupp et al. 2013: "Normativity accounts claim that overimitation arises when children view causally irrelevant elements as an essential part of an overarching conventional activity." Results "are not readily compatible with affiliation and causal confusion accounts, and they speak in favor of normativity accounts."
- **LEAD**: how the protest rate in Kenward 2012 splits by age (3 versus 5). This matters for any claim about synchrony with false-belief passing.
- **Tenet alignment**: Compatible, and at most suggestive (see Grading). The authors make no claim about metarepresentation or consciousness. Do not conscript them.

### Hoehl, Keupp, Schleihauf, McGuigan, Buttelmann & Whiten (2019), "'Over-imitation': A review and appraisal of a decade of research"
- **URLs**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dr.2018.12.002 (*Developmental Review* 51:90-108; Crossref); full text read from the OSF/PsyArXiv deposit https://osf.io/p37eu/download (DOI 10.31234/osf.io/p37eu).
- **Type**: Review, with a table of 54 studies.
- **Key points** (VERIFIED, preprint full text):
  - The appraisal of the three accounts: "The suggested reasoning processes (causal, affiliative, and normative) all capture important parts of the phenomenon and none can explain all instances of OI by itself."
  - On causal confusion: it "cannot fully account for the phenomenon of OI, since there are instances when children copy unnecessary actions even though they clearly "know better" and even state that the imitated action is "silly"". Yet "children's insights into the mechanisms of the task can substantially reduce OI."
  - On communication: "Contrary to predictions based on the natural pedagogy account, children imitated causally irrelevant actions at a similar frequency in the first phases of all experiments, irrespective of whether the model addressed them directly or not." And: "Thus, it seems that although communication is not necessary for OI to occur, it will help children to abandon it for a more efficient strategy."
  - A caveat for the fold: "In most OI studies (although not all: Whiten et al., 2016) the model did communicate with the children before or while showing" the action.
  - The opaque-box exclusion: copying there "does not fall under our definition of OI and should rather be considered learning about object functions".
  - The comparative record is thin: "a smaller number of ape studies generated results consistent with those of Horner and Whiten" (they cite Clay & Tennie 2017 and Nielsen & Widjojo 2011).
  - Open question on conscious processing: "Perhaps it often is, or comes to be routinized as, a form of 'automatic' behavior copying based on rapid unconscious assessment of situational demands and risks".
  - Function: "OI is a crucial adaptation to life in our artifact-rich and conventionally opaque human culture(s)".
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral survey. Its open question on unconscious copying is relevant to TM's falsifier 3 by analogy only, because that falsifier concerns teaching, not copying.

### Whiten, McGuigan, Marshall-Pescini & Hopper (2009), "Emulation, imitation, over-imitation and the scope of culture for child and chimpanzee"
- **URL**: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19620112/ (*Phil Trans R Soc B* 364(1528):2417-28).
- **Type**: Review of the authors' own experiments.
- **Key points** (VERIFIED, abstract):
  - The studies "question late twentieth-century characterizations of children as imitators, but chimpanzees as emulators".
  - "We conclude that chimpanzees rely more on imitation and have greater cultural capacities than previously acknowledged", while chimpanzees "selectively apply a range of social learning processes that include emulation".
  - "Initial results demonstrate a relative conservatism and conformity in chimpanzees' learning, contrasting with cumulative cultural learning in young children."
- **LEAD**: the ages of the "young children" in the cumulative-learning experiments. This bears on CC L126.
- **Tenet alignment**: Mixed. It narrows the imitation gap between the species, and it locates the gap at conservatism versus cumulative learning, which suits CC's "they fail at the third".

### Tennie, Call & Tomasello (2009), "Ratcheting up the ratchet: on the evolution of cumulative culture"
- **URL**: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19620111/ (*Phil Trans R Soc B* 364(1528):2405-15). This paper is **already in CC's References**.
- **Key points** (VERIFIED, abstract):
  - Chimpanzee traditions arise from "founder effects, individual learning and mostly product-oriented (rather than process-oriented) copying".
  - The human ratchet results from "(i) human social learning is more oriented towards process than product and (ii) unique forms of human cooperation lead to active teaching, social motivations for conformity and normative sanctions against non-conformity".
- **Tenet alignment**: Mixed. It lists process-oriented copying *alongside* teaching as a human-specific source of the ratchet. CC cites the paper for the zone of latent solutions but not for this two-source claim (see Sibling Drift).

### Clay & Tennie (2018), "Is Overimitation a Uniquely Human Phenomenon? Insights From Human Children as Compared to Bonobos"
- **URL**: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28741660/ (*Child Development* 89(5):1535-1544; Hoehl et al. cite it as 2017, the online-first year).
- **Key points** (VERIFIED, abstract): with visibly causally irrelevant actions, "only 3- to 5-year-old children (N = 77) readily copied, whereas no bonobo from a large sample did (N = 46)".
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. It shows the contrast is not chimpanzee-specific.

### Nagell, Olguin & Tomasello (1993), "Processes of social learning in the tool use of chimpanzees … and human children"
- **URL**: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8370271/ (*J Comp Psychol* 107(2):174-86).
- **Key points** (VERIFIED, abstract): with a rake-like tool, "Children in both cases did what the model did. Chimpanzee subjects, however, behaved identically in the 2 model conditions." The children were 2-year-olds. This is the classic source of the picture of children as imitators and chimpanzees as emulators, which Whiten et al. 2009 later qualify.

### Nielsen & Tomaselli (2010); Berl & Hewlett (2015): cross-cultural range
- **URLs**: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20483853/ (*Psychological Science* 21(5):729-36); https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25816230/ (*PLoS ONE* 10(3):e0120180).
- **Key points** (VERIFIED, abstracts):
  - Nielsen & Tomaselli: "From 18 months of age, children will routinely copy even arbitrary and unnecessary actions". Kalahari Bushman children over-imitated as urban children did, and this is "the first indication that overimitation may be a universal human trait".
  - Berl & Hewlett: "Aka children were found not to overimitate as expected, instead displaying one of the lowest rates of overimitation seen under similar conditions". Also "60% of Aka children used emulation rather than imitation, compared to 15% of Ngandu children, 11% of Aka adults, and 0% of Western children of similar age".
- **Use**: a counterweight to any universality claim.

### McGuigan, Makinson & Whiten (2011); Whiten et al. (2016): age and context
- **URLs**: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21241282/ (*Br J Psychol* 102(1):1-18); https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27466806/ (*PLoS ONE* 11(7):e0159920).
- **Key points** (VERIFIED, abstracts):
  - McGuigan et al.: "Surprisingly, copying of irrelevant actions increased with age, with the adults performing the task with less efficiency than the children."
  - Whiten et al. 2016 ran the task at Edinburgh Zoo with a confederate posing as a visitor: "Despite the absence of intentional demonstration, or social pressure to copy, a majority of both child and even adult observers included all causally irrelevant actions in their reproduction." The authors hedge transparency themselves: "This occurred even though causal irrelevance appeared manifest because of the transparency of the puzzle-box."
- **Use**: the strongest single datum for fidelity without a teacher.

### Non-ape comparisons: dogs
- **URLs**: Johnston, Holden & Santos 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27659592/ (*Dev Sci* 20(4)); Huber et al. 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29980941/ (*Learn Behav* 46(4):387-397); Fisher, Byrne & Johnston 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40742681/ (*J Comp Psychol* 140(2):117-125).
- **Key points** (VERIFIED, abstracts):
  - Johnston et al. tested dogs and dingoes: "We find clear evidence against overimitation in both species", with behaviour "like chimpanzees; Horner & Whiten, 2005".
  - Huber et al.: "half of the dogs replicated a causally irrelevant action that was demonstrated by their caregiver", though "the copying frequency in this study was low overall".
  - Fisher et al. 2026 compared dogs directly with 3- to 5-year-olds and "further support the hypothesis that dogs do not overimitate like children".
- **Use**: the contrast is not ape-specific. Domestication with human pedagogy-like exposure does not produce child-like over-imitation, on current evidence; the Huber line is the dissent.

- Schleihauf & Hoehl 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34529702/ (*PLoS ONE* 16(9):e0256614), VERIFIED abstract. This is the dual-process account: "results speak to a strong initial tendency to imitate perceivably irrelevant actions regardless of the model", adjusted later "after deliberate consideration".
- Marsh & Tunçgenç 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42275784/ (*J Exp Child Psychol* 271:106565), VERIFIED abstract. This is a pre-registered test with 60 children aged 5 to 8: "Contrary to the predictions of the Dual-Process Model, there was no difference…"; "Over-imitation was most prevalent when children were uncertain about the causality of unnecessary actions."
- Marin, Richert & Fong 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42959272/ (*Front Psychol* 17:1909142), VERIFIED abstract: "methodological inconsistencies and ecological gaps have made it difficult to draw broad conclusions about the nature and function of overimitation".
- Legare & Nielsen 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26440121/ (*Trends Cogn Sci* 19(11):688-699), VERIFIED abstract: "Imitation and innovation work in tandem to support cultural learning in children and facilitate our capacity for cumulative culture." Also: "For instrumental learning, with an increase in experience, high fidelity imitation decreases and innovation increases. By contrast, for conventional learning, imitative fidelity stays high, regardless of experience, and innovation stays low."
- Whiten, Horner & de Waal 2005, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16113685/ (*Nature* 437:737-40), VERIFIED abstract. Seeded tool-use methods spread and diverged in captive chimpanzee groups. Some chimpanzees that discovered the alternative method went on to match their group's method, "showing a conformity bias that is regarded as a hallmark of human culture". This complicates any claim that apes lack *conformity*. It does not show process-copying of irrelevant actions.

## Major Positions

### Automatic causal encoding (ACE)
- **Proponents**: Lyons, Keil and colleagues.
- **Core claim**: children treat an adult's intentional actions on a novel object as causally meaningful, so the extra action enters their causal model of the object. Over-imitation is a side effect of a learning mechanism that is normally adaptive for opaque artefacts.
- **Strongest form**: it predicts resistance to warnings and costs (Lyons 2007, 2011, verified) and explains why it is the *human* child who copies, because humans live among opaque artefacts.
- **Main problem**: children protest a puppet's omission even after the goal is reached (Kenward 2012), and they say the action is "silly" while copying it (Hoehl et al.). Both cut against a purely causal reading.
- **Relation to tenets**: a full physicalist rival with no metarepresentational premise.

### Social affiliation
- **Proponents**: Over and Carpenter; Nielsen.
- **Core claim**: what children copy is set by their social goals, their identification with the model and the group, and situational pressure. Faithful copying is a way of being like others.
- **Strongest form**: it explains the *selectivity* of copying that a purely causal account cannot. For example, priming third-party ostracism "increases affiliative imitation in children" (Over & Carpenter 2009, title, VERIFIED via PubMed PMID 19371357). Effects of in-group models are a LEAD.
- **Main problem**: Keupp et al. 2013 found protest patterns "not readily compatible with affiliation … accounts". Schleihauf & Hoehl 2021 found that children initially over-imitated an antisocial model as much as a prosocial one.
- **Relation to tenets**: a rival. Its explanation is motivational.

### Normativity
- **Proponents**: Kenward; Keupp, Behne and Rakoczy.
- **Core claim**: children parse the extra action as constitutive of a conventional activity (how the task ought to be done), and they enforce that norm on third parties.
- **Strongest form**: spontaneous normative protest at a puppet's omission, which grows when the demonstration stresses method over goal (Keupp et al. 2013).
- **Relation to tenets**: the reading closest to CC's "representing it as 'the way we do this'". See Grading for why it stays at *suggestive*.

### Dual-process and causal-uncertainty models (2020s)
- **Proponents**: Schleihauf & Hoehl (dual-process); Marsh & Tunçgenç (causal uncertainty).
- **Core claim**: on the dual-process model, children default to a blanket-copy heuristic and revise it under deliberate consideration. On the causal-uncertainty model, over-imitation is an active strategy for testing causal predictions.
- **State**: the 2026 pre-registered test did not confirm the dual-process prediction. The field's own 2026 methods critique (Marin et al.) warns against broad conclusions.
- **Relation to tenets**: both are rivals. The dual-process model's "deliberate consideration" stage is where a Map reader might look for a conscious, declarative step, but nothing in the abstracts licenses that reading.

### Comparative framing: emulators versus selective imitators
- **Tomasello school** (Nagell et al. 1993; Tennie et al. 2009): ape copying is product-oriented, and process-oriented copying plus teaching and norms are the human ratchet.
- **Whiten school** (Horner & Whiten 2005; Whiten et al. 2005, 2009): chimpanzees imitate when causal information is hidden and conform to group methods. The difference lies in selectivity and conservatism, not in an inability to imitate.
- **Current state**: both schools agree that children, unlike apes, copy *visibly* unnecessary actions. They disagree about how much ape transmission is imitative.

## Key Debates

### Is over-imitation a bug or the engine of cumulative culture?
- **Sides**: Legare & Nielsen 2015 and Hoehl et al. 2019 treat it as a functional adaptation to a conventionally opaque culture ("dual engines" alongside innovation). Its mechanical cost, copying inefficiency (McGuigan et al. 2011), is the sceptics' point. Shipton & Nielsen 2015 (*Hum Nat* 26(3):331-45; VERIFIED abstract, PMID 26156574) argue that the lithic record and mirror-neuron speculation "hint at the foundations of overimitation and shared intentionality around 2 million years ago", more than a million years before convincing evidence of cumulative culture.
- **Current state**: open. No study isolates over-imitation's contribution to *accumulation*, as distinct from its contribution to *fidelity*.

### Which account explains over-imitation?
- **Current state**: unresolved. The field's appraisal is that none explains all instances (Hoehl et al. 2019). The 2026 work splits further (Marsh & Tunçgenç) and doubts the comparability of methods (Marin et al.).

## Grading Against the Map's Theses

The grades below follow `project/evidential-status-discipline.md` (compatible, suggestive, discriminating).

- **Declarative-use thesis** (TM §Theory of Mind; the amplification reading in `concepts/consciousness-as-amplifier`): **compatible**. The human–ape contrast in over-imitation is real. But ACE, affiliation and the dual-process heuristic each predict it without any declarative metarepresentation, and none of the Map's readings predicts the contrast better. No discriminator exists.
- **Normative account and "practice as practice"** (CC §Why Metarepresentation Matters, step 1): **suggestive, not discriminating**.
  - Children treating an extra action as part of "an overarching conventional activity" has the content CC says requires representing a practice *as* a practice.
  - But Kenward and Keupp et al. make no claim of metarepresentation. Early norm-enforcement is plausibly a form of implicit practical understanding; the developmental norm literature beyond these abstracts is a LEAD.
  - The protest includes three-year-olds, which, if anything, presses CC's 4–5 synchrony (L126).
- **A finding that cuts against amplification**: children copy actions they themselves call "silly" (Hoehl et al., verified), and they persist despite "direct warnings" (Lyons 2007, verified). It does mean over-imitation must never be cited as an *exercise* of declarative control.
- **Effect on the amplification chain**: the evidence narrows where consciousness could matter in cumulative culture. Fidelity has a learner-side source that may be routinised and unconscious (an open question in Hoehl et al.). That leaves teaching's distinctive contribution at scaffolding and refinement, which is what TM's next sentence already says. It is consistent with TM's closing clause, "without being its sole condition".
- **Dualism, MQI, No Many Worlds**: no bearing.
- **Occam's Razor Has Limits**: no positive use. Do not argue that the variety of rival accounts licenses a non-physical posit.
