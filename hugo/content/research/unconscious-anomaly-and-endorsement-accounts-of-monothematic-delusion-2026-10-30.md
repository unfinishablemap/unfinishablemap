## Executive Summary

**Two-factor theorists do not hold that the Factor-1 anomaly is necessarily conscious, and the leading statement of the theory says that in Capgras it is not.** Coltheart, Menzies and Sutton (2010, raw full text) argue that because "people are not conscious of the activities of their autonomic nervous systems", the Capgras abnormality "is not an abnormality of which the patient is aware". The first delusion-relevant conscious event is the impostor belief, which unconscious Bayesian abductive inference delivers. They therefore replace "abnormal experience" with "abnormal data" (p. 264, 266), and they treat the strangeness patients report as "consequences of the delusion" (p. 264). Noordhof and Sullivan-Bissett (2021, raw) put the background plainly: anomalous experiences are "typically taken to be conscious by one-factor and two-factor theorists alike, though they need not be", citing Davies, Aimola Davies & Coltheart (2005, p. 222; *carrier only*) as the earlier statement of the "need not be".

**Endorsement and explanationist accounts both posit a conscious experience; they differ over its content.** Bayne and Pacherie (2004, raw HAL preprint) named both models. On the "endorsement model" the experience already has impostor content ("The woman I am looking at is not my wife"), and on the "explanationist version of the two-factor model" it is poorer ("this person looks a bit strange"), with the belief adopted to explain it. They supply the distinction the Map's scope rule needs: "one should not confuse the lack of experience with the experience of a lack." Garry Young (2011, 2014) calls the 2010 view "revisionist" and argues for an anomalous experience that co-occurs with the impostor thought before belief.

**The vmPFC record does not discriminate.** The four ventromedial patients (Tranel, Damasio & Damasio 1995) are documented only by absent skin conductance (SCR) and normal overt identification. No source read here records what familiar faces were like for them. SCR and conscious familiarity dissociate: prosopagnosics show the SCR without "a conscious feeling of familiarity" (Pacherie 2009, raw). Every account has a reading of the cases, and the cases are contested as controls (Corlett 2019, full text; Wilkinson 2015 via Noordhof & Sullivan-Bissett). The authors of the 2010 paper say they "know of no studies" bearing on their own conjecture about these patients (p. 281).

**Verdict on the scope rule.** The rule "only the anomalous experience is the Map's territory" **stands for Cotard** and **needs narrowing for Capgras**. For Capgras the Map's territory is Factor 1 *insofar as it is experienced*. Whether it is experienced before the belief is open, and on the most-cited two-factor statement it is not. Four Capgras sentences (L49, L63, L71, L87) state the experiential reading as fact; verdicts are below at [Sentence-Level Verdicts](#sentence-verdicts). **Dualism is unaffected either way**, and no theorist cited here is enlisted for it. **Recommended action: `fold`** into [capgras-delusion-and-the-affective-recognition-channel](/topics/capgras-delusion-and-the-affective-recognition-channel/), ~340 words plus ~150 words of references, **after the open P2 (todo ~L3662) lands**. ## The Four Research Questions, Answered

1. **Do two-factor theorists hold that the Factor-1 anomaly is necessarily conscious?** No. The early wording is experiential: Langdon & Coltheart (2000, abstract) have an "aberrant perception", and Davies et al. (2001, abstract, as used at Cotard L52) have "unusual experience". But Davies, Aimola Davies & Coltheart (2005, p. 222, via Noordhof & Sullivan-Bissett) already say the experience "need not be" conscious. Coltheart, Menzies & Sutton (2010) say that in Capgras it is not, while allowing that delusions of reference and persecution "may be examples of forms of delusion in which the initial abnormality ... is a conscious experience" (p. 265). Coltheart, Langdon & McKay (2011, accepted manuscript) describe the Capgras datum as "the unexpected absence of autonomic response". McKay (2012, fn 3 and fn 7, accepted manuscript) follows the 2010 paper in noting that the abnormal data "are not always accessible to consciousness". For Cotard, Davies & Coltheart (2022) adopt depersonalisation and derealisation symptoms as the seed ([cotard-delusion](/concepts/cotard-delusion/) L54), and those are experiential by their clinical definition. Corlett (2019), a critic, glosses the theory the experiential way: "people experience the lack of familiarity for their loved one (Factor 1)". The theorists split, and the critic reads the theory in the way its most-cited 2010 statement rejects.
2. **What do endorsement and explanationist accounts say?** Both say the anomaly is experienced. The **endorsement** account (Bayne & Pacherie 2004; Pacherie, Green & Bayne 2006; Pacherie 2009; Langdon & Bayne 2010, "received" delusions) holds that the experience encodes the delusional content. Bongiorno (2020, abstract) argues the endorsement model cannot be defended along Pacherie's or Wilkinson's lines and proposes that the Capgras experience is "metaphorical in character". **Explanationist** accounts hold that a poorer experience is explained by the belief. They come in one-factor form (Maher 1974, 1999, via carriers; Noordhof & Sullivan-Bissett 2021) and two-factor or bias form (Stone & Young 1997, abstract: faces "perceived as drained of their normal affective significance" plus a bias toward observational adequacy). The 2010 paper is explanationist in structure but unconscious in its explanandum, which is what Garry Young (2014) calls revisionist.
3. **What is the empirical status of "unconscious anomaly" proposals?** It is a theoretical stipulation, not an empirical finding. The 2010 argument is conceptual: experiences are by definition conscious, autonomic activity is not conscious, so the datum is not an experience. It rests on Mandler (1975), whom the paper quotes, rather than on any probe of what patients experience. The paper's one testable consequence, that vmPFC patients would at first form the impostor belief and then drop it, is untested by the authors' own account (p. 281). McKay (2012) argues it sits badly with their own observation that "the failure of autonomic response to a spouse's face is omnipresent". **Corlett's prediction-error account** does not assign the prediction error a conscious status in the 2019 paper: prediction error is defined there as "the mismatch between belief-based expectation and experience", and the vmPFC patients are modelled as lower-level damage whose prediction errors "are not precise or persistent enough to augur delusions" (Figure 2 legend). Whether aberrant prediction error is experienced, as aberrant-salience readings suggest, is a lead not checked here.
4. **Does the vmPFC record discriminate?** No. See [The vmPFC Record](#vmpfc).

### Coltheart, Menzies & Sutton (2010), "Abductive inference and delusional belief", *Cognitive Neuropsychiatry* 15(1–3), 261–287

- **Key passages** (raw, grep-verified; page from the running heads):
  - p. 264: "people are not conscious of the activities of their autonomic nervous systems, and so a man would not be conscious of a failure of his autonomic nervous system to respond when he encountered his wife."
  - p. 264: "the abnormality in Capgras delusion, which prompts the exercise of abductive inference ..., is not an abnormality of which the patient is aware." Quote the fragment "is not an abnormality of which the patient is aware" only. Do not splice across the ellipsis.
  - p. 264: "the first delusion-relevant event of which the patient is aware is the belief "That isn't my wife"". The chain before it (stroke, disconnection, absent autonomic response, abductive inference) is "unconscious"; "What's conscious is only the outcome that this chain of processes generated: the conscious belief "This person isn't my wife.""
  - p. 264: "there is no doubt that people with Capgras delusion do have abnormal (conscious) experiences ... But these experiences are not the cause of the delusion; they are consequences of the delusion."

### Bayne & Pacherie (2004a), "Bottom-up or top-down? Campbell's rationalist account of monothematic delusions", *Philosophy, Psychiatry, & Psychology* 11(1), 1–11

### Pacherie (2009), "Perception, emotions and delusions: the case of the Capgras delusion", in T. Bayne & J. Fernández (eds), *Delusion and Self-Deception*, Psychology Press, pp. 105–123
- **URL**: HAL ijn_00353597 (Wayback 2018-11-01; proof pages 105–124; the HAL record says "pp.1005-123, 2008", an evident typo). Routledge reissue chapter DOI 10.4324/9780203838044-12 (Crossref gives pp. 119–138, 2010). Cite the 2009 Psychology Press edition and treat the page range as approximate.
- **Key passage** (raw, p. 110): "Another possible motivation for positing such an integrative device would be to account for the transition from the unconscious autonomic response to the conscious feeling of familiarity. ... - **Key points** (raw):
  - 2014 abstract: "the delusional belief is posited as the first delusion-related event of which the patient is conscious. **Relation to tenets**: none of these positions bears on Dualism. If Factor 1 is unconscious, the signal is subpersonal on any metaphysics. If it is conscious, physicalist models supply it as an affective or interoceptive variable. **No position makes consciousness causally idle.** On the revisionist model the conscious belief is what the patient asserts and defends, and its failure to be revised is Factor 2. On Garry Young's model the experience "validates" the belief and the belief "authenticates" the experience (2014, p. 90). Nothing here concedes epiphenomenalism.

## The vmPFC Record Does Not Discriminate {#vmpfc}

**What the record contains** (raw sources only):
- Tranel et al. (1995, abstract): bilateral vmPFC damage, normal overt recognition, no discriminatory SCR to familiar faces.
- The 2010 paper (p. 266), via Tranel's personal communication: "these patients still correctly identify spouses and other family members ... they are not delusional".
- Corlett (2019, full text): anterior cingulate damage in all four; right DLPFC damage in EVR-318 and HS1065; SCR loss extending to "any psychologically salient visual stimuli"; "described as sociopaths".
- **Nothing about what the patients experienced.** No source read in this run or in the 2026-10-30 P2 check records a question put to them about familiar faces, or any report from them. The Tranel 1995 full text was not read. It is the one place a report could still be, and it is closed access.

**Why the silence cannot decide between accounts.** Each account predicts or tolerates it:
- **Revisionist**: no experience, so no report. Consistent. Its distinctive prediction, an early impostor belief, is untested (p. 281).
- **Endorsement**: the experience may be generated downstream of the SCR channel. In prosopagnosia the SCR is present without a conscious feeling of familiarity (Pacherie 2009). An absent SCR therefore licenses no inference to an absent or altered feeling.
- **Explanationist**: the patients may have felt something strange and rejected the impostor hypothesis. No one is recorded asking.
- **One-factor**: their experience differs, or their lesions do (Wilkinson, carrier; Corlett).
- **Prediction error**: imprecise lower-level errors.

**What would discriminate.** Two probes would: a structured phenomenological interview of non-delusional SCR-absent patients ("do familiar faces feel different?"), and the 2010 paper's own proposal of spouse confrontation "immediately after the occurrence of the brain insult". Neither has been reported in any source read.

**What exists on the other side.** Two non-delusional patients do report a familiarity anomaly. Vuilleumier (2003, abstract raw) describes hyperfamiliarity without delusion. Turner & Coltheart (2010) describe a post-surgical patient whose mother "Just didn't feel like her"; that case is carrier-only via Garry Young 2014, adapted. They show that a conscious familiarity anomaly without delusion occurs. They do not show that Capgras's Factor 1 is such an anomaly. Neither is a vmPFC case, and their SCR status is unknown here.

**Capgras patients' own reports are contested evidence.** Bayne & Pacherie read "my son's different but you have to be quick to notice it" as evidence of a changed experience. The 2010 paper treats the strangeness as post-belief, and treats claimed differences as confabulation: patients "will often mention differences (which must of course be fictitious)" (p. 280). Andrew Young (1999, via Garry Young fn 7) notes that patients "often have difficulty pointing them out". So Capgras L63's reliance on "patients' reports" that "describe its felt absence" rests on reports whose timing relative to the belief is the disputed point.

## Originators {#originators}

- **"Two-factor"**: the two-deficit proposal is Langdon & Coltheart (2000), whose abstract says "two deficits". The "two-factor" label appears in the title of Davies, Coltheart, Langdon & Breen (2001), "Towards a two-factor account". The 2010 paper (p. 281) describes "a two-factor theory of delusional belief, which has been developing over the past decade (Coltheart, 2007; Coltheart et al., 2007; Langdon & Coltheart, 2000)". Attribute the theory to the Macquarie group (Coltheart, Langdon, Davies, Breen, McKay), formalised in 2000–2001. Do not attribute it to Coltheart alone, or to Coltheart & Davies 2022, which is a review.
- **"Endorsement" / "explanationist"**: the labels are Bayne & Pacherie (2004a), drawing the distinction from Davies et al. (2001).
- **"Abnormal data"** in place of "abnormal experience": Coltheart, Menzies & Sutton (2010). The underlying "need not be conscious" point is earlier, in Davies, Aimola Davies & Coltheart (2005, p. 222), carrier only.
- **"Revisionist"** for the 2010 model: Garry Young (2014). It is a critic's label, not the authors' own.
- **"Lack of experience" vs "experience of a lack"**: Bayne & Pacherie (2004a). It is the analytically central distinction for the Map's scope rule.

**Draft passage** (every quotation grep-verified in raw text; sources below):

> ### Is Factor 1 Experienced? {#is-factor-1-experienced}
>
> The scope rule assumes there is an anomalous experience to claim. Most accounts, one-factor and two-factor alike, assume so too (Noordhof & Sullivan-Bissett 2021), and Bayne and Pacherie (2004), who named the two versions, split them by how rich the experience is. On the **endorsement** model the experience already carries the delusion's content, and the patient believes what he sees. On the **explanationist** model it is poorer, something like "this person looks a bit strange", and the impostor belief is adopted to explain it. They also draw the distinction this question turns on: "one should not confuse the lack of experience with the experience of a lack."
>
> Coltheart, Menzies and Sutton (2010) deny that any experience of a lack comes first. Because "people are not conscious of the activities of their autonomic nervous systems", the Capgras abnormality "is not an abnormality of which the patient is aware". The first delusion-relevant conscious event is the impostor belief, reached by unconscious abductive inference, so they speak of "abnormal data" rather than abnormal experience, and the strangeness patients report becomes a consequence of the delusion. Garry Young (2014) calls this the revisionist model and argues that an anomalous experience co-occurs with the impostor thought before the belief forms.
>
> The ventromedial cases cannot settle it. They are described by absent skin conductance and intact identification, and the available reports say nothing of what familiar faces were like for them. Skin conductance and felt familiarity also come apart: prosopagnosic patients typically show the familiar-face response without a conscious feeling of familiarity (Pacherie 2009). Coltheart, Menzies and Sutton conjecture that these patients at first held the impostor belief too, and say they "know of no studies" bearing on it.
>
> The Map's territory is therefore Factor 1 *insofar as it is experienced*, and whether it is experienced before the belief is an open empirical question. If the revisionist model is right, Capgras shows an unconscious signal coming apart from identification, and the felt-familiarity reading above loses its datum. Neither outcome bears on Dualism.

## Relation to Site Perspective (for the fold)

**Dualism (Tenet 1)**: unaffected either way. If Factor 1 is unconscious, Capgras becomes a case of a subpersonal signal plus a conscious belief, which every metaphysics can model. If Factor 1 is conscious, physicalist functional decompositions supply the felt variable, as the Capgras page already concedes. What changes is the Map's *reading of Capgras*. On the revisionist account the page's interface-architecture exhibit loses its felt datum, and the "consistent with" verdict at L87 becomes conditional on an empirical question about phenomenology.
**Bidirectional Interaction (Tenet 3)**: nothing, and no concession. On every account considered, conscious states (the belief, and on Garry Young's view the estrangement) figure in the clinical course. That is ordinary causation on any metaphysics and gives Tenet 3 no support.
**Occam's Razor Has Limits (Tenet 5)**: binds the Map's own tidiness. The Capgras page adopted the single experiential reading of Factor 1 without engaging the revisionist alternative. It is the same pattern the 2026-10-30 insight note found at CBS L86: a register settled by assertion. Equally, the revisionist account's economy (no experience, only data) is not a reason to adopt it: it rests on a definitional argument, and its one empirical prediction is untested.
**Minimal Quantum Interaction (Tenet 2), No Many Worlds (Tenet 4)**: not invoked.
