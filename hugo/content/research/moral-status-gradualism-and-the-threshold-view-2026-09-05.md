## Executive Summary

The question the Map registers as [P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2) — is moral status a threshold predicate or a magnitude? — has a large, live, and almost wholly uncited literature, and the two claims the Map makes about it (threshold status; graded stakes) each have a named published defender the Map has never engaged. On the **taxonomy**, Kagan's *unitarianism vs hierarchy* (2019), DeGrazia's *Unequal Consideration vs Unequal Interests* models (2008), the SEP's *threshold vs scalar conceptions* (Jaworska & Tannenbaum), Wendler's three-way split of *status-conferring / action-guiding / status-enhancing* properties (2023) and Carter's *positional vs scalar* status all carve the same distinction the Map calls status-versus-stakes; the Map's view is, in every one of these vocabularies, the unitarian / Unequal-Interests / threshold / no-status-enhancing-properties / positional-only option, and it should say so in the field's terms. On the **affirmative case**, the Map currently rests on Singer's equal consideration plus a consistency argument, while the literature offers at least five further routes — Regan's categorical inherent value, Korsgaard's tethered importance, the Rawls–Carter–Waldron range-property-plus-opacity-respect account of basic equality, Rachels's moral individualism as generalised by Wendler (no property enhances status, because every candidate turns out to be action-guiding through stakes), and Rossi's argument that equal consideration is *incompatible* with unequal status — and Wendler's 2023 book is a book-length statement of exactly the Map's conclusion. On the **strongest objection**, the arbitrariness challenge to any threshold (SEP §4; McMahan's seven challenges of 2008; Floris & Timmer 2025), the Map's Tenet 1 supplies a distinctive answer no unitarian in the literature has available: the status-conferring property is not a degree-cut on a continuous capacity but the *presence of a subject*, which is not a magnitude — at the cost of squaring this with the Map's own [degrees-of-consciousness](/concepts/degrees-of-consciousness/) and with Lee's argument (2020, 2023) that on most theories consciousness comes in degrees. Finally, [P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2)'s precise question — does enhancing the capacity that confers status raise status? — has a decade-old debate of its own (Buchanan 2009, Douglas 2013, DeGrazia 2012, Wilson 2012, Agar 2013) in which Buchanan argues the threshold reading is available only to *respect-based* and not to *interest-based* accounts; since the Map's criterion is interest-based, that is the objection the article most needs to answer.

## Key Sources

### SEP, "Grounds of Moral Status" (Jaworska & Tannenbaum) — the taxonomy of record
- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/grounds-moral-status/
- **Type**: Encyclopedia [raw]
- **Key points**:
  - §2 defines *full moral status* (FMS) as the highest degree: a stringent presumption against interference, a strong reason to aid, and equal weighting of comparable interests; "when two beings both have FMS, the reason not to interfere with them is equally strong" (§2.1, summariser paraphrase of a verified section).
  - §2.4 [raw]: "the reason to save an ordinary adult human being from death is much stronger than the reason to save, say, a rabbit from death. But this is not itself evidence of a higher moral status of the human being. The vast difference in the benefits of aid in the two cases could, on its own, lead to a difference in the strength of reasons" — the status/stakes separation stated in the encyclopedia's own voice.
  - §3 [raw]: "Those who accept that moral status comes in degrees have not developed fine-grained accounts of what each degree of status would involve."
  - §4 defines the **threshold conception** [raw]: "if the capacity to value grounds a high degree of moral status, then any being that has this capacity, regardless of how often or how well it can exhibit this capacity, has the same moral status as any other being with this capacity"; generalised: "for any X that is a sufficient ground for having n-degree of moral status, this status is not altered by factors such as how much of X a being has, how well it displays X, or the number of other features sufficient for n or lower degrees of moral status the being possesses." The **scalar conception** (Arneson 1999): "a higher degree of the capacity entails a higher status."
  - §4 states **the objection the Map must answer** [raw]: "it is not enough for those who hold the threshold view to stipulate that, for example, the capacity to do an activity well does not affect one's status. Once the importance of some feature, such as the capacity to value, is highlighted, it seems that not only the possession of the capacity but also how well one can exercise it is morally relevant to one's status." Illustrated with Arneson's impoverished being who "can set just a few ends and make just a few choices based on considering two or three simple alternatives" (Arneson 1999, pp. 119–120).
  - §4 also states the scalar view's cost [raw]: "if intelligence grounded FMS, then the scalar conception would claim that those who are more intelligent have a stronger right not to be killed than those who are not quite as intelligent. But this is highly at odds with the commonsense intuition that all such beings have an equally strong right not to be killed" (citing Wikler 2009).
  - §5.3 [raw]: DeGrazia 2008 "goes further by saying that beings who have additional rights due to more complex interests have a higher, and thus unequal, moral status compared to beings lacking those interests and rights" — i.e. the SEP reads DeGrazia's *Unequal Interests* model as itself a degrees-of-status view, which is a terminological choice the article should flag: on that usage the Map too "has degrees of status", since it grades stakes.
  - §5.3's residual complaint against equal-consideration views [raw]: they "are unable to account for the differential treatment of both conscious humans with severe irreversible cognitive impairments and infants who will die due to disease before acquiring cognitive sophistication, as compared with many animals (such as a dog), since here the affected interests are similar."
  - §5.6: the authors' own **person-rearing** account elevates status via the capacity to *incompletely realise* sophisticated activities within a rearing relationship; its stated limit [raw]: "there is no guarantee that this elevated status reaches the full array (and strength) of protections and entitlements associated with FMS."
- **Tenet alignment**: neutral as written. Relevant because the Map's criterion (a capacity for valenced experience) is exactly the kind of "capacity" §4 says a threshold view cannot merely stipulate to be degree-insensitive.

### Kagan, *How to Count Animals, more or less* (2019) with Sebo's *Mind* review (2021)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198829676.001.0001 ; review PDF https://jeffsebo.net/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/how-to-count-animals-review-december-2019.pdf
- **Type**: Book (OUP, Uehiro Series); review in *Mind* 130(518): 689–697, DOI 10.1093/mind/fzz089 (online 2020; author PDF dated December 2019 — this is the "Sebo 2019 review" of the harvest brief) [raw for the review]
- **Key points** (from Sebo's chapter-by-chapter summary, verified in his text):
  - Kagan's terminology: *standing* is "to count at all", *status* is "to count in a particular way or to a particular degree"; sentience and agency are each sufficient for standing (ch. 1).
  - Ch. 2 "Unitarianism" (pp. 37–57, Crossref) contains the concession the enhancement article already uses: a unitarian can favour the human over the mouse on stakes and can *describe* a systematic pattern of such favouring as higher status, "So, if we want to decide between unitarianism and hierarchy, then we need to do more than show that talk of higher moral status makes sense in cases where more well-being is at stake."
  - Ch. 4: the arithmetic — "we can calculate the value of your well-being by multiplying your level of well-being by your degree of moral status"; the Rethink Priorities page quotes the principle [raw via Schukraft]: "other things being equal, the greater the status of a given individual, the more value there is in any given unit of welfare obtaining for that individual" (Kagan 2019, p. 109). **This is the formula the Map's separation denies a second factor to**: all multiplication goes into welfare range, none into status.
  - Ch. 5: four status-grounding features — capacity for well-being, agency, potential status, modal status — each scalar.
  - Ch. 6: four worries — elitism, superior beings ("This is acceptable"), marginal cases (modal persons), normal variation (blocked by practical realism, ch. 11).
  - Ch. 8, **restricted deontology**: Kagan rejects a view on which deontological status is for persons only, because "The capacities that plausibly ground deontological status, such as agency and autonomy ... come in degrees. And, many animals have these capacities at least to a degree" — an argument the Map can borrow *against a second threshold at personhood* (McMahan, DeGrazia's two-tier) while rejecting Kagan's own hierarchical conclusion.
  - Sebo's verdict [raw]: "I think that everyone who has moral standing has it equally, and I am prepared to accept the consequences of this view. Granted, if a human has more well-being at stake than a mouse, then maybe we should favor the human all else equal on that basis. But we should not, in addition to favoring the human because they have more well-being at stake, also favor them because we assign greater moral weight to the well-being that they have." His methodological objection: Kagan "presents us with unitarian and hierarchical options and declares that 'few if any of us' will agree that the unitarian option is acceptable (p. 69)"; intuitions about "simple, idealized cases" in a domain "so heavily influenced by speciesism" will "anchor us to an unacceptably conservative and speciesist moral theory"; corrected, "the perceived gap in intrinsic moral status between, say, a human and a fish not only narrows but closes entirely." Sebo also flags an intrinsic/extrinsic ambiguity in "status" that inflates hierarchy intuitions.
- **Tenet alignment**: Kagan is the rival of record; Sebo's review is the Map's nearest published ally on the *verdict* (unitarianism with stakes-favouring) and states [P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2)'s separation in one sentence. Neither needs a view on what minds are.

### DeGrazia, "Moral Status As a Matter of Degree?" (2008) — author's PDF
- **URL**: https://philosophy.columbian.gwu.edu/sites/g/files/zaxdzs5446/files/2023-01/degrazia_moralstatus2.pdf ; DOI 10.1111/j.2041-6962.2008.tb00075.x
- **Type**: Paper, *Southern Journal of Philosophy* 46(2): 181–198 [raw]
- **Key points** (page numbers verified against the PDF's running footers):
  - p. 186: **Unequal Consideration Model** — "persons ... are due full moral consideration, whereas mice are due some, but less"; equal consideration defined as granting "roughly equal moral weight or importance to A's and B's (prudentially) comparable interests."
  - p. 187: "Avoiding some degree of suffering seems more or less equally important, prudentially, regardless of who the subject is, because the primary evil of suffering is its intrinsic or experiential badness. (The harm of death, by contrast, seems largely instrumental ...)" — DeGrazia himself identifies **suffering** as the comparable interest that isolates status from stakes, which is why the raccoon-and-person case (p. 187) and the enhancement pair have the shape they do.
  - p. 188: **Unequal Interests Model** — persons and mice both have status "but persons have more" only "in this limited sense, which refers to the noncomparability of certain interests"; "the best test case between the models may be whether it is equally problematic to inflict suffering on persons and to inflict it on nonpersons."
  - p. 192: two-tier vs sliding-scale; the sliding-scale keys degree of consideration to "the degree of your cognitive, affective, and social complexity"; a sliding-scale proponent "is likely to merge it, partially, with the two-tier model insofar as she is likely to defend equal consideration, and equal moral status, among persons" — i.e. DeGrazia already notes that hierarchists want an egalitarian tier at the top, the feature Floris & Timmer 2025 argue is unprincipled.
  - p. 193: **"even sentience comes in degrees"** — the response to a sentientist who rejects personhood-properties: "Sentience, after all, is the capacity to experience feelings ... And it seems natural to say that different sorts of sentient creatures have differing degrees of capacity to experience feelings, providing support once again for the sliding-scale model." This is [P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2)'s first shift condition stated by the rival of record.
  - pp. 193–195: the cumulative case for unequal consideration — beneficence stronger toward humans, pest control, insects ("if all sentient beings have equal moral status and insects are sentient, it would seem that we would be obliged to take insects quite seriously indeed. This is highly counterintuitive"), and meat-eating in desperate circumstances. The insect passage is the published form of the aggregation pressure the invertebrate article answers with an agent-side responsibility gradient; DeGrazia reads the same intuition as evidence for scalar *status*.
  - p. 196: the discipline demanded of a threshold theorist — "if we deny degrees of moral status, we need to be aware of the theoretical options available to our opponent and the substantial arguments she can adduce."
- **Tenet alignment**: framework-neutral; DeGrazia leaves the question open ("an issue I leave open", p. 195).

### Wendler, *Life Without Degrees of Moral Status* (2023) and "Degrees of Moral Status: The Problem of Relevance and the Need for a Threshold" (*Erkenntnis*)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780197675328.001.0001 ; https://doi.org/10.1007/s10670-023-00737-9
- **Type**: Book (OUP, 2023) [abstract, Crossref]; paper, *Erkenntnis* 90(4): 1275–1292 (online 2023, print 2025) [summariser snippet]
- **Key points**:
  - The book's abstract: "It distinguishes between moral status conferring, moral action guiding, and moral status enhancing properties, and it argues that degrees of moral status require moral status enhancing properties. It then considers whether some properties are moral status enhancing in the sense that they increase the moral status of those who possess them. This analysis reveals that there are no moral status enhancing properties—hence, no degrees of moral status." Motivated explicitly by the enhancement worry: "robots and genetically enhanced human beings could become significantly more important than the rest of us."
  - Ch. 3 (pp. 37–49) starts from Rachels's moral individualism — "individuals' properties are morally relevant only to the extent that they influence what they have at stake in specific circumstances" — and finds that "degrees of moral status cannot be based on moral action guiding properties" [abstract, Crossref].
  - Ch. 5 "Is It Really Worse to Kill Humans?" (pp. 74–90) and **ch. 6 "Is Suffering Really Worse in Humans?" (pp. 91–99)** run DeGrazia's test case at book length; ch. 7 "Does Respect Really Apply Only to Humans?" (pp. 100–118) takes on the Kantian second tier.
  - The *Erkenntnis* paper [summariser]: "two-factor" accounts (one set of properties confers status, another increases it) face "the problem of relevance and the need for a threshold", which "reveal that there are no moral status enhancing properties, hence, no degrees of moral status."
- **Tenet alignment**: **the closest published ally on the conclusion**, reached on wholly framework-neutral grounds. Wendler's triad maps onto the Map's vocabulary exactly: conferring = status, action-guiding = stakes, enhancing = what [P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2) denies exists. The article should adopt the triad and cite the book as the developed statement of the view rather than presenting the separation as the Map's invention. Read chs. 3–6 before drafting.

### McMahan, "Challenges to Human Equality" (2008) — author's PDF
- **URL**: https://www.philosophy.ox.ac.uk/files/challengestohumanequalitypdf ; DOI 10.1007/s10892-007-9020-9
- **Type**: Paper, *Journal of Ethics* 12(1): 81–104 (Crossref `issued` 2007 = online-first) [raw]
- **Key points**:
  - Restates the *Ethics of Killing* view (p. 94): "a 'two-tiered' account of the morality of killing, according to which the killing of individuals below a certain threshold of psychological capacity – which I called the 'threshold of respect' – is governed solely by a proper concern for their good (technically, their time-relative interests ...), whereas the killing of individuals above the threshold is governed by a requirement of respect for their inherent worth ... individuals above the threshold have equal worth."
  - Quotes Mulgan's objection and concedes it (pp. 94–95): "McMahan wants to combine a naturalistic, broadly Humean, picture of a world where continuous properties come in degrees, with a set of Kantian intuitions that clearly require sharp boundaries between persons and non-persons. This is an essentially unstable combination." — "In this he is obviously right."
  - Lists **seven challenges** to any threshold (pp. 95–97), "all of which I consider to be quite powerful." (1): "If the possession of these capacities, or their possession above a certain level, is what is necessary for inclusion within the scope of the equal wrongness thesis, how could it be that the degree to which these capacities are developed above the threshold is entirely irrelevant?" (2): "it is implausible to suppose that there was a point when each of us was suddenly transformed into the moral equal of others ... Because the relevant capacities are matters of degree and lack sharp boundaries, the difference between an individual just above the threshold and an individual just below it cannot be the difference between the possession of some capacity and the failure to possess that capacity." (3): the placement dilemma — low enough for young children admits chimpanzees; high enough to exclude animals excludes children and the permanently arrested. (4): if degree of harm matters for non-lethal wrongs, why not for killing?
  - Offers a **three-tiered** refinement (pp. 98–99): two thresholds, a consequentialist zone below the lower, "maximum inviolability" above the higher, and an *intermediate and variable* status between — "constraints, but the strength of these constraints varies with the level of psychological capacity"; assigned to "very young children, many of the radically impaired, and some higher nonhuman animals." Note the aside: "aliens with psychological capacities substantially higher than those of any human being might have a higher degree of inviolability" — McMahan grants the superior-beings result Kagan grants.
  - Conclusion (p. 104): "It is hard to avoid the sense that our egalitarian commitments rest on distressingly insecure foundations."
- **Tenet alignment**: McMahan is the SEP's named threshold advocate and is a sceptic about whether the threshold can be justified. Every one of his seven challenges presupposes that the status-grounding property "comes in degrees"; challenge (2) in particular — that the difference across the line "cannot be the difference between the possession of some capacity and the failure to possess that capacity" — is exactly what the Map's Tenet 1 denies for the one property it uses. The article's distinctive move is to show that a threshold drawn at *the presence of a subject* is not a threshold on a continuous scale at all, so the seven challenges do not apply to it as they apply to McMahan's threshold of respect. Its cost is that any *second* threshold (personhood, autonomy) inherits all seven — which is why the Map draws one.

### Floris & Timmer, "Arbitrariness and the threshold for moral status" (2025); Timmer, "On the Idea of Degrees of Moral Status" (2025)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1177/1470594X251398468 ; https://doi.org/10.1007/s10790-023-09970-5
- **Type**: Papers, *Politics, Philosophy & Economics* (2025) [abstract, Semantic Scholar]; *Journal of Value Inquiry* 59(4): 789–807 (online 2023) [summariser snippet]
- **Key points**:
  - Floris & Timmer: "for at least one degree to which an entity can possess the status-conferring property and that grounds moral status, there is some incrementally lower degree of possessing the property that does not ground moral status. Critics maintain that this renders any threshold for moral status arbitrary. In this paper, we reject common responses to this arbitrariness objection, such as that moral status thresholds are not arbitrary but merely vague. Instead, we defend the moderate discontinuity view. This view holds that thresholds denote moderate rather than radical shifts in moral status ... Crucially, it follows from this that there is no principled way to reconcile the commitment to the moral equality of persons with the commitment to the moral superiority of persons over nonhuman animals."
  - Timmer 2025 [summariser]: degrees of status can be specified along two dimensions, "(i) the weight of the reason to protect an entity's morally significant rights and interests; and/or (ii) the rights and interests that are considered morally significant" — the same two axes the SEP §3 lists.
- **Tenet alignment**: Floris & Timmer's conclusion is a **dilemma for Kagan's limited hierarchy and McMahan's two tiers**, and it is the same dilemma [P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2)'s consistency argument states from the other side: an egalitarian tier for persons sitting on top of a hierarchy below is unprincipled. The unitarian threshold at sentience escapes the dilemma by having no superiority tier. But their "moderate discontinuity" premise — that a threshold cannot mark a *radical* shift — is precisely what the Map denies: the shift from no subject to a subject is radical, and it is radical because it is not a shift in degree. Whether that reply is available depends on the status-conferring property not being a degree property (see Lee below).

### Buchanan, "Moral Status and Human Enhancement" (2009) — open PDF; and the reply literature
- **URL**: https://pgrim.org/pa2010reading/buchananmoralstatus.pdf ; DOI 10.1111/j.1088-4963.2009.01166.x
- **Type**: Paper, *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 37(4): 346–381 [raw]
- **Key points** (page numbers from the PDF's running headers):
  - p. 357: "The concept of human rights is a threshold concept, not a scalar one. What matters from the standpoint of the ascription of human rights is whether an individual has certain capabilities or interests; the fact that other individuals may have them to a greater degree is irrelevant ... Once the threshold is reached, how well one reasons practically or how well one engages in practices of mutual accountability does not affect one's moral status."
  - p. 359: "it is not clear whether that of post-persons makes sense. Merely augmenting the characteristics that make a being a person doesn't seem to be the sort of thing that could confer higher moral status ... equality of moral status of the sort we associate with personhood can accommodate many inequalities, including inequalities in the very characteristics that confer moral status. That is what it means to say that the concept of moral status is a threshold concept." — **[P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2)'s assertion, verbatim, published in 2009.**
  - pp. 361–362, **the objection the Map must answer**: the respect-based (Kantian/contractualist) view "can explain why any being who clearly has the capacity in question is owed equal respect and hence why having the psychological and motivational characteristics that constitute the capacity to a higher degree does not confer higher moral status", and concedes "a fuzzy lower boundary for this threshold". By contrast, "if having a good is what confers moral considerability, then having a higher good or being capable of greater well-being should always be relevant to how one ought to be treated" — the interest-based view "seems ill suited" to a threshold concept (p. 361). Buchanan's argument is therefore that **threshold status is available only to a respect-based ground, not to an interest-based one**. The Map's ground is interest-based (valenced experience). The reply the Map has, and should make explicit, is that "relevant to how one ought to be treated" is the stakes column — Buchanan's own contrast between *relevance to treatment* and *degree of status* is the separation — so the interest-based view is not ill suited; it simply locates the relevance where DeGrazia's Unequal Interests model locates it.
  - p. 363: "from the perspective of the respect-based view, it is hard to imagine what a higher threshold—one that required a higher moral status—would be like ... In the absence of an account of what the higher threshold would be like, the claim that there could be beings at a higher threshold who would have a higher moral status is not convincing."
- **Replies** (all metadata Crossref-verified, all 0 corpus hits):
  - **Douglas 2013**, "Human enhancement and supra-personal moral status", *Philosophical Studies* 162(3): 473–497, DOI 10.1007/s11098-011-9778-2 [abstract, PubMed]: "I argue that Buchanan's response fails. However, I then outline an alternative response ... though moral status-increasing human enhancements might render ordinary, unenhanced humans less immune to permissible harm, they need not worsen the overall distribution of this immunity across beings." Douglas thus grants that enhancement *could* raise status (against [P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2)) and answers the harm worry distributively. His definition, as quoted by Schukraft [raw]: "[o]ther things being equal, a being with higher moral status will enjoy stronger and/or broader basic rights or claims than a being of lesser moral status" (Douglas 2013, p. 476).
  - **DeGrazia 2012**, "Genetic enhancement, post-persons and moral status: a reply to Buchanan", *Journal of Medical Ethics* 38(3): 135–139, DOI 10.1136/medethics-2011-100126 (with author reply to commentaries, 38(3): 145–147); **Wilson 2012**, "Persons, post-persons and thresholds", *JME* 38(3): 143–144, DOI 10.1136/medethics-2011-100243; **Agar 2013**, "Why is it possible to enhance moral status and why doing so is wrong?", *JME* 39(2): 67–74, DOI 10.1136/medethics-2012-100597, and "Still afraid of needy post-persons", *JME* 39(2): 81–83; **Agar 2014**, *Truly Human Enhancement* (MIT), ch. Abstracts not retrievable this session; the symposium's shape (Buchanan: threshold, no post-persons; DeGrazia and Agar: post-persons possible; Wilson: on thresholds) is stable across the sources surveyed.
  - **DeGrazia 2016**, "Modal Personhood and Moral Status: A Reply to Kagan's Proposal", *Journal of Applied Philosophy* 33(1): 22–25 [abstract]: examines "implications for dogs who would count as modal persons when genetic enhancements are capable of transforming them into persons" — enhancement used as a reductio of Kagan's modal personism, a lever the Map can reuse.

### The affirmative-equality routes (what ChatGPT 2026-09-05 §5 asked for)
1. **Equal consideration of equal interests** (Singer 1993) — already the Map's; Rethink Priorities' Schukraft 2020 [raw]: "even though mice and humans have the same moral status, it doesn't follow that humans and mice have the same capacity for welfare. Hence, alleviating human and mice suffering may not have equal moral importance"; "Status-adjusted welfare is neutral on the question of degrees of moral status. Unitarians assign all creatures with moral standing the same moral status, so for the unitarian, status-adjusted welfare just collapses to welfare." The Moral Weight Project (Fischer 2024, *Weighing Animal Welfare*, OUP, DOI 10.1093/9780197745793.001.0001; open access) assumes "hedonism ... and unitarianism (equal amounts of welfare count equally, regardless of whose welfare it is)" and grades *welfare ranges* — an applied, quantitative implementation of exactly the Map's architecture, with the sentience question bracketed. **Rossi 2010** [abstract, PubMed], *Kennedy Institute of Ethics Journal* 20(3): 251–276: "equal consideration is not compatible with unequal moral status, thereby forcing those who would justify significantly different moral protections for humans and animals to argue for unequal consideration" — i.e. the SEP's reading of DeGrazia's Unequal Interests model as "degrees of status" is contested; on Rossi's reading the Map's view has no degrees of status at all.
2. **Categorical inherent value** (Regan). Regan 1985 [raw, the *In Defense of Animals* essay]: "The genius and the retarded child, the prince and the pauper, the brain surgeon and the fruit vendor, Mother Teresa and the most unscrupulous used-car salesman—all have inherent value, all possess it equally"; "Inherent value, then, belongs equally to those who are the experiencing subjects of a life"; "All who have inherent value have it equally, whether they be human animals or not." This is the "rights threshold triggered by having a point of view" ChatGPT named: subject-of-a-life is a presence property; the equality of inherent value is *stipulated as categorical* rather than derived, which is the weakness the SEP §4 objection targets. Book: *The Case for Animal Rights* (1983), where the categorical/degree claim is made (page not verified this session; commonly cited around pp. 235–241 — verify before quoting).
3. **Tethered importance** (Korsgaard 2018, *Fellow Creatures*, OUP) [summariser, NDPR review by Bernstein]: the claim that humans are more important than animals is "almost senseless" because "importance or value is a 'tethered' property: to be important is to be important to someone"; every creature "for whom things can be good or bad" is an end in itself. An anti-ranking argument that does not go through equal consideration at all: there is no neutral standpoint from which a subject's importance could be *more*. It fits the Map's phenomenal value realism (value is *for* a locus) unusually well and is uncited anywhere in the corpus.
4. **Range property plus opacity respect** (Rawls 1971 §77; Carter 2011, "Respect and the Basis of Equality", *Ethics* 121(3): 538–571, DOI 10.1086/658897; Carter 2024, "Basic Equality and the Contexts of Opacity Respect", in *How Can We Be Equals?*, OUP, pp. 127–148; Waldron 2017, *One Another's Equals*, Harvard UP) [summariser, SEP "Egalitarianism" §1.6]: the basis-of-equality literature confronts the same puzzle — grounding capacities vary continuously — and answers with a *range property* ("while we can differ in the degree to which we hold it, we all hold the property") plus, in Carter, a normative injunction not to look: opacity respect is "a refusal to evaluate persons' varying capacities". The SEP notes the risk that range properties are "arbitrarily speciesist, seemingly picked specifically to exclude non-human animals". For the Map this is double-edged: it is the most developed account of why a threshold can ground equality despite scalar underlying capacities, *and* it makes the Map's threshold look like one more stipulated range unless the Map can say why "has a point of view" is not a range on a scale but a different kind of property. Carter's later "Two Concepts of Moral Status" (positional vs scalar) [unverified] is reported to make exactly the two-concept split the Map needs; obtain before citing.
5. **No status-enhancing properties** (Rachels 2004, "Drawing Lines", in Sunstein & Nussbaum (eds), *Animal Rights: Current Debates and New Directions*, OUP, pp. 162–174; Wendler 2023): every candidate property that would raise status turns out, on inspection, to be action-guiding through what its bearer has at stake — the *translation* argument the enhancement article runs on the equal-interests pair, generalised to a universal claim. This is the affirmative argument with the best fit to what the Map already says, and it has a book behind it.
6. **Separateness of persons / non-aggregation** (Taurek 1977, "Should the Numbers Count?", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 6(4): 293–316; Nozick 1974) — named by ChatGPT; not directly on threshold status. Its relevance is that under a non-aggregative deontology equal status becomes *action-guiding* by construction (each subject's claim is a separate constraint, not a summand), which is Kagan's chs. 7–9 territory: he argues deontologists too must accept hierarchy because unitarian constraints make "killing a fish to save a human" wrong. The article can note this as where the status/stakes separation is *least* verbal — constraints do not translate into stakes — and where the Map, which has no worked deontology, is most exposed.

### Degrees of the capacity itself — Lee, Birch, DeGrazia p. 193, and the Map's own concept page
- **Lee 2020**, "Does sentience come in degrees?", *Animal Sentience* 5(29), DOI 10.51291/2377-7478.1607 (commentary on Birch 2018, "Degrees of sentience?", *Animal Sentience* 3(21), DOI 10.51291/2377-7478.1353, whose abstract [Crossref] proposes "a framework for assigning degrees of protection in proportion to degrees of sentience"); **Lee 2023**, "Degrees of consciousness", *Noûs* 57(3): 553–575, DOI 10.1111/nous.12421 [abstract, Crossref]: "I argue that whether consciousness comes in degrees ultimately depends on which theory of consciousness turns out to be correct. But I also argue that most theories of consciousness entail that consciousness comes in degrees." Lee's 2025 "Consciousness Makes Things Matter" (*Philosophers' Imprint* 25, DOI 10.3998/phimp.1956), already cited at `concepts/consciousness-value-connection`, argues phenomenal consciousness is what makes something a welfare subject.
- **Why this is the pivot.** [P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2)'s first shift condition is "a defensible account of degrees of the *capacity itself*, as distinct from degrees of what the capacity delivers". Lee, Birch and DeGrazia (p. 193) each supply a candidate. The Map's answer has to distinguish *degrees of experience* (intensity, richness, temporal grain — all conceded, all stakes) from *degrees of being an experiencer* (denied: a locus is present or not). Lee's analysis is theory-relative, so the article can say which theories make subjecthood degreed (IIT, on which `concepts/degrees-of-consciousness` L88 already notes "consciousness does not appear at a threshold") and note that the Map's interactionist dualism is not among them. The seam with `concepts/degrees-of-consciousness` ("Consciousness is not binary", L36; Tenet 5 invoked against on/off treatment, L122) must be closed explicitly: that page grades *level and content within a subject*; nothing there grades the presence of the subject. `voids/threshold-void` and `topics/ethics-of-consciousness-invertebrate-question` L51 ("the presence of a subject is binary") already say this and should be linked.

### Practical-difference question — is the distinction verbal?
- **Allegri 2022** [abstract], *Relations* 9(1–2), DOI 10.7358/rela-2021-0102-alle: "lessens the role of such opposition – especially its practical relevance – by emphasizing that, regardless of the fact of conceiving moral status in terms of all or nothing or in gradual terms, what really counts in our attitude towards non-human animals is to assign them an important moral consideration"; proposes equal consideration of interests as the compromise. Allegri is the published statement of ChatGPT's "terminological allocation" charge, and the enhancement article's discriminator case is the published-shape reply (DeGrazia p. 188's test case, run with stakes fixed). Sebo's review makes the complementary point that the two views diverge most where stakes are held equal and least in aggregate policy ("even if Kagan were right that we should accept hierarchy, it would still be the case that we should prioritize nonhumans over humans in many cases in practice").

### McMahan's time-relative interest account — the paradigm stakes-grading device
- *The Ethics of Killing* (2002), ch. 3 and pp. 232–242 (per Buchanan's n. 18) [summariser, NDPR review by Lacewing]: the badness of death is discounted by the strength of the prudential relations (psychological unity) between the victim now and the goods lost; "the wrongness of killing human beings who have little psychological unity, such as fetuses, infants, and the congenitally severely cognitively impaired, is proportional to their time-relative interests." McMahan pairs this *interests* tier with a *respect* tier for persons (the two-tiered account above, confirmed [raw] in McMahan 2008 p. 94; the "intermediate status" of pp. 264–265 acknowledged at 2008 n. 15). TRIA is the most worked account in the literature of how a *stakes* variable (time-relative interest) can carry the whole intuitive load that hierarchists put on status — for killing. The Map's position is McMahan's interests tier extended to everyone with standing and his respect tier deleted; the article should say so and say why (Kagan ch. 8; Floris & Timmer; McMahan's own seven challenges, which attack the respect threshold and not the sentience one).

### Jaworska & Tannenbaum — the relational route to elevated status, and its critics
- "Person-Rearing Relationships as a Key to Higher Moral Status", *Ethics* 124(2): 242–271 (2014), DOI 10.1086/673431; "Persons and Moral Status", in *Persons* (OUP 2019), pp. 334–362; SEP §5.6 above. Critique: **Timmerman & Fischer 2019**, *Thought* 8(2): 119–128, DOI 10.1002/tht3.413 [abstract]: person-rearing accounts "generate extremely implausible consequences when combined with the accounts of flourishing Jaworska and Tannenbaum need." Relevance: this is the leading *relational* ground for a higher status tier, designed to keep infants and the cognitively impaired above animals without species membership; it is a two-factor account in Wendler's sense and inherits his objection. Not a rival to the Map's threshold; a rival to its refusal of a second tier.

### The utility-monster residue
- Nozick 1974, p. 41 (already cited); **Chappell 2021**, "Negative Utility Monsters", *Utilitas* 33(4): 417–421, DOI 10.1017/S0953820821000169 [abstract]: "our intuitions may be reversed by considering a variation in which the utility monster starts from a baseline status of massive suffering" — the intensity-ceiling worry is symmetric, and its negative form (an enhanced subject whose *suffering* ceiling is raised) is not obviously a monster at all. **Vallentyne 2005**, "Of Mice and Men: Equality and Animals", *Journal of Ethics* 9(3–4): 403–433, DOI 10.1007/s10892-005-3509-x: the mirror-image problem for unitarian *egalitarians* — mice, being worse off, would have the stronger claim on resources; this is the engine of Kagan's ch. 3 argument from distribution, and it is the case where the enhancement article's observation (prioritising the *unenhanced* patient as worse off "is not implausible at all") does the most work. Kagan's own reply route (multiply welfare by status) is the thing [P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2) refuses; Chappell and Vallentyne together show the residue is a residue of aggregation, not of the threshold.

- **Unitarian / threshold** — *Core claim*: one status for all with standing; all grading is of interests, welfare range, or time-relative interest. *Key arguments*: equal consideration (Singer); the translation argument (Rachels, Wendler); categorical inherent value (Regan); tethered importance (Korsgaard); Sebo's debunking of hierarchy intuitions; Rossi's incompatibility argument. *Relation to tenets*: framework-neutral on the verdict; Tenet 1 supplies the reading of the threshold on which the arbitrariness objection misses.
- **Limited hierarchy** (Kagan) — *Core claim*: status scalar with capacity for well-being, agency, potential and modal status; persons equal by practical realism. *Key arguments*: ch. 3 distribution; ch. 7–9 deontological thresholds; intuitions in idealised pair cases. *Relation to tenets*: no tenet conflict; the Map's disagreement is normative and rests on which intuition survives (enhancement article). Exposed to Floris & Timmer's dilemma at its egalitarian tier.
- **Two-tier respect/interests** (McMahan 2002; refined to three tiers 2008) — *Core claim*: consequentialist treatment below a threshold of respect, equal inviolability above, intermediate variable status between. *Relation to tenets*: the Map keeps the interests tier and deletes the respect tier; McMahan's seven challenges are the Map's best borrowed argument against *any* capacity-graded second threshold.
- **Person-rearing relational elevation** (Jaworska & Tannenbaum) — a two-factor account; Timmerman & Fischer's flourishing objection.
- **Precaution / expected-value under uncertainty** (Birch 2024; Sebo 2018, 2025) — not a rival: a credence over threshold-crossing, not a degree of status (enhancement article already says this; the "one-in-a-thousand" figure attributed to Sebo 2025 is [unverified] and must not be quoted without the book).

## Key Debates

### Is the threshold arbitrary?
- **Sides**: SEP §4, Arneson, Mulgan, McMahan 2008 (challenges 1–3), Floris & Timmer 2025 vs threshold theorists (Buchanan's "fuzzy lower boundary"; Regan's categorical stipulation; Carter's opacity respect).
- **Core disagreement**: whether a status-conferring property that comes in degrees can ground a degree-insensitive status. Every party to the published debate assumes the property *does* come in degrees.
- **Current state**: unresolved; McMahan's own verdict is scepticism. **The Map's opening**: deny the shared assumption for its one status-conferring property. This is where Tenet 1 does distinctive work and where the article must meet Lee 2023 and DeGrazia p. 193 head-on.

### Can an interest-based ground be a threshold ground?
- **Sides**: Buchanan 2009 pp. 361–362 (no: only respect-based grounds) vs the Map, Sebo, Wendler, Rethink Priorities (yes: interest magnitude is action-guiding, not status-raising).
- **Current state**: Buchanan's asymmetry is unanswered in the literature surveyed; the Map's answer is the status/stakes separation, which it should now state as a reply to Buchanan by name.

### Is the difference between unitarianism and hierarchy action-guiding?
- **Sides**: Allegri 2022, ChatGPT 2026-09-05 (verbal) vs DeGrazia's p. 188 test case, Wendler ch. 6, the enhancement article's pair (a verdict changes when stakes are fixed).
- **Current state**: the discriminator exists; what remains contested is whether the intuition it elicits (the same pain counts the same whoever feels it) is more secure than Kagan's.

### One threshold or two?
- **Sides**: McMahan, DeGrazia's two-tier, Jaworska & Tannenbaum, Kagan's restricted-deontology opponents (a personhood tier) vs the Map, Wendler ch. 7, Kagan ch. 8 (no principled second line).
- **Current state**: Kagan and Floris & Timmer both argue, from opposite ends, that a second line at personhood cannot be held; the Map should cite both.

### Does enhancement raise status?
- **Sides**: Buchanan 2009 (no) vs Douglas 2013, Agar 2013/2014, DeGrazia 2012 (possibly yes) — with Wilson 2012 on thresholds and Douglas 2021 on whether status is even a benefit.
- **Current state**: live, and the Map's [P-MS2](/positions/moral-status/#p-ms2) is a contribution to it that currently cites none of it.
