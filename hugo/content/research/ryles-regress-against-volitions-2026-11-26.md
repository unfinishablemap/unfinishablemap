# Research: Ryle's Regress against Volitions and the Map's Volitionist Theory of Action

**Date**: 2026-11-26
**Search queries used**: Ryle regress of volitions reply McCann "Volition and Basic Action" 1974 Philosophical Review; Ginet "On Action" 1990 volition "actish" phenomenal quality simple mental act regress Ryle; "Ryle" regress volitions "voluntary or involuntary" reply volitionism "intrinsically" McCann Ginet open access pdf; Hornsby Actions 1980 "every action is an event of trying" "precedes and causes a contraction of muscles"; "regress" "volitions" Ryle volitionism defended recent article 2021–2025. Also archive.org advanced search and full-text download (Ryle; Locke), Crossref metadata and chapter-abstract lookups (McCann, Ginet, Lowe, Weir, Steward, Weatherson, Valaris, Prichard, Cleveland, O'Shaughnessy, Hornsby), direct SEP, NDPR and Cambridge Core fetches.

## Executive Summary

Ryle's argument is the fourth of four objections to volitions in chapter 3, "The Will", of *The Concept of Mind* (1949: 62–69). It is a dilemma with an escape clause. If volitions are involuntary, the actions they produce cannot be voluntary. If they are voluntary "in the sense assumed by the theory", each needs a prior volition, *ad infinitum*. If they are neither, they cannot be virtuous or wicked either (1949: 67). The standard reply exploits Ryle's own qualifier: a volition is an action *intrinsically*, not by issuing from a prior act (Ginet 1990; McCann 1998; framed as the reply to Ryle by Schlosser's SEP *Agency*). That reply is built for **non-causal** volitionism. The Map's version is different. Its trying is **agent-caused** ([P-A6](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a6)), so the regress reappears one step back, at the agent's causing of the trying. The Map already answers it there: it denies Lowe's premise that agents cause only by acting, and ends the regress at a substance whose causing is basic. The host page says this at L77, and the 2026-10-29 outer-review synthesis (R2) accepted it as the answer to a regress dilemma two reviewers raised. **No Map page names Ryle**, and none connects L77's terminus to the oldest objection it answers. Topics and concepts are both full (370/370), so the verdict is **fold**: about +327 words into `topics/philosophy-of-action-under-dualism` §Volitionism, plus two optional one-sentence pointers. Answering the regress is purely defensive. It shows the hybrid is coherent and gives no evidence for Bidirectional Interaction.

## Key Sources

### Ryle, *The Concept of Mind* (1949), ch. 3 "The Will"
- **URL**: https://archive.org/details/gilbert-ryle-concept-of-mind (public djvu text of the Hutchinson's University Library edition, "First Published - October, 1949", this copy a 1951 reprint). The lending copies `conceptofmin00ryle` etc. returned "Item not available".
- **Type**: Book (primary). **Read in full and grepped** for ch. 3 §§1–2 (pp. 62–69). Page numbers below come from this edition's running heads. Other editions (Penguin 1963; Chicago 2002; Routledge 2009) paginate differently.
- **Key points** (all quotations grepped in the raw text; OCR slips corrected only where noted):
  - **Target and scope.** Ryle sets out "to refute the doctrine that there exists a Faculty, immaterial Organ, or Ministry, corresponding to the theory's description of the 'Will' and, accordingly, that there occur processes, or operations, corresponding to what it describes as 'volitions'" (p. 63). The refutation "will not invalidate the distinctions which we all quite properly draw between voluntary and involuntary actions" (p. 63). Volition is "an artificial concept" (p. 62), and he hopes to show it belongs with "'Phlogiston' and 'animal spirits'" (p. 62).
  - **Why he files volitions under the ghost.** "It is just an inevitable extension of the myth of the ghost in the machine" (p. 63). "So the language of 'volitions' is the language of the para-mechanical theory of the mind" (p. 64).
  - **First objection (no reports).** "No one ever says such things as that at 10 a.m. he was occupied in willing this or that, or that he performed five quick and easy volitions and two slow and difficult volitions between midday and lunch-time" (p. 64). He asks: "Can they be sudden or gradual, strong or weak, difficult or easy, enjoyable or disagreeable?" (p. 64). If these questions cannot be settled, "then it is fair to conclude that their existence is not asserted on empirical grounds" (p. 65).
  - **Second objection (unknowability, including to the agent).** Others can only infer volitions. Nor can the agent know his volition caused the movement: "for all he knows, his volition may have had some other movement as its effect and the pulling of the trigger may have had some other event for its cause" (p. 66).
  - **Third objection (interaction).** "Transactions between minds and bodies involve links where no links can be" (p. 66).
  - **Fourth objection: the regress.** "So what of volitions themselves? Are they voluntary or involuntary acts of mind? Clearly either answer leads to absurdities" (p. 67; OCR "Icads"). "If I cannot help willing to pull the trigger, it would be absurd to describe my pulling it as 'voluntary'. But if my volition to pull the trigger is voluntary, in the sense assumed by the theory, then it must issue from a prior volition and that from another ad infinitum" (p. 67). The escape clause: "It has been suggested, to avoid this difficulty, that volitions cannot be described as either voluntary or involuntary. 'Volition' is a term of the wrong type to accept either predicate" (p. 67; OCR "sugecsted", "cither"). Ryle's rejoinder is that the predicates "'virtuous' and 'wicked'" would then fail too, "a conclusion which might embarrass those moralists who use volitions as the sheet-anchor of their systems" (p. 67).
  - **Diagnosis.** "the doctrine of volitions is a causal hypothesis, adopted because it was wrongly supposed that the question, 'What makes a bodily movement voluntary?' was a causal question" (p. 67).
  - **The same regress against choosing.** If choosing is voluntary, "it would have in its turn to be the result of a prior choice to choose, and that from a choice to choose to choose" (p. 68).
  - **Effort and bracing.** Ryle grants that I may, "clenching my fists and gritting my teeth, brace myself" (p. 68), but holds that "the operations of resolving and nerving ourselves are themselves members of the class of creditable or discreditable actions", so they cannot be the volition behind an action (pp. 68–69).
- **Tenet alignment**: Conflicts with Tenet 3 *as the Map renders it* (an inner act causing movement), and Ryle frames the whole doctrine as the ghost myth (conflict with Tenet 1's dualism in spirit). The regress itself, though, is neutral between dualism and physicalism (see Relation to Site Tenets).

### Schlosser, "Agency", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (first published 2015; substantive revision 28 Oct 2019)
- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/agency/
- **Type**: Encyclopedia. **Raw HTML grepped.**
- **Key points**:
  - Defines volitionism: "Volitions themselves are entirely uncaused and they are sui generis acts: they are acts in virtue of their intrinsic properties, not in virtue of some extrinsic or relational property (such as having the right causal history)". It "differs sharply from both the event-causal and the agent-causal framework" (citing Ginet 1990; McCann 1998; "see also Lowe 2008").
  - The regress and the reply, in one place: "A common objection to volitionist accounts is that they generate a regress of mental acts (Ryle 1949). Arguably, though, this objection begs the question." Volitions explain overt actions, and "There is no need to appeal to further mental acts of the will in order to explain why volitions are actions, because volitions are actions sui generis (see Enç 2003 for discussion)."
  - The price: "Volitionist theories stipulate as primitive what appears to be in need of explanation. In particular, they do not explain what an agent's exercise of control consists in, as the agent is merely the subject or the bearer of volitions (O'Connor 2000: 25–26; Clarke 2003: 17–24)." A second cost, that brain events are not "entirely uncaused", is pressed against volitions realised in the brain.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. It supplies the reply and the cost the Map must weigh.

### Clarke, Capes & Swenson, "Incompatibilist (Nondeterministic) Theories of Free Will", *SEP* (first published 2000; substantive revision 17 Nov 2026)
- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/incompatibilism-theories/
- **Type**: Encyclopedia. **Raw HTML grepped.**
- **Key points** (SEP paraphrase unless marked as an SEP-relayed quotation):
  - Ginet: "each basic action is characterized by an actish phenomenal quality, its seeming to the agent as if she is directly making happen the event that is her basic action." Ginet "holds, it cannot be true that we really are agent-causes of what we do". **So Ginet rejects agent causation outright.**
  - The intrinsic-quality reply's known counterexample: "As Ginet (1990: 9) allows, an event with the indicated intrinsic quality might be brought about by direct stimulation of someone's brain". The SEP authors add that this "would hardly seem to be an exercise of the subject's agency."
  - McCann (1998: 180): a basic action is "a spontaneous, creative undertaking" and "intrinsically intentional". SEP relays his claim that spontaneity "has a certain sui generis character that renders it incapable of being reduced to anything else" (1998: 185).
  - O'Connor's model: "agents do not cause free decisions; rather, a free decision is a causally complex event, consisting of the agent's causing her coming to have a certain intention."
  - Lowe (2008: chs. 6–9 and 2013) is filed with the noncausal theories.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. It shows that the intrinsic reply belongs to theories that reject the Map's agent-causal half.

### Steward, "Do Actions Occur Inside the Body?", *Mind & Society* 1(2): 107–125 (Crossref date 2000-09; the White Rose cover says 2001)
- **URL**: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/3343/ (author's version, **raw PDF grepped**). DOI 10.1007/BF02512316.
- **Key points**: Hornsby (1980) defends "(P) many actions are bodily movements (in a special sense of the word "movement") (Q) all actions are tryings; and (R) all actions occur inside the body", and "she designates (R) the principal thesis of her essay (1980 , p.14)".

### Weatherson, "Intellectual Skill and the Rylean Regress", *Philosophical Quarterly* (online 2016), doi:10.1093/pq/pqw051
- **URL**: https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/10023/15726/1/RegressPQ_Aug16.pdf (**raw PDF grepped**)

### Locke, *An Essay concerning Human Understanding*, II.xxi.25
- **URL**: https://archive.org/details/essayconcerningh00lock (1824 edition, djvu text **grepped**; OCR "ihnt" corrected to "that")
- **Key point**: those who ask whether a man is at liberty to will "must suppose one will to determine the acts of another, and another to determine that; and so on in infinitum."

## Major Positions

### The regress as Ryle states it
- **Proponent**: Ryle (1949: 67).
- **Core claim**: a dilemma. Involuntary volitions cannot make actions voluntary. Voluntary volitions, "in the sense assumed by the theory", need prior volitions without end. Escaping by declaring volitions "of the wrong type" for either predicate costs the moral appraisal volitions were meant to anchor.
- **Its hinge**: the qualifier "in the sense assumed by the theory", meaning that being voluntary is *issuing from a volition*. Every reply attacks this hinge.

### Reply 1: intrinsic (sui generis) actionhood
- **Proponents**: Ginet (1990), McCann (1974; 1986; 1998); Lowe in part (uncaused volitions). Schlosser's SEP frames this as the answer to Ryle.
- **Claim**: volitions are actions in virtue of an intrinsic feature (Ginet's *actish* phenomenal quality; McCann's spontaneity and intrinsic intentionality), not in virtue of a cause. So no prior volition is needed.
- **Costs**: (i) it stipulates a primitive, and the agent is "merely the subject or the bearer of volitions" (O'Connor; Clarke; via Schlosser). (ii) Brain stimulation could produce an event with the intrinsic quality, which Ginet himself allows (1990: 9, via SEP). (iii) **Ginet rejects agent causation**, so the Map cannot borrow his reply wholesale.

### Reply 2: identity (the trying is the action)
- **Proponents**: Hornsby (1980); O'Shaughnessy (1973; 1980, modified). Wilson & Shpall group Ginet here too.
- **Claim**: by the Davidson–Anscombe identity, the act of trying to move the arm *is* the act of moving it. Nothing stands behind the action whose own voluntariness would need explaining. Basic actions stop the regress.
- **Cost for the Map**: Hornsby's tryings occur "inside the body" as physical events (thesis R). The Map's trying is the non-physical subject's contact point. The identity reply covers the trying→movement step but leaves untouched what makes the *trying* the agent's own, which is where the Map's agent causation enters.

### Reply 3: the agent-causal terminus
- **Proponents**: Chisholm (1964) on immanent causation; O'Connor (2000), for whom a free decision *is* "the agent's causing her coming to have a certain intention" (SEP).
- **Claim**: the regress ends at the agent, a substance whose causing is not an event and needs no prior act.
- **The objection it faces (Lowe, via Griffith)**: "Because all agents cause by acting, they cannot cause their own volitions on pain of regress (7)". Lowe therefore keeps volitions uncaused and makes the agent–volition relation "internal" (8).
- **This is the Map's reply.** See "What the Map's version can use and what it owes" below.

### Reply 4: deflation of "trying"
- **Proponent**: Cleveland (1997), per SEP Wilson & Shpall.
- **Claim**: "trying to F" does not name a distinctive mental act, so there is no inner act for the regress to iterate on. Ryle's own positive view points the same way.
- **Relation to tenets**: incompatible with [P-A6](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a6), which needs the trying to be a real act at the interface.

**Version.** The Map's volitionism is a three-part hybrid:
1. **Lowe's volitionist structure**: agents cause bodily movements by willing.
2. **Hornsby's word "trying"** for the basic act, with her thesis (Q) but not her physicalist (R).
3. The **classical agent-causal stopping point**, in which the volition is *agent-caused*, contra Lowe, and the regress ends at the substance ([P-A2](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a2)'s Chisholmian immanent causation).

It is **not** Ginet's or McCann's version: both are non-causal, and Ginet denies agent causation. **O'Shaughnessy has zero content hits.** The commitment is carried by **[P-A6](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a6)**, with the regress-relevant terminus supplied by **[P-A2](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a2)**.

## What the Map's Version Can Use and What It Owes

1. **The usable reply is Reply 3, already stated at L77.** The Map "treats the trying as agent-caused, which requires denying Lowe's premise that agents cause only by acting, and it ends the regress at a substance whose causing is basic". Synthesis R2 (`reviews/outer-review-synthesis-2026-10-29`) ruled that this answers the regress dilemma the Claude and Gemini reviewers raised. The 2026-11-02 deep review's stability note says: "Do not re-flag it." **This note does not re-flag it.** What is missing is only the *pedigree*: L77's regress is Ryle's argument displaced one step back, and no page says so.
2. **Reply 1 is available only as a supplement.** The Map may hold that the trying is intrinsically active *and* agent-caused. Agent causation then does authorship work: it answers the bearer complaint and Ginet's own brain-stimulation case. The intrinsic feature does the regress-stopping work. Borrowing Ginet or McCann for *that* job must be flagged as the Map's combination, since Ginet rejects agent causation.
3. **What the terminus commits the Map to (an articulation, not a defect).** Ryle's regress forces a choice that no Map text yet states:
   - (a) **Product reading**, which is [P-A6](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a6)'s wording ("direct causal product is ... a volition"). The trying is an action *because* the agent causes it. The agent's causing is then an exercise of power that is not itself an act needing a volition. That is Ryle's "neither voluntary nor involuntary" escape, applied to the causing rather than to the volition. Ryle's rejoinder that such things cannot be "virtuous" or "wicked" then has to be met: appraisal attaches to the agent as source ([P-A2](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a2)), and Weatherson's rejection of "Only volitional actions are normatively assessable" supports this independently.
   - (b) **Identity reading** (O'Connor's model). The trying *is* the agent's causing, here of the neural selection. "Agent-caused trying" is then loose talk, and [P-A6](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a6)'s "direct causal product" wording would need to change.
   Both readings are consistent with L77. This belongs in a positions-evolve audit of [P-A6](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a6) or the next deep review. It is not part of this fold, and it is not a correction.
4. **Ryle's other three objections are already partly met elsewhere, unnamed:**
   - The second ("for all he knows, his volition may have had some other movement as its effect") is almost exactly the host's own concession under "Deviance is relocated, not removed" and the [agency-void](/voids/agency-void/) verification circularity ([P-A4](/positions/agency-and-will/#p-a4)). Ryle used it as a reductio. The Map owns it as a standing limit.
   - The third (interaction) is the interaction problem, handled in [history-of-the-interaction-problem](/topics/history-of-the-interaction-problem/) and [objections-to-interactionism](/concepts/objections-to-interactionism/).
   - The first (no reportable phenomenology) is answered in part by [phenomenology-of-choice-and-volition](/concepts/phenomenology-of-choice-and-volition/) §Effort ("Volitional acts vary in felt difficulty"). Ryle's own counter is that bracing oneself is itself an action, not a volition (1949: 68–69).

## Key Debates

### Does the regress beg the question?
- **Sides**: Schlosser: "Arguably, though, this objection begs the question". Ryle: the qualifier "in the sense assumed by the theory" shows that the theory *itself* defines voluntariness as issuing from a volition.
- **Core disagreement**: whether volitionists are committed to the derivative sense of "voluntary" for volitions themselves.
- **Current state**: on Schlosser's diagnosis, the reason "the view is widely rejected" is the *stipulation* cost, not the regress. That is one encyclopedist's reading, not a survey.

### Is the trying a prior mental act or the action itself?
- **Sides**: traditional volitionalism (inner willing that causes movement) versus the identity view (Hornsby; O'Shaughnessy, modified) versus deflation (Cleveland).
- **Bearing**: the Map's "trying" sits between the first two. It is the action (with Hornsby), and it is the mind's contact point (with traditional volitionism). That makes it exposed to Ryle wherever it is treated as a separate cause.
