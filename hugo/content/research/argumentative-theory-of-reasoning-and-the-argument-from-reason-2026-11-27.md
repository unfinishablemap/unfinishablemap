## Executive Summary

**Verdict: `fold`.** Mercier and Sperber's argumentative theory is the strongest naturalist account of what reasoning is *for*. Its core claim is that the function of reasoning "is to devise and evaluate arguments intended to persuade" (Mercier & Sperber 2011: 57). It deserves engagement on the Map, but as a rival inside existing pages. A standalone article is blocked (topics 370/370, concepts 370/370, re-measured with `count_section_files` at 10:2xZ) and is not needed. The driver's three questions get these answers:

1. **Does it address the argument from reason's claim about normativity?** Mostly not. It is a theory of the function and mechanism of reasoning. It explicitly sets aside whether an "intuitively good argument is truly a good argument" (p. 59), and its sympathetic critics note that formal theories of rationality are "not the focus" of the book (Chater & Oaksford 2018). It does hit two sub-claims the Map makes. The first is the evolutionary objection-and-reply at `topics/argument-from-reason` L157, whose reply (1) it defeats for logic and mathematics. The second is the Map's picture of the lone conscious reasoner tracking entailment, which the evidence on myside bias and selective laziness makes the exception.
2. **Where does it concede ground?** In five places, each verified in the BBS text (see [Concessions on both sides](#concessions)). It accepts Harman's distinction between the causal steps of an inference and the logical steps of an argument, which is close kin to Lewis's two senses of "because". It makes acceptance "an epistemic decision that we take at a personal level". It defines reasoning proper by awareness of an argument. It grants evaluation "an epistemic function". And it applies itself to its own authors.
3. **What would discriminate the two?** Nothing identified. Every behavioural datum the theory explains is predicted equally by a dualist who accepts its function claim. The argument from reason, as the Map states it, is "metaphysical, not behavioural" (`topics/argument-from-reason` L161), so this evidence can neither support nor undercut it. There is still a real asymmetry, an inference-to-the-best-explanation (IBE) form objection. The theory *predicts* the pattern of reasoning's successes and failures from its core, while the Map's account only accommodates that pattern. By the Map's own rule (`project/evidential-status-discipline` L122), a compatibility verdict does not discharge that objection.

**Tenet grade: compatible, non-discriminating** for Dualism and Bidirectional Interaction; no bearing on the other three. The theory's largest value for the Map is methodological: it supplies the mechanism behind the "public criticism" corrective at level 3 of the voids admission test. Its evidence also limits that corrective's reach. The gains are clearest where tasks "have a demonstrably valid solution" (p. 72), and like-minded groups polarise.

- **Key points (verbatim)**:
  - Thesis: "Our hypothesis is that the function of reasoning is argumentative. It is to devise and evaluate arguments intended to persuade. Reasoning so conceived is adaptive given the exceptional dependence of humans on communication and their vulnerability to misinformation." (p. 57)
  - Bias: "Skilled arguers, however, are not after the truth but after arguments supporting their views. This explains the notorious confirmation bias." (p. 57). The target article says "confirmation bias"; "myside bias" appears in the response (p. 99: "untrained participants should exhibit a myside bias") and is the term of the later literature.
  - Mechanism: reasoning is an "intuitive metarepresentational mechanism, a mechanism for representing possible reasons to accept a conclusion – that is, for representing arguments – and for evaluating their strength" (p. 58). "arguments are the output of one mechanism of intuitive inference among many that delivers intuitions about premise-conclusion relationships" (p. 59).
  - Inference vs argument: "As Gilbert Harman (1986) has justly argued, it is a common but costly mistake to confuse the causally and temporally related steps of an inference with the logically related steps of an argument." (pp. 58–59, across a page break)
  - Awareness: "People may be aware of having reached a certain conclusion – be aware, that is, of the output of an inferential process – but we claim that they are never aware of the process itself."   - Grounding left open: of the Cogito, "What is not at all obvious in this particular case are the reasons for accepting that this intuitively good argument is truly a good argument, and philosophers have been hotly debating the issue" (p. 59). Generally: "all arguments must ultimately be grounded in intuitive judgments that given conclusions follow from given premises." (p. 59)
  - Personal level: "If we accept a conclusion because of an argument in its favor that is intuitively strong enough, this acceptance is an epistemic decision that we take at a personal level." (p. 59)
  - Vigilance: "The task of epistemic vigilance is to evaluate communicator and the content of their messages in order to filter communicated information." (p. 60, citing Sperber et al. 2010)
  - Groups: on the Wason selection task, individual performance is "hovering around 10% of correct answers", but "when participants had to solve the task in groups, they reached the level of 80% of correct answers" (p. 63). "Most participants are willing to change their mind only once they have been thoroughly convinced that their initial answer was wrong" (p. 63). (Moshman & Geil's own abstract gives 9% vs 75%; quote theirs if the figure is used.)
  - Polarisation: Sunstein-style group polarisation "is specific to artificial contexts in which people debate even though they tend to agree in the first place. When group members disagree, discussions often lead to depolarization" (p. 63). "Taber and Lodge (2006) have demonstrated that, in the domain of politics, attitude polarization is most easily observed in participants who are most knowledgeable" (p. 67).
  - Asymmetry: the theory "maintains that there is an asymmetry between the production of arguments, which involves an intrinsic bias in favor of the opinions or decisions of the arguer whether they are sound or not, and the evaluation of arguments, which aims at distinguishing good arguments from bad ones and hence genuine information from misinformation" (p. 72).
  - Truth wins only where demonstrable: participants "concur in recognizing sound arguments. Since these tasks have a demonstrably valid solution, truth does indeed win. If we generalize to problems that do not have a provable solution, we should at least expect good arguments to win, even if this is not always sufficient for truth to win" (p. 72).
  - Darwin line: "But, for one Darwin, how many Paleys?" (p. 73), on motivated reasoning that happens to land on truth.

  - "we stress that reasoning is not only for convincing but also for evaluating arguments, and that as such it has an epistemic function" (abstract, p. 94).
  - "Our claim is that argumentation is the main function of reasoning" (p. 96). "We do not dispute that reasoning can be used for individual ratiocination." (p. 97)
  - Against De Neys and Stupple & Ball: "this indicates that reasoning is not geared towards pure logical validity, but that it takes into account other factors, such as believability" (p. 99).

## Major Positions

### The argumentative (interactionist) theory
- **Proponents**: Hugo Mercier, Dan Sperber; empirical programme with Trouche, Claidière, Landemore and others.
- **Core claim**: reasoning is one intuitive inference mechanism among many, specialised for representing and evaluating arguments. Its main function is to produce arguments that convince and to evaluate arguments others offer. Production is biased toward one's own side and lazy; evaluation is demanding. The truth-conducive unit is the group that disagrees.
- **Key arguments**: myside/confirmation bias reread as a design feature; reason-based choice; selective laziness; group gains on intellective tasks; polarisation in like-minded groups.
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral on Dualism; neutral on Bidirectional Interaction. It is a function-and-mechanism theory, silent on what realises the mechanism.

### Exaptation and deliberative-function rivals (within naturalism)
- **Proponents**: Evans; Frankish (co-option); Godfrey-Smith & Yegnashankaran (deliberative in function, dialogic in structure); Dutilh Novaes (sociocultural tuning, group-level).
- **Core claim**: argumentation may be the origin, but individual reflective reasoning is now a function too. M&S accept the possibility but deny it is the *main* function (response p. 97).
- **Relation to site tenets**: these rivals matter to the Map because they keep the individual reasoner as a locus of truth-seeking. The argument from reason's paradigm case lives here.

### The argument from reason (the Map's position)
- **Proponents**: Lewis, Reppert, Hasker, Plantinga (EAAN as a sibling); the Map's `topics/argument-from-reason` and `topics/consciousness-and-the-normativity-of-reason`.
- **Core claim**: if all belief formation is fully explained by non-rational physical causes, no belief is held *because* its grounds entail it. Physicalism then undercuts its own justification. Grasping a norm, as distinct from implementing it, needs consciousness.
- **Relation to the argumentative theory**: the two answer different questions. The theory answers *why reasoning exists and when it tracks validity*. The argument asks *what makes tracking validity rational rather than merely reliable*. The overlap lies in the Map's evolutionary reply and its phenomenological picture of reasoning (next section).

## The Three Questions {#three-questions}

### 1. Does the argumentative theory address the argument from reason?

Mostly it does not, by its authors' own framing. Three verified passages show where the theory stops:

- It grounds every argument in "intuitive judgments that given conclusions follow from given premises" (p. 59). The authority of those judgements is exactly what the argument from reason questions. The theory tells us when the judgements are triggered and when they are reliable. It does not say what makes them correct.
- Of the Cogito, it says that "the reasons for accepting that this intuitively good argument is truly a good argument" are "not at all obvious" and are debated by philosophers (p. 59). That remark is about one case, so **do not generalise it into "M&S leave validity to philosophers"**. The general point stands on the first bullet.
- Sympathetic reviewers name the gap. Oaksford says "an account of argument strength is required" (BBS p. 84). Chater & Oaksford note that formal theories of rationality are "not the focus of Mercier and Sperber's book" (2018). Sperber & Mercier's own answer is that the module "evaluates reasons on the basis of their metacognitive properties" (2018). That is an answer about the cues evaluation uses, not about the authority of the norm.

The theory does reach two parts of the Map's case:

- **The evolutionary objection-reply** (`topics/argument-from-reason` L157; parallels at `concepts/reasons-responsiveness` L92 and `topics/consciousness-and-the-normativity-of-reason` L105). Reply (1), that evolution "doesn't address non-survival-relevant beliefs (philosophy, mathematics, abstract reasoning)", fails for logic and mathematics. On the theory, selection for vigilance targets argument quality in whatever domain arguments are traded. The data on the selection task (Moshman & Geil 1998) and on demonstrative problems in large groups (Mercier & Claidière 2022) are domain-general. Reply (1) survives only for questions without demonstrable answers, philosophy and metaphysics among them. Reply (2), that reliability presupposes the truth norm, is untouched. The theory presupposes "good arguments" as the target of vigilance (p. 72) and does not analyse them.
- **The picture of reasoning.** The Map's examples are a lone reasoner who holds premises together and believes *because* the conclusion follows (`topics/argument-from-reason` L113; `phenomenology-of-intellectual-life`). On the evidence, that picture describes the least reliable case, production, where people "are biased and lazy" (Mercier 2016). The reliable case is evaluating someone else's argument amid disagreement. The argument from reason needs only *some* belief formed because valid, so this does not refute it. It does mean the Map's illustrations should draw on evaluation (being moved by another's good argument) rather than on self-generated inference, and should not imply that conscious reasoning is generally validity-tracking. The theory's authors put it bluntly: "reasoning is not geared towards pure logical validity" (response p. 99).

### 2. Where does each side concede ground? {#concessions}

**The argumentative theory concedes:**
1. **Lewis's distinction, in Harman's form.** "it is a common but costly mistake to confuse the causally and temporally related steps of an inference with the logically related steps of an argument" (pp. 58–59). This is close kin to the causal vs evidential "because" (`topics/consciousness-and-the-normativity-of-reason` §Two Senses of "Because"). The theory relocates the logical relation into the *content* of a represented argument, and treats the causal process as an intuitive inference about that content. The Map can say the theory accepts the distinction and offers a representational account of how logical relations become causally relevant. Whether representing a logical relation amounts to grasping it is the implementing-vs-grasping question. The theory does not address it.
2. **Personal-level epistemic agency.** "this acceptance is an epistemic decision that we take at a personal level" (p. 59).
3. **Awareness is constitutive of reasoning proper** (p. 58). This is awareness of an argument, in an access sense; the theory says nothing about phenomenal character. It denies awareness of the *process* (p. 58), which constrains how the Map describes "the work of tracing implications".
4. **An epistemic function.** Evaluation gives reasoning "an epistemic function" (response abstract, p. 94). Truth-sensitivity is real on the theory; it sits in the audience.
5. **Self-application without collapse** (p. 101). The authors grant they may have shown confirmation bias in arguing for the theory, and they rely on public criticism to correct it. This is a localised debunker of argument *production*, not a global debunker of reason, so it escapes the self-stultification charge the Map presses against global debunking. Its escape route is social reliabilism, which is the rival the Map's reliabilism reply already targets.

**The Map should concede:**
1. Reply (1) at `topics/argument-from-reason` L157 fails for logic and mathematics (above).
2. The theory explains, from its core, a body of data the Map's account does not predict: production bias, stringent evaluation, selective laziness, gains in disagreeing groups, polarisation in like-minded ones, belief bias. The Map's account can accommodate each finding but predicts none of them. This is an IBE-form objection of the kind `project/evidential-status-discipline` L122 says a compatibility reply does not discharge. Record it as open.
3. Selective laziness bears on the Map's introspective evidence. The *felt* force of an argument changes with whether the reasoner takes it to be their own: 56% and 58% rejected their own arguments once they believed them to be someone else's (Trouche et al. 2016). If phenomenal grasp is what binds us to valid conclusions, the Map owes an account of why the same argument's felt force flips with attributed ownership. One available reply is that grasping is an effortful act engaged in evaluation and withheld in production. That reply accommodates the finding without predicting it.

### 3. What would discriminate the two?

No discriminating test was identified. The evidence sorts as follows:

- **At the level of the argument's thesis**: none. Trouche et al. (2014) show argument *quality* changing minds where confidence does not. A dualist and a physicalist predict this equally, so it is not evidence for Bidirectional Interaction (see Guards).
- **At the level of the Map's auxiliary claim that grasping validity needs consciousness**: suppose validity-sensitive evaluation (rejecting invalid arguments, as in Trouche et al. 2016) ran without conscious access. Or suppose conscious load (DeWall et al. 2008) impaired production while sparing evaluation. Either result would test whether *access* to the argument is required. Neither would separate phenomenal from access roles, and a global-workspace physicalist predicts the same dependence. Baumeister, Masicampo & DeWall themselves read the conscious-dependence of logical reasoning as support for an interpersonal *function* of consciousness (BBS p. 74), not for dualism.
- **At the level of the explanandum "when does reasoning track validity?"**: the theory wins on loveliness (above). The Map's honest position is *compatible*, with the IBE challenge open.
- **What would move the Map's case**: the normativity question is conceptual, so no experiment discriminates it. Two developments would bear on the case's credibility. The first is a worked naturalist account of argument *strength* that analyses the "ought" in non-normative terms; Oaksford and Hahn's Bayesian account is the live candidate. The second is a dialogical theory of deduction that derives validity's authority from adversarial practice (Dutilh Novaes 2020). The first would press the Map's reliabilism reply directly; the second would press it on deduction. Neither was read in full here.

## Key Debates

### Main function or origin? (evolutionary claim)
- **Sides**: M&S (main function is argumentative) vs Evans, Frankish, Godfrey-Smith & Yegnashankaran (exaptation, co-option, deliberative function); Dogramaci (the evolutionary premise is unargued and false); Norman (intention-alignment, unread).
- **Core disagreement**: whether the benefit of persuading others to believe what you already believe is real and selectable.
- **Current state**: open. Sterelny finds "the evolutionary case persuasive" while doubting the modular framework.

### Is myside bias an adaptation?
- **Sides**: M&S (feature of production) vs Dutilh Novaes 2018 (questions "myside bias as an optimum/adaptation"); Stanovich (domain-specific, unrelated to intelligence, a blind spot for the cognitively able).
- **Current state**: the bias is robust; its adaptive status is contested.

### Do groups correct the bias outside the lab?
- **Sides**: M&S, Mercier & Landemore, Mercier & Claidière (yes, in disagreeing groups, clearest on demonstrable problems) vs the SEP's caution about real-world interference, and self-persuasion in the field (Schwardmann et al. 2022).
- **Current state**: well supported for intellective tasks; uncertain for factual and value questions; and no evidence was found for existential or metaphysical questions.

### Modularity
- **Sides**: Sperber & Mercier vs Sterelny, Dutilh Novaes, Chater & Oaksford (*Mind & Language* 2018). This debate does not bear on the Map's question. Leave it out of any fold.
