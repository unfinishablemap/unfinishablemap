## Executive Summary

**Bleuler coined the term, but not for inaction.** *Doppelte Buchführung* first appears in Bleuler's 1911 monograph (p. 45, in the section on intact functions). It describes patients who "know the real state of affairs as well as the falsified one" and answer from either, or both at once (1950 translation, p. 56). Its other two uses concern orientation (p. 47) and a "multiple bookkeeping" of personality (p. 120). Bleuler's famous inaction passage ("Kings and Emperors, Popes, and Redeemers engage, for the most part, in quite banal work", 1950 p. 129) sits in a different chapter and does not use the term. The later literature joins the two: Bortolotti and Broome (2012) treat behavioural inertness as "evidence for the phenomenon of 'double bookkeeping'". Louis Sass revived the concept (Sass 1994; Sass 2014; credit per Porcher 2024), and Parnas, Urfer-Parnas and Stephensen (2021) extend it across schizophrenia-spectrum phenomena.

**Double bookkeeping counts against unrestricted doxasticism but establishes no rival.** The argument from action guidance runs: beliefs guide action; some delusions do not; so those delusions are not beliefs. Doxasticists answer that inertness has motivational causes (Bortolotti & Broome 2012; Bayne & Pacherie 2005, p. 185), and Bleuler himself traced it to affect and apathy. They also answer that inertness is not general. In one admission study, "Half of the sample reported that they had acted at least once in accordance with their delusions" (Wessely et al. 1993). That record counts harder against the imagination account, which Bayne and Pacherie say faces "a problem of over-activity" (p. 173). Porcher (2024) states the balance: the argument can "at best" undermine "the generality of a doxastic account" without establishing a general alternative.

**What Factor 2 is a deficit in depends on the attitude reading.** The Capgras page's description of Factor 2 as "a separate impairment of belief evaluation" (L69) is the two-factor theorists' own, doxastic statement. On Currie's account, where some delusions are imaginings misidentified as beliefs, the second deficit would be a failure to monitor one's own attitude. On Schwitzgebel's in-between reading, the question partly loses its determinate answer. The Capgras scope rule already sets Factor 2 aside, so **no Capgras edit is needed**.

## The Research Questions, Answered

1. **Who coined "double bookkeeping", and in what context?** Eugen Bleuler, *Dementia praecox oder Gruppe der Schizophrenien* (1911). The raw German has three occurrences in the OCR text: "in vielen Beziehungen eine doppelte Buchführung haben" (p. 45); "Überhaupt tritt die doppelte Buchführung nirgends so hervor wie bei der Orientierung" (p. 47); "Auch die Person hat ihre mehrfache Buchführung" (p. 120). Zinkin's 1950 translation renders the first two as "a kind of 'double-entry bookkeeping'" (p. 56) and "Nowhere does the 'double-entry bookkeeping' stand out more prominently than in orientation" (p. 59), and the third as "multiple bookkeeping" (p. 147). The context is the chapter on intact simple functions, where Bleuler argues that orientation and registration survive. The term covers what patients know and how they answer, not how they act. The coining paragraph's example is person-misidentification: the physician "is now here as Dr. N.," and "at other times he becomes the former lover" (1950, p. 56). Parnas et al. (2021) and Porcher (2024) say he used the term again in the 1916 *Lehrbuch*; this note did not check that.
   **Development.** Sass (1994) built a solipsistic reading of Schreber on it: patients "often seem to live in two parallel but separate worlds: consensual reality and the realm of their hallucinations and delusions" (p. 21), and the two worlds "differ according to their felt ontological status" (pp. 42–43). Sass's 2014 chapter "Delusion and Double Book-Keeping" is metadata-only here. Parnas, Urfer-Parnas and Stephensen (2021) extend double bookkeeping beyond delusion to hallucination, insight, *Anderssein* and the prodrome. They trace it to "an instability in the affective ('auto-affection') articulation of selfhood" (abstract).
2. **The doxastic debate.** The SEP entry calls the doxastic conception "dominant among psychologists and psychiatrists" (§4.2). Its defenders are Bayne and Pacherie (2005), Bortolotti (2009; 2012, "modest doxasticism") and Miyazono (2018, via SEP). Bortolotti (2012) concedes that "some delusional phenomena are not belief states (e.g., inserted thoughts that are not endorsed at all or delusional moods) or that they are borderline cases", and holds that "most delusions, typical delusions, are sufficiently endorsed to be beliefs". The rivals are of four kinds. On the imagination account, some delusions are imaginings misidentified as beliefs (Currie 2000; Currie & Jureidini 2001; Currie & Ravenscroft 2002). Egan's (2009) "bimagination" is a hybrid attitude. On the in-between reading, delusion is "belief gone half-mad" (Schwitzgebel 2012). On the acceptance reading, delusions are acceptances (Frankish 2009). Phenomenologists (Sass; Gallagher 2009; Parnas et al. 2021) deny that schizophrenic delusions are beliefs about the shared world at all. **No source was found in which Gendler applies alief to delusion** (two WebSearch probes; SEP Delusion has zero "Gendler"/"alief" hits). The alief mapping in the fold is the Map's construction.
3. **What each view makes of Factor 2.** See the [positions table](#positions). Only the doxastic answer is the two-factor theorists' own ("a failure of normal belief evaluation", Langdon & Coltheart 2000, via the sibling note). The other rows are this note's inference from each view's account of what goes wrong. None of these authors was found mapping their view onto Factor 2.
4. **Does double bookkeeping tell for or against the doxastic view?** Against its *generality*, and not decisively. The inaction premise fails as a universal (Wessely et al. 1993; Förstl et al. 1991). The doxastic side has a motivational explanation, which Bleuler anticipated: since "the affects, and with them the drives do not have to correspond to the delusion, it follows that the reaction to the delusion is frequently also inadequate" (1950, pp. 128–129). The action record hurts the imagination account more (Bayne & Pacherie 2005, p. 173). Folk ascription sides with belief: assertion is "more powerful than even a robust and consistent track record of non-verbal behaviour" (Rose, Buckwalter & Turri 2014, abstract). The phenomenologists read double bookkeeping as against *both* the doxastic and the imagination accounts. Parnas et al. say the delusional reality "should not be considered as some kind of fiction, fantasy or imagination". **This note's reading of Bleuler:** his own usage (two orientations co-held and switched "according to the circumstances") fits an in-between or compartmentalised profile at least as well as "held but not acted on".

## Originators {#originators}

- **"Double bookkeeping" / *doppelte Buchführung***: Bleuler 1911, p. 45 (first of three uses). The term is not Sass's, Bortolotti's or Parnas's. Zinkin's English adds "double-entry" and "a kind of". The modern application to behavioural inertness is an extension, not Bleuler's definition. Parnas et al. (2021) say Pinel and Esquirol observed the phenomenon without the term.
- **Revival**: credited to Sass by Porcher (2024): "Louis Sass, who is responsible for the revival of interest in double bookkeeping". That is Porcher's assessment; mark it as his.
- **"Doxastic account of delusions"** (the harvest found 0/0 hits): Bortolotti (2011, abstract): "The doxastic account of delusions is the view that delusions are genuine instances of belief." SEP uses "doxastic conception"; Bayne and Pacherie's title says "doxastic conception"; Porcher says "doxasticism".
- **"Metacognitive model/account"**: Bayne and Pacherie's (2005, p. 165) label for Currie's view: "Because Currie's account involves reference to deficits in the monitoring of mental states we will call it the metacognitive model." Currie's own term, as they report it, is "cognitive hallucinations". His 2000 abstract calls Frith's "metarepresentational" formulation "unhelpful", so do not call Currie's view metarepresentational.
- **"Bimagination"**: Egan 2009, p. 263 (Google Books snippet; index entry "Bimagination, 263, 270").
- **"Mad belief"**: Schwitzgebel 2012, adapting Lewis's (1980) "mad pain". His target is Bortolotti's 2009 book, which he cites as 2010.
- **Two-factor model**: the Macquarie group, formalised 2000–2001 (see the sibling note's Originators section). Do not credit it to the double-bookkeeping literature.

### Parnas, Urfer-Parnas & Stephensen (2021), "Double bookkeeping and schizophrenia spectrum: divided unified phenomenal consciousness", *European Archives of Psychiatry and Clinical Neuroscience* 271(8), 1513–1523
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/s00406-020-01185-0 (PMC8563555; full text raw via NCBI BioC)
- **Type**: Clinical-phenomenological paper
- **Key points**: Bleuler "did not provide any conceptual elaboration of this phenomenon or its possible mechanisms" (abstract). "Double bookkeeping is not simply a reflection of harboring conflicting attitudes." Double bookkeeping is "most clearly observed and informative in non-acute, stable patients or in patients in the initial stages of their illness. In the acute psychosis with flamboyant symptomatology, the patients tend to conflate their psychotic world with the shared world and may enact their psychotic experiences". "Schizophrenic delusions are not beliefs about worldly matters, rather they concern a different realm transcending the shared-social world." By contrast, "delusions in delusional disorder (paranoia) possess a clearly mundane, 'empirical' character". "Lack of insight is perhaps the domain of experience where double bookkeeping is most clearly manifest." The paper uses Bleuler's Kings passage (their ref. 3, p. 129) as its epigraph.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The title's "phenomenal consciousness" is psychopathological description. Do not enlist it for dualism.
- **Scope caution**: the paper ties double bookkeeping to the schizophrenia spectrum. Its application to neurological monothematic delusion (Capgras) is not theirs.

### Bortolotti & Broome (2012), "Affective dimensions of the phenomenon of double bookkeeping in delusions", *Emotion Review* 4(2), 187–191
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1177/1754073911430115 (abstract raw, Crossref)
- **Key points**: "It has been argued that schizophrenic delusions are 'behaviourally inert.' ### Bayne & Pacherie (2005), "In defence of the doxastic conception of delusions", *Mind & Language* 20(2), 163–188
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0268-1064.2005.00281.x (HAL ijn_00353586 via Wayback, journal pagination)
- **Key points**:

### Empirical record (abstracts raw)
- **Wessely et al. (1993)**, "Acting on delusions. I: Prevalence", *BJP* 163(1), 69–76, https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.163.1.69. The sample was 83 consecutively admitted deluded subjects; informant interviews covered 59 of them. "Half of the sample reported that they had acted at least once in accordance with their delusions." Informant data also suggested "half". "Violent behaviour in response to delusions was uncommon." Self-reports and informant reports were unlinked. "Actions associated with abnormal beliefs are more common than has been suggested."
- **Buchanan et al. (1993)**, "Acting on delusions. II", *BJP* 163(1), 77–81, https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.163.1.77. Self-described acting went with being aware of supporting evidence and with "feeling sad, frightened or anxious as a consequence of the delusion".
- **Förstl et al. (1991)**, *Psychological Medicine* 21(4), 905–910, https://doi.org/10.1017/s0033291700029895. There were 260 case reports of misidentification syndromes (174 Capgras), and "Forty-six of the patients were reported to show violent behaviour".

## Major Positions {#positions}

| View | Proponents | What a delusion is | What double bookkeeping shows | What Factor 2 would be a deficit in |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Doxastic (modest) | Bayne & Pacherie 2005; Bortolotti 2009, 2011, 2012; Bortolotti & Broome 2012 | A belief, typically; some delusional phenomena are not, or are borderline (Bortolotti 2012) | Belief plus failed motivation (avolition, affect, surroundings, fear of hospitalisation), or context-sensitive belief; inertness is not general | Belief evaluation. This is the two-factor theorists' own statement |
| Imagination ("metacognitive", B&P's label) | Currie 2000; Currie & Jureidini 2001; Currie & Ravenscroft 2002 | For *some* delusions, an imagining misidentified as a belief | What imagination predicts; the action record is its "problem of over-activity" | *Inferred*: monitoring of one's own attitude (loss of the sense of agency, after Frith). B&P doubt such a deficit can be content-specific |
| Hybrid | Egan 2009 | "Bimagination": features of both | Intermediate circumscription | Not addressed in the snippets read |
| In-between (dispositional) | Schwitzgebel 2012; cf. Tumulty 2011 | Neither determinately believed nor not | The mixed dispositional profile itself | *Inferred*: partly indeterminate, since "belief evaluation" presupposes a determinate belief |
| Acceptance | Frankish 2009 | An acceptance (a policy-level state) | Not read beyond the abstract | *Inferred*: a policy-level failure; unread |
| Phenomenological | Sass 1994, 2014; Gallagher 2009; Parnas et al. 2021 | For schizophrenia, a different "reality" with its own felt ontological status, neither belief about the shared world nor imagination | The core phenomenon: two realms, co-inhabited | The question "targets the wrong world" (Gallagher, via Porcher) |

**Relation to the tenets**: no position bears on Dualism. Each describes attitude-individuation in terms a physicalist can accept. **No position makes consciousness causally idle.** On every view the delusional content is asserted and defended, and on all but the most inert cases it is acted on.

## Key Debates

### The argument from action guidance
- **Sides**: Currie and co-authors, Sass, Tumulty, Schwitzgebel (each differently) against Bayne & Pacherie, Bortolotti, Bortolotti & Broome, Miyazono.
- **Core disagreement**: whether action guidance is constitutive of belief, and whether delusional inaction is excused by motivation or context.
- **Current state**: unresolved. The prevailing balance (Porcher 2024; SEP §§4.1–4.2) is that the argument limits the generality of doxasticism and favours heterogeneity over any rival.

### Does double bookkeeping belong to schizophrenia or to delusion as such?
- **Sides**: the phenomenological literature ties it to the schizophrenia spectrum and contrasts it with mundane, acted-on paranoid delusions (Parnas et al. 2021). The analytic literature presses the same inaction objection for Capgras (B&P 2005, p. 165; Schwitzgebel 2012; Bortolotti 2012).
- **Current state**: unaddressed as a question. **Writers should not assume that Bleuler's or Parnas's double bookkeeping applies to neurological Capgras.** The analytic authors make that transfer, and it should be attributed to them.

## Relation to Site Perspective (for the fold)

**Dualism (Tenet 1)**: nothing evidential. Whether a delusion is a belief, an imagining or an in-between state is a question about attitude individuation that physicalists answer in all these ways.
**Bidirectional Interaction (Tenet 3)**: no support and no concession. The fold sharpens the alief article's back-end speculation: a belief that fails to steer for want of motivation is not a case of a guide held in check, so inaction cannot be counted as evidence of control. On every view considered, the delusional content figures in what the patient says and often in what he does. That is ordinary causation on any metaphysics.
**Occam's Razor Has Limits (Tenet 5)**: a caution on the doxastic default. The DSM and most clinicians' "false belief" is the simple classification. Double bookkeeping shows why it is contested, but the parsimony of any one rival is no reason to adopt it either (Porcher's heterogeneity point).
**Minimal Quantum Interaction (Tenet 2), No Many Worlds (Tenet 4)**: not invoked.
