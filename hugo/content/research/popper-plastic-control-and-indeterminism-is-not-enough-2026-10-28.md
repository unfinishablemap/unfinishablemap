Popper's texts do grant the structural half. In the first Darwin Lecture (8 November 1977; *Dialectica* 1978; reprinted 1987) he wrote, with a footnote pointing back to *Objective Knowledge* pp. 226–29 as the view he was revising: "I have changed my mind on this issue. ## Executive Summary

Popper's answer to the chance-versus-determinism dilemma has three layers, written across seventeen years. (1) In the 1965 Compton lecture he states the dilemma's third horn as a requirement ("something intermediate in character between perfect chance and perfect determinism", p. 228), adopts "Compton's postulate of freedom" (freedom as "a subtle interplay between something almost random or haphazard, and something like a restrictive or selective control", pp. 231–232), names the required control "plastic" as against "cast-iron" (p. 232), and then builds the positive theory: every organism is "a hierarchical system of plastic controls—as a system of clouds controlled by clouds" (p. 245), whose subsystems make "almost random" trial movements that higher controls suppress by error-elimination; the soap bubble is his existence proof that softly controlled physical systems exist (pp. 248–250); consciousness is "just one of many interacting kinds of control" (p. 251), "produced by physical states; yet it controls them to a considerable extent" (p. 251). His own verdict on quantum routes stands in the conclusion: the attempt to use quantum indeterminacy "leads to chance rather than freedom, and to snap-decisions rather than deliberate decisions" (p. 255). A 1974 addition concedes he has "not solved the problem of how such interaction takes place" and suspects it "insoluble—not only for interaction between mental and physical states, but more generally" (p. 255). (2) The 1973 afterword restates the lecture's title thesis — the phrase "indeterminism is not enough" is already in the lecture (pp. 230, 232) — as a claim about worlds: "World 1 is open towards World 2, just as World 2 is open towards World 3" [GB p. 129]; "Indeterminism is not enough: to understand human freedom we need more; we need the openness of World 1" [GB p. 130]. The argument for that openness runs through World 3 (the bulldozer argument "clearly depends on World 3" [GB p. 118]), the same dependence Gray found in *The Self and Its Brain*. (3) In 1977 Popper withdrew the snap-decision objection in its general form: "A choice process may be a selection process, and the selection may be from some repertoire of random events, without being random in its turn" (1987, p. 147), offering it as "a promising solution to one of our most vexing problems, and one by downward causation".

Does plastic control escape the penny-toss objection? It answers the **grain** complaint by construction (a decision is a maturing sequence of trials and eliminations, so no single event is the decision), and it moves the **Schlick** complaint up one level rather than dissolving it: the trials are "almost random" and the controls that select among them are themselves either determined or not; Popper leaves that regress open, and Frederick (2010) presses it ("it is freedom that is the chance element"). The Map's selection posit is structurally Popper-1977 (non-random selection over a random repertoire) with two commitments Popper never made: a quantum locus for the repertoire, and a Born-preserving default. On the second, Popper is a witness for the opposite horn: in 1977 he objected that probabilistic laws fix frequencies that a law-respecting selector could not alter, and wanted World 1 open to interference, which is exactly the deviation horn of the Map's [bias-without-deviation dilemma](/positions/quantum-interface/#mechanism-debt).

## Key Sources

### Popper, "Of Clouds and Clocks" (1965; *Objective Knowledge* ch. 6)
- **URL**: raw OCR of the revised Clarendon edition on disk (see header); the 2026-09-17 note names the the-rathouse.com scan as the original download.
- **Type**: Lecture / book chapter (second Arthur Holly Compton Memorial Lecture, Washington University, 21 April 1965).
- **Page-anchored content** (all grep-verified between running heads):
  - **p. 228** — penny-toss verdict; "What we need for understanding rational human behaviour—and indeed, animal behaviour—is something intermediate in character between perfect chance and perfect determinism—something intermediate between perfect clouds and perfect clocks." Hume's and Schlick's thesis "that there cannot exist anything intermediate between chance and determinism seems to me not only highly dogmatic (not to say doctrinaire) but clearly absurd".
  - **pp. 229–230** — Compton's Yale-engagement passage (*Freedom of Man*, pp. 53 f., n. 42); "Compton shows here very beautifully that mere physical indeterminism is not enough. We have to be indeterminists, to be sure; but we also must try to understand how men, and perhaps animals, can be 'influenced' or 'controlled' by such things as aims, or purposes, or rules, or agreements." (p. 230)
  - **pp. 230–231** — the two problems: *Compton's problem* ("the influence of the universe of abstract meanings upon human behaviour", p. 230) and *Descartes's problem* ("how can it be that such things as states of mind—volitions, feelings, expectations—influence or control the physical movements of our limbs?", p. 231). Popper ranks Compton's problem "more important than the classical body-mind problem" (p. 231). **n. 43** (p. 231): "like Compton, I am almost a Cartesian, in so far as I reject the thesis of the physical completeness of all living organisms … I am, however, less of a Cartesian than Compton: I am even less attracted than he was by the master-switch models".
  - **pp. 231–232** — "Compton's postulate of freedom": "the solution must explain freedom; and it must also explain how freedom is not just chance but, rather, the result of a subtle interplay between something almost random or haphazard, and something like a restrictive or selective control—such as an aim or a standard—though certainly not a cast-iron control." Then (p. 232): "the idea of a 'plastic control', as I shall call it in contradistinction to a 'cast-iron control'."
  - **p. 232** (§XIII) — "the existing indeterministic theories are unsatisfactory. They are indeterministic; but we know that indeterminism is not enough, and it is not clear how they escape from Schlick's objection, or whether they conform to Compton's postulate of freedom plus control." The title phrase of the 1973 essay is therefore 1965 vintage.
  - **pp. 232–234** — master-switch / tiny-baby critique (already on the Map page); **p. 234**: considered decisions "are reached by a kind of maturing process which is not well represented by the master-switch model", and deliberation "always works by trial and error or, more precisely, by the method of trial and of error-elimination".
  - **p. 239** (§XVI) — first use of the term in the positive theory: the higher functions of language "do not replace the lower ones … but they establish a kind of plastic control over them—a control with feed-back." Critical arguments "are a means of control: they are a means of eliminating errors, a means of selection."
  - **pp. 240–241** (§XVII, solution to Compton's problem) — "the control of ourselves and of our actions by our theories and purposes is a plastic control. We are not forced to submit ourselves to the control of our theories, for we can discuss them critically, and we can reject them freely" (pp. 240–241); "the control is far from one-sided. Not only do our theories control us, but we can control our theories (and even our standards): there is a kind of feed-back here." (p. 241)
  - **p. 242** (§XVIII) — the general theory: "a certain view of evolution as a growing hierarchical system of plastic controls, and of a certain view of organisms as incorporating—or in the case of man, evolving exosomatically—this growing hierarchical system of plastic controls." Mutations "may be interpreted as more or less accidental trial-and-error gambits, and 'natural selection' as one way of controlling them by error-elimination." Twelve theses follow (pp. 242–244), with the schema P₁ → TS → EE → P₂ and its multiple-trials form (thesis 8, p. 243).
  - **p. 245** (§XIX) — "Each organism can be regarded as a hierarchical system of plastic controls—as a system of clouds controlled by clouds. The controlled subsystems make trial-and-error movements which are partly suppressed and partly restrained by the controlling system." Example: posture maintained by "almost random" muscle contractions corrected by error-elimination, "by more or less the same method by which an automatic pilot keeps an aircraft steadily on its course." **n. 55**: the method "does not operate with completely chance-like or random trials (as has been sometimes suggested), even though the trials may look pretty random; there must be at least an 'after-effect'"; the organism "establishes controls which suppress or eliminate, or at least reduce the frequency of, certain possible trials".
  - **pp. 248–250** (§XXII, existence of plastic controls) — "Can we show that plastic controls exist? Are there inorganic physical systems in nature which may be taken as examples or as physical models of plastic controls?" (p. 248). He wants "a simple physical model of Peircean indeterminism; a purely physical system resembling a very cloudy cloud in heat motion, controlled by some other cloudy clouds" (p. 248). Spectrum: uncontrolled gas (left), gas in an iron cylinder ("a 'cast-iron' control", p. 249), the soap bubble in the middle, "a very primitive and in many respects an excellent example or model of a Peircean system and of a 'soft' kind of plastic control" (p. 249): two clouds controlling each other, "the control is mutual; it is plastic, and of a feed-back character" (p. 249). Limit of the analogy (p. 250): "at least in some organisms, molecular fluctuations are apparently amplified and so used to release trial-and-error movements. At any rate, amplifiers seem to play important roles in all organisms … Yet there are no amplifiers in the soap bubble." Conclusion (p. 250): "natural physical cloud-like systems which are plastically and softly controlled by other cloud-like systems do exist."
  - **pp. 250–252** (§XXIII, Descartes's problem) — consciousness "grows from small beginnings" (p. 250) and gains significance "when it begins to anticipate possible ways of reacting" (p. 251). "Consciousness, from this point of view, appears as just one of many interacting kinds of control; and if we remember the control systems incorporated for example in books … then consciousness can hardly be said to be the highest control system in the hierarchy." (p. 251) "Consciousness in turn is, we may conjecture, produced by physical states; yet it controls them to a considerable extent." (p. 251) The upshot: "our mental states control (some of) our physical movements, and that there is some give-and-take, some feed-back, and so some interaction, between mental activity and the other functions of the organism. The control will again be of the 'plastic' kind" (pp. 251–252), with the pianist's body that "does not always do what we want it to do" (p. 252). Then the states-not-substances passage already on the Map page (p. 252, n. 62).
  - **pp. 254–255** (conclusion) — "it is unsatisfactory to look upon the world as a closed physical system—whether a strictly deterministic system or a system in which whatever is not strictly determined is simply due to chance: on such a view of the world human creativeness and human freedom can only be illusions. The attempt to make use of quantum-theoretical indeterminacy is also unsatisfactory, because it leads to chance rather than freedom, and to snap-decisions rather than deliberate decisions. I have therefore offered here a different view of the world—one in which the physical world is an open system." (pp. 254–255) He calls his theory "too humdrum and too speculative at the same time" (p. 255).
  - **p. 255, "(Added 1974.)"** — he retracts the phrase "immediate solution" to the body-mind problem: his discussion "gives good evolutionary (or functional) reasons in favour of interaction. Of course, I have not solved the problem of how such interaction takes place; and indeed I suspect that this problem is insoluble—not only for interaction between mental and physical states, but more generally. For while, for example, we know that electrical charges repel one another, we have no 'ultimate explanation' … of how they do it, even if we accept Maxwell's theory. We do not have any general theory of causality".
- **Tenet alignment**: Aligns with Bidirectional Interaction and the denial of closure; neutral-to-hostile on Minimal Quantum Interaction (he grants a quantum locus only for snap-decisions, p. 228, and for "molecular fluctuations … amplified", p. 250); aligns with Occam's Razor Has Limits in spirit (his "tiny baby" jibe is an anti-parsimony move: a smaller interface is not thereby a better theory).

### Popper, "Indeterminism Is Not Enough: An Afterword" (1973; *The Open Universe* Addendum 1, pp. 113–130)
- **URL**: https://books.google.co.uk/books?id=c-utnssYgaQC (search-within); original: *Encounter* 40(4): 20–26 (not reached; unz.com 403).
- **Type**: Essay.
- **Structure** [GB p. viii, contents]: "The Worlds 1, 2, and 3. The Reality of the Three Worlds. The Reality of Worlds 1 and 2. The Reality and Partial Autonomy of World 3. The Human Situation and the Natural World."
- **Snippet-verified content**:
  - **[GB p. 113]** opening: "My topic here is human freedom. By human freedom I mean what has usually been called 'free will'. I shall however avoid the term 'will', lest discussing it sidetrack us into sterile problems of terminology." He restricts himself "to questions of the freedom to create" works of art and "explanatory theories in science", setting moral freedom aside. He refers the reader to the Compton lecture as "readily available".
  - **[GB pp. 117–118]** the bulldozer argument for the reality of Worlds 1 and 2: "my second and main argument proceeds very differently. It starts from the assertion that World 3 objects, such as theories, do in fact strongly interact with the physical World 1" (p. 117); the chain runs from World 3 plans through "the World 2 planning by the human mind" to "the World 1 of the human brain, which in turn acts upon our limbs which steer the bulldozers" (pp. 117–118); and "The effectiveness of this argument clearly depends on World 3. If World 3 exists and is at least in part autonomous, and if, further, plans in World 3 do affect World 1, then it seems to me inescapable that there also …" (p. 118; snippet ends). He is "not a monist but a pluralist" (p. 117).
  - **[GB p. 122]** three emergences that Hume "would have called, grudgingly, a miracle": life, "a World 2 of animal consciousness, of feelings of joy and of pain", and "the emergence of the human brain, and of the human mind and of human reason".
  - **[GB p. 126]** "what we want to understand is not only how we may act unpredictably and in a chancelike fashion, but how we can act deliberately and rationally. The famous probabilistic constancy of such chance events as the posting of letters bearing no address may be an interesting curiosity, but it has no similarity whatever to the problem of the freedom to …" and, same page: "Returning now to quantum mechanics, I want to point out that the indeterminism of a dice-playing God, or of probabilistic laws, fails to …" (snippet ends). Also p. 126: the introduction of World 3 "allows us to prove not only the indeterministic character of this universe, but its essential openness or incompleteness."
  - **[GB p. 128]** the music passage (full text carried by Frederick 2010, p. 25, cited there as 1982a, 128): mere indeterminism "makes human creativity a matter of sheer chance"; the creation of music "has to be explained at least partly in terms of the influence of other music … and, most important, in terms of the inner structure, the internal laws and restrictions, which play such a role in music and in all other World 3 phenomena—laws and restrictions whose absorption (and whose occasional defiance) are immensely important for the musician's creativity." The GB snippet verifies the tail from "restrictions, which play such a role" to "creativity. Thus our freedom and"; the head is Frederick-carried.
  - **[GB p. 129]** "his mind—his World 2—acts upon the physical World 1. Thus World 1 is open towards World 2, just as World 2 is open towards World 3. This is of fundamental importance; for it shows that nature, or the universe to which we belong, and which contains as parts the Worlds 1, 2, and 3 …" Also p. 129: "the openness of World 3 is a consequence of Gödel's theorem that axiomatized arithmetic is not completable".
  - **[GB p. 130]** "human freedom is indeed part of nature, but it transcends nature—at least as it existed before the emergence of human language and of critical thought, and of human knowledge. Indeterminism is not enough: to understand human freedom we need more; we need the openness of World 1 …" (snippet ends; the continuation presumably "towards World 2, and of World 2 towards World 3" is **not verified**). And: "Thus we live in an open universe. We could not make this discovery before there was human knowledge. But once we have made it there is no reason to think that the openness depends exclusively upon the existence of human knowledge."
- **What the afterword does and does not contain**: no snippet for "plastic control", "snap", "amplif", "Compton" (beyond the p. 113 reference to the lecture), "Heisenberg", "downward" — but the index is sparse (see header), so treat as unverified absence. Frederick's reading (p. 23) is that the afterword "avoids discussing free will and moral responsibility" and focuses on creative freedom; that matches the p. 113 snippet.
- **Tenet alignment**: The openness thesis aligns with Bidirectional Interaction; its *argument* (World 3 → World 2 → World 1) is the route the Map's framework cannot use (see the Popper page §Why the Argument Cannot Be Borrowed).

### Popper, "Natural Selection and the Emergence of Mind" (Darwin Lecture, 8 Nov 1977; *Dialectica* 1978; 1987 reprint pp. 139–155)
- **URL**: https://www.informationphilosopher.com/solutions/philosophers/popper/natural_selection_and_the_emergence_of_mind.html (full text, aggregator); verification: Google Books search-within on id `QnFiTrCzg5oC` (1987 reprint), p. 147.
- **The retraction (1987, p. 147; §2)** — verbatim, the aggregator's inserted sentence removed: "The selection of a kind of behavior out of a randomly offered repertoire may be an act of choice, even an act of free will. I am an indeterminist; and in discussing indeterminism I have often regretfully pointed out that quantum indeterminacy does not seem to help us;¹¹ for the amplification of something like, say, radioactive disintegration processes would not lead to human action or even animal action, but only to random movements. I have changed my mind on this issue.12 A choice process may be a" (both p. 147). Footnotes (aggregator-carried): n. 11 "Cp. my *Objective Knowledge*, chapter 6, pp. 226–29."; n. 12 "See also p. 540 of J. C. Eccles and K. R. Popper, *The Self and Its Brain* (1977)."
- **Downward causation (same section, aggregator-carried)**: "We may speak of downward causation whenever a higher structure operates causally upon its substructure." Credited to "Donald Campbell and Roger Sperry" (n. 10: Campbell 1974 in Ayala & Dobzhansky, *Studies in the Philosophy of Biology*, pp. 179–86; Sperry 1969, 1973). Mechanism: the "molecular chaos" of particle motions "provides, as it were, the opening for the higher-level structure to interfere. A random movement is accepted when it fits into the higher level structure; otherwise it is rejected." And: "the chancelike character of mutations, which may go back to quantum indeterminacy, explains how the abstract invariances of the environment … can, by selection, have a downward effect on the concrete living organism".
- **Reading**: the retraction is general (a "repertoire of random events"), and the only quantum attribution in the passage is for *mutations*. Popper does not say in 1977 that human choice selects among quantum events; he says selection over randomness is a coherent third category, which is what the 1965 lecture had denied of master-switch models. Section 3 of the lecture ("Huxley's Problem") is the anti-epiphenomenalist argument the Map page already flags via Gray.

### Frederick, D. (2010). "Popper and Free Will." *Studia Philosophica Estonica* 3(1): 21–38.
- **URL**: https://ojs.utlib.ee/index.php/spe/article/download/spe.2010.3.1.02/9345/12981 (pdftotext, read in full). DOI in the PDF's link annotations: 10.12697/spe.2010.3.1.03 (the download URL carries .02; record the DOI from the publisher page before citing).
- **Key points**: reconstructs Popper's view as indeterminism + openness of physical laws to mental interference + plastic control by World 3 (pp. 22–24); reports Popper's two objections to Compton-type models and the 1977 retraction of the first (p. 23); then argues the account "never really gets to grips with the problem" (p. 24): capricious and snap decisions would be random on Popper's criterion, and even deliberated decisions are guided but not determined by World 3, so "it is freedom that is the chance element" (p. 26) and Hobart's and Russell's objections stand. He proposes repairs via Popper's propensity theory and Hornsby's analysis of action.
- **Tenet alignment**: Frederick takes property dualism "for ease of exposition" and says the free-will problem is independent of the mind-body problem (p. 23); his criticism is the luck objection in Popperian dress and applies to the Map's selector as much as to Popper's hierarchy.

## Major Positions

### Popper 1965: plastic control as a hierarchy of trial-and-error controls
- **Core claim**: freedom is "a subtle interplay" of near-random trials and selective, non-cast-iron control (pp. 231–232); organisms are hierarchies of such controls (pp. 242, 245); consciousness is one control level among several and is produced by, yet controls, physical states (p. 251); the physical world is "an open system" (p. 255).
- **Key arguments**: Compton's engagement (meanings control behaviour); language-function hierarchy (§§XIV–XVII); the soap-bubble existence proof (§XXII); the evolutionary-functional argument for interaction (§XXIII; restated as "good evolutionary (or functional) reasons" in 1974).
- **Relation to site tenets**: supplies the Map's Horn 3 structure without a quantum locus and without a mechanism; concedes (1974) that the how of interaction is unsolved and perhaps insoluble.

### Popper 1973: the openness of World 1 to World 2
- **Core claim**: indeterminism is necessary and insufficient; what freedom needs is causal openness of World 1 to World 2 and of World 2 to World 3 [GB pp. 129–130].
- **Key arguments**: the bulldozer argument [GB pp. 117–118]; Gödelian openness of World 3 [GB p. 129]; the three emergences [GB p. 122].
- **Relation to site tenets**: the conclusion is the Bidirectional Interaction tenet; the argument runs through World 3 and is unavailable to the Map (Gray's diagnosis, already on the page, now confirmed from Popper's own text: "The effectiveness of this argument clearly depends on World 3").

### Popper 1977: selection over a random repertoire
- **Core claim**: "A choice process may be a selection process, and the selection may be from some repertoire of random events, without being random in its turn" (1987, p. 147).
- **Relation to site tenets**: the Map's selection posit in Popper's words, with downward causation as the category. The Map adds the quantum locus (Minimal Quantum Interaction) and the Born-preserving default, neither of which Popper endorsed.

### The Map's selection posit (for comparison)
- Consciousness biases which quantum-indeterminate outcome occurs, toward patterns expressing its content ([Bidirectional Interaction](/tenets/#bidirectional-interaction), [Minimal Quantum Interaction](/tenets/#minimal-quantum-interaction)); default reading preserves Born statistics, carrying the [mechanism debt](/positions/quantum-interface/#mechanism-debt).

## Key Debates

### Does plastic control escape the penny-toss objection Popper raised against others?
- **Sides**: Popper 1965 (yes, by relocating freedom from the micro-event to the control hierarchy); Frederick 2010 (no: the chance element reappears at the level of the control's own selections); Hannay 1972 (title promises a critique; unread).
- **Analysis**: Popper's two complaints against master-switch models were *grain* (all decisions become snap-decisions, p. 233) and *Schlick* (chance is the only alternative to determinism, p. 232). The hierarchy answers grain outright: a decision is "a kind of maturing process" (p. 234) of many trials and eliminations, so no single event carries it. Against Schlick the hierarchy offers an existence proof that intermediate systems exist (the soap bubble, p. 250) but no account of why a control's act of elimination is neither determined nor chance; n. 55 (p. 245) says only that trials are not "completely" random because earlier controls reduce some frequencies. The 1974 addition concedes the mechanism gap. The 1977 retraction then states the third category directly (non-random selection over random trials) as a solution "by downward causation" — which is the structure Popper had refused Compton, now accepted with the micro-event demoted from decision to raw material.
- **Current state**: unresolved in Popper's own terms; his final position is that the third category is coherent and exemplified, and that *how* the selecting level acts on the selected is unknown. That is also where the Map stands, with the difference that the Map names the locus.

### Born-preservation: Popper as a witness for the deviation horn
- **Sides**: Popper 1977 (SIB; "it only gives us indeterminism, not …"; Frederick's gloss: the agent must be able to interfere with probabilistic laws) versus the Map's default ([ensemble-level-epiphenomenalism](/concepts/ensemble-level-epiphenomenalism/): selection that preserves Born statistics exactly is observer-indistinguishable in aggregate).
- **Core disagreement**: whether a selector that respects quantum frequencies is "too restrictive" to be freedom (Popper) or is the minimal and physically safe posit (Map). Popper's "openness of World 1" is a claim that World 1's laws are incomplete, i.e. deviation; the Map's Minimal Quantum Interaction tenet is a claim that no law is broken. This is the cleanest place where Popper and the Map part company, and it should be stated on the page rather than left implicit.

### World 3 dependence
- The 1973 argument for openness is World-3-first [GB p. 118], and in 1965 consciousness is explicitly *not* the top of the hierarchy because World 3 systems control it (p. 251). The Map can take the hierarchy-of-controls picture with consciousness as a control level; it should not take the claim that abstract contents control consciousness causally, which is the part Gray found doing all the argumentative work.
