## Summary

"Release phenomenon" is the clinical name, fixed in ophthalmology by Cogan (1973), for positive sensory content that appears when a sensory system loses its afferent input: the deafferented system, no longer constrained by the signal, emits what it already knows how to build. The literature groups Charles Bonnet syndrome (CBS), tinnitus and phantom limb as the canonical three (Painter et al. 2018 name exactly these; Schultz & Melzack 1991 brought CBS into the family by calling its hallucinations "phantom visual images"; Roberts et al. 2010 and Shore et al. 2016 call tinnitus a "phantom" sensation and trace it to deafferentation). Musical hallucinosis in acquired deafness (Griffiths 2000) and hallucination under total blindfolding of sighted subjects (Merabet et al. 2004) pass the same test. Hypnagogic imagery is release-shaped but not deafferentation-shaped and belongs beside the family rather than in it; eigengrau and phosphenes fail the test outright and the Map's current grouping of them with CBS is a defect the article should correct; Anton's syndrome is the contrast case in which the generator, not the input, is lost. On every reading in the literature the content is learned or innately scaffolded, stereotyped and non-corrective, and the mechanism is a recovery of spontaneous firing (neuronal compensation) that produces, rather than prevents, the phantom. The family lends Tenet 3 nothing; on Tenet 5 it is the case where the economical account is also the right one. Its one live open question, carried from the CBS page, is partial penetrance: why some deafferented patients release and most do not. The fresh checks show the penetrance is not uniform across the family (roughly one in five for CBS, "many" without tinnitus after hearing loss, 60–80% for phantom sensation, 77% under five-day blindfolding), and that the only explanation on offer in the sources — preserved versus lost inhibition (Roberts et al. 2010; Painter et al. 2018) — is a measurable cortical variable, not a gap for the Map to fill.

## The Sense Discipline, Stated Once

The article should carry this paragraph, or a tightened version of it, and every host page should pipe to it rather than restate it.

A release phenomenon is *the emission, by a sensory system deprived of its afferent input, of positive content that the system already encodes: learned or innately scaffolded, organised by the system's own representational structure, and non-corrective, in that it restores nothing of the lost signal and carries no information about the present state of the periphery.* Three glosses fix the sense:

1. **Neuronal compensation is affirmed; phenomenal compensation is denied.** After deafferentation, central neurons recover and overshoot their spontaneous firing rates and synchrony — "spontaneous, compensatory, neural activity" in daSilva Morgan et al. (2025), "overcompensate in the case of CBS" in Reichert et al. (2013), "increased spontaneous firing rates and synchrony" in Shore et al. (2016). That recovery is the compensation the literature affirms, and it is what *generates* the phantom. Nothing in any member of the family restores, reconstructs or approximates the lost scene, sound or limb-state. The Map retired the second sense on the CBS page; the family page generalises the retirement.
2. **The family is evidentially neutral between the filter and production readings of perception.** Both predict release. The filter tradition is itself release-shaped (vivid endogenous experience when a constraint relaxes: `concepts/filter-theory` L67, L77, L99–101 make this claim about psychedelics and hypnagogia), and the production tradition has disinhibition, homeostatic plasticity and strong priors. The datum that would discriminate — released content not traceable to the deafferented system's own structure — is not reported for any member.
3. **"Release" is a claim about the source of content, not about the metaphysics of its being experienced.** A release account says which structure emits which content. Whether the emission is experienced because it is cortical activity, or because something non-physical receives it, is untouched; the Map's standing argument on that point lives at [predictive-processing-and-dualism](/topics/predictive-processing-and-dualism/) and is not re-run by this family.

## Membership Criteria

Proposed by this note; the literature groups the members by family resemblance and does not state criteria. The article should present them as the Map's reconstruction.

- **C1 — Loss of afferent constraint.** Strict form: damage to receptor, nerve or projection pathway (deafferentation proper). Weak form: input withdrawn while the pathway is intact (blindfolding; sleep-onset gating). The generator must survive; if the generator is destroyed, nothing is released.
- **C2 — Positive content in the deprived modality.** Something is added to experience, not merely subtracted; the content falls in the modality (or body-map) that lost its input.
- **C3 — Endogenous organisation.** The content tracks the system's own structure (cortical stream specialisation, the tonotopic hearing-loss region, the body schema), not the missing stimulus.
- **C4 — Non-corrective.** Restores nothing; insight into unreality is typical, though not criterial (phantom limbs are felt as real limbs; late hypnagogia loses reality monitoring).
- **C5 — Mechanism of the disinhibition type.** Downregulated inhibition, homeostatic hyperexcitability, raised spontaneous firing and synchrony. C5 is the family's working hypothesis rather than a proven member-by-member fact, and it is the criterion phantom limb now strains.

Partial penetrance is a family *trait* to be checked, not a criterion.

## The Two Fresh Raw Checks

**Tinnitus — do Eggermont & Roberts frame it as a deafferentation release phenomenon in their own words?** Partly. The 2004 abstract (PubMed, raw) calls tinnitus "an auditory phantom sensation (ringing of the ears) experienced when no external sound is present" and attributes it to "Downregulation of intracortical inhibition induced by damage to the cochlea or to auditory projection pathways". The word "deafferentation" does not appear in that abstract, and the Map's auditory page (L65) is right to put the mechanism no more strongly than the reviews do. The word *does* appear, with Eggermont as second author, in Roberts et al. (2010, raw): "deafferentation of tonotopically organized central auditory structures leads to increased neuron spontaneous firing rates and neural synchrony in the hearing loss region." The word "release" appears in none of the three tinnitus abstracts; the tinnitus literature's own term is *phantom* (2004, 2010, 2016 all use it), and *maladaptive plasticity* (2016 title). So: deafferentation framing verified at raw via the 2010 symposium paper; "release" is the ophthalmological and Map term, imported, and the article should say that the family name comes from the visual side (Cogan 1973) while the auditory side says "phantom".

**Phantom limb — Melzack's neuromatrix versus a release reading.** The 1990 abstract (PubMed, raw) states the deafferentation cases plainly — amputation, destroyed sensory roots, "A complete break of the spinal cord also often leads to a phantom body below the level of the break" — and then adds the datum that a pure release reading cannot carry: "A substantial number of children who are born without a limb feel a phantom of the missing part, suggesting that the neural network, or 'neuromatrix', that subserves body sensation has a genetically determined substrate that is modified by sensory experience." Melzack's claim is therefore that the content is *generated by a network that never needed the input*, so deafferentation unmasks rather than creates it. That is compatible with C1–C4 but reframes C5: the mechanism is the persistence of a generator, not disinhibition of one, and the Makin programme's persistent-representation findings (`topics/phantom-limb-phenomena` L63–65) point the same way. The family's mechanism criterion is therefore weakest at the member that gave the family its vocabulary — Schultz & Melzack (1991, raw) "highlights the sensory deprivation framework" for CBS by analogy with phantom limbs, and the phantom-limb literature has since moved on from the simplest version of that framework while the CBS literature has not. The article should carry this asymmetry rather than smooth it.

## The Partial-Penetrance Open Question

The CBS page leaves one family-level question: Painter et al. (2018, raw) find "no satisfactory explanation as to why some individuals develop hallucinations, while many do not." Checked against the other members:

- **Tinnitus shows the same shape.** Roberts et al. (2010, raw): "many older individuals with impaired hearing do not have tinnitus, possibly because age-related changes in inhibitory circuits are better preserved." Eggermont & Roberts (2004, raw) give the converse direction — "Most but not all cases are associated with hearing loss" — which is a different fact (tinnitus without measurable loss) and should not be confused with penetrance. Nondahl et al. (2011, raw) supply a cohort figure: hearing impairment raises the odds of tinnitus 3.20-fold against a 10.6% background prevalence, so impairment is a strong risk factor and nothing like a determinant. Population prevalence of any tinnitus is 14.4% pooled, rising to 23.6% over 65 (Jarach 2022, raw).
- **Phantom limb does not.** Penetrance after acquired loss is 60–80% (per the Map's page); the minority case is the amputee *without* a phantom. The open question there runs the other way, and the congenital base rate (~20–33%, contested) is the figure that resembles CBS.
- **Acute total deprivation is high.** 77% of blindfolded sighted subjects hallucinated within five days (Merabet 2004, raw).

So partial penetrance is family-wide only in the weak sense that no member reaches 100%; the figure runs from about one in five (CBS) to about four in five (phantom limb, blindfolding). Two sources offer the same explanatory variable: Roberts (2010) hypothesises that preserved inhibition protects against tinnitus, and Painter (2018) measures that CBS hallucinators have elevated cortical responses where non-hallucinating patients with the same eye disease do not. The gating variable the literature proposes is *how much inhibition is lost*, and it is measurable. The Map should record the question as open, record that the candidate answer is cortical, and say — as the CBS page already says of its four gaps — that none of this is a gap for dualism to fill.

## Tenet Assessment

- **Dualism (Tenet 1)**: Neutral. Release names the source of content; it says nothing about why the content is experienced. The filter theorist accepts release outright and gives up any claim that released content is mind-side.
- **Minimal Quantum Interaction (Tenet 2)**: Not engaged.
- **Bidirectional Interaction (Tenet 3)**: By subtraction. The family is one-way on its face, cortex to experience, and lends the tenet nothing. This is consistent with, and generalises, the CBS page's withdrawal of 2026-10-14. Any host page that uses a family member as evidence for two-way traffic is making the withdrawn argument under another name.
- **No Many Worlds (Tenet 4)**: Not engaged.
- **Occam's Razor Has Limits (Tenet 5)**: In the economical direction. The release account is the simple one and the Map endorses it; Tenet 5 does not license resisting a simple account where nothing is incomplete in the relevant respect.
