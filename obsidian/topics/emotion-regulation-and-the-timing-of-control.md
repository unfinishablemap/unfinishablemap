Emotion regulation research asks where in an emotion's time course control acts. James Gross's process model sorts strategies by where they first act, from choosing a situation to modulating a response already under way, and splits them into antecedent-focused and response-focused halves (Gross 1998a, 1998b). The intuitive corollary, that earlier control is easier and better, has not survived intact. Sheppes and Gross (2011) narrowed it to a "process-specific timing hypothesis". Pooled effectiveness does not follow stage order (Webb, Miles & Sheeran 2012). And a family label does not fix when a strategy acts: expressive suppression can act as early as distraction, apparently when used preventively (Paul et al. 2013).

Regulation also needs no conscious regulatory goal or supervision and no awareness of regulating, since implicit regulation can run without all three. That shows these are unnecessary, not that the episode goes unperceived or unfelt, nor that consciousness is idle where an explicit goal is held. The Unfinishable Map can say where a conscious contribution would sit: at the regulatory goal, the decision to regulate, the choice of strategy and pre-episode policy ([[#placement|explained below]]). Every such stage is modelled without an experiential term, so the location is fit, not evidence. The same calibration covers the reappraisal experiment that Stapp's quantum Zeno model, widely cited on the Map, takes as its worked example ([[#stapp-example|the Stapp example]], explained below).

## Five Points and Two Halves {#five-points}

Gross (1998a) proposes that emotion "may be regulated at five points in the emotion generative process", ordered by where each family first acts:

| Family | Example strategies | Half | Where it first acts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Situation selection | approaching or avoiding a situation | antecedent | before the situation is met |
| Situation modification | changing the situation's features | antecedent | in the situation |
| Attentional deployment | distraction, concentration | antecedent | on what is attended |
| Cognitive change | reappraisal, distancing | antecedent | on the appraisal |
| Response modulation | expressive suppression | response | on the generated response |

The coarser cut, proposed in Gross (1998b), is defined by timing. Antecedent-focused strategies act "before the emotion response tendencies have become fully activated", response-focused ones "once an emotion is already underway" (Gross & John 2003). "Suppression" here means *expressive* suppression, hiding a feeling's outward signs. In Gross's (1998b) disgust-film experiment, reappraisal and suppression both reduced expressive behaviour, but "reappraisal decreased disgust experience, whereas suppression increased sympathetic activation". Gross and John's trait studies, by their own statement, do not test the model's temporal ordering, so timing claims must rest on experiments.

The extended process model gives the regulatory act its own time course, treating regulation as "one type of valuation" with "three emotion regulation stages (identification, selection, implementation)" (Gross 2015); a later statement adds a fourth, in which "monitoring occurs to track success" (McRae & Gross 2020). Two clocks must then be kept apart: *when in the emotion* a strategy acts, and *which stage of the regulatory decision* is in play. "Selection" on the second clock means choosing a strategy. It is distinct from *situation* selection, and from the conscious selection the Map discusses elsewhere.

## What the Timing Studies Measure {#timing}

Within single episodes, strategies differ in when they first change the response. In an fMRI study of 17 women watching disgust films, "Reappraisal resulted in early (0-4.5 sec) prefrontal cortex (PFC) responses" and lower amygdala and insula responses, while "Suppression produced late (10.5-15 sec) PFC responses" and higher ones (Goldin et al. 2008). In EEG, "distraction reduced the late positive potential (LPP) earlier than reappraisal" (Thiruchselvam et al. 2011). On passive re-exposure, though, "images with a distraction (but not reappraisal) history elicited a larger LPP than images with an attend history", so faster was not more durable.

The family label does not fix the time. Paul et al. (2013) found that "expressive suppression affected emotional responding as early as distraction", and suggested that suppression "was used preventively". In Li et al. (2017), suppression "works as early as distraction in the high-intensity condition", but with low-intensity images only distraction reduced the early LPP. "Response-focused" names a target; when a given use acts is an empirical matter.

Distraction "was effective even when initiated late", while reappraisal "was less effective when initiated late, suggesting a possible point of no return for this strategy" (Sheppes & Meiran 2007). Earlier studies had begun reappraisal at the situation's onset and found it cognitively free; beginning it late revealed "for the first time a cognitive cost for reappraisal" (Sheppes & Meiran 2008). The cost was measured as ego depletion, a larger Stroop effect afterwards than after late distraction, with no unregulated control group, so it carries the depletion record that [[mental-effort#depletion|the mental-effort article]] reviews. Late distraction instead impaired memory for the film.

Timing claims should rest on such time-resolved designs, not on which regions activate. A meta-analysis found left ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, anterior insula and supplementary motor area "consistently activated independent of the regulation strategy" (Morawetz et al. 2017). Goldin's sample is small and single-site, so neural claims here lean on meta-analyses.

## Earlier Is Not Uniformly Better {#not-uniform}

The model's rationale assumed what Sheppes and Gross (2011) call the "generic timing hypothesis": that down-regulating emotions before they are "up and running" "is always easier". They replace it with a "process-specific timing hypothesis", glossing timing as emotion intensity. Strategies that target early processing stages "require minimal effort", so their efficacy should be "relatively unaffected by emotion intensity"; those that target later stages "require effort that is proportional to the intensity of the emotional response".

Family-level effectiveness does not follow stage order either. Pooling experiential, behavioural and physiological outcomes across 306 experimental comparisons, Webb, Miles and Sheeran (2012) found that attentional deployment "had no effect" (d+ = 0.00), response modulation a small effect (0.16) and cognitive change a small-to-medium one (0.36). Within attentional deployment, distraction worked (0.27) and concentration did not (−0.26). These estimates compare strategies, not earlier and later starts of one, so they cannot show whether starting a given strategy earlier helps. That abstract pools only these three families, and the 2026-10-23 search behind this article found no pooled estimate for situation selection or modification. Bonanno and Burton (2013) call the assumption that a strategy is consistently good or bad "the fallacy of uniform efficacy"; in daily-life sampling in the United States and China, momentary context sensitivity and repertoire use were associated with reduced distress, while "results for feedback responsiveness were less consistent" (Chen et al. 2024). The experimental record above comes from short, instructed laboratory tasks, and field studies do not reliably reproduce laboratory links between situation and strategy. Two haunted-house studies of untrained participants "failed to find relationships between emotional intensity and strategy usage" (Mitchell et al. 2025), and daily-life associations between situational factors and strategies "appear to be largely different from the results found in the laboratory" (Wilms et al. 2020).

Writing on self-control five years after Sheppes and Gross's narrowing, Duckworth, Gendler and Gross (2016) state the generic form: "as a rule, earlier intervention is best". The claims concern different domains, a rule of thumb for self-control policy and a strategy-specific account of regulatory effort, so both can hold. Within one strategy, earlier preparation can reduce effort: in an EEG study of reappraisal, a reactive group "used more effort" than a proactive group for the same reduction in negative experience, indexed by the frontal LPP (Wang et al. 2022).

## Regulation Without a Regulator's Awareness {#implicit}

Emotion regulation does not require a conscious regulatory goal, conscious supervision or awareness of regulating. Koole and Rothermund (2011) broadly define implicit emotion regulation as "any process that operates without the need for conscious supervision or explicit intentions" that aims to change an emotional response, and judge it "likely to be pervasive in everyday life". Gyurak, Gross and Etkin (2011) give an uninstructed laboratory case, emotional conflict adaptation, which "proceeds without awareness": participants report no awareness of the task's key processes, the regulation among them. The adaptation effect has since proved hard to replicate: a secondary analysis was "unable to fully replicate" it, and a meta-analysis found published effects "significant but small" (Yin et al. 2024). Affect labelling, itself a conscious verbal act, has effects resembling explicit regulation, yet it "may not even feel like a regulatory process as it occurs" (Torre & Lieberman 2018). Later experiments complicate that 2018 reading of labelling as regulation: naming the emotions that aversive images evoked impeded subsequent regulation by reappraisal and by mindful acceptance (Nook et al. 2021), and two further studies (total N = 226) replicated the reappraisal impairment and found no delayed benefit (Ariely et al. 2026).

Braunstein, Gross and Ochsner (2017) separate two axes: "the nature of the emotion regulation goal", "ranging from implicit/nonconscious to explicit/conscious", and "the nature of the emotion change process", "ranging from more automatic to more controlled". A conscious goal can therefore be served by an automatic process, and explicit and implicit regulation "have porous boundaries" (Gyurak et al. 2011).

This record cuts against any reading on which conscious supervision is necessary for regulation. It does not show consciousness idle when an explicit goal is held, any more than an automatic route to an outcome shows that a deliberate route contributes nothing. Nor does felt effort show that consciousness regulates, since effort appears only where a conflict is noticed ([[alief-and-acting-against-belief#noticing-condition|the noticing condition]]) and may be a cost signal ([[mental-effort]]). Introspection certifies neither efficacy nor its absence ([[positions/agency-and-will#^p-a4|P-A4]]).

## Is There a Before? {#generation-regulation}

The timing question presupposes an emotion that exists before regulation acts on it, which constructionist theories contest. Gross and Feldman Barrett (2011) arrange theories on a continuum and find that toward the constructionist end "the conceptual separation of emotion generation and emotion regulation becomes increasingly suspect". Kappas (2011) goes further: "For all practical purposes unregulated emotion is not a realistic concept."

The co-authored paper maps perspectives, and neither author concedes the other's view. If generation and regulation are one process, "where does control enter?" becomes a question about where a goal-directed influence bears on a continuous construction. This article's timing talk assumes the distinction as it holds toward the appraisal end of the continuum; [[valence-as-a-natural-kind]] engages constructionism directly.

## Where a Conscious Contribution Would Sit {#placement}

This section gives the Map's interpretation, for which none of the affective scientists cited argues; their models need no experiential term.

The literature itself puts explicit control at three points: the goal, at the conscious end of Braunstein's first axis; the identification and strategy-selection stages, which decide whether to regulate and how; and policy moments made before the episode, which have the clearest evidence of effective control: if-then plans for emotion pooled at d+ = 0.91 against no instructions and 0.53 against goal-intention instructions (Webb, Schweiger Gallo et al. 2012). If a non-physical selector operates in emotion regulation, the Map's reading places its ordinary work at these points. Monitoring, which tracks whether a strategy under way is succeeding, would be a further one. The timing experiments above supply goal and strategy by instruction, so they test implementation, not the stages where the placement sits. Choice studies do test selection, and model it. In laboratory tasks people relatively preferred reappraisal at low intensity and distraction at high (Sheppes et al. 2011), a pattern the field studies cited above do not reliably reproduce, and a review of 219 studies identified eighteen determinants and proposed that choice turns on the person, the emotion, the situation and its social context (Matthews et al. 2021). That leaves room for a conscious chooser but makes selection a modelled stage rather than one isolated choice point, which is why the placement there is fit, not evidence. Identification is the emotional form of [[alief-and-acting-against-belief#noticing-condition|the noticing condition]], and pre-episode plans are the [[self-control-as-skill-and-scaffolding#where-selection-sits|self-control article's]] "moments of policy", the shape [[skill-delegation]] describes.

The placement is fit, not evidence. Regulation is modelled as valuation (Gross 2015), each stage has an implicit form, and two physical architectures, valuation (Etkin et al. 2015) and [[consciousness-selecting-neural-patterns#the-active-inference-rival|active inference]], predict the same timing record, including late reappraisal's weaker effect. So does a physicalist account on which conscious access is physically realised and causally active; even a demonstrated conscious contribution would not by itself favour the Map's reading. Nor can the record disconfirm the placement, since a contribution found absent at one stage can be relocated earlier; the disagreement is one of framework. As the self-control article says, "The fit discriminates nothing."

Seneca offers a precedent. *De Ira* 2.4.1 divides anger's onset into three *motus* ("movements"; Aubrey Stewart's 1900 translation has "emotion"): the first "is involuntary" (*non uoluntarius*), the second "is combined with a wish, though not an obstinate one", the third "is already beyond our control, because it overrides reason". The voluntary moment falls between an involuntary onset and a point of no return. Seneca was a Stoic corporealist writing about assent, his movements do not map onto Gross's stages, and the parallel is no evidence.

### The Stapp Example {#stapp-example}

Schwartz, Stapp and Beauregard (2005), cited on about fifteen Map articles for the quantum Zeno mechanism, take as their worked example Ochsner et al.'s (2002) experiment, in which reappraisal raised lateral and medial prefrontal activation and lowered amygdala and medial orbitofrontal activation. On their reading, "the effort of the subject to ‘reappraise’ causes the ‘reappraise’ template to be held in place", and that holding "causes the suppression of the limbic response". The reading is the 2005 authors'; Ochsner's abstract attributes the modulation to prefrontal cortex. A 2026-10-23 search found no live Map article discussing that experiment, so the mechanism's worked case sat in a literature the Map had not examined.

Three calibrations apply. First, the Zeno account is [[zeno-dynamics-on-a-decohered-mixture|a posit]], and the data have a physical route: a 48-study meta-analysis reads reappraisal as "the use of cognitive control to modulate semantic representations of an emotional stimulus" (Buhle et al. 2014). Second, reappraisal neuroimaging shares the prefrontal top-down route of psychotherapy and placebo, so it adds no independent stream to the convergence that [[clinical-neuroplasticity-evidence-for-bidirectional-causation]] already down-counts, where "the data do not adjudicate". Third, in Stapp's model the agent chooses and holds the question (von Neumann's Process 1) and leaves the outcome to nature ([[stapp-quantum-mind]]). On its own terms the example illustrates a context-selection placement that the Map's tenets register without adopting.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]** is the tenet engaged, and the article gives it a location rather than support. Whether a selector acts at all remains "a posit the interface argument leaves open, not a result it secures" ([[tenets#^tenet-3-standing|Tenet 3's standing]]). Process-model findings are substrate-neutral: at every temporal resolution reached so far, each stage is modelled without an experiential term. The Map gains a precise location and loses any claim that the location is evidentially special, which cuts against citing emotion regulation as support and equally against reading implicit regulation as a refutation.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]]** gains nothing from earlier regulation needing less effort, an economy a physical controller enjoys equally. The tenet's minimality is fixed by the empirical record the interaction must respect (no detectable energy injection, no Born-statistics or conservation violation), not by how few or how small the interventions are.

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** neither gains nor loses from the timing record. **[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]]** is not engaged.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]** binds the Map's own reasoning. It blocks dismissing a conscious contribution because valuation models are simpler, and supplies no reason to posit one where those models already suffice.
