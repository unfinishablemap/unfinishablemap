Kent Berridge and colleagues spent three decades showing that reward is not one thing. "Wanting" a reward (incentive salience: the motivational pull toward it) and "liking" it (an operational hedonic reaction—in rats, the positive orofacial response to sweetness—not as such felt pleasure) have **separable neural substrates** and can be pulled apart experimentally. In the rodent paradigms that established the distinction, dopamine drives wanting while small opioid "hedonic hotspots" in the nucleus accumbens shell and ventral pallidum amplify liking; in human pharmacology the mapping is less clean (explained below). An animal can even be made to "want" a cue it has never "liked." This is a well-supported affective-neuroscience distinction.

The Unfinishable Map lays an *interpretive* question over this settled base, the **value-in-mechanism fork** (defined below): when a reward is good or a pain is bad, does that affective value reside in the neural mechanism itself, or in the conscious experience associated with it? The honest answer this article defends: the dissociation **constrains** the space of accounts—it rules out any naive "dopamine = pleasure = value" picture and shows motivation and hedonic impact to be mechanistically separable—but it does **not** by itself settle the fork. A mechanism-only physicalist reading appears to explain every Berridge finding without remainder; the data sharpen the question without answering it. A shared terminology map (explained below) fixes how Berridge's constructs relate to the utility and reinforcement-learning vocabulary of [[affective-forecasting-gap]].

## The Empirical Dissociation

The core finding is a clean double role for reward. Berridge and Robinson (1998) argued that mesolimbic dopamine does not mediate the *hedonic impact* of rewards at all. Instead it mediates **incentive salience**—"wanting," the attribution of motivational value to reward-associated stimuli—which is separable both from hedonic impact and from reward-prediction learning. The quotation marks Berridge places around "wanting" and "liking" are deliberate: they are labels for *core processes*, bracketing the question of whether either is consciously felt.

The anatomy backs the split. Peciña and Berridge (2005) showed that microinjecting a μ-opioid agonist into a roughly one-cubic-millimetre "hedonic hotspot" in the rostrodorsal medial shell of the nucleus accumbens **doubled** the positive orofacial "liking" reactions to sweetness, while opioid-driven *eating* ("wanting"/consumption) spread across the entire medial shell. The substrate that amplifies the hedonic reaction is anatomically distinct from the substrate that amplifies pursuit.

The motivational direction yields the sharpest result. In Tindell, Smith, Berridge and Aldridge (2009)—"wanting what was never liked"—rats given a cue paired with intensely salty (normally aversive) taste showed enhanced motivation toward it the moment they entered a salt-depleted state, **before concentrated salt had ever elicited a positive "liking" reaction**. Ventral pallidal neurons appear to recompute the cue's incentive salience from current bodily state, not from any stored hedonic memory. Berridge (2023) generalises the point to prediction itself: "in some situations desire can decouple completely from memories and from learned predictions of outcome value"—rats can be made to "want" what is "predicted to hurt". "Wanting" is therefore not a forecast of "liking", a distinction the terminology map below relies on.

Two further results frame the landscape. Berridge and Robinson (2016) build their incentive-sensitization *theory* of addiction on exactly this gap: on their account, addictive "wanting" can grow over time while "liking" stays flat or falls—craving without rising pleasure. It is a theory rather than the settled description of addiction: craving, pleasure, habit, withdrawal relief and compulsive control failure vary across substances, stages and patients. And Berridge and Kringelbach (2015) draw the distinction this article turns on: they separate the *objective core "liking" reaction* (a measurable neural-behavioural signature) from *subjective conscious pleasure*, treating felt pleasure as a further layer beyond the mechanistic signature.

### The mapping is paradigm-specific {#human-pharmacology}

The dopamine-for-wanting, opioid-for-liking mapping is paradigm-specific: cleanest in the rodent microinjection paradigms that established it, blurred under systemic antagonists in humans. Three randomised placebo-controlled studies used the same compounds, the dopamine D2/D3 antagonist amisulpride and the opioid antagonist naltrexone. In Korb et al. (2020) both drugs lowered effort and increased negative facial reactions during anticipation, only naltrexone reduced positive facial reactions during consumption, and subjective wanting and liking ratings were unmoved by either. In Soutschek et al. (2021) naltrexone selectively reduced *wanting* and amisulpride had no robust behavioural or neural effect. In Massaccesi et al. (2024) neither drug changed behaviour, though naltrexone lowered medial orbitofrontal activity during consumption of the most valued rewards and amisulpride had no clear anticipation effect. Human opioid blockade reaches motivation as well as hedonic reaction; dopamine blockade at these doses often reaches neither. The dissociation stands; the slogan does not export. Korb's sample also shows the objective/subjective gap: the facial "liking" measure moved while the reports did not, so neither measure alone says what was felt.

## The Value-in-Mechanism Fork {#the-fork}

All parties accept the dissociation. The dispute is where *value*—affective goodness or badness, the thing that actually matters—resides.

**The mechanism-only reading** holds that affective value just *is* the operation of these reward circuits. The "liking" reactions are the value; conscious pleasure, where it occurs, is identical to or supervenient on a brain state with no further ontological work to do. Its strongest support is that the dissociation is fully explained at the mechanistic level: orofacial "liking" reactions persist in decerebrate rats and anencephalic human infants—subjects lacking a functioning forebrain—suggesting a value-signature that need not invoke rich conscious experience. It is also the more parsimonious option.

**The value-in-experience reading** (the Map's) holds that value exists only *for* an experiencing subject. The mechanistic "liking" reaction is then a correlate, cause, or effect of hedonic value—not the value itself. A "liking" reaction with no accompanying experience, if such a thing occurs, would carry no genuine value, because nothing would *matter* to anything. This is the position of [[consciousness-value-connection]] and [[phenomenal-value-realism]].

The value-in-experience reading's honest wedge is a modest one: Berridge's *own* taxonomy already separates objective "liking" from subjective conscious pleasure. If the field's leading framework treats the measurable signature and the felt feeling as even nominally distinct, the question "does value track the signature or the feeling?" is live rather than confused. The dissociation fractionates reward into pieces—motivation here, hedonic signature there—that need not be experienced, which raises, without answering, whether unexperienced "liking" is value at all.

### What the dissociation does and does not settle

What it **secures** (empirical): motivation and hedonic impact are mechanistically separable; there is no single neural *hedonic* currency; dopamine does not mediate the hedonic reaction; an organism can want what it never liked.

What it does **not** secure is the stronger claim that there is no common neural value currency at all. Distinct systems for incentive salience and consummatory reaction are compatible with a later-stage, domain-general subjective-value representation in ventromedial prefrontal and orbitofrontal cortex (Levy & Glimcher 2012; Bartra, McGuire & Kable 2013), and Berridge himself, in the neuroeconomics handbook, places a decision-utility signal used at the point of choice alongside the wanting/liking fractionation rather than against it (Berridge & O'Doherty 2014). Fractionation at one stage says nothing against integration at a later one. The Map engages the common-currency rival in [[valence-and-conscious-selection]], which states the felt-currency premise as a dissociation claim—felt valence as a selection variable distinct from the computed one—rather than a denial that any computed currency exists.

What it does **not** settle (interpretive): whether value resides in experience rather than mechanism. The dissociation rules out the naive unified-hedonic-dopamine picture and is otherwise **neutral** between the two readings. Treating it as proof of the dualist reading would be the evidential over-reach the Map's [[evidential-status-discipline]] exists to prevent: coherence with the Map's view is not independent support for it.

A trap worth marking: Berridge is a physicalist affective neuroscientist whose constructs are operational; he is not a dualist and his work is not evidence for dualism. Citing him correctly means crediting him with the *empirical dissociation* and the *objective-versus-conscious distinction*, then doing the interpretive work transparently as the Map's own.

There is also a live mechanistic rival to keep in view: the reward-prediction-error account (Schultz 2016) reads phasic dopamine as coding the error δ between received and predicted reward—a teaching signal that updates a learned value—rather than incentive salience. Berridge (2023) sharpens the divergence by showing that "wanting" can float free of the learned value δ maintains; the fork sits *above* that dispute, since both are mechanism-level accounts that the value-in-experience reading treats as describing correlates rather than the locus of value.

A second pressure comes from the opposite direction. The value-in-experience reading leans on there being a *subject* for whom hedonic value exists, and a no-self critic will ask what that subject amounts to if it is not simply more mechanism. The Map need not settle that here: its claim is the modest one that *whatever* the experiencing locus turns out to be, an unfelt "liking" reaction has no one it is good for, and so carries no value in the sense the fork is about. If a fully impersonal process could nonetheless instantiate genuine value, the reading would lose its motivation—a real cost to track, not a settled point. The dedicated treatment is [[no-self-objection-to-phenomenal-value]], which argues that the "someone" the reading needs is a momentary experiential locus rather than a persisting self, and marks the for-no-one reading of contentless-awareness reports as the rival it cannot yet refute.

| Predicted utility | Kahneman & Snell 1992 | A judgement of the experienced utility an outcome will have; the *object* of what [[affective-forecasting-gap]] calls anticipated valence | Only insofar as present anticipatory affect (below) carries it | The fallible forecast a selector might consult |
| Decision utility | Kahneman, Wakker & Sarin 1997 | The weight an outcome carries in choice, read off the choice made | No—inferred from behaviour | Not a currency selection can consult; "selection runs on decision utility" is near-circular |
| Experienced utility | Kahneman, Wakker & Sarin 1997 | The hedonic quality of an outcome as lived (*instant*) or as later assessed (*remembered*) | Yes | On the Map's reading, what constitutes value; the forecast's target |
| Learned value (V/Q) | Reinforcement learning; see [[reinforcement-learning-reward-signals-and-machine-valence]] | A cached estimate of expected reward, updated by δ, that a predictor-driven chooser consults | No | The value-blind rival's chooser input |
| Reward-prediction error (δ) | Schultz 2016 | The difference between received and predicted reward, coded by many midbrain dopamine neurons; the teaching signal that updates learned value | Contested—see [[affective-forecasting-gap]] | An update, not what a chooser consults |
| Incentive salience ("wanting") | Berridge & Robinson 1998; Berridge 2023 | Motivational pull attributed to cues, dopamine-mediated in the rodent paradigms; can decouple completely from learned predictions of outcome value and from "liking" | Bracketed by Berridge—may occur without conscious desire | Neither a forecast nor a pleasure; sets up the field of weighted options |
| Objective "liking" | Peciña & Berridge 2005; Berridge & Kringelbach 2015 | The measurable hedonic reaction (orofacial signature; opioid hotspots), present in decerebrate rats | Bracketed by Berridge—occurs without a forebrain | The mechanism-only horn: value is here |
| Conscious pleasure | Berridge & Kringelbach 2015, as a layer beyond "liking" | The felt hedonic quality of the outcome—experienced utility's phenomenal side | Yes, by definition | The value-in-experience horn: value is here |
| Present anticipatory affect | [[affective-forecasting-gap]] | The dread or relish felt *now* while simulating an outcome—anticipated in its object, experienced in its vehicle; an instant utility of its own | Yes | The forecasting article's candidate selection currency; the felt vehicle of predicted utility |

Three identifications the map blocks. "Wanting" is not predicted utility: incentive salience can rise for an outcome remembered as disgusting or predicted to hurt, so it is not a forecast of "liking". The error δ is not learned value: the teaching signal updates the quantity a chooser consults, and a felt forecast would supplement or rival the learned value, not the update. And objective "liking" is not conscious pleasure: the orofacial reaction is a signature Berridge's own taxonomy separates from felt enjoyment, so establishing "liking" establishes nothing about phenomenal value.

The map also keeps two questions apart. This article's fork asks in which *row* value resides—objective "liking" or conscious pleasure. The forecasting article asks which row selection *consults*—learned value, "wanting", or predicted utility carried by present anticipatory affect. The questions are independent: value could reside in conscious pleasure while selection ran entirely on learned value, and selection could consult present affect while that affect remained a correlate rather than the locus of value. The falsification conditions below test the second question where the rows collide—when "wanting" and "liking" pull apart.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The fork engages **Tenet 3 (Bidirectional Interaction)**. If consciousness causally influences the physical world, the most plausible place for that influence in the reward domain is **selection**: among the live action-options the mechanism prepares, felt value tips which one becomes actual (see [[valence-and-conscious-selection]] and [[dopamine-and-the-unified-interface]]). On this reading, dopaminergic "wanting" sets up the field of weighted options, and conscious hedonic value does selective work the mechanism alone does not.

This is a tenet-coherent *hypothesis*, not a finding, and it must carry explicit falsification conditions. The value-sensitive-selection option predicts that:

- **When wanting and liking conflict, conscious pleasure—the felt quality, not merely the objective "liking" signature—should track which outcome is selected.** If choices were fully predicted by the incentive-salience signal with felt pleasure adding nothing, the value-sensitive reading would be disconfirmed for that case.
- **Disrupting the conscious hedonic signal while leaving wanting circuitry intact should change selection**, not merely change reported feeling. If selection is invariant under such manipulation, value is doing no selective work.
- **A complete mechanistic model of choice that needs no experiential term** would leave the value-in-experience reading explanatorily idle, a metaphysical add-on, which by the Map's own [[evidential-status-discipline]] would be a cost, not a vindication.

The Map holds the value-in-experience reading because of its prior commitments in [[consciousness-value-connection]]—not because Berridge's findings settle it. The wanting/liking dissociation earns its place here as the cleanest empirical *anchor* for the fork: it shows the brain genuinely fractionates reward, which makes "where is the value?" a sharp question. Whether anything is at stake in an unfelt "liking" reaction is, on the Map's view, the whole question—and the neuroscience, for all its precision, is silent on the phenomenology.
