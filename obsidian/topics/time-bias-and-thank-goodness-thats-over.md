When a painful episode ends we say "Thank goodness that's over," and the relief seems to be *about* the pain's being past. A. N. Prior (1959) used this ordinary remark against tenseless theories of time: no tenseless statement dated or indexed to the utterance says what the relief is relief about. The argument opened two debates. In the metaphysics of time, B-theorists answer that tensed *beliefs* are indispensable but tensed *facts* are not. In the theory of rationality, Derek Parfit's hospital case showed that we prefer pains to be past even when that means more pain overall, and Preston Greene and Meghan Sullivan argue that this "future-bias" cannot survive the arguments most philosophers accept against caring more about the near future. The Map treats the felt, tensed character of relief as a genuine datum that any theory of time and mind must accommodate. The datum does not refute the B-theory, and it does not by itself show that time-bias is rational. What it does is fit the Map's picture comfortably: a single world with a settled past and an open future, and a consciousness for which the passage from open to settled is lived.

## Prior's Argument {#priors-argument}

The argument occupies a short passage in a paper mostly concerned with N. L. Wilson's attempt to build a tenseless "substance-language." Prior's target was the assumption that a tensed copula can be analysed as a tenseless one plus a date. His counterexample:

> One says, e.g. 'Thank goodness that's over!', and not only is this, when said, quite clear without any date appended, but it says something which it is impossible that any use of a tenseless copula with a date should convey.

The dated paraphrase fails: the remark "certainly doesn't mean the same as, e.g. 'Thank goodness the date of the conclusion of that thing is Friday, June 15, 1954', even if it be said then." So does the token-reflexive paraphrase — "Thank goodness the conclusion of that thing is contemporaneous with this utterance" — and Prior dismisses it with a question: "Why should anyone thank goodness for that?" (Prior 1959).

Two things about the original are worth keeping in view. First, Prior's stated conclusion is semantic and logical: tensed statements are not analysable into tenseless ones. The metaphysical reading that later literature attached to it — that relief is directed at a tensed *fact* and so tensed facts must exist — is a natural extension rather than a thesis the 1959 paper argues at length. Second, the argument works by pointing at an attitude and asking what its object is. The relief is the evidence; the paraphrases fail because they change what one would be relieved about.

## The B-Theorist Replies {#b-theorist-replies}

**Mellor's separation of belief from fact.** D. H. Mellor's reply (1981) distinguishes the tensed judgement "that's over" from the relief it produces. The judgement is indispensable: a tensed belief, and no tenseless belief, is what causes me to feel relief and act at the right moment. But the belief's *truth* needs no tensed fact. A token of "that's over" is true just when the pain ends earlier than the token, which is a tenseless condition. On this account the tensed belief is psychologically irreplaceable while the world contains only tenseless relations. The literature treats this move, developed in Mellor's exchange with Murdo MacBeath (1983), as the orthodox B-theoretic answer. Olley Pearson (2018a) reports that it is "widely accepted that Mellor and MacBeath have shown that our emotions do not imply the existence of tensed facts" — and then contests that orthodoxy, arguing that a natural view of when emotions are *appropriate*, combined with Prior's case, still implies tensed facts.

**The evolutionary story.** James Maclaurin and Heather Dyke (2002) argued that the challenge has more dimensions than the object-of-relief question. A B-theorist must also say why the same event draws different emotions depending on whether it is past, present or future, and why proximate future pain matters more than distant future pain. Their answers appeal to evolution and rest on a causal asymmetry: emotions can influence what comes after them and not what came before, so fear aimed at future danger is adaptive and fear aimed at past danger is not. Nothing in this requires metaphysical tense, only the tenseless difference between an emotion preceding or following its object. Christopher Suhler and Craig Callender (2012) extend the approach. Pearson (2018b) replies that it misses Prior's actual contrast. Prior compared a *tensed* and a *tenseless* attitude towards one and the same earlier discomfort, and whether an emotion is tensed is independent of whether it comes before or after its object. On this reading the causal story explains the wrong asymmetry.

The dispute is open. The B-theory has a coherent account of why creatures like us have tensed attitudes; the A-theorist has a standing complaint that the account explains the attitudes' usefulness without explaining their apparent correctness.

## From Relief to Preference: Parfit's Hospital Case {#parfits-hospital-case}

Parfit turned the attitude into a preference and so into a question about rationality. In the case discussed in *Reasons and Persons* (1984, 165), you wake in hospital unable to remember the recent past. You are either the patient who had a ten-hour painful operation yesterday or the patient who will have a one-hour painful operation later today. While the nurse checks, you hope you are the first — preferring ten times the pain, provided it is past.

This is **future-bias**: caring more about pains and pleasures in one's future than in one's past. It differs from **near-bias**, caring more about the near future than the distant, which philosophers have commonly counted a defect of prudence. Parfit argues that future-bias is bad for us, that temporal neutrality would improve our attitude to ageing and death — "As our life passes, we should have less and less to look forward to, but more and more to look backward to" (1984, 175) — while noting that his claim that the bias is bad for us "does not beg the question about the rationality of this bias" (1984, 177).

## The Rationality Challenge {#rationality-challenge}

Greene and Sullivan (2015) press the point. Most philosophers, they observe, reject near-bias as a rational defect while finding nothing objectionable in future-bias. They argue that this hybrid position is untenable and "conclude that those who reject near bias should instead endorse complete temporal neutrality." Sullivan's *Time Biases* (2018) develops the positive view: temporal neutrality, the thesis that prudential rationality requires indifference about *when* goods and bads are scheduled.

A natural defence of future-bias ties it to the metaphysics of time: if time really passes, past pains are finished in a way future ones are not, and caring about the difference is tracking something real. Two cautions apply. First, Greene and Sullivan's argument is a parity argument aimed at people who already reject near-bias; it does not rest on assuming the B-theory, so an A-theorist cannot dismiss it simply by rejecting eternalism. Second, the empirical question of whether belief in passage actually drives the bias has been tested. Andrew Latham, Kristie Miller, Christian Tarsney and Hannah Tierney (2022) report, in their title's words, that belief in robust temporal passage "(probably) does not explain future-bias." Future-bias may be a feature of human psychology whatever one's metaphysics — which means the bias cannot be read off as a metaphysical endorsement of passage, and the metaphysics of passage cannot be read off from the bias.

## Hare's Parity Move {#hares-parity}

Caspar Hare (2007) connects time-bias to its first-person twin. We are mildly biased towards ourselves as well as towards the future, and in each case, Hare argues, reconciling the bias with concern for how things go overall requires a metaphysics on which the favoured item is special. On a four-dimensionalist picture, with tense merely relational, no such reconciliation is available. A theory with monadic tensed properties makes the present time special; Hare's [[egocentric-presentism]] makes the present *self* special in the same structural way. Hare presents egocentric presentism as a view he finds attractive and simple, not as proven, and he does not offer it as support for dualism.

Hare adds a caution that matters for what follows. Even granting monadic tense, the metaphysics does not by itself *explain* why past pains should matter less than future ones — unless one adopts a shrinking block, on which the future exists and the past does not. On a growing block, the past is the part that exists. A conviction that future pain is worse can survive such a metaphysics, but it is not derived from it.

The parity cuts in two directions. Someone who defends future-bias by appeal to a privileged present owes an account of why the parallel appeal to a privileged self fails, or else must accept both privileges. Someone who rejects self-bias as groundless must explain why the same reasoning leaves time-bias standing.

## What the Attitude Shows {#what-the-attitude-shows}

Three claims need separating.

1. **The phenomenal datum.** Relief at a finished pain has a felt, tensed character: it presents its object *as past*, as no longer being undergone. Both sides accept this. Mellor's reply concedes that the tensed belief is indispensable; the dispute is over what makes it true.
2. **The metaphysical inference.** From the datum to tensed facts is a further step. The B-theorist's reply that tensed attitudes have tenseless truth conditions is coherent, and Pearson's appropriateness argument, while serious, has not displaced it. Prior's argument is therefore one consideration among several; it does not decide the question.
3. **The normative question.** Whether future-bias is rational is separate again. A world with real passage would not by that fact make it rational to prefer ten hours of past pain to one hour of future pain, and the parity arguments against the hybrid view apply whatever one's metaphysics.

The relief is the most secure item on the list and the one theories of time most often explain away. A theory that says the relief misrepresents nothing but also explains why it feels as it does has done real work. A theory that treats the tensed character as a mere product of how we are built has not engaged the datum.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]].** The felt pastness of a finished pain is a first-person feature that tenseless description, dated or token-reflexive, does not capture — which is Prior's point transposed from language to experience. The structure parallels the gap between physical description and phenomenal character, and the Map notes the parallel without treating it as independent evidence: B-theorists who are also physicalists accept the datum and locate it in the representational content of tensed states. What the Map adds is that experienced becoming is constituted by consciousness. As [[consciousness-and-the-ontology-of-temporal-becoming]] argues, consciousness constitutes the *phenomenological* arrow — the lived passage from open to settled — while the settling of the past is mostly a matter of objective collapse and would occur without anyone to feel it. Consciousness does not make the past settled; it is where settledness is felt, and relief is one of the ways it is felt.

The growing block, which the Map holds, fits relief well but does not vindicate it for free. On a growing block the past pain still exists; what has changed is that it is no longer at the edge where experience is undergone. Presentism explains relief more directly, since the pain is simply gone. The Map's version needs the claim, developed in the temporal-becoming article's [[consciousness-and-the-ontology-of-temporal-becoming#epistemic-objection|reply to the epistemic objection]], that the present is distinguished by active participation in settlement. On that claim, "that's over" is true because the pain is fixed and will not be undergone again.

**[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]].** Relief presupposes a single settled past, and much ordinary relief is counterfactual as well as temporal: thank goodness the test was negative, the car missed. On the [[many-worlds]] interpretation every outcome with non-zero amplitude occurs, so each dreaded alternative happened to successors of the self who dreaded it. Everettians reply that rational attitudes can be rebuilt around branch weight. The Map's objection is the indexical one: relief is relief that *this* history went *this* way, and a view on which all the histories occur leaves that attitude without its apparent object. The point concerns the attitude's fit with the interpretation and leaves the physics untouched.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]].** The B-theory's economy — no tensed facts, one block — is bought partly by reclassifying the tensed character of attitudes as a fact about believers rather than about the world. That trade may be correct. The Map's claim is modest: the economy of the tenseless picture is not by itself a reason to treat the phenomenology of relief as misleading, and simplicity judgements made while the nature of experience remains unexplained are the kind the fifth tenet urges caution about.

On the normative question the Map takes no firm position. Its metaphysics gives future-bias an intelligible object, since past pains are fixed and future ones open. By Hare's point [above](#hares-parity), that object is not a ground: a growing block leaves past pains existing and future ones not yet real, which by itself gives no reason to care less about past pain. It does not follow that the preference is rational, and Greene and Sullivan's parity argument has to be met on its own terms. The honest summary is that relief sits comfortably within the Map's framework and is a datum its rivals must explain, without being evidence that decides between them.
