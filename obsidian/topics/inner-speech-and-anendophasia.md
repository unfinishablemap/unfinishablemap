Inner speech is the experience of language without overt articulation — the sense of hearing yourself think, often in something like your own voice. **Anendophasia** is the name Nedergaard and Lupyan proposed in 2024 for its absence. The term is about two years old, it is not an established clinical category, and the existence claim it appears to carry — that some people have no inner speech whatever — is under live dispute in the journal that introduced it. Lind has argued that no compelling evidence establishes complete absence. The coiners, replying, granted that their data support a continuum of inner-speech experience rather than a demonstrated zero. Hurlburt has since argued that people with little or no inner speech do exist and are probably common, while the line between *completely* and *mostly* anendophasic may be impossible to draw in practice.

The Map's interest in this literature is an argument about whether language is constitutive of thought, and the honest position is that the argument does not need the contested claim. What the evidence supports is weaker and sturdier: inner speech is intermittent even in people who report having it; explicit thought regularly occurs with no experienced words or images at all ([unsymbolized thinking](#unsymbolized-thinking), discussed below); and reflective life proceeds in people whose inner speech is greatly reduced. That is enough to embarrass the view that inner speech partly constitutes reflective awareness, and it does not require anyone sitting at exactly zero. None of it settles what such a person's experience is *like*. That question is poorly served by self-report, and the Map's own commitments give reason to hold it open rather than close it.

## How Often Inner Speech Actually Occurs

The folk picture is of an unbroken inner monologue, which is why its absence sounds remarkable. Descriptive experience sampling does not find that monologue. Heavey and Hurlburt (2008) collected ten randomly cued moments from each of thirty participants, drawn from a stratified sample of college students, and coded what was present at the cue. Five phenomena recurred: inner speech, inner seeing, feelings, sensory awareness, and unsymbolized thinking. Each occurred in roughly a quarter of sampled moments, with wide variation between individuals. Higher frequencies of inner speech were associated with lower levels of psychological distress.

If that frequency generalises beyond the sampled population, typical people spend most of their waking lives not engaged in inner speech. Absence of inner speech at a given moment is the ordinary case, not the exotic one.

Hurlburt, Heavey and Kelsey (2013) characterised the form. Inner speaking occurred in many but certainly not all moments of sampled experience; its most common form was speaking in one's own naturally inflected voice with no sound produced; and both form and frequency varied substantially across individuals. The authors noted that their findings diverged from earlier research and attributed the divergence to methodological differences — a caution that travels with the numbers.

Alderson-Day and Fernyhough (2015) provide the standard integrative review, covering the developmental-internalisation tradition, typical and atypical populations, and a multicomponent model. Their conclusion is that inner speech performs significant cognitive functions despite the variability observed among individuals and across the lifespan. That is a claim about function, compatible both with the Map's position and with the position the Map rejects.

## The Anendophasia Dispute {#the-dispute}

**What was measured.** Nedergaard and Lupyan (2024) ran four studies comparing a low-inner-speech group (N = 46) with a high-inner-speech group (N = 47), assigned by self-report questionnaire. The low group performed worse on verbal working memory and had more difficulty with rhyme judgements. Task-switching performance and categorical effects on perceptual judgement were unrelated to inner-speech differences. Those four measures are the whole empirical basis. The studies did not measure whether anendophasic people plan, create, or reflect normally, and they did not measure whether phenomenal experience persists in the absence of inner speech. The dissociation between verbal task performance and phenomenal life is exactly the gap a philosophical reader wants closed, and this study does not close it.

**The challenge.** Lind (2025a), in the same journal, accepted the behavioural findings as an original contribution while rejecting the inference from them — together with prior empirical and anecdotal data — to the conclusion that some people have no inner speech at all. His verdict was that no compelling evidence establishes complete absence.

**The concession.** In an unpublished reply, Lupyan and Nedergaard accepted that their data support a continuum of inner-speech experience rather than establishing complete absence, while arguing that self-reports of absent inner speech should be treated as data worth investigating rather than as error. This is the pivotal turn in the exchange, because the concession comes from the coiners. Any article that continues to assert demonstrated absence is asserting something the originating authors have declined to assert. (The reply is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed; the same applies to Lind's subsequent reply (2025b), which argues that *anendophasia* is ambiguous between self-perceived low prevalence and complete lack, and that respondents' presuppositions about their own minds shape their questionnaire answers.)

**The adjudication.** Hurlburt (2026) distinguishes *fidelity* — faithfulness to experience as it actually occurs — from *validity* in the psychometric sense of construct validation, and argues that anendophasia is an experiential phenomenon rather than a construct. On his reading, Nedergaard and Lupyan investigated it as a construct and drew phenomenon-level conclusions, a mismatch of method to claim. He separates *completely* from *mostly* anendophasic individuals, judges that anendophasic or at least mostly anendophasic people do exist and are probably frequent, and observes that in practice the completely/mostly distinction might be impossible to draw.

That last position is the most defensible one currently available, and it belongs to none of the other parties. It supports *many people have little or no inner speech* while withholding *some people demonstrably have none*. The Map adopts that register. The case is open but unsettled, and the honest modal is suggestive rather than demonstrative.

**Why the self-report problem is not a technicality.** Lind's objection is an [[introspection]]-reliability argument, the same worry the Map already takes seriously about imagery self-report. Asking someone to generalise about their own inner life in the abstract is a different epistemic act from asking what was present at a randomly cued moment. That is a general question about first-person data, not a local complaint about one study, and the Map should apply its scepticism symmetrically rather than only where the answer is inconvenient.

## Unsymbolized Thinking: The Sturdier Evidence {#unsymbolized-thinking}

A better-secured finding was available before *anendophasia* was coined and does the same argumentative work without depending on a contested existence claim.

Hurlburt and Akhter (2008) describe **unsymbolized thinking**: the experience of an explicit, differentiated thought that includes no experience of words, images, or any other symbols. They argue it is a distinct phenomenon rather than an incompletely formed inner speech or a vague image, and that it is one of the five most common features of inner experience. They also note that many people, including many professional students of consciousness, believe such an experience to be impossible. On their account, its occurrence indicates that much experienced thinking takes place without any experience of words or other symbols.

Three features make this the stronger evidence base. It is a *positive* phenomenological datum about what thinking is like, rather than an absence inferred from a questionnaire split. It comes from within-subject momentary sampling rather than between-group retrospective self-report, which is the methodological standard the critics of anendophasia are themselves invoking. And it concerns ordinary people rather than a disputed minority, so the constitutive view's difficulty is general rather than confined to a population whose existence can be denied.

The corresponding cost is that the phenomenon is reported through the same introspective channel whose reliability is at issue. Sampling aspires to fidelity by cueing specific moments and training participants, but it has narrowed the question being asked rather than escaped the first-person predicament. The Map treats this as a limitation on the whole class of evidence, not one that afflicts only unwelcome results.

## Three Names for Three Absences

*Aphantasia* was coined in 2015, *anauralia* in 2021, *anendophasia* in 2024: three names in a decade for three phenomenal absences, each introduced on self-report evidence, each raising the same question about how far introspective report can license a claim about what is missing.

Hinwar and Lambert (2021) supply the empirical link between the first two. Across 128 participants including 34 self-reported aphantasics, auditory imagery (BAIS-V) and visual imagery (VVIQ-M) were strongly associated, Spearman's rho = 0.83. Most self-reported aphantasics also reported weak or entirely absent auditory imagery. The associations can dissociate but rarely do: the aphantasic group contained one individual with typical auditory imagery, and the 29-person no-auditory-imagery group contained one individual with typical visual imagery. The authors proposed *anauralia* for the absence of auditory imagery.

Anauralia is not anendophasia. Auditory imagery is broader than inner speech, and inner speech has components — motor, semantic, agentive — that a purely auditory measure does not capture. The two absences are neighbouring rather than identical, and the Map does not treat evidence about one as evidence about the other.

The [[phenomenology-vs-function-axis|phenomenology-versus-function]] structure is shared, and the answers differ in a way worth reporting rather than smoothing over. For [[aphantasia]], the Map's reading is that the missing phenomenology comes with remarkably little deficit at the task level: the cognitive work gets done by other means. For anendophasia, the measured answer is partly the opposite — verbal working memory and rhyme judgement did show group differences, even as task-switching and categorical perception did not. Reduced inner speech appears to cost something specifically verbal. That asymmetry is inconvenient for a tidy story about phenomenology floating free of function, and it is what the data say.

## What This Costs the Constitutive View

Dennett, Carruthers and psychologists in the Vygotskian internalisation tradition hold, in varying forms, that higher-order consciousness — self-reflection, [[metacognition]], narrative identity — requires language, with inner speech serving as a partly constitutive medium rather than a tool. Carruthers (2002) makes the cognitive-functions case in its most explicit form.

The Map's standing reply, developed in [[consciousness-and-language-interface]], is that this confuses correlation with constitution: language and higher-order consciousness co-develop because both require neural maturation and social interaction, not because one generates the other. What the inner-speech literature adds is a burden of explanation that does not rest on the contested case. If inner speech occupies roughly a quarter of sampled moments in typical people, the constitutive view owes an account of what sustains reflective awareness during the other three-quarters. If unsymbolized thinking is a common feature of ordinary experience, it owes an account of explicit differentiated thought that arrives without any symbol at all. And if there are people whose inner speech is greatly reduced and whose reflective lives nonetheless proceed, it owes an account of them too — whether or not anyone turns out to be at zero.

None of these is decisive. A determined defender can reply that unconscious linguistic processing does the constitutive work even when nothing verbal is experienced, which relocates the thesis below the level sampling can reach. That reply is available, and it is the reason this article does not claim the constitutive view has been refuted. It has been made to pay, in explanatory debt, for a picture of the mind that the sampled record does not match.

## What Would Challenge This View?

**1. Sampling could prove unreliable in the relevant way.** Hurlburt's fidelity claim for descriptive experience sampling is a claim, not a demonstration. If cued sampling were shown to systematically manufacture the categories it reports — if participants trained to look for unsymbolized thinking reliably produced it where independent measures found symbolic content — the sturdier evidence base would weaken along with the contested one.

**2. Lind could be right in the strong form.** His argument is live, not dispatched. If further work showed that reports of absent or minimal inner speech track presupposition rather than experience, then the anendophasia case would need to be withdrawn entirely rather than merely softened, and the Map would be left leaning on unsymbolized thinking alone.

**3. Hurlburt could be right that the line is undrawable.** If the *completely*/*mostly* distinction is undrawable as a matter of principle rather than of current method, then no future evidence will establish complete absence, and any argument requiring it is permanently unavailable. The Map's position is built to survive this; arguments elsewhere that lean on the stronger reading are not.

**4. The verbal deficits could turn out to be larger than measured.** Nedergaard and Lupyan found costs on verbal working memory and rhyme judgement. If a broader battery found reflection, planning, and self-evaluation impaired in low-inner-speech populations rather than intact, the tool reading would be under real pressure and the constitutive reading correspondingly strengthened. The nulls on task-switching and categorical perception are the current evidence against this, and they are a thin basis for a general claim.

## Relation to Site Perspective

This material bears on [[tenets|Tenet 1]] (Dualism) through the constitutive question. If reflective consciousness were partly constituted by inner speech, a central part of conscious life would be built out of a linguistic capacity that is plainly a physical-neural achievement, and the Map's claim of irreducibility would lose ground where it matters most. The evidence reviewed here does not establish the Map's position; it removes an obstacle and transfers a burden.

The Map's reading is that inner speech stands in a *use*-relation to consciousness rather than a constitution-relation: consciousness employs language as a representational format, the way a sculptor employs a chisel. Inner speech and its variants illustrate the two layers of semantic content the Map distinguishes — functional and phenomenal — without settling whether those layers in fact come apart. This is the restraint the corpus already applies to large language models, which handle the functional layer with great facility while the phenomenal question stays open, and where the Map's register goes no further than treating workspace-like signatures of access consciousness as observed ([[positions/ai-consciousness-scope|P-AC4]]). Anendophasia is a human instance of the same structural point and inherits the same limit.

[[tenets|Tenet 5]] (Occam's Razor Has Limits) does specific work here. The simplest story about inner experience — that thinking is inner speech, that everyone has it, that its absence would be a dramatic deficit — is tidier than the sampled record and wrong in at least three places. Simplicity was an unreliable guide precisely where the data were thin, which is the tenet's claim.

A framework-relative caution belongs at the end. Nothing in this literature tells us what it is like to be an anendophasic person, or whether anything is. Behavioural measures index verbal task performance; sampling reports index what a trained participant says was present at a cue. The Map's commitment to irreducibility is exactly a commitment that such measures underdetermine the phenomenal facts, so it cannot consistently read a phenomenal conclusion off them when they happen to point the Map's way. What the framework licenses is *withholding* — declining to convert an absence of reported words into a claim about the presence or absence of experience. The discipline is set out in [[evidential-status-discipline]], the general shape of the limit in [[imagery-void]].

One further connection is worth flagging. Inner speech not recognised as one's own is the clearest natural case of a source-attribution failure, which is why the auditory-verbal-hallucination literature intersects this one. The same inner speech can be experienced as self-produced or as alien, so its phenomenal character is not fixed by its linguistic content — a point that supports the use-relation framing and is developed in [[source-attribution-void]].
