A single momentary choice does not make a life. Agency worth the name extends across time—you commit to a career, sustain a relationship, pursue a project through setbacks. This *diachronic agency* requires more than a sequence of disconnected decisions: it requires some structure that connects past choices to present commitments to future intentions. Personal narrative is one important route to that structure and the route this article develops—the medium through which a [[the-self-minimal-narrative-and-substantial|substantial self]] organises its selections into coherent temporal patterns rather than a story told about agency after the fact. It is not the only route. Plans and policies, standing values, semantic self-knowledge, habit, embodiment and social scaffolding can each carry the coordinating load, and some people sustain long commitments while reporting almost no narrative self-experience ([[#non-narrative-routes-to-coordination|discussed below]]). The Unfinishable Map's claim is accordingly the weaker one: diachronic agency requires *some* coordinating structure, and whichever route supplies it, that structure organises the selections of a persisting subject rather than manufacturing one.

## Synchronic and Diachronic Agency

Most philosophical work on [[topics/free-will|free will]] focuses on what might be called *synchronic agency*—the capacity to choose at a moment. Can you select among alternatives right now? Are your current decisions genuinely yours? The [[phenomenology-of-agency-vs-passivity|phenomenology of agency]] confirms that individual moments of choosing have a distinctive experiential character: effort, authorship, the [[phenomenology-of-choice-and-volition|felt narrowing]] of open possibilities to one.

But human agency is not merely momentary. Consider what it takes to write a book. No single decision—choosing a topic, drafting a chapter, revising a paragraph—constitutes the act. The book emerges from thousands of coordinated choices spread across months or years, held together by an intention that persists through interruptions, doubts, and competing demands. The agent who finishes it must be the same agent who started, and must maintain something connecting early choices to later ones.

This is diachronic agency: the capacity to act as a unified agent across time, organising sequences of choices into coherent projects that no single moment contains. It presupposes synchronic agency but adds what synchronic agency alone cannot provide: temporal coherence.

## Narrative as a Structure of Diachronic Agency

What provides this coherence? Personal narrative is the Map's primary answer and the one developed here. Not narrative in the literary sense—not a polished autobiography—but the pre-reflective temporal structure through which an agent understands their choices as forming a connected pattern. This is what the Map calls [[narrative-coherence]]—the intelligible organisation of experience into a unified temporal whole. Other structures can supply the same coherence, and the [[#non-narrative-routes-to-coordination|non-narrative routes]] are set out below.

When you decide to keep working on a difficult project rather than abandon it, you are not making a bare synchronic choice. You are interpreting your present situation through a narrative that connects your past commitment to your current effort to your anticipated completion. The past commitment gives the present effort its meaning. The anticipated future gives both their point. Remove the narrative and the synchronic choice—"keep going or stop?"—loses the context that makes it the choice it is.

[[anoetic-noetic-autonoetic-consciousness|Autonoetic consciousness]] provides the phenomenological machinery for this route. Mental time travel allows you to re-inhabit past decisions with the felt sense that *you* were the one who made them, and to project yourself into future scenarios where *you* will experience the consequences. Where the capacity is intact, this temporal self-projection is much of what makes the narrative route compelling from the inside: past choices are experienced as *yours* and future consequences as *yours to face*, and that experience supplies a large part of the reason to maintain commitments. It is not a precondition of maintaining them. The dissociation cases discussed below hold the same autobiographical facts without any re-experiencing and coordinate extended lives regardless.

Paul Ricoeur's concept of *narrative identity* captures this structure. For Ricoeur, the self is neither a fixed substance nor a mere collection of events but a character constituted through emplotment—the activity of weaving events into a story with a beginning, middle, and anticipated end. The "who" of action emerges through the narrative, not prior to it.

## Against Mere Psychological Continuity

[[parfit-reductionism|Parfit's reductionism]] offers a deflationary account: you are connected to your future self through overlapping chains of memory and intention, and personal identity is nothing "over and above" this psychological continuity. Parfit's deeper point is that identity itself does not matter—what matters is the continuity relation, regardless of whether it constitutes a persisting self.

But even granting Parfit's framework, psychological continuity alone cannot ground diachronic agency. Continuity connects temporal stages without *organising* them. A chain of overlapping memories and intentions links you to your past self, but it does not explain why your choices form coherent projects rather than random sequences. Two agents could have identical psychological continuity—the same memories, the same standing intentions—while one's choices compose a meaningful life and the other's do not.

Consider two people who both remember deciding to become a doctor and both intend to complete their training. One understands the decision as part of a coherent arc: a calling discovered through specific experiences, pursued through specific sacrifices, directed toward specific goals. The other simply finds themselves with medical ambitions they cannot integrate into anything larger. Both are psychologically continuous with their past selves, but only the first has a structure to draw on when obstacles arise—and they always arise. The organised agent can ask "Does this setback threaten the project I am pursuing?" and adjust. The merely continuous agent can only ask "Do I still want to continue?"—a synchronic question that cannot reach the diachronic reasons for perseverance.

A materialist might respond that prefrontal planning networks and hippocampal replay explain narrative organisation without any non-physical substance. The brain's executive functions coordinate goals across time; neural habit formation sustains projects through difficulty. The Map acknowledges these mechanisms as genuine. The question is whether they *constitute* narrative agency or *implement* it—whether the planning network is the agent or the instrument through which the agent plans. The pathological cases discussed below suggest implementation: when neural mechanisms fail, the agent loses *capacity* for diachronic agency while remaining the same conscious subject.

The Buddhist no-self tradition offers a different challenge. Rather than reducing the agent to neural processes, dependent origination denies the agent altogether: each moment arises conditionally from the previous, creating an appearance of unified agency without any persistent self. The Map's response is phenomenological. The capacity to *reorganise* one's commitments—not merely to have them change but to actively revise them, reinterpreting past choices and redirecting future plans—presupposes a subject for whom past, present, and future are simultaneously available. Dependent origination explains succession; it does not explain the agent's synoptic grasp of their own temporal arc.

## Non-Narrative Routes to Coordination

The claim that narrative supplies this organisation is easy to overstate, and the sharpest objection to overstating it is Galen Strawson's. "Against Narrativity" presses two prongs. The descriptive prong denies that human beings generally experience themselves narratively: Strawson distinguishes *diachronic* people, who experience the self as extended through time, from *episodic* people who do not, and counts himself among the latter—"I have absolutely no sense of my life as a narrative with form." The normative prong denies that a life must be narrated to go well. [[narrative-void|The narrative void]] develops the descriptive prong at length, as evidence that narration is built rather than given and therefore varies in depth across minds.

Against a claim that narrative is *constitutive* of diachronic agency, the descriptive prong is decisive, and the Map concedes it. Episodic people sustain careers, relationships and long projects. If they do so without experiencing their lives as stories, then whatever coordinates their choices across time is not autobiographical narrative, and narrative cannot be a condition of extended agency.

Several structures are candidates for the coordinating work, and they are not mutually exclusive. Michael Bratman's planning theory locates diachronic self-governance in plans and policies—intentions that settle matters in advance, resist casual reconsideration, and coordinate an agent with their own future self without any story being told. [[wholeheartedness-void|The Map's reading of Bratman]] is that plans and policies relocate the opacity of identification rather than dissolving it; that reading presupposes they can carry the coordination, which is the point here. Standing values do similar work: a commitment to honesty shapes decades of choices without requiring the agent to emplot them. So does semantic self-knowledge—knowing *that* one is a doctor, a parent, a person who finishes things—which survives when episodic re-experiencing does not. The clearest case is severely deficient autobiographical memory: healthy adults, with no injury and intact semantic memory, who [[episodic-memory#Clinical and Non-Clinical Dissociations|report a lifelong inability to relive their own past]] and run ordinary extended lives anyway. Habit, embodiment and social scaffolding complete the list. An agent held to a project by colleagues, a schedule and a trained body is coordinated by structures no part of which is a self-told story.

The revised claim is that diachronic agency requires *some* coordinating structure, of which narrative is one important and phenomenologically salient route. Where the Map still disagrees with Strawson is downstream of the concession, and that disagreement sits at the framework boundary rather than inside his argument. His positive view is that the subject is a "thin subject"—[[self-and-self-consciousness|short-lived, with no persistence across gaps in consciousness]]. The Map holds instead that the coordinating structures organise the selections of a subject that does persist. Nothing in "Against Narrativity" bears on that question, and nothing in the case developed below refutes the transience thesis from inside Strawson's own commitments. The two disagreements are separable, and running them together would let the anti-narrativity argument look like an objection to the substantial self when it is not.

## Narrative and the Substantial Self

The [[the-self-minimal-narrative-and-substantial|three-layered account of selfhood]] distinguishes the minimal self (bare first-person perspective), the narrative self (autobiographical construction), and the substantial self (irreducible conscious particular). Diachronic agency illuminates the relationship between the latter two layers.

The narrative self, understood as mere construction, seems too fragile to ground diachronic agency. Stories can be rewritten, memories confabulated, life-plans revised. If narrative identity is "all the way down"—if there is nothing beneath the story—then diachronic agency rests on fiction. The agent who perseveres through difficulty does so because of a story that could have been different, and the choice to maintain *this* story rather than *that* one has no deeper anchor.

The substantial self provides the anchor. Narrative is not self-standing but the autobiographical expression of a substantial conscious particular whose [[haecceity]] persists through the narrative's revisions. When your life story changes—through trauma, conversion, radical reinterpretation of your past—*you* persist through the change. The narrative self is revised; the substantial self who revises it continues.

This means diachronic agency has a two-level structure, and the argument generalises beyond the narrative route. At the surface level, the agent organises choices through some coordinating structure—narrative, or the plans and standing values canvassed above—connecting past, present, and future into coherent projects. At the deeper level, the substantial self grounds that structure, providing the continuous conscious particular whose choices it organises. Neither level is sufficient alone. The substantial self without any coordinating structure would make momentary choices but could not compose them into a life. A structure without the substantial self would be an unanchored story, or an unowned plan—a pattern with no one living it.

## The Temporal Phenomenology of Projects

What does diachronic agency feel like from the inside? The [[temporal-consciousness|Bergsonian]] tradition offers resources here. Lived time is not a sequence of discrete moments but a qualitative flow in which the past permeates the present and the future is anticipated. When you are absorbed in a long-term project, you experience what might be called *project-time*: a temporal horizon structured by the project's demands rather than by clock-time.

A musician preparing for a recital experiences the months of practice not as disconnected sessions but as a single unfolding arc. Early technical work, intermediate interpretive exploration, final polishing—each phase colours the next. The present moment is experienced *as* a phase of a larger temporal whole. These extended horizons are built from sustained sequences of moment-to-moment [[temporal-consciousness-structure-and-agency|temporal selections]]—each choice of when to advance, when to pause, when to persist becoming part of the arc, with their kairos moments and felt timing windows the micro-structure from which project-time is composed.

This connects—speculatively—to the [[time-collapse-and-agency|consciousness-constituted time]] framework. If consciousness participates in constituting temporal structure through its involvement in quantum collapse, then diachronic agency may not merely *occur within* time but partially *shape* the temporal structure it inhabits. The core argument does not depend on this quantum hypothesis. But if the hypothesis holds, the agent's sustained intention would do more than fill pre-existing time; it would organise the experienced temporal field around the project's demands, creating the extended "now" within which long-term agency operates.

## Breakdowns of Diachronic Agency

Pathological cases reveal the structure by exhibiting its failures.

**Severe amnesia** disrupts the memory component. A patient who cannot form new long-term memories can make synchronic choices—deciding what to eat, whether to smile at a visitor—but cannot sustain projects across days. Each morning begins without narrative connection to the previous evening. Diachronic agency collapses to a series of disconnected synchronic episodes. Yet the minimal self persists: [[witness-consciousness|experience still belongs to someone]]. What is lost is not selfhood but the temporal organisation that enables extended agency—and the loss is not specific to narrative, since the patient who cannot retain a story cannot retain a plan either.

**Depression** often manifests as narrative collapse. The future appears closed rather than open; past choices seem meaningless rather than purposeful; the present feels disconnected from any larger project. Clinicians recognise this as anhedonia and avolition, but the phenomenological structure is narrative: the agent can no longer emplot their experience into a meaningful temporal arc. Synchronic choices remain possible—you can get out of bed—but they float free of any organising narrative.

**Dissociative identity** presents a different disruption. Multiple narrative identities compete for expression through a single body. Each identity may exercise diachronic agency within its own narrative, but the narratives are mutually inaccessible. The substantial self—if there is one underlying the multiple narratives—faces the question of which narrative expresses its genuine agency.

These breakdowns show how much organising work narrative does in the lives that rely on it. They do not show that narrative was the only structure capable of the work. Each pathology disrupts a coordinating capacity rather than establishing that the capacity had to be narrative: the amnesic patient loses the plans along with the story, and in depression standing values go slack at the same time as the sense of an arc. What the cases establish is that some coordinating structure is required, and that where narrative is the structure carrying that weight, its collapse takes extended agency with it.

## Moral Responsibility Across Time

Diachronic agency grounds a distinctive form of [[moral-responsibility|moral responsibility]], explored further in [[moral-implications-of-genuine-agency]]. You are responsible not only for individual choices but for the projects those choices compose. The person who builds a career on exploitation is responsible for sustaining the pattern, not only for each exploitative act; the person who cultivates a virtue over decades deserves credit for the commitment that produced the acts.

This creates an asymmetry that purely synchronic accounts miss. A single generous act by someone with no pattern of generosity differs morally from the same act performed as part of a lifelong commitment to giving. The acts may be identical in their synchronic properties—same amount donated, same beneficiary, same proximate motivation—but they differ in their diachronic context, and that context is morally relevant.

The Map's framework supports this. If the substantial self is a genuine [[agent-causation|agent-cause]]—not merely a link in a causal chain—then it is responsible not only for individual selections but for the patterns those selections create over time. Diachronic responsibility presupposes diachronic agency, which presupposes some coordinating structure—narrative, plans, standing values—and that structure in turn presupposes a substantial self whose choices it organises.

## Relation to Site Perspective

Diachronic agency connects the Map's commitments in a distinctive way, linking the metaphysics of consciousness to the practical reality of living a life.

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]**: If consciousness were reducible to physical processes, diachronic agency would be nothing more than a complex pattern of neural events—a story the brain tells itself. The narrative would have no author, only a mechanism. The Map's dualism provides the substantial self whose irreducibility grounds the claim that narrative organises genuine agency rather than decorating a deterministic sequence.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]**: Diachronic agency is bidirectional interaction extended across time. Each choice in a sustained project is an instance of consciousness selecting among physical possibilities. The project *as a whole*—the pattern of selections sustained across months or years—represents a higher-order exercise of causal influence that no single selection contains. Narrative is one structure through which consciousness maintains this higher-order influence; plans, standing values and trained habit are others.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]]**: The minimality constraint matters for diachronic agency because it clarifies the *scale* of consciousness's involvement. Each individual choice requires only a tiny quantum-level influence—a selection among physically permitted outcomes. The impressive temporal coherence of diachronic agency emerges not from massive interventions but from sustained sequences of minimal selections, organised by narrative or some other coordinating structure into patterns no single selection contains.

**[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]]**: The objection here is about exclusion, not continuity. An Everettian can grant the agent a branch-local project—a determinate history of choices sustained along one branch, with the psychological continuity and reasons-responsiveness that perseverance involves. What branching cannot grant is that the abandonments did not also happen: every project sustained here is equally abandoned by a counterpart elsewhere, so no commitment excludes the alternative it was a commitment against. That the unchosen be *globally* nonactual is a [[tenets/background-commitments|posit the Map adopts]] rather than a conclusion the agency case delivers, and the disagreement sits at the framework boundary. The Map's rejection of many-worlds preserves the singular temporal trajectory within which diachronic agency operates.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]**: A reductionist might insist that "diachronic agency" is just a description of sequential choices and "narrative" just a cognitive afterthought, with coherence explained by habit, reinforcement, and memory alone. The plural model set out above makes part of this easy to grant—habit really does coordinate choices across time, and the Map now counts it among the routes. What parsimony still cannot absorb is the capacity to *reorganise*: to reinterpret past choices, redirect current efforts, and revise future plans in a single unified act, which needs the synoptic grasp described earlier rather than a mechanism that carries a disposition forward. The simpler account cannot capture what it is like to live a life rather than merely undergo a sequence.
