If consciousness selects among the alternatives that decoherence leaves, where in a conscious life would the selecting happen, could a person find those moments by watching their own mind, and do they fall where choices are made? On the evidence the strong answer fails. Self-observation cannot locate, time or confirm a selection, and nothing shows that selections coincide with felt choice. Three independent results block it: on Tegmark's estimates decoherence in neural tissue runs at least ten orders of magnitude faster than the neural processes a decision is built from, the introspected time of deciding is inferred after the act, and no agent can certify its own causal efficacy from the inside. What survives is narrower. Self-observation sorts decisions into kinds (arbitrary, effortless, effortful, withheld), and The Unfinishable Map's [[post-decoherence-selection|post-decoherence selection]] hypothesis leaves exactly one statistical place where a difference could show: statistics conditioned on the conscious state. A kind of conscious state is a candidate for that conditioning. Whether that exposes the Map to a null result turns on a question its own register has not settled, namely which conditioning contexts count as public; on either answer the phenomenology of choice does not become evidence for anything.

The first five sections report what the sources say. The Map's reading is confined to [[#what-the-map-makes-of-this|What the Map Makes of This]] and to the tenet section after it.

## A Role Without a Clock

Measurement theory gave the observer's self-knowledge a job early on, and never a time at which the job is done. Von Neumann (1932/1955) argued that the world must be divided into an observed part and an observer, and that "The boundary between the two is arbitrary to a very large extent." His chain of possible cuts runs through the instrument and the eye to "his retina, nerve tracts and brain" and ends at the observer's abstract "ego". He stated a psycho-physical parallelism and nowhere said that consciousness causes collapse. A cut that can be placed anywhere along the chain fixes no instant either.

London and Bauer (1939) named the faculty involved. The observer possesses "a characteristic and quite familiar faculty" which they call the "faculty of introspection", and by it "he attributes to himself the right to create his own objectivity—that is, to cut the chain of statistical correlations". Their verb is *cut*, and what they describe reads as an entitlement, though they call it a "creative action" and its causal reading is disputed. The passage identifies no privileged moment and does not say that a mind pushes on a wavefunction. Wigner (1961) used a friend's testimony to the same end: whether the friend saw the flash "was already decided in his mind, before I asked him." That bounds the event from above and dates nothing. Tegmark (2000) records that Wigner later "changed his mind and gave up this idea".

Breuer (1995) supplies the limit. He shows "that it is impossible for an observer to distinguish all present states of a system in which he or she is contained, irrespective of whether this system is a classical or a quantum mechanical one and irrespective of whether the time evolution is deterministic or stochastic." Self-measurement is incomplete on any physics.

## What Decoherence Leaves, and How Fast

Tegmark (2000) calculated decoherence times in the brain of roughly 10⁻¹³ to 10⁻²⁰ seconds, against dynamical timescales of 10⁻³ to 10⁻¹ seconds for neural processes, and concluded that the degrees of freedom relevant to cognition "should be thought of as a classical rather than quantum system". He works a decision example himself. Grant, he says, a superposition "at the level of a single thought or snap decision where the outcome feels unpredictable to us". Such superpositions "decohere already during their process of formation", and "The final result is a statistical mixture of the two states". Tegmark is an opponent of quantum accounts of mind and takes this to show that nothing quantum remains to matter. His 10⁻¹³ figure is for microtubules, and Hagan, Hameroff and Tuszyński (2002) contest it, obtaining 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁴ seconds on other assumptions; his neuron-firing figures of 10⁻¹⁹ to 10⁻²⁰ seconds are untouched by that reply, and the argument here needs only those. Physics offers no decision-sized quantum moment: on Tegmark's figures, between 10¹⁰ and 10¹⁹ decoherence times pass while one neural process unfolds.

## The Grain of Experience and the Time of Deciding

No measure of experience comes near that scale. Pöppel (1997) reports "Functional system states with a duration of 30 ms" and an integration window of about three seconds. Herzog and colleagues (2016) argue that features become conscious together at discrete moments, "sometimes even hundreds of milliseconds after stimuli were presented". Fekete and colleagues (2018) reply that "the data advanced to support discrete perception are in fact compatible with a continuous account of perception". On every figure, experience could register only vast aggregates of physical events.

Introspection also fails as a clock at its own scale. Libet's experiments (Libet 1985) timed the reported urge to move against the readiness potential. Banks and Isham (2009) delayed a feedback tone and the reported time of deciding moved with it: "participants' report of their decision time is largely inferred from the apparent time of response." Matsuhashi and Hallett (2008), probing in real time instead of asking for recall, found that "the mean time of the conscious intention to move was 1.42 s before movement", about a second earlier than the recall method gives. Both studies used simple spontaneous movements; whether the inference effect holds for deliberate choices is untested.

Reports of *what was chosen and how* fare better when an interviewer guides the recall. Petitmengin and colleagues (2013) repeated a choice-blindness experiment with a guided retrospective interview, and "The subjects who were assisted detected the manipulation in 80% of cases." The access is retrospective; it concerns the content and course of a past choice and does not time it.

## Where the Noise Is and Where the Effort Is

Two maps of decision can be drawn, and they do not coincide.

The first is drawn from neural timing. Schurger, Sitt and Dehaene (2012) model the readiness potential as accumulation to a threshold, so that "the precise moment at which the decision threshold is crossed leading to movement is largely determined by spontaneous subthreshold fluctuations in neuronal activity". Their noise is classical, their task is arbitrary self-paced movement, and they separate the crossing from the conscious decision, "the feeling of an urge or intention to move that may or may not play a causal role." Maoz and colleagues (2019) found readiness potentials for arbitrary choices and "strikingly absent for deliberate ones", concluding that "deliberate decisions are generally not driven by random fluctuations. Rather, it is the values of the decision alternatives that mainly drive the decision and ultimately lead to action." They do not say deliberate decisions are determined. Khalighinejad and colleagues (2018) found variability falling before self-initiated action and suggest its "key precursor … may manifest as a reduction in neural noise."

The second map is drawn from experience. James (1890) held that "The immense majority of human decisions are decisions without effort." An express consent "comes in when the neutralization of the antagonistic and inhibitory idea is required", and only in a minority of cases is there the "slow dead heave of the will". Kane (2002) placed indeterminacy exactly there, writing that the "uncertainty and inner tension we feel at such soul-searching moments of self-formation is thus reflected in the indeterminacy of our neural processes themselves." That is a postulate; no measurement accompanies it. Palmer (2025) reports that Kane's latest book gives up the doubled effort of his earlier work, so that if the single effort fails "no choice at all would have been made at that time", and that the luck worry returns: "it seems to be a matter of chance that John did do what he was trying to do".

| Candidate moment | Timescale | Evidence of stochastic timing | How it is experienced |
|---|---|---|---|
| A decoherence event (Tegmark) | 10⁻¹³–10⁻²⁰ s | Yields a mixture | Not at all |
| Threshold crossing in arbitrary action (Schurger) | Late in the readiness potential | Yes, classical fluctuations | An urge; report partly inferred |
| Deliberate choice (Maoz) | Seconds | None found; value-driven | Reasons weighed, often without effort |
| Effortful resolution (James; Kane) | Unmeasured | Asserted by Kane, untested | Rare; felt as one's own work |
| Veto (proposed by Libet) | Until about 200 ms before movement | None found | Withholding |

The veto row needs its hedges. Libet (1985) proposed that "conscious will can function in a permissive fashion"; the veto is often called "free won't", a label that is not his. Schultze-Kraft and colleagues (2016) measured a deadline, cancellation being possible "if stop signals occurred earlier than 200 ms before movement onset", for stops cued from outside. A self-originated veto has not been shown.

## Felt Openness Without Indeterminism

The deflationary rival holds that a choice would feel open to its maker under determinism too. Planck (1932) kept causation universal and argued that the causal method cannot be turned on one's own present act, since the observing subject would be the object, "for no eye can see itself" (p. 163). MacKay (1960) made the point logical: no prediction of a choice can be one the agent is correct to believe beforehand, so the conviction of freedom is "the entirely justifiable corollary of these peculiar logical facts" (p. 37). James conceded half of this already: "Even in effortless volition we have the consciousness of the alternative being also possible", and his verdict was that "the question of free-will is insoluble on strictly psychologic grounds."

## What the Map Makes of This

### Where selection would sit

The Map's [[positions/quantum-interface|quantum-interface register]] prefers post-decoherence selection (P-Q1) because "selection acts on already-decohered branch-outcomes rather than on coherent neural superpositions", and it ranks Stapp's question-choice and quantum-Zeno holding (P-Q4) and Orch-OR (P-Q5) below it. Tegmark's snap decision is then a fair picture of the Map's starting point with the selector removed: two decohered outcomes and no account in the physics of which one the subject finds herself in. He treats the mixture as the end of the matter; the Map reads it as improper, since "decoherence yields an improper mixture rather than realised outcomes with one already picked out". Both accept the timescales.

Two further commitments limit what experience could find. On the Map's ordering, "objective reduction fixes when, where and in which basis every collapse occurs", and consciousness contributes only which outcome is realised in a suitable brain. That ordering still owes a combined law, but as it stands the occasion is physics' to set. And the [[positions/agency-and-will|agency register]] places contact at the volition (P-A6: "the trying is the minimal contact point at which selection-only biasing operates"), while conceding that "no measurement distinguishes a basic-act trying from a sufficiently fine-grained event-causal antecedent". The Map names a locus and owns that the naming is metaphysical.

### The open fork

How many selections does one decision contain? Speculatively, either selection runs at something like the decoherence rate, in which case no selection is individually experienced and a felt choice is at most the aggregate of an astronomical number of them; or there is one selection per coarse-grained alternative, such as a firing pattern or a threshold crossing, in which case something must say what counts as an alternative. On the Map's reading, [[quantum-darwinism-and-consciousness|quantum Darwinism]] makes the second horn available without requiring it. Under P-Q10, "The Map does not currently possess a worked formal model", so it cannot say which horn holds, and this article does not choose. Only on the second horn could a selection have a decision-sized grain at all.

The two maps above sharpen the difficulty. The one physically specified, decision-sized stochastic event is Schurger's threshold crossing, found where experience reports least authorship. The moments experience marks as most one's own, effortful and deliberate, show no noise signature in the studies read. The Map's natural candidate, the effortful trying, is therefore unevidenced as a site of indeterminacy. P-A3's claim that "consciousness selects among brain-prepared options" survives the timing data, as that entry argues; it gains no positive support from them.

### Answering Planck and MacKay

On openness the Map has no reply, and should not manufacture one. Breuer's result is, on the Map's reading and not his, Planck's point in theorem form: an embedded observer cannot fully read its own state whether or not its dynamics are open, so meeting that limit carries no information about indeterminism. Felt openness is conceded as non-discriminating. Effort is not predicted by the self-observation limit, but cost-signal accounts fit its distribution, as [[phenomenology-of-choice-and-volition|the Map's page on volition]] already grants. The register says as much at P-A1: "the deliberative phenomenology alone settles nothing between libertarian and compatibilist readings".

What remains is a question the rival leaves open on its own terms. MacKay's analysis shows that felt freedom does not *need* physical indeterminacy; it does not show there is none, and he says so: "nothing physically odd need accompany a free action, though we have no way as yet of knowing whether it does" (pp. 39–40). Whether anything does is a statistical question. If no statistical difference is ever found, that channel closes, at a cost the register has not settled; if P-Q9's mechanism and psychophysical channels close too, the disagreement between the Map and Planck sits at bedrock: whether a selection occurs at all would be a commitment the tenets carry and experience cannot adjudicate.

### What self-observation cannot do

P-A4 states the limit: "no agent can certify its own causal efficacy by introspection, because every such check is performed by the faculty whose causal status is in question". The limit is symmetric, since "epiphenomenalism cannot verify itself either", so nothing here concedes that consciousness is inert. The claim is that efficacy is not verifiable from the first person, by anyone, for either answer. The timing studies add that the report is not even a reliable timestamp. Self-observation classifies episodes without detecting selections.

### What could be tested

Under P-Q2, "outcome frequencies stay Born-exact in every publicly conditionable context; only conditionals on the conscious state itself are free". The Map's bias is per outcome; in the tenets' words it "acts on which single outcome is realised, not on the aggregate measure". P-Q9 names the remaining exposure: "a deviation conditioned on intention, task or subject would test the corridor itself", and "No brain-internal grain has yet been tested". A conscious state has to be classified before anything can be conditioned on it, and the first-person kinds are candidate classifications: effortful against effortless, completed against withheld, and arbitrary against deliberate, though Maoz sets the last by task design, without self-report. Kinds differ physically, as Maoz's signature shows, so a raw difference shows nothing. The test is the residual one P-Q9 states: dependence of outcome on kind "surviving conditionalisation on the measured physical state". No experiment of that form has been specified, and without a worked model (P-Q10) its outcome variable is undefined.

Two cautions keep this honest. First, the register does not say with one voice what such a test would show. On P-Q9's wording a preregistered null at a well-defined kind counts against the Map, since closing this channel moves the framework "toward the global shield it denies being". On P-Q2's wording a recorded kind is a public label, the default corridor predicts exactly that null, and a positive result would leave the corridor and revise P-Q2. The entries disagree about the grain at which Born statistics are preserved. Pending a settlement, the proposal has empirical bite on the first reading only. Second, the categories inherit introspection's unreliability: a noisy classification weakens the test, and Petitmengin's result, which concerns a past choice's content, not its kind, is at most a reason to hope guided recall reduces the noise.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]].** Nothing in this article argues for irreducibility. Breuer's limit holds for classical systems, so the self-opacity of an observer is no mark of the non-physical. The tenet is presupposed here and untouched.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]].** The tenet constrains the channel, and the timescale gap is why the Map places selection after decoherence. The phenomenology of choice is not evidence for the tenet. Self-observation's contribution is to say which conditionals a test should use, with the null as a live risk.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]].** The Map holds that consciousness does causal work and holds, with P-A4, that this cannot be read off experience. Whether selections coincide with felt choices is open: the Map's locus is the trying, its physics sets the occasion, and no model connects the two.

**[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]].** The Map reads the decohered mixture as awaiting a single actual outcome; Tegmark, with the same numbers, does not. Finding oneself with one decision made is what both readings predict, so the experience of choosing does not separate them.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]].** The Planck–MacKay account is simpler and is not dismissed for that, nor is selection preferred for its richness. The tenet binds the Map's reasoning in both directions and leaves the question to the test.
