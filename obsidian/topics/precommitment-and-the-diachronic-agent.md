Precommitment is acting at one time to change what one will do later, through a causal route outside the agent: Ulysses has himself bound to the mast so that his later wish to steer for the Sirens cannot reach the ship's course. Jon Elster's 1979 definition requires the binding act to "set up some causal process in the external world", which excludes internal personal rules. On that definition precommitment is the one self-control strategy, among those The Unfinishable Map discusses, built to remove or price the agent's later override. If-then plans, resolutions and [[skill-delegation|skill delegation]] all keep that override. The contrast follows from the definition rather than from any measurement.

The literature reads binder and bound as two agents, as one imperfectly rational agent, or as bargaining interests ([[#three-readings|explained below]]), and its economic models need no fact about personal identity. The Map holds, as a commitment rather than a finding, that binder and bound are one persisting subject. That makes precommitment self-government, but it does not settle whose verdict should prevail when the two disagree, which, on one recent survey, the advance-directive debate mostly decides without appeal to identity. Any conscious contribution to a bound outcome would sit at the act of binding ([[#placement|explained below]]). A physical planner precommits equally well, so the placement is fit, not evidence. The field record on commitment devices is mixed: take-up is often low, average effects are real, and many binders mis-size their own bonds.

## What Precommitment Is {#definition}

The economist Robert Strotz named the strategy. An agent who foresees his preferences shifting "may 'precommit' his future behaviour by precluding future options so that it will conform to his present desire as to what it should be" (Strotz 1955–56, p. 165). He called it "the strategy of precommitment" (p. 173).

For Elster, Ulysses' predicament, "being weak and knowing it", called for a theory of imperfect rationality (1979, ch. II, p. 36). His definition, "a first approximation", includes, among others, three conditions: one carries out a decision in order to raise the probability of carrying out another later (i); its effect "must be to set up some causal process in the external world" (iii); and it "must be an act of commission, not of omission" (v). Condition (iii) "also excludes what Ainslie calls 'private side bets'". Elster revisited the subject in 2000, revising earlier claims about constitutions and marriage; the definition here is the 1979 one.

Bryan, Karlan and Nelson define a commitment device as "an arrangement entered into by an agent who restricts his or her future choice set by making certain choices more expensive, perhaps infinitely expensive", with two further conditions, one excluding strategic purposes toward others (2010, p. 673). Ulysses' ropes set an infinite price; a forfeitable deposit sets a finite one.

Two terms need fixing. Elsewhere on the Map, "self-binding" usually names the symmetric discipline [[tenets#^occams-limits|Tenet 5]] imposes on parsimony arguments; here it means binding one's own later conduct. "Diachronic" means extended across time, as in [[diachronic-agency-and-personal-narrative|diachronic agency]].

Three neighbouring strategies work inside the agent and so fall outside condition (iii): Richard Holton's resolutions ([[akrasia-and-weakness-of-will|the akrasia article]]), Michael Bratman's plans and policies ([[diachronic-agency-and-personal-narrative|the diachronic-agency article]]) and George Ainslie's personal rules ([[the-divided-will]]). Each can be dropped when the time comes, which is how each fails. The line is definitional, not a disagreement about cases.

## Three Readings of Binder and Bound {#three-readings}

**Two agents.** For Strotz the agent jettisons yesterday's plan because "to-day he is a different person with a new discount function" (p. 173). For Thomas Schelling, "People behave sometimes as if they had two selves", and "The two are in continual contest for control" (1980, pp. 95–96). Each second self is a modelling device, defined by a shifted discount function or marked by "as if". None of the economic texts cited here claims that two numerically distinct subjects are present.

**One imperfectly rational agent.** Elster's Ulysses, "being weak and knowing it", achieves by indirect means what a fully rational agent would do directly. Bryan, Karlan and Nelson likewise locate the need for commitment devices in "intrapersonal conflict" (p. 672).

**Interests rather than selves.** Ainslie's hyperbolically discounting interests bargain by recursive self-prediction rather than by negotiation between literal future selves ([[the-divided-will#five-rivals|the divided-will article]]). Since condition (iii) excludes private side bets, Ainslie and Elster mostly describe different tools.

**The idiom as an identity claim.** It becomes one in Derek Parfit's Russian nobleman (1973), as Elster quotes the passage (2000, §I.6). A young socialist due to inherit vast estates fears his ideals will fade. He "signs a legal document which will automatically give away" the land and asks his wife to promise not to consent to its revocation. "He might add, 'I regard my ideals as essential to me. If I lose these ideals, I want you to think that I cease to exist.'" The disowning is the nobleman's self-interpretation inside Parfit's case, and Parfit's [[parfit-reductionism|reductionism about identity]] is a view the Map rejects. Elster's gloss is that "He intends to neutralize the effect of a preference change, not to prevent it." Annette Dufner, following Parfit, states what turns on the case: "If psychological continuity is relevant here, she would still have to be seen as bound by her commitment. If connectedness is relevant, she might not be" (Dufner 2025).

## The Override Removed {#override-removed}

On [[self-control-as-skill-and-scaffolding#where-selection-sits|the Map's reading]] of the self-control literature, ordinary success happens at moments of policy, each of which keeps a later override. An if-then plan hands a response to a cue that the agent can still decline. [[skill-delegation|Skill delegation]] makes the override definitional: "The defining feature of delegation—as opposed to mere loss—is that the delegator retains the capacity to re-engage." A resolution can be abandoned.

Precommitment runs its effect through the world, so the later override is removed or priced by design. On the bound matter there may be nothing left to choose, or only a costlier menu. The boundary is not sharp everywhere. On the Map's reading, situation modification that removes an option from the world, as clearing the house of cigarettes does, meets condition (iii) and so counts as precommitment, as does Ainslie's own first tactic, extrapsychic commitment. The exclusivity claim is scoped to the strategies the Map discusses (situation selection, if-then plans, Ainslie's four tactics, Holton's resolutions and skill delegation).

The bound preferences stay live. At the mast Ulysses hears the song and orders his release, and Strotz notes that later regret "(at least for the moment) is to be expected, for otherwise precommitment would have had no purpose" (p. 173).

## Identity and Authority {#identity-authority}

Two questions are easily run together: whether the bound person is the binder, and whose verdict should govern when they disagree.

Schelling posed the second for someone who asks in advance to be helped to die "if certain conditions befall me" and warns others not to heed the later self "who might surface and speak with my voice". He asks how anyone can tell "whether this is the moment of truth or the moment of derangement", calls this "the problem of authenticity", and offers no general test for it (1980, p. 104).

In the advance-directive debate, Dufner reports that "not a lot of people have explicitly argued that it is identity as such that matters here (for an exception see Dresser 1986)". In her summary, Ronald Dworkin argues that dementia patients cannot form or revise what he calls critical interests, while Agnieszka Jaworska holds that what matters is the patient's remaining "capacity to value". The dispute is open, and its main lines turn on capacity and interests rather than on sameness of person.

In bipolar disorder, a self-binding directive is one by which people "commit themselves to treatment during future episodes of mania, even if unwilling" (Gergel & Owen 2015, abstract). In a UK survey, "463 (82%) of the 565 participants endorsed self-binding directives", most of them citing "a determinate shift to distorted thinking and decision making when unwell" (Gergel et al. 2021). The respondents came from a charity's mailing list and reported their own diagnoses, so these are not population rates. Spellecy (2003) argues that the charge of strong paternalism against Ulysses contracts "can be answered", justifying some enforcement "under the rubric of weak paternalism". This literature treats the well and the ill person as one rights-holder whose capacity fluctuates, and when such directives should be enforced remains contested.

## The Binder's Poor Model of the Bound {#field-record}

Strotz already saw the puzzle: "What needs to be explained is not that people do precommit their future actions, but that the practice is not still more wide-spread" (p. 173).

Two flagship field trials found low take-up and real effects. A commitment savings account in the Philippines was accepted by 28.4 percent of those offered it, and at twelve months "average savings balances increased by 81 percentage points" for those offered it, relative to controls (Ashraf, Karlan & Yin 2006, abstract). A smoking-cessation contract was taken up by 11 percent, and those offered it were 3 percentage points more likely to pass a six-month test (Giné, Karlan & Zinman 2010, abstract).

Many binders also mis-size their bonds. In one trial, "While the average effect on bank savings is large, 55% of clients default and incur monetary losses"; the author suggests the chosen penalties were too low, and finds the correlations of measured sophistication with take-up and default consistent with predictions that "partial sophisticates", who foresee their future bias only in part, "adopt weak commitments and then default" (John 2020, abstract). In a gym-attendance study, take-up was high and raised exercise, but about half of those who took contracts for higher attendance also took contracts for lower attendance, take-up showed "little association" with measured actual or perceived time inconsistency, and the authors' structural estimates imply that offering the contracts lowered consumer surplus (Carrera et al. 2022, abstract). No meta-analysis was consulted, so the record is reported as mixed, without a pooled effect.

## Where a Conscious Contribution Would Sit {#placement}

This section gives the Map's interpretation; none of the authors above argues for it, or for a non-physical selector.

**At the binding.** If a non-physical selector contributes to the bound outcome, its contribution is the earlier act of commission. In the self-control article's terms, the binding is a moment of policy with no retained override, the limiting case of that scheme.

**Structuring, by analogy only.** The self-control article reads forming an if-then plan as what Dretske (1988) calls a [[causal-relevance-of-content#response-families|structuring cause]] of the cued act, so that the selector's relevance would then be past-tense unless it exercises its retained override. A binding device fits only by analogy, since the structuring Dretske credits to reasons is learning that recruits a state to cause a movement, while a device removes or prices options in the world and so structures which effects the later wish can have. The cost carries over without the escape clause, since on the bound matter there is no override to exercise. In [[causal-relevance-of-content#causal-history-reply|the causal-relevance article]]'s words, history "defeats the idleness inference but establishes no efficacy".

**Neither idle nor proven.** Externalising the binding does not show that the decision to bind was idle, and the bound will stays causally live: Ulysses' later will moves his voice and limbs, and the device blocks only its route to the outcome. Nor does the deliberateness of binding show that consciousness does it. Felt effort may be a cost signal ([[self-control-as-skill-and-scaffolding#effort-as-evidence|the self-control article]]), and conscious efficacy cannot be verified from the first person ([[agency-void]]).

**Steward's settler, traded away.** [[the-divided-will#luck-objection|The divided-will article]] reports Helen Steward's argument that weakness of will may be "essential to the very existence of such an agent" (2012, p. 161), and glosses the reason in its own words: a settler of matters at the time of action "must be able not to act on an intention already formed". Steward distinguishes her view sharply from Cartesian dualism. On the Map's reading, precommitment is an agent using its earlier settling to suspend its later settling on one matter. That fits a settler and a physical planner equally, and it moves the luck objection's contrastive question to the earlier time.

**The fit discriminates nothing.** Mis-sized commitments fit the self-control article's "candidate-relative, low-bandwidth and fallible" selector and a fallible physical planner equally, and Strotz's consumers are physical planners in formal models that predict precommitment with no experiential term.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]** gains a location without support. Whether a selector acts at all remains "a posit the interface argument leaves open, not a result it secures" ([[tenets#^tenet-3-standing|Tenet 3's standing]]), and precommitment neither requires nor reveals one.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]]** gains nothing from the economy of binding, in which one choice governs many later ones. That is fewness of interventions, not the empirical-constraint minimality the tenet names, and a physical controller enjoys the same economy.

**[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]].** Whether the man begging at the mast is the one who arranged the bonds has, the Map holds, a fact of the matter. That fact comes from the [[tenets/background-commitments|determinate-subject posit]], whose diachronic persistence the Map holds as a commitment rather than a finding: rejecting many-worlds secures only indexical identity—a single bearer of *this* experience—and branch-relative accounts deliver determinate continuity facts too. Against the two-self idiom of Strotz, Schelling and the nobleman, the posit denies that two subjects are present. That makes precommitment self-government rather than paternalism over another person, and it leaves the authority question open, since, on Dufner's survey, the advance-directive literature mostly does not rest a directive's force on identity. Nor is precommitment evidence for persistence, because the economic models run on any account of identity. A Madhyamaka-style analysis, on which the "I" is an aggregate of conditions with no further fact to persist, remains a bedrock disagreement ([[buddhism-and-dualism]]).

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** neither gains nor loses, since the precommitment literature is silent on whether experience reduces to physical process. **[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]** keeps the Map from dismissing a conscious contribution because a physical planner explains precommitment more simply, and gives it no reason to posit one where those models suffice.
