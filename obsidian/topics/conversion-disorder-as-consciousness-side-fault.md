Conversion disorder — known in DSM-5 as functional neurological symptom disorder (FND) — supplies the catalogue's cleanest *architectural-decoupling* exhibit, with the load-bearing caveat that the architecture is the robust finding and the consciousness-side-fault *reading* of it is a further, contestable step the data do not force. The phrase names a precise diagnostic situation. A patient cannot move an arm, cannot see, or cannot stand, yet the motor cortex, the motor neurons, the muscles, and the sensory pathways are all intact and capacity is demonstrably preserved under some task conditions — distraction, involuntary recruitment, sedation, hypnosis. The deficit is not degradation of the substrate; the substrate executes when conscious volition is not directed *at* the affected part. The discriminating evidence is suggestive but thin: under propofol sedation or hypnosis the "paralysed" limb can move, on the strength of small uncontrolled series rather than a settled clinical literature. The motor system executes commands when consciousness is *sedated or disconnected*, but not when consciousness is *awake and functionally severed* from the channel. That same pattern is, however, fully describable as an intra-cerebral inhibitory process; modern clinical practice reads FND as a positive, rule-in neuropsychiatric diagnosis (attention-driven dysfunction, automatic movement, heterogeneous mechanisms, comorbidities), not as a proof of a consciousness-to-brain interface. The architectural decoupling is the evidence; the interface localisation is the Map's interpretation, developed and discounted below.

That asymmetry is what makes conversion disorder an exhibit rather than a curiosity. It is the third route of a three-route convergence the Map relies on: structural disconnection (lesion, as in alien hand syndrome), pharmacological disconnection (anaesthesia and its [[active-reboot|active reboot]]), and psychogenic disconnection (conversion disorder) all reach the same finding — that the channel between conscious volition and motor execution is a separable, independently-failable component of the interface. In the psychogenic case the failure is realised as *active over-generation* of a precise inhibitory prior the system fulfils (the Bayesian-FND account, below), so "the channel is separable" is an architectural-level description whose physical realisation is additive rather than a passive break. Conversion disorder reaches that finding through *neither* structural damage *nor* drug administration, which is what gives the convergence its evidential bite.

## The Clinical Picture: Rule-In, Not Rule-Out

The defining feature of modern FND diagnosis is that it is a *positive* diagnosis. For most of the twentieth century, "conversion" or "hysteria" was a diagnosis of exclusion — what remained after organic disease was ruled out. The contemporary framework, developed largely by Jon Stone and colleagues, inverts this. FND is diagnosed by *rule-in* signs: positive clinical findings demonstrating internal inconsistency or incompatibility with recognised neurological disease (Stone, Carson & Sharpe, 2005).

The DSM-5 criteria codify this shift. Functional neurological symptom disorder requires (a) at least one symptom of altered voluntary motor or sensory function, and (b) *clinical findings providing evidence of incompatibility between the symptom and recognised neurological or medical conditions*. DSM-5 also dropped the DSM-IV requirement that a psychological stressor be identifiable. The diagnostic weight now rests on demonstrable incompatibility, which is the consciousness-side-fault signature stated in clinical terms.

The rule-in signs are themselves the evidence. **Hoover's sign** is the paradigm case for functional leg weakness: hip extension in the "paralysed" leg is weak on direct voluntary testing but returns to normal strength involuntarily when the patient flexes the *opposite* hip against resistance. The muscle, the nerve, and the cortical output are all intact — the strength is present, but only when volition is not directed *at* the affected limb. The one prospective test of the sign in routine practice — an unblinded cohort of 337 patients presenting with suspected stroke — put Hoover's sign at 63% sensitivity (95% CI 24–91) and 100% specificity (95% CI 97–100) for functional leg weakness (McWhirter, Stone, Sandercock & Whiteley, 2011). The wide sensitivity interval reflects the eight functional cases the estimate rests on: the sign is well characterised as highly specific and only loosely characterised as sensitive. Other rule-in signs follow the same logic: a functional tremor entrains to or disappears during voluntary rhythmic movement of the unaffected hand; a functional seizure shows a normal video-EEG during the event. In each case the capacity is demonstrably preserved and emerges precisely when conscious effort is redirected away from the deficit.

This is the clinical structure that the *consciousness-side-fault* description captures. The deficit is not in the capacity but in the *access* — and the access fails as a function of where conscious volition is directed, which is why distraction, involuntary recruitment, sedation, and hypnosis can all restore the capacity that direct conscious command cannot.

## The Sedation and Hypnosis Observations, and What They Are Worth

The most suggestive observation about conversion paralysis is that the affected limb can move under conditions where the *awake, intending* subject is removed from the loop. Two such conditions are reported, and the evidential grade of each matters as much as its direction.

Under **sedation**, movement can return. The systematic source is Stone, Hoeritzauer, Brown & Carson (2014), who sedated eleven patients with severe functional paralysis or fixed dystonia using propofol: five of the eleven were cured or had major improvement. That is an uncontrolled case series whose own authors conclude the technique "deserves randomized evaluation" — and it is *sedation*, not general anaesthesia. Under **hypnosis**, the dissociation appears from the other direction: hypnotic suggestion can reproduce a paralysis in healthy subjects, and the imaging of hypnotic paralysis resembles the imaging of conversion paralysis. That resemblance rests on two single-case PET studies — Marshall et al. (1997) on conversion paralysis and Halligan, Athwal, Oakley & Frackowiak (2000) on hypnotic paralysis — which share two authors and a paradigm, so their agreement is not the independent replication it can be mistaken for, and the hypnotic study drew critical correspondence in the same journal.

The structure those observations point at is this. In organic paralysis from a motor-pathway lesion, the limb does not move under sedation or hypnosis either — the substrate is damaged, and no change in the subject's conscious state can restore a capacity the hardware no longer supports. In conversion paralysis it can move, because the hardware is intact and the fault lies in the awake subject's access to it. The shape of that contrast is worth having; its evidential grade is one small uncontrolled series plus two single cases, which is a promising dissociation rather than a demonstrated one.

Two disciplines apply. The rule-in signs, which have group-level backing, carry more of the architectural weight than this test does. And even granting the observation, reading it as localising the fault to a consciousness-to-brain *interface* rather than to an intra-cerebral inhibitory process is a further step it does not compel — a purely intra-cranial account (below) appears to predict the same sedation and hypnosis findings.

## What Neuroimaging Shows

The functional-imaging literature on conversion paralysis has matured from single-case PET studies into group functional-connectivity findings across two decades, and parts of it have replicated.

The foundational case is Marshall, Halligan, Fink, Wade & Frackowiak (1997), who used PET to study a woman with functional left-leg paralysis. When she *attempted* to move the paralysed leg, primary motor cortex did not activate; instead, the right orbitofrontal and anterior cingulate cortices activated — regions associated with action inhibition and emotional regulation. The motor system was not failing to receive a command; a higher-order region appeared to be *suppressing* the command before it reached motor output.

Vuilleumier (2005) synthesised the picture across symptom types: selective decreases in frontal and subcortical (basal ganglia–thalamocortical) circuits during hysterical motor deficits, in somatosensory cortices during hysterical anaesthesia, and in visual cortex during hysterical blindness — with the affected primary regions structurally intact. The pattern is a *functional decoupling* between intention/limbic systems and the executing cortex, not degradation of that cortex.

The connectivity-era follow-up sharpens this. Voon, Brezing, Gallea & Hallett (2011) found aberrant activity in the supplementary motor complex and elevated limbic (amygdala) engagement during motor preparation in motor conversion disorder, consistent with abnormal top-down limbic influence on motor planning. Across the 2014–2024 functional-connectivity literature, a recurring network signature has emerged: increased connectivity between the amygdala and the supplementary motor area — the one element with a direct independent replication — abnormal temporoparietal junction (TPJ) coupling, the TPJ being central to the *sense of agency*, and altered communication between limbic, motor, and self-agency networks. The recurring theme is that conversion motor symptoms involve disrupted *communication* within an intact set of regions, with the disruption concentrated at the junction between limbic/self-agency processing and motor execution. The picture is not uniform, and the non-uniformity should be stated rather than smoothed: amygdala *responses* have come out normal or hypoactive in some functional-movement-disorder and functional-seizure cohorts, most studies compare patients only against healthy controls, and few adjust for the mood and anxiety comorbidities that are near-ubiquitous in FND. The connectivity signature is a recurring finding, not a settled one.

The current mechanistic synthesis has a cognitive-psychology precursor in Brown (2004), whose integrative model set out to explain how compelling symptoms can exist in the absence of organic pathology; Edwards, Adams, Brown, Pareés & Friston's (2012) Bayesian account of "hysteria" gives that project a predictive-processing formalism, reframing the deficit as *active generation* rather than passive failure: an abnormally precise intermediate-level motor prior is fulfilled by the system, so the symptom is the brain's own confident prediction overriding sensory evidence. The increased amygdala→motor coupling is the affective precision-weighting that installs the prior. This matters for the calibration discipline below: active generation is a clean intra-cerebral mechanism, so the imaging does not by itself favour a consciousness-side channel over a within-brain inhibitory-cum-predictive loop.

What the imaging adds is *intactness with anomalous control*: the primary motor and sensory cortices retain their capacity, and what fails is the coupling that would let directed conscious volition recruit them — the imaging-level statement of what Hoover's sign shows at the bedside.

## Three Mechanically Distinct Routes, One Architectural Finding

Conversion disorder earns its place in the catalogue by completing a convergence. Three perturbation routes, sharing no mechanism, reach the same conclusion about the descending motor channel.

| Route | Mechanism | Exhibit | What it shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Structural** | Lesion / disconnection | Alien hand syndrome | The motor-command channel can be severed by physical damage while motor capacity persists |
| **Pharmacological** | Receptor-level chemical interruption | Anaesthesia / active reboot | The channel can be closed by drugs and reopened through a dedicated molecular pathway |
| **Psychogenic** | Functional decoupling via over-generation of an inhibitory prior, no structural damage | Conversion disorder | The channel can fail without lesion or drug — purely as a function of the awake subject's directed volition |

[[neurological-dissociations-as-interface-architecture#Motor Descending — Alien Hand Syndrome|Alien hand syndrome]] is the structural route: damage to the corpus callosum or supplementary motor area severs the channel through which conscious intention governs motor output, and the hand acts purposefully while the patient disowns the action. Conversion disorder is the inverse presentation through a non-structural route — capacity intact, but conscious command cannot recruit it because the system is fulfilling a competing inhibitory prior. Alien hand shows action without conscious selection; conversion shows conscious selection without action. Both isolate the same channel by showing it can fail independently of the motor substrate.

The pharmacological route runs through [[anaesthesia-and-the-consciousness-interface|anaesthesia]] and the [[active-reboot|active-reboot]] finding. Agents that close the channel through mechanistically distinct routes nonetheless emerge through a shared KCC2-degradation pathway (Hu et al., 2023) — dedicated reopening machinery, across a span best read as three mechanism classes plus one within-class replication rather than the four the agent count invites (see [[cross-mechanism-convergence]]). That machinery is neural throughout, and describing it as machinery *of an interface* is interpretation laid over a wholly physical finding. The parallel to conversion disorder therefore holds at the architectural level only: the *same channel* can be closed and reopened by chemical means, with motor capacity preserved throughout.

The convergence is what the [[epistemology-of-convergence-arguments#compound-signatures-convergence-without-a-shared-mechanism|epistemology of convergence arguments]] calls a *compound signature*: agreement among routes that share no mechanism, so it cannot be one upstream cause sampled three times. Lesion, receptor pharmacology, and psychogenic decoupling are mechanistically disjoint by construction, and because the psychogenic route requires neither damage nor drug, it is the one that most directly excludes "the perturbation broke the substrate." The convergence earns the *architecture* robustly; it does not, on its own, settle that architecture's dualist significance.

## What Conversion Disorder Cannot Deliver

A consciousness-side-fault exhibit is only as good as its honesty about what it does not show. Three limits matter.

**The psychogenic-vs-organic discriminating-test design space is still active.** The rule-in signs discriminate *functional from organic* with high specificity in well-selected cases, and the sedation observation points the same way on much weaker evidence, but no single test is decisive across all presentations and misdiagnosis in both directions is documented. The clinical reality is a battery of signs with varying sensitivity, not a clean dichotomy. The Map's interest in conversion disorder as evidence depends on the rule-in framework holding, and that framework is robust but not yet complete — a point the [[falsification-roadmap-for-the-interface-model|falsification roadmap]] should track, because a deterioration in the rule-in evidence base would weaken this exhibit.

**The interface reading is not forced by the imaging.** The Marshall and Vuilleumier findings — orbitofrontal/cingulate inhibition of motor output, limbic–motor decoupling — seem fully describable as an *intra-cerebral* inhibitory process: one brain region suppressing another, with no commitment to a non-physical volitional channel. "Directed volition cannot recruit the limb" redescribes without loss as "the inhibitory circuit engages specifically when attention is directed at the affected part" — a claim entirely within the physical brain, and one a sophisticated functionalist can extend to the whole conversion picture. The consciousness-side-fault *description* is accurate at the level of substrate-intact decoupling; the *interface localisation* is the contestable step.

**Conversion disorder is dissociable from depression and suggestibility in some studies but not all.** If conversion symptoms were simply a downstream expression of depression or of high hypnotic suggestibility, the exhibit would lose its specificity — it would be evidence about mood or trait suggestibility, not about the volitional channel. Reported rates of comorbid dissociative conditions in FND vary widely: a 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis found prevalence spanning roughly 8% to 80% depending on subtype, population, and assessment method (Campbell et al., 2022). That spread is itself the point, and elevated hypnotic suggestibility behaves similarly — clearest in dissociative disorders, more variable in FND. The heterogeneity cuts both ways. It protects the exhibit from reduction to a mood or suggestibility artefact where the dissociation is clean; it also means any claim built on "conversion disorder shows X" must be scoped to the presentations where the consciousness-side-fault signature is unambiguous.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Map reads conversion disorder as bearing on two tenets, with the evidential weight carefully partitioned between a robust architectural layer and a live-hypothesis interpretive layer, per the [[evidential-status-discipline|evidential-status discipline]].

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]].** The consciousness-side-fault signature — intact motor substrate, capacity recoverable under sedation or hypnosis, deficit indexed to the awake subject's directed volition — is among the more suggestive clinical patterns for an interface between conscious volition and motor execution. The architectural finding (the descending channel is a separable, independently-failable component) sits at the *strongly-supported* tier, but the support is unevenly distributed and the distribution matters: the rule-in signs and the group connectivity work carry it, while the sedation recovery — five of eleven patients in an uncontrolled series its authors want randomised — is a *realistic possibility* on its own and must not be quoted as though it were the robust part. The Map's reading of *what that separability might signify* — that the failing component could be a genuine consciousness-to-brain interface rather than an intra-cerebral inhibitory loop — sits at the *live-hypothesis* tier. The robustness of the architecture must not be allowed to lend its weight to the interface reading; sophisticated functionalism appears to predict the identical clinical and imaging picture, and conversion disorder alone supplies no discriminator between the two.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]].** Conversion disorder is most directly a datum about the *descending* direction — consciousness-to-brain causation. It reveals that the channel through which conscious volition issues motor commands has components that can fail independently of the motor hardware, which is what an account of downward causation through a structured interface predicts. The reading is not forced: an epiphenomenalist or functionalist redescribes the failure as altered connectivity within the physical domain, with the felt "I cannot move it" tracking the inhibitory state rather than causing anything. The Map registers conversion disorder as consistent with bidirectional interaction and as raising the explanatory cost on accounts that deny any volitional channel, without treating it as a demonstration that closes the question.
