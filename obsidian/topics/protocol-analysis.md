**Protocol analysis** treats what people say while working on a task as data, to be explained by a model of how the words were produced. On the model K. Anders Ericsson and Herbert A. Simon proposed (1980; book 1984, revised 1993), a verbal report is a valid trace of thought when subjects voice information they are already attending to. It fails when the question asks for something never attended to, such as a reason, an explanation or a generalisation, because the subject must then infer instead of remember. On this account the inaccurate reports Nisbett and Wilson assembled in 1977 come from delayed, general and "why" probes, not from verbal report as such. Their reply relocates the *cause* of those failures to procedure. It does not move the [[process-content-distinction|content/process line]]: subjects report what they attended to, and the analyst infers the process.

The method's central prediction, that thinking aloud leaves task accuracy unchanged, is well supported by a 2011 meta-analysis of 94 studies, with domain-limited exceptions; whether thinking aloud is introspection is disputed. "Content" here means information held in short-term memory during a task, not phenomenal character, so the method is neutral between dualism and physicalism and silent on reports of what experience is like. Its value for The Unfinishable Map is precision about which first-person reports carry evidential weight, and why.

## The Model: Reports With a Mechanism {#model}

The 1980 abstract opens with the thesis: "The central proposal of this article is that verbal reports are data." Reports need an account of the mechanism that produces them, and Ericsson and Simon grant them no privileged status as direct observation (p. 217). The model is built "within the theoretical framework of human information processing". Information is *heeded* when it is attended to, or held in short-term memory (STM), and a subject thinking aloud voices what is currently there: in mental multiplication, operands and intermediate products (p. 225). What never enters STM cannot be reported: automated processes such as recognition and retrieval from long-term memory, and still less neural events. Practice shrinks what is reportable, since automation removes intermediate products from STM (p. 225).

Thinking aloud predates them (they cite users from the 1920s onward), and who coined "protocol analysis" is unsettled. Their contribution was the processing model and its levels of verbalisation.

## Three Levels of Verbalisation {#levels}

The model distinguishes three levels (pp. 219, 227):

- **Level 1**: direct articulation of information already in verbal code.
- **Level 2**: verbal recoding of non-verbal information, such as a visual image, without further processing.
- **Level 3**: articulation after scanning, filtering, inference or generative processes have modified the information.

Level 3 is broader than explanation. It covers any instruction to select or transform what is attended to before speaking, such as reporting only certain information or giving a reason for each move.

At Levels 1 and 2, verbalising should leave the course and structure of the task process unchanged, though recoding at Level 2 may slow it. At Level 3 the process itself should change (p. 227). The abstract compresses this: "Verbalizing information is shown to affect cognitive processes only if the instructions require verbalization of information that would not otherwise be attended to."

A reactive procedure changes the process, and the change can be an improvement. In Gagné and Smith's (1962) Tower of Hanoi study, as Ericsson and Simon discuss it, stating a reason for each move greatly improved performance on a harder transfer problem (p. 230). The cost falls on the report, which now describes a modified process.

Probes are sorted as concurrent or retrospective, specific or general, and undirected or directed, with hypothetical questions as a further kind (pp. 220–224). General "interpretive" probing is unreliable (p. 221), directed probes cue what the experimenter expects (p. 222), and immediate retrospection on a specific, short episode is reliable while delay degrades it (p. 226).

## The Reply to Nisbett and Wilson {#reply}

Nisbett and Wilson (1977) argued that people often cannot report accurately on what influenced their responses, and answer instead from *a priori* causal theories. Ericsson and Simon's reply (pp. 245–247) makes three moves.

**Scope.** Nisbett and Wilson, they complain, proposed no definite model and rested their interpretations on an undefined distinction between access to "content" and access to "process" (p. 245). Yet their own list of what people do know (the focus of attention, current sensations, emotions, evaluations, plans, and the intermediate results of mental operations) is exactly what the STM model makes reportable. One item is missing, awareness of ongoing processes, and any real disagreement lies there (p. 245).

**Procedure.** Three differences separate the reviewed studies from the procedures the model recommends (pp. 246–247). Some questions supplied enough background for subjects to answer from general theory, as when subjects told they had taken more shock than average were asked why. Many probes sought information never in memory: comparisons with other conditions, hypothetical cases, generalisations across trials. And in most of the studies the lag between process and probe was long. In the abstract's words: "The inaccurate reports found by other research are shown to result from requesting information that was never directly heeded, thus forcing subjects to infer rather than remember their mental processes."

**Conclusion.** Every inconsistency Nisbett and Wilson cite, Ericsson and Simon argue, arises where the model predicts no veridical report, so verbal reports should no longer be dismissed as "introspection" (p. 247). That is their reading of the literature, not an independent finding.

The concession matters as much as the denial. On the model, subjects report the heeded states a process passes through, never the process itself; the analyst reconstructs it (pp. 217–218). The diagnosis moves from introspective incapacity to faulty procedure, but the content/process asymmetry stands, written into the model.

### The Conditions-of-Access Replies

Smith and Miller (1978) argued that the 1977 thesis was non-falsifiable as stated and never defined the processes subjects supposedly cannot report. They urged researchers to "focus not on the question of whether people have access to process but on the more productive question of the conditions of such access." Ericsson and Simon cite them as broadly agreeing (p. 247). A decade on, White (1988) found that the term *process* "lacks clear, adequate and valid definition" and that "Of the recent studies, none has achieved unambiguous support for, or falsification of, even a weak form of the proposal." Both attack the formulation; neither argues for privileged access.

Wilson's own later work converges on the Level 3 analysis. Wilson and Schooler (1991) found that analysing reasons lowered the quality of preferences: "Analyzing reasons can focus people's attention on nonoptimal criteria". Requested reasons change the process they are meant to report on.

Did Ericsson and Simon refute Nisbett and Wilson, then? White's verdict is the fair one: the question stayed open.

## Where the Content/Process Line Falls {#line}

| Reportable with validity | Not reportable, or invites inference |
|---|---|
| Information heeded during the task, in sequence | Automated sub-processes, recognition, retrieval from long-term memory, neural events |
| Intermediate results, such as partial products in mental arithmetic | The transitions between states: how one led to the next |
| Concurrent thinking aloud; immediate retrospection on a specific, short episode | Delayed, general, hypothetical or between-condition probes |
| Undirected instructions to verbalise whatever thoughts occur | Directed requests for reasons, explanations, selected information or prescribed description |

**The line is architectural; access to it is procedural.** Procedure decides whether a report draws on memory or on inference, but it never moves process into the reportable column. On the model, process is knowable only by third-person reconstruction from a valid trace. Whether any report reaches past heeded content to the process itself, the evidence does not adjudicate: the model rules that out, and White found the question still open.

**Reactivity and validity are separate questions.** A report can leave the process undisturbed and still be incomplete, or blind to what is not verbal. As Jonathan Schooler (2011) put it, "the nonreactivity of thinking aloud does not necessarily speak to its validity."

## Reactivity: The 2011 Meta-Analysis and Its Exceptions {#reactivity}

Fox, Ericsson and Best (2011) tested the level predictions in a meta-analysis of 94 studies with nearly 3,500 participants. The "think-aloud" effect size "is indistinguishable from zero (r = -.03)", even after controlling for task type, while "procedures that entail describing or explaining thoughts and actions are significantly reactive, leading to higher performance than silent control conditions." All reporting procedures tended to add time. The outcome measured was task accuracy, not report validity.

The exceptions cluster where processing is non-verbal or depends on insight:

- **Insight.** Schooler, Ohlsson and Brooks (1993) found that "concurrent, nondirective verbalization impaired the solving of insight problems but had no effect on noninsight problems", the strongest exception at Levels 1–2.
- **Age.** Fox and Charness (2010), Fox being the meta-analysis's first author, found that thinking aloud *raised* older adults' scores on Raven's Matrices (d = 0.73 and 0.92 in two experiments), a reactive effect where the model predicts none.
- **Verbal overshadowing.** Describing a robber impaired later lineup identification in a registered replication, robustly but with strong dependence on task timing (Alogna et al. 2014). This is directed description after the event, a Level 3 procedure, so the model expects reactivity; it is an exception in direction, since the meta-analysis found that describing raised performance.

## Is Thinking Aloud Introspection? {#introspection-dispute}

The meta-analysis provoked a dispute that remains open. Schooler (2011), broadly sympathetic, suggested that William James would likely have seen thinking aloud as "a scientifically rigorous form of introspection", while noting that it "is less able to capture inchoate cognitions." Ericsson and Fox (2011) replied that "Thinking aloud is not a form of introspection but a qualitatively different methodology": it "involves only focusing on a challenging task while concurrently giving verbal expression to thoughts entering attention", requiring neither inner observation nor generated descriptions or explanations.

Schwitzgebel's *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry on introspection discusses the protocols among introspective methods. It takes their convergence with eye movements and response latencies as support for the view that "people’s reports about their stream of thoughts often accurately reflect those thoughts", then turns at once to counter-evidence.

The Map does not settle the classification. On either answer, what the evidence supports is the reportability of the thought stream: task-directed, largely verbal information passing through attention.

## What Protocol Analysis Does Not License {#limits}

*Content* has two senses that must be kept apart. Ericsson and Simon's content is information: the symbols, operands and intermediate results held in STM. Even the current sensations and emotions they accept as reportable (p. 245) enter the model as heeded items to be voiced, not as descriptions of what they are like. Discussions of introspection, the Map's included, often use *content* for phenomenal character. Protocol analysis validates reports in the first sense only. It therefore neither rebuts nor is threatened by Schwitzgebel's (2011) catalogue of introspective failure (whether people dream in colour, the character of peripheral vision, the structure of inner speech), which concerns the character of experience. Its blind spots are real as well: thinking aloud captures inchoate, non-verbal cognition poorly and can disrupt insight. Read across by analogy to language-model reasoning traces, the conditions hold only with re-anchoring: heeded information becomes what the task forces through the trace, and reactivity inverts, since a trace is useful when the answer depends on it ([[chain-of-thought-as-verbal-protocol]]).

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]].** Protocol analysis serves Tenet 1 in one way only: it states precisely which first-person reports carry evidential weight. That blocks the inference from "process reports fail" to "verbal reports are unreliable", which would undercut every report the Map's arguments use. It supplies no evidence for irreducibility, since what it validates, the information content of a task-directed thought stream, sits comfortably within the information-processing model it was built on. The phenomenal reports the irreducibility arguments rely on need their own warrant, set out at [[phenomenological-evidence]]. Protocol analysis shows the form that warrant should take: a theory of the instrument that predicts when reports fail.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] and [[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]].** The model gives a mechanism-level reason for a line the Map draws at [[process-content-distinction]]: whatever links intention to neural outcome is not introspectable, because automated and recognition processes leave nothing in STM to report. That fits both tenets without evidencing either. Nor does the model's silence about phenomenal causation favour [[epiphenomenalism]]. An account of which information reaches report says nothing about what that information is like, and so nothing about whether its phenomenal character does causal work.
