Philosophy of mind usually counts two evidential channels for consciousness: **third-person measurement** (what instruments and observers record about a subject) and **first-person report** (what the subject discloses through trained introspection). A third channel has been comparatively neglected because it only exists between two minds in live contact: the evidence that arises when you are *engaged with* and *responded to by* another mind in real time. Second-person neuroscience supplies the interactive paradigms; second-person phenomenology describes what the participant knows by engaging — two senses this article keeps apart. This article treats reciprocal engagement as a bounded evidential channel — established in the first sense, a hypothesis in the second — that reaches facets of another mind spectating cannot, yet does not deliver another's phenomenal interior and does not, by itself, settle the metaphysics of mind.

The claim to be careful about is *epistemic*: reciprocity is itself a source of evidence, over and above the richer stimulus conditions it creates, and nothing in that claim settles the metaphysics. The Map endorses the channel while declining the enactivist ontology that some of its architects build on top of it — a distinction developed in the [Relation to Site Perspective](#relation-to-site-perspective) section below, and turning on the **enabling-versus-constitutive** contrast introduced next.

## The "Dark Matter" Argument

The naming source is Schilbach and colleagues' 2013 *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* target article "Toward a second-person neuroscience." Their central observation is that standard social neuroscience isolates its subject: a person lies in a scanner and *watches* social stimuli — faces, gaze cues, videos of interaction. This is spectating, not interacting. Schilbach et al. write that the neural mechanisms underlying real social encounters "are only beginning to be studied and could — paradoxically — be seen as representing the 'dark matter' of social neuroscience." The point of the analogy, on this article's reading, is that the coupling the paradigms are built to exclude is largely invisible to the instruments.

Their empirical wager is that social cognition is *different in kind* during interaction than during observation — an "interactor versus observer" or "online versus offline" distinction — rather than merely quantitatively richer. They name two constituents of the second-person approach: emotional **engagement** and **social interaction**, the latter characterised by reciprocal relations in which each partner's perception and action feed the other's. You are treated as, and treat the other as, a "you" — a second person one addresses. The methodological upshot is direct: to study minds as they actually operate socially, study them while they are *coupled*, using genuinely interactive designs.

Schilbach et al. do not argue for dualism, and this article does not enlist them for it. Their framework is broadly cognitive-neuroscientific and, in its enactivist neighbourhood, non-dualist. What the Map takes is the methodological distinction — the identification of a real evidential channel — without inheriting the surrounding metaphysics.

## What Only Engagement Reveals

Two senses of "second-person" need separating first. *Second-person neuroscience* is third-person measurement made honest by interactive paradigms — Schilbach et al. propose studying real-time social encounters in a genuinely interactive manner — and the turn-taking corpora and hyperscanning results below are recordings of interacting subjects, coded from outside. This sense is established; it widens the third-person channel. *Second-person evidence* is the participant's own knowing-by-engagement, what being addressed and responded to discloses to the one inside the exchange. That is the candidate third channel, resting on Reddy's developmental work and the phenomenology of the engaged stance, and it is the hypothesis held open under [the sui generis question](#the-evidential-status-discipline). The three bodies of work below belong to the first sense.

**Developmental priority.** Vasudevi Reddy, in *How Infants Know Minds* (2008), proposes a "second-person solution" to the problem of other minds: on the reading her reviewers summarise, we know other minds insofar as we can *respond* to them — knowing-by-engagement rather than knowing-by-inference. Her developmental evidence is that pre-verbal infants tease, joke, show self-consciousness, and track others' attention and expectations *within* face-to-face engagement, well before any explicit "theory of mind." What this shows is that engagement does not depend on an inferential theory of mind, which removes one reducibility route for the channel (second-person as theory-mediated third-person inference) but not the other (second-person as first-person experience of contingency). Reddy takes engagement to *dissolve* the problem of other minds; the Map keeps her evidence and declines the dissolution, because what engagement discloses is the other as a responder, and the Map's realism about a phenomenal interior beyond the responding — which Reddy would reject as a residual Cartesian picture — is a commitment this article inherits rather than argues. Colwyn Trevarthen's classic work on primary intersubjectivity (1979) supplies the earliest datum — reciprocal, affectively attuned face-to-face protoconversation in infants only weeks old, the mother–infant exchanges whose systematic timing M. C. Bateson (1975) had already documented.

**Reciprocal timing.** Stephen Levinson's "interaction engine" (2006) posits an evolved package of pre-linguistic pragmatic competencies — joint attention, rapid nested turn-taking, repair, recognition of communicative intention — that scaffolds language. The turn-taking signature is quantitative: in Stivers et al.'s 2009 ten-language corpus the cross-language mean response offset is +208 ms, each language's mean falls within about 250 ms of that figure, and each language's modal offset lies between 0 and +200 ms; gaps of that order are far shorter than the 600 ms or more that speech production needs to encode even a single word from scratch (Levinson & Torreira, 2015). That suggests *predictive coupling* — each partner modelling and anticipating the other — which exists only inside live interaction. A spectator watching a recording cannot exhibit it, because there is no one to predict them back. Contingent timing thus makes "reciprocity" operational rather than merely evocative.

**Dyad-level observables.** *Hyperscanning* records two or more brains simultaneously (dual-EEG, dual-fMRI, or fNIRS), enabling measurement of **inter-brain synchrony** — a quantity that exists only across a dyad and is therefore unavailable to single-subject neuroscience by construction. Dumas et al. (2010), recording nine dyads with dual EEG during spontaneous imitation of hand movements, found that episodes of interactional synchrony coincided with inter-brain synchronisation in the alpha–mu band over right centroparietal regions, with the higher-frequency coupling turning asymmetric, which the authors read as reflecting the differential roles of model and imitator; Redcay and Schilbach's (2019) review concludes that the neural mechanisms supporting interaction differ from those engaged by observation. These are observables the isolating paradigm does not produce.

The common thread is a **contingency signal**: each partner's behaviour shifting with the other's, in a loop that exists only while both are live. Dyadic contingency is third-person-measurable — hyperscanning and turn-timing corpora record it from outside — but it is produced only under live coupling, so no recording of either party alone contains it and no introspective report can supply it, because the relevant fact is relational. Whether the participant being responded to has additional, irreducible access to that coupling is the open question taken up under the sui generis heading below.

## The Evidential-Status Discipline

A channel is not a proof, and the second-person literature carries its own cautions that honest use must foreground.

**The synchrony-artifact problem.** Inter-brain synchrony can arise from *common input* — both partners see and hear the same things — or from shared bodily rhythm (breathing, movement entrainment), rather than from genuine mind-to-mind coupling. Establishing that a synchrony measure is evidence of *shared cognition*, let alone shared experience, rather than correlated stimulation, is an open and contested inferential step. Burgess (2013) found that the standard hyperconnectivity measures reported coupling in simulated data that contained none, and in "pseudo-pairs" of separately recorded participants whose EEG rhythmicity merely varied in the same way between conditions — spurious connections that reflect real similarities and so survive statistical control. The existence of a dyad-level observable does not certify what it observes.

**The contextual/enabling/constitutive fork.** De Jaegher, Di Paolo, and Gallagher (2010) distinguish three explanatory roles interaction could play: it might be merely a **context** for individual cognition, an **enabler** that unlocks capacities and evidence, or partly **constitutive** — part of what the cognition *is*. This taxonomy is the crux. Reading interaction as *constitutive of consciousness* pushes toward an anti-individualist, enactivist metaphysics on which meaning is made *between* agents (Fuchs & De Jaegher's "participatory sense-making" and "mutual incorporation," 2009). Reading it as *enabling* keeps the evidential gain — interaction discloses what spectating cannot — without dissolving the individuality of the conscious subject. The distinction between these readings is where the argument turns.

**The sui generis question.** Whether the second-person channel is genuinely irreducible — supplying evidence *neither* introspection *nor* observation could supply — or is ultimately first-person-plus-third-person recombined, is itself disputed. The strongest version of the article's claim is that the contingency signal of being-responded-to is irreducible; that claim is worth stating precisely and holding as a hypothesis rather than a result. The field's own gaze-contingent paradigms sharpen the question. Pfeiffer et al. (2014) had participants judge, under fMRI, whether an animated agent's gaze was controlled by another person or by a program; the agent was always the program, yet interactions *experienced* as human recruited the ventral striatum while those experienced as computer-driven engaged attention networks — in the authors' words, "the mere experience of engagement in social interaction is sufficient to recruit this system." The neural signature can therefore follow the participant's take on being responded to rather than the fact of it. The enabling reading can absorb this, since on that reading the participant's stance is part of what the channel delivers; but until a design separates being responded to from taking oneself to be, the irreducibility hypothesis lacks its discriminating test, and this article does not pretend otherwise.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Map reads second-person neuroscience as a methodological finding it can adopt while quarantining the metaphysics several of its architects favour.

**On [[tenets#^occams-limits|Tenet 5]] (limits of third-person method).** The [[methodology-of-consciousness-research|Map's methodology]] already argues that a science built to exclude subjectivity will not fully capture consciousness, and that the persistent need for *dual* methods — first-person and third-person — is best explained by ontological difference. The second-person channel extends this picture rather than overturning it. It widens the evidence base with a genuinely new class of observable (dyadic contingency, reciprocal timing, inter-brain coupling) and thereby sharpens exactly *what* third-person science can and cannot reach. And here the Map's move is distinctive: even this widened base does not deliver another's qualia. You gain rich evidence of coupling and responsiveness; you do not thereby occupy the other's point of view. The inference from that residue to ontological difference is made in the [[methodology-of-consciousness-research#Relation to Site Perspective|methodology article]], as an inference to the best explanation of the persistent need for dual methods; this article inherits that argument rather than adding to it. What a widened third-person base shows is that the residue survives the widening, which is what **[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** leads the Map to expect.

**On [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Tenet 3]] (bidirectional interaction).** The second-person channel presupposes that states of the other make an outward, detectable difference — that engagement, expression, and responsiveness push information about a mind into the shared physical arena where another mind can pick it up. That is what Tenet 3 leads the Map to expect, and the evidence is consistent with it. It is equally consistent with physicalism, which locates those states in the brain, and with epiphenomenalism, on which the neural correlates do the pushing while the phenomenal state rides along: nothing in reciprocal timing or inter-brain synchrony discriminates "phenomenal state causes expression" from "correlate causes expression." The Map does not over-claim here. The bridge — why the coupling would require the phenomenal state itself to be the cause — is argued in [[interactionist-dualism]] and [[the-epiphenomenalist-threat]], and second-person data do not make it.

**Where the Map holds the line.** The Map endorses the *enabling* reading and declines the *constitutive-of-consciousness* reading. Interaction can unlock evidence and capacities without being part of what phenomenal consciousness *is*. Treating the dyad as the bearer of experience — rather than as the arena in which two bearers couple — would dissolve the ontological individuality of consciousness the Map maintains. The enactivist inference from "meaning is co-regulated between agents" to "cognition is not individually located" overreaches for the Map's purposes; the phenomenology of the engaged stance can be harvested without it. The Map treats this as a case of taking method without ontology; the enactivist would deny that the two come apart.

**On phenomenal authority.** The [[phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence|first-person channel]] grounds evidential standing in the direct givenness of experience to its subject. The second-person channel does not compete with that authority; it gives it a social dimension. Being addressed as a "you" is evidence *to* you about another's responsiveness, but the other's phenomenal authority over their own experience remains theirs. The channels are complementary: first-person authority over one's own experience, third-person measurement of physical correlates, and second-person evidence of live coupling. *If* the second-person channel is irreducible, three-way non-convergence joins the two-way non-convergence the [[methodology-of-consciousness-research|methodology article]] already reads dualistically; if it is not, this article's evidential contribution is confined to widening the third-person channel.

## The Third Channel, Bounded

Second-person neuroscience is established: live interaction is behaviourally signed by contingent timing and neurally measurable at the level of the dyad, observables the observer paradigm never produced. Second-person evidence — the participant's knowing-by-engagement, present before any theory of mind — is a candidate third evidential channel, held as a hypothesis. If the hypothesis holds, it reaches what spectating and introspection cannot: the evidence of being-responded-to. Even then it is evidence about coupling and responsiveness, and no window into another's interior. It neither closes the [[problem-of-other-minds|problem of other minds]] nor settles the case for dualism. What it does is enrich the map of the evidence and, in the same motion, make vivid how much remains beyond reach once the map is as full as engagement can make it. For the Map, that remaining distance is the point.
