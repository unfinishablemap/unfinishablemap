Between the expulsion of Christian Wolff from Halle in 1723 and Kant's *Nova dilucidatio* of 1755, German academic philosophy reversed itself on mind-body causation. Physical influx (*influxus physicus*) — the doctrine that finite substances genuinely act on one another, so that the soul really moves the body and the body really affects the soul — went from a scholastic leftover that Leibniz's pre-established harmony had supposedly retired to the position argued for by Gottsched, Knutzen, Reusch, Crusius and the young Kant. On the historiography the Map relies on here (Watkins 1995, 1998, 2005; Lorini 2016), the direction of travel is settled; whether influx became the *dominant* academic view in those words is a stronger claim that this page has not verified at source and does not assert. Read that way, the episode is the one period in a major philosophical culture in which something close to the Map's [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] tenet carried the field.

This page is a reception history rather than a survey of positions. It takes the reversal as its starting fact, asks what did the work (the answer, developed under [[#Why Influx Won: Four Strands|the four strands below]], is that the decisive pressures were theological, institutional and redefinitional, and none of them empirical), and closes on the leading influxionist's own concession that the mechanism he had won the argument for could not be understood. Leibniz's pre-established harmony is not re-explained: the Map already carries it at [[history-of-the-interaction-problem#Pre-Established Harmony|the interaction-problem history]] and in [[occasionalism#Sibling Non-Interactionist Solutions|the occasionalism article]]. One disclosure applies throughout: no Latin or German primary text was consulted for this page. Every sentence about Knutzen, Reusch, Gottsched and Crusius rests on the English renderings of Watkins (2003) and Lorini (2016), and is cited to them, never to the original.

## The Reversal, as the Historiography Reports It

Watkins's 1998 title states the trajectory outright — *From Pre-established Harmony to Physical Influx* — and Lorini (2016) treats the shift as the organising fact of the period, giving a whole section to "Wolff's Disciples and the Physical Influx". Frederick Rauscher's review of Watkins (2005) summarises that book's first chapter as the debate "between proponents of Leibniz's pre-established harmony (Wolff, Baumgarten, Meier) and the rival physical influx theory" of Knutzen and Crusius, and reports Watkins's finding that "even Leibniz's own followers altered his theory to allow for the possibility of physical influx and physical monads" (Rauscher, NDPR). That is the evidence this page has for the reversal: two specialist historians who take it as given, and a reviewer's summary of a third treatment. It supports a documented shift; it does not, from what the Map has read, license the sentence "physical influx became the dominant academic position", which is left to Watkins 1998 — cited below as the paper that would settle it, not as one whose argument this page has absorbed.

The sequence, as far as it can be reconstructed from Lorini and the Stanford Encyclopedia entry on Wolff: Gottsched's *Erste Gründe der gesammten Weltweisheit* (1733–34) redefined influx and argued for it; Knutzen's *Commentatio philosophica de commercio mentis et corporis per influxum physicum explicando* (Königsberg, 1735) and Reusch's *Systema metaphysicum* (1735) defended it in the same year, both extending it beyond the mind-body case to substances generally; Baumgarten's *Metaphysica* (1739) mounted the counter-attack on a monadological footing — Lorini writes that "The year 1739 marks a turning point" — and Meier's *Beweis der vorherbestimmten Harmonie* (1743) continued the Halle defence; Crusius's *Entwurf der nothwendigen Vernunft-Wahrheiten* (1745) and Knutzen's *Systema causarum efficientium* (1745) answered; and Kant presupposed influx in 1747 and argued for it in 1755. A bibliographic caution attaches to Knutzen: the 1745 title contains the 1735 title as its "seu" clause, so the two may be one work re-issued; that is unconfirmed, and this page cites them as its sources do without asserting either.

A correction the sources insist on: this was not Wolff versus Knutzen. The standard framing, "Wolffian pre-established harmony displaced by physical influx," overstates Wolff's investment. According to the Stanford entry, Wolff held that "the pre-established harmony is a more probable hypothesis than the other two", and thought "that nothing significant turns on settling this contentious issue" (Hettche & Dyck 2024). Lorini's abstract puts the contrast sharply: Wolff "is skeptical about the monadology and accepts pre-established harmony as a valid hypothesis only concerning the soul-body relationship," whereas Baumgarten "endorses the monadological theory and therefore adopts pre-established harmony in its universal value." The committed defenders of harmony were Baumgarten and Meier; Wolff was its lukewarm patron. Lorini goes further and calls the influx theory of Knutzen and Reusch "only allegedly Wolffian, since the Wolffian texts do not allow room for a strong defense of it." The reversal, then, ran within a school that had never been as committed as its opponents claimed, which is part of why it could happen.

## Why Influx Won: Four Strands

The question that matters for the Map is why influx won. On the Map's reconstruction from Lorini and the Stanford entry, four strands can be separated, in descending order of confidence. Watkins 1998 presumably gives its own explanation; that paper is unread here, so what follows is the Map's ordering of the evidence its other sources supply rather than a report of the specialist consensus.

### Not Empirical

No observation, experiment or measurement decided between pre-established harmony and physical influx. The two theories were, and remained, equivalent about every observable event: a body moving when a soul wills is exactly what harmony predicts and exactly what influx predicts. Whatever moved the German academy between 1723 and 1755, it was not data. The Map is most confident of this strand because it follows from the structure of the two theories rather than from any historical source.

### Theological and Moral

The Pietist case against Wolff was that pre-established harmony entails fatalism and therefore destroys the imputability of action: if my deeds were laid down at creation, I can neither be praised nor blamed for them. Crusius's *Entwurf* (1745), on the standard account, runs its anti-Wolffian metaphysics on the same concern, holding that a sufficient reason for every event leaves no room for the freedom that responsibility requires; that account has not been checked here against Crusius's own pages, and Watkins 1995, the paper titled for Gottsched, Knutzen and Crusius, is the place to check it. Influx was the option on which a soul could originate bodily action, and so the option on which people could be held to account. Part of why it won is that it was the theory that let you hold people responsible — the eighteenth-century form of the connection the Map draws between interaction and [[free-will]].

### Institutional and Coercive

The Stanford entry records the shock plainly: "On 8 November 1723, the king issued an edict removing Wolff from his university position and ordering him to leave the lands of Electoral Brandenburg within 48 hours on pain of hanging" (Hettche & Dyck 2024). The Pietists, the entry says, "exploited their personal connections with the king, who was allegedly persuaded that Wolff's endorsement of the pre-established harmony represented a threat to military discipline (as the acts of deserters would be pre-established and so not subject to sanction)." The hedge is the Stanford entry's own — "allegedly persuaded" — and it should stay: this is a reported episode, not a documented deliberation. Wolff returned to Halle only on 6 December 1740, under a new king. In the intervening seventeen years Knutzen held his chair in Pietist Königsberg. A doctrine need not be refuted if its leading professor can be exiled, and the rehabilitation of 1740 was a rehabilitation of Wolff, not of the harmony.

### Redefinition Rather Than Refutation

This is the subtlest strand and the one most worth the Map's attention. Influx did not answer the classical objections so much as change what it was claiming, until the objections no longer had a target.

The ancient objection to influx was the migration of accidents: on the literal picture, a property flows out of one substance and into another, and nothing coherent can be said about a property in transit. Gottsched's move, as Lorini reports it, was to deflate the word. He "proposes to understand the word influx not literally (as a 'flowing'), but in a metaphorical sense, as the power of a substance to act directly on another" (Lorini 2016, citing Gottsched §1067). Once nothing flows, no accident migrates, and the objection has nothing to grip. Knutzen made the same move against the same objection, arguing, in Lorini's rendering, that influx "does not implies the migration of accidents from one substance to another, but only a change in the substance subjected to the action of another." The Stanford entry's gloss of Wolff's own definition shows how far the deflation had already travelled: the system of physical influx is the one "according to which one substance produces a state in another directly through its own activity" (Hettche & Dyck 2024). That is a definition of ordinary causation, not of anything flowing.

The second objection was conservation, and here the influxionists turned Leibniz's physics against Leibniz's metaphysics. The conservation argument as inherited from the seventeenth century assumed the Cartesian quantity of motion. Gottsched, Lorini reports, "stresses that Leibniz had demonstrated this law – in the formulation given by Descartes and his followers – to be false, insofar as it is not motion, but the *vis viva* that is conserved." Knutzen went one step further and denied that the conservation law reached the soul at all, because "Leibniz deduces indeed this law from the principle of inertia, which cannot hold for the soul" (Lorini 2016, citing Knutzen 1735 §53). Whatever one makes of the physics, the form of the reply is worth marking: the objection presupposes that the system under discussion is one to which the conservation law applies, and the influxionist denies exactly that presupposition. The Map's own treatment in [[conservation-laws-and-mental-causation]] makes a structurally similar move on very different physics; the resemblance is noted below, not claimed as continuity.

The third objection was heterogeneity: how can an unextended thing act on an extended one? Crusius's solution, on Watkins's reading, was to homogenise the forces, "identifying a specific force that both minds and bodies have, namely the force to move" (Watkins 2003, p. 10, citing Crusius 1745, p. 680). Knutzen's positive argument ran nearby: a simple substance whose defining character is the force to move itself thereby has the force to move other things, with impenetrability doing supporting work (Lorini 2016, citing Knutzen 1735 §§28–29).

The last and most elegant redefinition was to reread the opposing school's own commitments. Lorini quotes Watkins (1998, p. 183) — a passage this page has only through Lorini — to the effect that Knutzen and Reusch maintain that "nothing Leibniz and Wolff say prohibits interpreting this 'well-founding' relationship [between simple and composite] as a causal relationship," since simples are necessary conditions for their composites just as a cause is a necessary condition for its effect. Influx, on this reading, was not the rival of the Leibniz-Wolff metaphysics but a permissible interpretation of it.

Put together, the theory that won in 1755 was not the theory that had been refuted in 1700. On the Map's reconstruction, that is the sense in which the victory was philosophical: won by redefinition, not by refuting the objections on their own terms.

## The Pre-Critical Kant

Kant makes the episode consequential, and the easy story about him is wrong in two places.

The first is direction. Watkins (2003) shows that in the *Gedanken von der wahren Schätzung der lebendigen Kräfte* (1747) Kant "had simply presupposed" physical influx through his conception of force as activity, and that the *Nova dilucidatio* (1755) is where "he provides an explicit argument" for it. The argument runs through what Kant calls the principle of succession: "Substances can change only insofar as they are connected with other substances; their reciprocal dependence determines the mutual change of state" (Kant, *Nova dilucidatio*, Ak. 1:410, in Kant 1992, as quoted by Watkins 2003). Kant supports it, in Watkins's summary, with three arguments "all based on the idea that the kind of causally isolated substances invoked in pre-established harmony are incapable of undergoing change given the way in which determinations are posited by grounds in a substance." So far the easy story holds: "The principle of succession explicitly aims to establish physical influx by refuting pre-established harmony" (Watkins 2003, p. 11). But Watkins's abstract corrects the target. Kant's arguments "are directed primarily against the conception of grounds and existence held by Wolff, Baumgarten, and Meier, and only secondarily against Leibniz". Kant was arguing with his contemporaries, and Leibniz was a secondary casualty.

The second is influence. The traditional story makes Knutzen Kant's teacher and the man who converted him to influx and to Newton. A revisionist current, associated with Manfred Kuehn, trims this substantially. Watkins reports that Kuehn reads Kant's apparent reference to Knutzen in the *True estimation* as "a sarcastic put-down, not as a compliment" (Watkins 2003, p. 10 n. 7, citing Kuehn 2000), and Watkins's own analysis shows that Kant's solution differed from Knutzen's and Crusius's alike: Kant defined force generally, as activity, rather than attributing one particular force — the force to move — to minds and bodies, and so did not need to homogenise mind and matter as they did. The claim this page can support is the narrow one: Kant argued for influx in a milieu that Knutzen had helped make arguable, and his argument was not Knutzen's. Whether Knutzen converted him is contested, and the deflationary reading is the more recent.

## Knutzen's Concession

Knutzen, the leading influxionist, at the moment influx was carrying the field, conceded in print that the mechanism could not be understood. Watkins translates §42 of the *Systema causarum efficientium* (1745) in a footnote: "The very specific mode of physical influx or action by which the mind influences its body and the body in turn influences the mind, cannot be distinctly understood by us on account of the nature of the thing" (Knutzen 1745 §42, trans. Watkins 2003, p. 10 n. 9). Watkins's gloss is that Knutzen "expresses a certain degree of skepticism about our ability to understand the specific causal mechanisms that pertain to mind-body interaction in particular". The rendering is Watkins's English of Knutzen's Latin and has not been checked against the original here.

The concession places Knutzen on the horn the Map's interaction-problem history assigns to Régis: the interactionist who keeps dualism and genuine causation and pays in intelligibility, declaring the interaction real yet incomprehensible. Knutzen is the better instance for the Map's purposes because he was not a dissenter but the winner. The theory that prevailed over pre-established harmony did so without claiming to explain what harmony had been invented to avoid explaining.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Map reads the episode as evidence about the history of the interaction problem, not as evidence for interactionism; the two uses need keeping apart.

**For Tenet 3, a precedent.** [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] holds that consciousness causally influences the physical world. The influx debate shows that a position of that shape was defended, within a law-governed physics that its defenders took seriously, by the leading academic philosophers of a major culture for a generation, and that it prevailed over the most sophisticated non-interactionist alternative ever devised. That is compatibility, not support. Nothing that happened in Halle or Königsberg makes interaction more likely to be true; it makes the claim that interaction is an obvious non-starter within a physics of conservation laws harder to hold, since people who knew the conservation arguments once held otherwise. The influxionists' physics is also not the Map's. Knutzen's reply that inertia and hence conservation "cannot hold for the soul" is not a reply the Map can use; the Map's own response to the conservation objection runs through the locality and symmetry-conditionality of modern conservation laws and through selection among quantum outcomes rather than injection of force, and it is developed in [[conservation-laws-and-mental-causation]] and [[objections-to-interactionism#Conservation Laws|the objections article]]. What the two replies share is only their form — each denies that the system in question is one the conservation premise was entitled to assume — and the Map records the resemblance as a resemblance.

**For Tenet 5, a narrower lesson than "parsimony fails."** [[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]] holds that simplicity is an unreliable guide to truth when knowledge is incomplete. The tempting reading of the influx debate is that the simpler, law-preserving theory — pre-established harmony, which needs no unexplained causal traffic and no exception to conservation — lost, and that parsimony therefore failed. The Map declines that reading, because the four strands above show harmony losing for reasons that had nothing to do with whether simplicity tracks truth: a theological argument about imputability, a royal edict, and a redefinition of what its rival was claiming. The lesson the Map draws is smaller and more defensible. Which theory a philosophical culture holds is settled, at least sometimes, by pressures orthogonal to the theory's simplicity, so the fact that a community has converged on the more parsimonious option is weak evidence that parsimony was what did the converging — and, by symmetry, the fact that a community has converged on the less parsimonious option is weak evidence that parsimony was overruled by truth. This is a framework-conditional implication: it follows from Tenet 5's premise that the epistemic weight of simplicity depends on the completeness of the surrounding knowledge, and it is offered as an illustration of that premise, not as an independent argument for it. A reader who holds that parsimony is reliable can read the same history as a community failing to apply a good rule.

**On Knutzen's concession and the Map's own debt.** The Map's position differs from Knutzen's at precisely the point where he stopped. The quantum-selection proposal attempts what Knutzen declared could not be attempted: a specification of how the influence works. The Map's [[tenets#^tenet-3-standing|own standing note on Tenet 3]] records that the attempt is incomplete — the interface argument shows downward causation to be available, not actual, and no worked toy model of the coupling exists. So the honest comparison is that Knutzen conceded the mechanism could not be distinctly understood, and the Map holds that it has not yet been. The difference between those two claims is the difference between a void the Map maps as unexplorable and one it maps as unexplored, and the Map's wager is on the second.
