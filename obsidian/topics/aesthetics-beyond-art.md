Most of what strikes us aesthetically is not art. A sunset, a valley, a made bed, a clean glass, weather, the neat-or-messy state of a desk — none of these were made for contemplation, and whether the concepts analytic aesthetics built for art transfer to them is itself contested. Two philosophical subfields exist to handle the remainder: the **aesthetics of nature** (environmental aesthetics), which studies natural beauty as a category in its own right, and **everyday aesthetics**, roughly two decades old, which studies the aesthetic character of ordinary life. They share a founding move and leave two questions open that bear directly on The Unfinishable Map: the **scope problem** — whether an aesthetic that is everywhere still discriminates anything — and the **ontological locus** question sharpened by the sunset with no observer (both explained below).

The Map's interest is that both questions cut in two directions at once, and the Map holds neither as settled. The pervasiveness of low-key aesthetic quality could *strengthen* the case for the [[aesthetics-and-consciousness|irreducibility of aesthetic experience]] — felt evaluative character would be a constant texture of consciousness rather than a rare peak phenomenon, harder for a reductive account to quarantine. Or it could *dilute* that case: if even a clean glass carries "aesthetic quality," the word may be doing no discriminating work, and a functionalist story about valence and salience may absorb the whole domain. This article lays out both subfields and the tensions; it does not claim the tensions resolve in the Map's favour.

## The Art Model and Its Limits

For most of the twentieth century, analytic aesthetics effectively equated aesthetics with the philosophy of art. The concepts were built accordingly: disinterested contemplation, the framed aesthetic object, the free play of imagination. Whether those concepts transfer beyond art is the question that founds both subfields, and the different answers to it generate the main rival theories in each.

Ronald Hepburn's 1966 essay "Contemporary Aesthetics and the Neglect of Natural Beauty" is credited with reopening the subject. Hepburn argued that art frequently supplies a *misleading* model for appreciating nature: appreciating a painting means standing before a framed object made for contemplation, whereas appreciating a valley means being *inside* an unframed environment, moving through it, taking it in through every sense at once — as rich as art-appreciation, and structurally different in kind. That art-versus-nature disanalogy is the ground the entire subsequent debate stands on — and the parallel move, decades later, founded everyday aesthetics too.

The two subfields divide the remainder between them: environmental aesthetics takes landscapes and wilderness, everyday aesthetics the larger residue of non-art, non-scenic, ongoing life — grooming, cooking, tidying, commuting, the ambient qualities of a familiar room.

## Natural Beauty

### The Central Axis: Cognitivism Versus Its Rivals

The field's organizing disagreement is whether appropriate appreciation of nature requires *knowledge*, in the way appreciating a painting arguably requires knowing something of art history.

**Scientific cognitivism** answers yes. Allen Carlson's Natural Environmental Model holds that appreciating a natural environment appropriately requires regarding it, as the Stanford Encyclopedia survey he co-authored with Glenn Parsons puts it, "in terms of the characterization of that environment provided by natural science." Geology, ecology, and evolutionary biology are not decoration on the experience; they fix the appropriate object of appreciation and the categories under which it is seen — much as Kendall Walton argued that what we correctly perceive in an artwork depends on the category we assign it. Knowing that a whale is a mammal, or that a seemingly barren wetland is a dense ecosystem, changes what there is to appreciate. Carlson's aim is partly to secure *objectivity*: a distinction between correct and incorrect appreciation, rather than mere pleasant reaction.

Three families of view resist the primacy of knowledge.

**The aesthetics of engagement** (Arnold Berleant) rejects the distanced, contemplative stance inherited from Kant. Disinterested appreciation, on Berleant's account, isolates, distances, and objectifies — the wrong posture for environments, which have no frame and no fixed viewpoint. The appreciator is continuous with the surroundings, perceiving bodily and multisensorially, seeking what Parsons and Carlson's survey sums up as "total immersion" rather than a detached view. Berleant's project is phenomenological — a description of how environmental perception actually works — rather than a metaphysics of mind.

**The arousal model** (Noël Carroll) holds that being emotionally *moved* by nature is a legitimate aesthetic response that cognitivism wrongly excludes. In "On Being Moved by Nature," Carroll objects that cognitivism excludes certain very common appreciative responses to nature — the thrill at a towering waterfall, the sense of homeyness in a silent grove. These require no science to be genuine. Carroll offers this against cognitivism, not as a theory of consciousness, though he speculates that some such responses might have a naturalistic, even evolutionary, grounding.

**The imagination model** (Emily Brady, building on Hepburn) centers imagination, emotion, and meaning in appreciating nature well. Brady adds a normative constraint — "imagining well" — so the account is disciplined by the object rather than being anything-goes subjectivism. Her larger project deliberately integrates subjective and objective approaches rather than choosing between them.

Between the poles sits a **pluralist, skeptical middle**. Malcolm Budd argues that nature can be appreciated "as nature" without any single mandatory model, and is skeptical that scientific framing is required for appropriate appreciation. His position is a standing foil to strong cognitivism and a reminder that the field has reached no consensus. The cognitivism-versus-non-cognitivism axis remains open.

### Positive Aesthetics: Is All Wild Nature Beautiful?

Carlson also defends a striking further thesis: **positive aesthetics**. Insofar as nature is pristine — untouched by human hands — it is always aesthetically good and never ugly. The claim is startling against art criticism's routine negative judgments: no comparable verdict applies to a swamp, a scree slope, or a tangle of dead wood once they are seen under the right scientific categories, which reveal order, function, and adaptive fit where the untutored eye saw only mess.

Positive aesthetics is contested — though not by Glenn Parsons, who develops a scientific defence of it alongside Carlson's. Budd and Stan Godlovitch find the thesis unintuitive and inadequately justified, and ask whether "all pristine nature is beautiful" is a substantive discovery or a stipulation smuggled in by the choice of scientific categories: decide in advance to appreciate everything in terms of ecological function and positive verdicts may be built into the method rather than found in the world. A second objection is the scope problem arriving early, on the nature side — a verdict that never comes out negative supports no comparative ranking, which is what environmental planning actually needs, so universality is bought at the price of discrimination. The thesis also sits ambiguously on the observer question: it can read as locating beauty *in the object* (nature is good in itself), or as a claim about the deliverances of a *knowledgeable observer* (nature comes out good when correctly appreciated). That ambiguity is exactly what the sunset-with-no-observer section below turns on.

## The Everyday

### What the Subfield Studies

Everyday aesthetics insists that the aesthetic does not begin at the gallery door or the scenic overlook. It suffuses the mundane: a made bed, a clean glass, the texture of a well-done chore, weather, the neat-or-messy state of a desk.

Two books secured the field's foothold. Yuriko Saito's *Everyday Aesthetics* (Oxford, 2007) argues that Western aesthetics neglected the everyday because of its "almost exclusive emphasis on art," and Thomas Leddy's *The Extraordinary in the Ordinary* (Broadview, 2012) foregrounds a neglected vocabulary of everyday aesthetic terms — 'neat,' 'messy,' 'pretty,' 'lovely,' 'cute,' 'pleasant,' 'sparkle,' 'shine' — that ordinary talk uses constantly and traditional aesthetics ignored (Leddy had opened this program in earlier articles on surface qualities and on "sparkle and shine"). The sharpest general statement of the field's premise is Sherri Irvin's "The Pervasiveness of the Aesthetic in Ordinary Experience" (2008), which argues that ordinary experience is "replete with aesthetic character."

### Continuity with Art, or a Break from It

Leddy answers largely yes. On his account everyday aesthetics is closely related to the aesthetics of art, and the ordinary becomes aesthetic through a heightened attention that lends it an "aura" — the extraordinary latent in the ordinary, brought out by a mode of looking continuous with how one attends to a painting. This is the *continuity* pole.

Saito answers largely no. She argues that the Kantian model of disinterested contemplation is the wrong template for the everyday, because everyday aesthetic response is typically *action-oriented and non-disinterested* — we do not merely contemplate the messy kitchen, we are moved to clean it. Her examples are deliberately unglamorous: personal grooming, the choice of a pet, garden design, the transience of ambient qualities. This is the *break-from-art* pole. Arto Haapala adds a further twist against the art model from a different angle: everyday aesthetic value, he argues, arises paradoxically from *familiarity* and being-at-home rather than from the strangeness or distance that art appreciation cultivates.

The disagreement is not merely terminological. If the everyday is aesthetic only when we borrow art's contemplative stance, then everyday aesthetics is a footnote to art aesthetics. If instead ordinary engaged life has its own aesthetic mode, the field describes something the art-centred tradition genuinely missed.

### The Scope Problem

This is the field's defining and unresolved problem, and the one that bears most directly on the Map. If aesthetic character genuinely pervades ordinary experience — if messiness, clutter, the feel of clean laundry, and mere bodily comfort all count — does "aesthetic" still mark a distinction, or has it swollen until it discriminates nothing?

The **expansive** camp welcomes the broad scope. Irvin's contribution is the most pointed: she argues that experiences which are "simple, lacking in unity or closure, and characterized by limited or fragmented awareness" are not thereby disqualified from being aesthetic. Kevin Melchionne, in "The Definition of Everyday Aesthetics" (2013), offers a bounding definition meant to keep the field coherent without contracting it: everyday aesthetics concerns the *recurring, ongoing* aesthetic engagements of daily routine and self- and home-management, not one-off extraordinary encounters. His companion paper "The Point of Everyday Aesthetics" (2014) supplies the payoff — a *well-being* accrued across a life rather than banked in peak episodes.

The **containment** camp fears dilution and proposes thresholds. Three responses recur. One is a sliding scale of intensity: some aesthetic qualities are major-league or heavy-weight and others minor-league or light-weight, the latter still genuinely aesthetic but distinguished by degree. A second is a *defamiliarization* threshold — the everyday counts as aesthetic when attention transfigures it (the Leddy-style "aura") — though Saito objects that making defamiliarization a precondition risks losing the very everyday-ness of everyday experience; the point was to find the aesthetic *in* the ordinary, not by escaping it. A third worries about intersubjectivity: if everyday aesthetic response bottoms out in private bodily sensation, it may collapse into subjective relativism and lose the shared standard (*sensus communis*) that aesthetic judgment traditionally claimed.

No resolution commands the field. That is precisely why the scope problem, rather than any settled doctrine, is what the Map must engage.

### Saito's Moral-Aesthetic and World-Making Judgments

Saito develops a distinctive further claim, most fully in *Aesthetics of the Familiar: Everyday Life and World-Making* (Oxford, 2017), her considered response to a decade of controversy over the field's boundary and status. Everyday aesthetic taste, she argues, is not ethically inert: it shapes the built and social world — what Saito calls *world-making* — exerting a powerful influence on the state of the world and the quality of life. Hence a normative upshot: she argues for a "social responsibility" to cultivate aesthetic literacy and to guard against "aesthetic manipulation."

Critics who prefer to keep aesthetic and moral evaluation in separate boxes resist this as conflating two normative domains. Saito's reply is that in the everyday the two are already entangled: beauty standards that damage health, or an aesthetic of disposability that degrades an environment, are aesthetic and moral facts at once. The debate is live, and the Map takes no side on whether the entanglement is genuine or a category mistake.

## Where Does Aesthetic Value Live? The Sunset With No Observer

Is a sunset over an empty planet beautiful? It has wavelengths and atmospheric scattering, but is there beauty there with no one to see it? The live hub [[aesthetics-and-consciousness]] opens on exactly this intuition and then turns to art. It is where both beyond-art subfields touch the Map's core interests — and where the Map must be most careful about what the literature does and does not establish.

The question is the **ontological locus of aesthetic value**. Three readings are available, and the honest starting point is that environmental aesthetics has mostly been busy with the cognitivism axis, not with this one. The observer question is comparatively under-theorized *within the field itself*.

**The object-realist reading**: the beauty is in the sunset, a property of the arrangement of light and atmosphere, present whether or not anyone perceives it. Carlson's cognitivism gives this reading a serious, non-trivial form. If aesthetic merit tracks mind-independent scientific facts — the actual physics of scattering, the actual ecology of a valley — then a knowledgeable-but-absent-observer story is coherent: the features that ground correct appreciation are there in the object, waiting. This is a genuine rival to any observer-dependent account, and it deserves full weight rather than caricature.

**The response-dependent, phenomenal reading**: beauty is realized only in a conscious encounter. On this reading the sunset with no observer has wavelengths but no beauty, because beauty is an *evaluative* feature that arises when a subject encounters an arrangement and something in the encounter carries evaluative charge. Berleant's immersive engagement, Carroll's felt arousal, and Brady's imaginative activity all foreground the conscious encounter, and to that extent the practice of the field leans toward the response side. Saito's action-oriented everyday response points the same way from the mundane end. This is the reading congenial to the Map — but the Map presents it as *one* reading of the sunset intuition, not as a demonstrated result.

**The deflationary gloss**: the whole "in the object versus in the encounter" dispute may be a verbal one. If aesthetic value is a *response-dependent property* — like a color, roughly, real but constitutively tied to how the object affects a suitable perceiver — then asking whether it is "really" in the sunset or "really" in the mind may be asking to choose between two descriptions of one fact. The Map does not get to treat its preferred reading as defeating this deflationary option; the verbal-dispute worry is live.

The crucial honesty point, flagged in the Map's own research: the step from "aesthetic value is observer-dependent" to "aesthetic value requires *consciousness* in the Map's strong sense" is largely the Map's extension, not an established position in environmental aesthetics. Berleant, Carroll, and Brady foreground the perceiving subject without thereby endorsing dualism or a metaphysics of phenomenal value. Enlisting them as evidence *for* consciousness-dependence would over-read the field. What the field supplies is the terrain — an unresolved question about where aesthetic value lives — onto which the Map maps its own commitments.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]] — congenial, not entailing.** The live treatment in [[aesthetics-and-consciousness]] argues that aesthetic quality "suffuses ordinary consciousness far beyond art galleries" and treats that pervasiveness as underappreciated evidence for irreducibility. Everyday aesthetics supplies exactly the pervasiveness data — Irvin's "replete with aesthetic character," Saito's mundane examples, Leddy's neat/messy vocabulary — and the response-dependent reading of natural beauty fits the Map's [[topics/phenomenal-value-realism|phenomenal value realism]], on which aesthetic value is intrinsic to a conscious encounter rather than a free-standing property of unperceived matter. On a *strengthening* reading, this broadens the Map's case: if even a clean glass, a well-made bed, or a sunset carries irreducible felt-evaluative character, then the [[explanatory-gap|explanatory gap]] is not a boutique problem about rare experiences but a pervasive feature of mind.

But the same literature supplies the counter-move, and the Map presents it at full strength rather than as a foil. The field's own central worry is that pervasiveness *trivializes*: a category that applies to everything may discriminate nothing. Give that its due and a *deflationary* reading becomes available. Melchionne's routine/well-being framing and Irvin's rejection of unity both invite a functionalist gloss on which "aesthetic quality" is just ordinary affective perception — valence plus salience plus attention, each mechanistically respectable. On this reading the very ubiquity that looks like evidence is instead a sign that the aesthetic is cheap and everywhere *because* it is nothing more than ordinary valenced experience, fully tractable without dualism. On the nature side the parallel caution holds: Carlson's cognitivism offers a coherent partly observer-independent rival, and the deflationary gloss offers a way to dissolve the dispute. The Map does not think these readings are obviously correct, but they are not obviously wrong either, and neither the pervasiveness datum nor the sunset intuition decides between them. The Map's reading is a *reading*, offered at the strength the evidence licenses and no higher.

One point sharpens the caution. Irvin is **dual-use**, not an ally. Her pervasiveness thesis helps the Map's premise, but the very same paper denies that everyday aesthetic experience requires *unity or closure* — and the unity of aesthetic experience is one of the premises the case in [[aesthetics-and-consciousness]] rests on. Enlisting Irvin for pervasiveness while ignoring that she cuts against the binding argument would be selective citation. The honest position across both subfields is that the beyond-art literature both feeds and complicates the Map's case.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] — thematic only.** Saito's action-oriented, world-making picture is congenial to the Map's claim that aesthetic value moves us to reshape the physical world — we tidy, arrange, and beautify because of how things strike us aesthetically. Aesthetic response to nature has historically motivated its preservation; Brady in particular treats the intersection of aesthetic and ethical value and the role of aesthetics in conservation. The Map notes these alignments while flagging that Saito's thesis is about the *ethical and social* consequences of taste, not about mental causation or dualism; the fit is thematic, and should not be overstated into shared metaphysical commitment. The dedicated treatment of the value-grounding of preservation is [[phenomenal-normativity-environmental-ethics]], which is environmental *ethics*, not aesthetics; this article does not develop the preservation link into an argument.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]].** The scope problem and the observer question are clean instances of the tenet's caution. The deflationary readings win on parsimony: "aesthetic quality is just ordinary valenced perception," and "beauty is a property of the object," are both simpler than positing an irreducible evaluative phenomenal character arising only in a conscious encounter. The Map's fifth tenet holds that simplicity is an unreliable guide when knowledge is incomplete — so the economy of the deflationary accounts is a consideration, not a verdict. Whether the pervasive felt-evaluative texture of ordinary experience, and the felt beauty of an unwitnessed sunset, are discardable labels or a genuine phenomenal residue is exactly what remains open.
