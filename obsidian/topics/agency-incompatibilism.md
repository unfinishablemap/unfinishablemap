Helen Steward's **agency incompatibilism** is "the idea that agency itself is inconsistent with determinism" (Steward 2014a). To act, on her view, is to settle some matter at the time of acting, and a matter the past and the laws already fix cannot be settled then. Her settlers are animals, human and many non-human, and her agent causation is "simply the influence of a whole animal on its own parts" (2014b). Her aim is "a truly naturalistic libertarianism" (2014a), and the Unfinishable Map cannot recruit her. She answers the Map's question, *who settles?*, as the Map does, with the agent rather than the agent's states, and holds that the settler is wholly physical.

The verdict comes first, and it is the Map's own analysis. On sourcehood and luck the Map's non-physical settler adds nothing that Steward's animal lacks: both views end in a primitive exercise of the agent's power, and she reaches that terminus without a second ontological category. The disagreement lies in three places, set out in [[#comparison|the comparison]] (explained below). A non-physical settler escapes her hardest problem, how a whole acts on its own parts without reducing to them, but pays with the pairing and interface debts the Map already owes, so the exchange is a trade. The Map's leverage on subjectivity, which she writes into the concept of an agent and expects biology to explain, comes from the hard problem of consciousness, not from the free-will debate. And her scope, which counts habitual movements and animal doings as settlings, presses a question the Map's agency articles have not answered.

## Agency Incompatibilism Stated {#the-thesis}

Steward's central notion is settling. A true agent has the power "genuinely to settle with her actions for at least some propositions p, whether or not it will be the case that p" (2014a), where a matter is "metaphysically settled only as I act (and not before)" (2013, reply to Garnett). Prior causes generally act by "influence, not determination": "It remains the agent herself who has to convert this influence into activity" (2014a).

The label is hers, adopted "rather late in the day" (2014a). For the leeway/source distinction she cites Kane and Pereboom, then fuses its halves: "one could only be a true source if one had leeway" (2014a). A 2021 paper argues that leeway is "part and parcel of what it is to be the agential source of an action", drawing on the Aristotelian idea of agency as a two-way power (Steward 2020; 2021, abstracts), and distinguishes that argument from Kevin Timpe's for the same conclusion.

The modal demand is weak. "All that is required for Agency Incompatibilism is that no individual action be a necessitated event" (2014a). The agent need not have been able to choose B instead of A; the action need only be "a non-compulsory exercise of power on his part" (2012, p. 195, as quoted by Buckareff 2013).

The scope is wide. Agency is "a power common to a wide range of animals", and covers what we do "unthinkingly, habitually, unconsciously, without reason" (2014a). Scratching an ear or crossing one's legs "feel agentive" (2013, reply to Broadie). Hence: "Causation by the mental, then, cannot be the key to understanding what is agential about agency, because so much agency proceeds without it" (2014a).

From there the argument runs to physics. Animals act, so determinism is false. Animal agency proves nothing, but "it is a very good reason for believing in" indeterminism (2012, p. 6 n. 15, as quoted by Buckareff). Replying to Helen Beebee, she reports that "my central commitment is actually to indeterminism, rather than to incompatibilism" (2014b). Whether our concept of agency really is incompatibilist she treats as "an empirical question", answered in Chapter 4 from developmental psychology (2014b).

## Two Determinisms and Top-Down Causation {#two-determinisms}

Steward separates two threats. One is the determination of the present by the past. The other is "the bottom-up determination of the activities going on within a large and complex system by the activities going on in its parts", and microphysical indeterminism "addresses only the first" (2014a). Quantum indeterminacy, "by itself, is inadequate to make the problem disappear", though replacing a microphysically deterministic picture with an indeterministic one "will, I think, still prove to be necessary" (2012, p. 11).

Her answer to the second threat is top-down causation by the whole animal, and "The important relations between animal and parts have to be synchronous, not diachronic" (2014b). The argument turns on coincidence. Lower-level laws leave unexplained why so many neural events line up at the right moment; the explanation cites the animal ("the animal was trying to dance!"). Agency then needs "causal pluralism, plus top-down causation, plus indeterminism", the last because the top-down power must be "an indeterministic power, such that it remains an open possibility at each moment whether or not it will be exercised" (2014b).

None of this is dualist. "Agent causation is not a strange input from some other ethereal realm into the physical world; agent causation is simply the influence of a whole animal on its own parts. And since animals are physical, their influence on things is also physical" (2014b). The one "dualism" she relies on, the non-identity of an animal with its body, "needs to be sharply distinguished from Cartesian dualism" and "has no tendency whatever to imply" an "immaterial soul together with a material body" (2012, p. 16 n. 37). The publisher's abstract calls the book "a non-dualistic version of libertarianism".

She is candid about the cost. Chapter 8 concedes "a very difficult issue" about how an animal's causation "could fail to reduce to causation of effects by the parts of that same animal" (2012, ch. 8 abstract). She says she answered it "admittedly programmatically", and that the prospect can seem hopeless "in my darker moments ... even to me" (2014b).

## The Luck Concession {#luck-concession}

Chapter 6 takes on Alfred Mele's formulation of the luck problem and argues that "the libertarian should make an important concession to the compatibilist"; the incompatibilism that results, she argues, "can meet the Challenge from Chance" (2012, ch. 6 abstract). In her worked case Joe decides to move in with his girlfriend. The libertarian, she writes, "ought not to fear the conclusion that, on this understanding, it can indeed be said to be a matter of luck that Joe decides to move in with his girlfriend rather than not, since this is a conclusion she embraces in any case". That luck is "a crucial part of her position, not an unforeseen disastrous consequence" (p. 131). Where reasons make one option clearly best, a chance of some rationally inexplicable alternative "cannot be the sort of alternative possibility on which the libertarian should insist" (p. 143).

What the agent settles then moves. In a clear case Joe's reasons should determine what he decides, but the particular event at its particular time need not be causally determined (2013, reply to Garnett). Steward denies that this confines settling to timing and fine detail: "Settling only begins where reasons fail to dictate a unique course of action – but that, in my view, is nearly everywhere", because reasons "quite vastly underdetermine" what to do at any moment (2013, reply to Boxer). In 2013 she also revised her concession about constrained cases. Imagining reasons that fix every detail of a movement, she wrote that "it is in the deployment of the necessary care and attention that we see the agent's settling of what occurs", so "perhaps contra my original position" a possibility of not acting that matters remains even where reasons fix only whether and when to act (reply to Garnett).

Critics deny that the move works. Neil Levy reads her as conceding "that the luck objection is persuasive with regard to existing versions of libertarianism", and argues that the fine-grained aspects she leaves to the agent "are lucky for us and, since this is true, they cannot play the role of grounding our agency" (Levy 2013, abstract). Levy also reads her as leaving broadly individuated action types to the compatibilist; her later reply to Karin Boxer holds instead that "we settle which actions occur under the descriptions folk psychology would offer up – at least most of the time". Michael Garnett concludes that the Challenge from Chance "remains an important source of dialectical advantage for the compatibilist" (2013, abstract). No reply by Steward to Levy's paper was located.

## What the Symposia Conceded {#symposia}

Two journal symposia examined the book, in *Inquiry* (2013) and *Res Philosophica* (2014). In both she conceded that her case against compatibilism had a narrow target. To Sarah Broadie: "my critique of compatibilism really focuses only on one, rather specific type of compatibilism", the type that reduces agency to the causation of movements by mental states. To Randolph Clarke, who found her main argument "wanting" (Clarke 2014, abstract): "I regret my over-exclusive focus on the Causal Theory" (2014b). She kept the thesis, arguing case by case against the non-reductive alternatives and resting her positive case on Chapter 4's developmental evidence.

Her reply to Sean Clancy holds the line that matters most for the Map. Of his deterministic agent she writes, "What settles the outcome is not Rufus, but his properties", and her agent causation "can only exist at all in an indeterministic universe" (2013).

## What the Map's Settler Adds, and What It Does Not {#comparison}

This section is the Map's analysis. No published exchange between Steward and an interactionist dualist was found.

**Sourcehood and luck: nothing.** Her reply to Clancy is the Map's own reasoning that states and properties cannot stand in for the agent, made by a physicalist about animals. The Map's reply to luck ends in "a legitimate terminus" that "says *who* settles the outcome, not why it went one way" ([[agent-causation#the-luck-objection|agent causation]]), and her account ends there too. Van Inwagen's replay, which grants the agent-cause and still finds chance, presses both settlers alike ([[rollback-argument-against-agent-causation|the rollback argument]]). Where the Map adds that reasons guide selection and make a choice intelligible, the reasons do that work, and a physical settler selecting on reasons gets the same. On akrasia, [[the-divided-will#luck-objection|the divided-will article]] records that this reply fails, and that accepting the surd as the price of sourcehood is "the direction Steward's argument points". Her "nearly everywhere" would make that article's other reply, confining selection to choices with no settled ranking, less of a retreat than it reads. It leaves akrasia untouched, since akrasia is defined by a ranking the agent acts against.

**The persisting subject: a physical candidate.** [[where-the-substance-commitment-enters|The Map's diagnostic]] argues that agent causation needs a persisting substance to do the causing, since without one "the agent originated the action" loses its referent. Steward supplies such a substance, the animal, and it is physical. The substance requirement therefore does not reach the non-physical by itself, which matches the finding in [[agent-causation-and-acting-for-reasons]] that dualism does not decide how agents act for reasons.

**Reduction: a trade.** Steward must hold that the animal's efficacy is realised in its parts yet "does not reduce to the connected efficacies of its various related parts" (2014b), and her account of how is programmatic. A non-physical settler is not composed of the parts it influences, so bottom-up determination of a whole by its parts does not bind it. It pays instead with the [[pairing-problem|pairing problem]] and with the [[positions/quantum-interface#^mechanism-debt|mechanism debt]] of an interface whose selection leaves public statistics Born-exact. Each view carries a debt where the other is clear.

**Subjectivity: possible leverage.** Among the features of the mature concept of agency Steward lists "An agent is a centre of some form of subjectivity" (2012, pp. 71–2, quoted in 2014b). She thinks it "quite likely, as John Searle has argued, that consciousness will turn out to be essentially a biological phenomenon, as will agency", while granting "we are not yet in a position to be able to be sure that this is so" (2012, p. 91). If the [[hard-problem-of-consciousness|hard problem]] defeats that bet, a settler defined partly by subjectivity needs more than biology. That leverage comes from the philosophy of mind, and nothing in the free-will debate supplies it. It reaches only what an agent is, since she denies that mental causation is what makes an act agential.

**Scope: a cost to the Map.** For Steward, habitual, sub-intentional and animal doings are settlings. The Map's [[agent-causation]] article calls agent causation "attenuated in scripted behavior" and treats [[skill-delegation|skilled automaticity]] as "*delegation*, not absence". That article, [[free-will]] and the [[positions/agency-and-will|agency register]] never take up whether animals settle; the free-will article says only that genuine choice may need counterfactual reasoning some species appear to lack, and [[animal-consciousness]] extends selection by felt value to animals only conditionally. The Map owes an answer. Either its settler is at work wherever something acts, animals included, or delegated and unreflective doings are settled by something other than the agent, and on Steward's concept they would then fail to be actions.

One caution follows. Her placing of settling in "care and attention" reads close to the Map's [[attention-as-interface|attention-as-interface]] material, but she means an animal's physical top-level control, so it gives that model no support.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]].** Steward rejects the first tenet in the form the Map needs. Agency incompatibilism is neutral between her settler and the Map's, so it gives the tenet no support, and the Map's case that its settler is non-physical has to rest on the hard problem.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]].** Her top-down causation agrees with the third tenet in form only: a whole affects its parts, but both are physical and nothing crosses an ontological boundary. The Map's settling at the interface fits the tenet without evidencing it, and Steward argues that the free-will motive for reading the tenet's selection as the agent's own origination can be met without dualism. Nothing in her argument tells against the efficacy of consciousness. The tenet keeps the standing [[tenets#^tenet-3-standing|the tenets page]] gives it, a posit the interface argument leaves open.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]].** She thinks microphysical indeterminism necessary and holds it certainly insufficient, and the sources read name no physical locus for her top-down openness; a 2016 chapter claims only that the view "can be argued to be consonant with our best physics" (abstract). Her two determinisms give the Map a test. Quantum openness answers determination by the past. On the Map's picture, bottom-up determination is answered by a settler that is no part of the system it influences, an answer her naturalism cannot use.

**[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]].** Nothing read here shows her discussing many-worlds. On the Map's reading, settling whether *p* presupposes that the unchosen alternative stays unactual, the global-exclusion condition [[agent-causation]] says the Map posits alongside sourcehood.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]].** Her view is simpler than the Map's by one ontological category. The fifth tenet lets the Map decline to treat that as decisive and gives it no positive reason to add the category; that reason has to come from the hard problem.
