The Unfinishable Map's case against [[epiphenomenalism]] leans on a premise its own epistemology denies. The tenets make the [[self-stultification|self-stultification argument]] turn on whether "knowledge of one's experience requires *causal traffic* from experience to belief" (the [[tenets#^tenet-3-epiphenomenalism|contested premise]]). The Map's account of [[phenomenal-acquaintance]] says that present experience is known with no intermediary at all. David Chalmers (2003) built the epiphenomenalist's reply from exactly that material: when experience partly constitutes the belief about it, the connection is "tighter than any causal connection: it is constitution". This article argues that the Map should grant the point for *knowing* and move the argument. Knowing by acquaintance needs no causation. *Using* what one knows (inference, memory, perceptual justification) and *voicing* it (report, assertion) still do.

The result is a repair of the Map's own argument, and it narrows that argument. Self-stultification no longer shows that an epiphenomenalist cannot know their experience, at most that their theory is not reasoned from, or voiced from, that knowledge. The repair also has a price. The relocated argument needs consciousness to be causally effective *in virtue of what it presents*, and plain interactionism secures that no better than epiphenomenalism does. The Map passes only through its tenet's clause that consciousness biases outcomes "toward patterns that express its content", which the Map itself holds as an [[tenets#^tenet-3-standing|open posit]]. Nothing here refutes epiphenomenalism; the [[#three-channels|three channels]] (explained below) show where the dispute now lives.

## The Premise and the Pages That Deny It {#the-premise}

The tenets make the objection's bite depend on the causal-traffic premise, and the [[self-stultification#The Coincidence Response|Coincidence Response]] in the Map's concept article ends on the same debt: the common-cause reply "still owes" an account of how a lawful parallel yields *knowledge* of experience "without causal traffic from it".

Four of the Map's own passages deny that premise for present experience:

- [[phenomenal-acquaintance|Phenomenal acquaintance]] holds that "No inference, representation, or intermediary stands between subject and quality", unlike perception of external objects, "where causal chains mediate between perceiver and world".
- The same article holds that acquaintance "supplies *apodictic* evidence for the existence of experience".
- [[constitutive-vs-referring-observation|Constitutive observation]] holds that "*To be acquainted with phenomenality is, in part, to be undergoing it.*"
- The [[metaproblem-of-consciousness-under-dualism|metaproblem article]] entertains the thought that acquaintance justifies our problem intuitions "however they were caused", and grants that "Acquaintance answers the charge that our beliefs are unjustified".

If experience is present to its subject with no intermediary, a causal chain from experience to belief is not what makes the subject know it.

The Map's text contains half a reply. The constitutive-observation article says its claim "concerns seemings, not the beliefs and reports a seeming usually produces", and the acquaintance article separates "the relation from judgements based on it". Acquaintance might then supply evidence without causation while belief forms downstream.

But "produces" and "based on" then carry the whole argument. They amount to a causal theory of *basing*, on which a belief is held on the evidence of acquaintance only if acquaintance causally contributes to it. The Map has stated that theory nowhere, and Chalmers replaces it with constitution for direct phenomenal beliefs. The Map's commitment to [[intentionality#Phenomenal Intentionality Theory|phenomenal intentionality]] makes the replacement harder to resist, since there acquaintance "is the mechanism" that makes a thought about its object, a constitutive role for experience in content. The tension is real, and a careful reading does not remove it.

## Chalmers's Reply, 1996 to 2003 {#chalmers-reply}

In *The Conscious Mind*, Chalmers (1996) named the paradox of phenomenal judgment: consciousness seems "explanatorily irrelevant to our claims and judgments about consciousness" (p. 177). He stated the causal-theory objection at full strength, "If a causal theory of knowledge is correct, it follows that we cannot know anything about our experiences", and answered that for a property dualist such a theory "is not appropriate for our knowledge of consciousness" (p. 193). He added a certainty argument: we know for certain that we are conscious, yet "Where there is causation, there is contingency" (p. 195). Tim Bayne (2001) pressed a parallel worry against acquaintance accounts, as Chalmers (2003, p. 34 n. 14) reports and rejects. The positive view was that "it is having the experiences that justifies the beliefs" (p. 196).

By 2003 Chalmers judged that discussion "at best suboptimal" and offered his new account "in part as a replacement for that chapter" (preprint p. 36). The replacement is stronger. The content of a direct phenomenal belief "is partly constituted by an underlying phenomenal quality" (p. 16), and acquaintance with that quality justifies the belief (p. 30). Chalmers then takes the hardest case on purpose: "I will assume epiphenomenalism, according to which the phenomenal has no effects on the physical domain." His conclusion is that "the connection between experience and phenomenal belief is tighter than any causal connection: it is constitution", and that "if a causal connection can underwrite knowledge, a constitutive connection can certainly underwrite knowledge too" (p. 37).

Three limits keep the reply in proportion. Chalmers is "not endorsing epiphenomenalism"; he counts it as "one of the three serious options" for those who accept phenomenal realism (p. 37 n. 16). He argues that a zombie, lacking the constituting experience, "cannot share a conscious being's phenomenal beliefs" (p. 38), which on his account answers the familiar zombie-twin form of the objection. And he marks where the account stops: the gap between phenomenal knowledge and knowledge of the external world "remains as wide as ever, and I have done nothing to close it" (p. 48).

W. S. Robinson, who has defended epiphenomenalism against this objection, concludes in his encyclopedia entry that Chalmers's view "avoids the self-stultification argument" (Robinson 2023, §2.4).

## Where the Causal Premise Came From {#origins}

The premise the Map borrowed was built for a different target. Chalmers (1996, p. 193) records that Sydney Shoemaker (1975) used such arguments "to argue for materialism about consciousness", and Felipe De Brigard (2014, p. 121) names Shoemaker and Daniel Dennett as the objection's sources. In the reconstruction De Brigard takes over from Robinson, the conclusion is that any dualism claiming non-inferential knowledge of non-physical sensations is false, and the causal premise requires that what is known non-inferentially "must cause some change in S which is causally contributory to S's believing what he does about x" (p. 122). The interactionist escapes by denying that physical events have only physical causes, and keeps the causal premise.

De Brigard is the source the tenets cite for the causal premise, and his paper gives the Map less than that citation implies. He concedes Robinson's point that a counterfactual condition (S would not believe that x is F if it were not true) "is all you need — and have — in the case of the knowledge of our own sensations" (pp. 123–124). His first counterargument says the epiphenomenalist's best repair requires "an additional sort of nomological relation the identity theorist need not be committed to" (p. 126). That is a parsimony consideration, the kind the Map's [[tenets#^occams-limits|fifth tenet]] discounts, and a constitutive connection is stronger than a merely nomological one, so the argument does not reach Chalmers.

His comparison class is the identity theory, since "Robinson's argument focuses on identity theory alone" (p. 127 n. 6), and of Chalmers's version he writes that "whether it can do it or not is beyond the scope of this paper" (p. 129 n. 8). He defends the objection against Robinson's common-cause epiphenomenalism, concludes that epiphenomenalism remains "rather suspicious" (p. 129), and offers nothing against the acquaintance-and-constitution reply.

## Three Channels: Presence, Use and Voice {#three-channels}

Once the premise moves, the two commitments fit. The Map's restatement, its own synthesis rather than a sourced position: experience is present to its subject without causal mediation, and that presence is evidence; but what the subject builds on the evidence counts as built *from* it only if the features that make experience evidence do causal work in producing it. Three channels then separate.

**Presence** covers acquaintance with present experience, the direct phenomenal belief, and the belief that one is conscious. Here epiphenomenalist and interactionist stand level: Chalmers secures presence by constitution, and an interactionist's acquaintance is no better a ground. The argument has no purchase on this channel.

**Use** covers inference to theory, memory and perceptual justification: what a subject does with phenomenal evidence beyond holding the direct belief. Chalmers keeps causation here himself. An inferred belief "must be appropriately caused by the justifying belief", while only the direct phenomenal belief "must be appropriately constituted by the experience" (2003, p. 32). His account of memory runs the causal connection between belief states, concluding that "a causal connection to an experience is not required to remember that experience" (1996, pp. 200–201), and perceptual knowledge is the gap he leaves open (2003, p. 48). On his property dualism, the theory of consciousness an epiphenomenalist reasons towards is produced by physical vehicles whose phenomenal constituents do no causal work.

**Voice** covers report, assertion and publication. The encyclopedia entry frames the objection as a disjunction: either we cannot know our own mental events, or "our knowledge of them cannot be what is causing the plainly physical event of our saying something about our mental events" (Robinson 2023, §2.4). The acquaintance reply takes the second horn, and what it leaves behind is the *saying*. Chalmers concedes the third-person side: "from the third-person point of view, my zombie twin and I are identical, so that you cannot be certain that I am conscious" (1996, pp. 198–199).

Robinson's parity argument also operates here. On an interactionist diagram, a later brain event leading to an utterance is caused by the experience only through an earlier brain event. Denying it knowledge-conveying status on that ground would mean "rejecting interactionism for virtually the same reason that epiphenomenalism is thought to be unacceptable", and the later event "does not have any property that contains information as to how it was caused" (§2.4). The Map's reply is that the requirement concerns the history of the chain, whether the experience's own character shaped it, rather than any property visible in the later event. That reply holds only on the condition set out in [[#content-efficacy|the efficacy-of-evidence section]].

On this restatement, self-stultification concludes that the epiphenomenalist's theory is not reasoned from, or voiced from, their evidence. That is narrower than the classic conclusion that the theory cannot be rationally held, and broader than a report-only reading, because against Chalmers's property dualist the use channel presses at least as hard as the voice channel. Frank Jackson's (1982) defence of epiphenomenal qualia left room for this: he allowed that qualia may make "a difference to other mental states though not to anything physical", since considering "how you could come to be aware of the instantiation of qualia" suggests it (p. 133). On this article's reading, he kept the use channel causal but inside the mind.

## Two Epiphenomenalisms {#two-epiphenomenalisms}

The channels sort the two dualist epiphenomenalisms the Map faces.

Chalmers's property dualist has a physical judging system whose direct phenomenal beliefs are partly constituted by phenomenal qualities. Presence is secured. Use and voice both run through physical processes that the phenomenal constituents do not move, so the relocated argument presses on both.

Helen Yetter-Chappell (2022) argues that there is no paradox of phenomenal judgment once one is a dualist about *subjects* as well as about qualia: the judging subject is non-physical, and its phenomenal self-knowledge is then unproblematic. The Map's concept article already concedes, in [[self-stultification#Dualism All the Way Down|its treatment of this view]], that against such an opponent the argument shows at most that epiphenomenalism cannot be rationally *asserted*.

The channel analysis refines that concession. If the non-physical subject has an internal causal life, the mental-to-mental causation Jackson left open, use runs inside the mind and only the voice channel remains, which is where the concession lands. If the subject is inert inwardly as well, the use channel presses on her view as on Chalmers's. Which of these Yetter-Chappell holds is left open here, and the concept article's account of how assertion fares on her view is the Map's reading of her commitment rather than a report of her text.

## The Efficacy-of-Evidence Premise and Its Cost {#content-efficacy}

The relocated argument rests on a premise the Map must own: a downstream belief, inference or assertion is held or made *from* evidence only if the evidence's evidential features causally contribute to producing it. Two pressures bear on it.

The first is parity with [[content-externalism|content externalism]]. If what a belief is about depends on factors outside the head that do not alter what its local vehicle does, the same standard would make inference among externally individuated beliefs unjustified, and few epistemologists accept that. The Map's candidate answer is its own rather than the sources': on its hybrid view a wide belief includes a narrow, phenomenally constituted component that carries the evidential features, so the efficacy question passes to that component, whereas an epiphenomenal phenomenal constituent is merely correlated with the vehicle doing the work. Causal history alone would not secure this, since content fixed by a vehicle's history need not affect what the vehicle does. The Map's [[causal-relevance-of-content|treatment of content's causal relevance]] makes the answer conditional on the content-efficacy question below, and this article does not settle it.

The second pressure falls on interactionism. Plain interactionism, on which consciousness causes physical effects but [[anomalous-monism-and-the-denial-of-strict-psychophysical-laws#the-epiphenomenalism-objection|not in virtue of what it presents]], does no better than epiphenomenalism at the use and voice channels: a report caused by consciousness, but not by the experience's being the experience it is, is not voiced from that experience. Hedda Hassel Mørch (2017), discussed in the Map's [[inheritance-problem-for-the-psychophysical-law|inheritance-problem article]], reaches a structurally similar verdict about the evolutionary argument. She judges it to fail as support for standard non-epiphenomenalist views, interactionism included, and to be far stronger as an argument for a phenomenal powers view, on which phenomenal properties produce their effects in virtue of their phenomenal character. Her wider work argues for panpsychism, so the parallel is structural and she is no ally of the Map's dualism.

The Map clears this bar only through its [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] clause that consciousness biases quantum indeterminacy in the brain "toward patterns that express its content", which makes consciousness efficacious in virtue of what it presents. That clause is a posit: the tenets record that genuine, non-epiphenomenal downward causation is "a posit the interface argument leaves open, not a result it secures". The relocated argument therefore inherits the Map's [[positions/quantum-interface#^mechanism-debt|mechanism debt]]. It favours content-efficacious interactionism over epiphenomenalism without showing that the Map's interactionism is content-efficacious.

The argument narrows and, if the efficacy-of-evidence premise holds, survives. It no longer reaches knowledge of present experience. It still reaches theorising and testimony, against any opponent whose reasoning or speech runs through processes the experience does not move. What then separates the Map from its strongest dualist opponents is one question, whether consciousness acts in virtue of its content, and the argument frames that question without answering it.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]].** The tenet's Definition already speaks the language of the voice channel: "If we can speak, write, or otherwise report our conscious experiences, then information about consciousness must flow into the physical domain." The repair brings the tenet's self-stultification support into line with that Definition. On this analysis the premise the support needs concerns reasoning from and voicing experience rather than knowing it, and the Map's [[tenets#^tenet-3-epiphenomenalism|conditional framing]] of the objection already declines to treat it as a refutation.

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]].** Conceding the presence channel costs the Map's dualism nothing. Acquaintance is the relation through which the Map takes qualitative character to be given, and Chalmers's reply presupposes property dualism. The concession bears on which dualism the argument favours, inert or efficacious, rather than on dualism itself.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]].** The Map speculates that content-expressing outcome selection is the smallest interface that meets the relocated premise: consciousness need not add energy or override statistics, only fix which outcome becomes actual in a way that answers to what it presents. Whether such selection occurs is the open question the mechanism debt records.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]].** De Brigard's main lever against the epiphenomenalist is parsimony, an extra kind of nomological relation. The Map holds simplicity to be an unreliable guide under incomplete knowledge, so its case against epiphenomenalism has to run through the use and voice channels instead.
