Kant's Paralogisms of Pure Reason (*Critique of Pure Reason*, A341–405 / B399–432) argue that the bare "I think" proves nothing about the thinker's substantiality, simplicity, or identity over time. The Unfinishable Map posits that a determinate subject persists through time, so the question is whether that subject survives the Paralogisms. On the Map's own reconstruction it does, because the Map *posits* its subject rather than *inferring* it from self-consciousness, and a paralogism is a defective inference. The subject is still exposed at [[#three-exposures|three points]] (explained below), wherever the corpus argues instead of positing: when felt [[mine-ness]] or felt unity is treated as evidence for a determinate subject; when the [[unity-of-consciousness|unity of consciousness]] is taken to show that the subject is *simple*; and in the posit's diachronic clause, the claim that one subject persists across time. At each point Kant's diagnosis applies in full.

One Map commitment takes a form Kant never considered: if conscious states condition quantum outcomes, a subject index is written into the physical selection law. If that index ever left a trace in data, the subject's individuation would be determined through its effects, by the empirical route Kant's chapter leaves open ([[#what-escapes|What Escapes the Dialectic]], explained below). At present the index is only compatible with the evidence, because the Map holds downward causation to be available rather than shown actual. The readings of Kant used here are contested in the scholarship, and the mapping of Kant's arguments onto the Map's commitments is the Map's own, not any Kant scholar's.

## Rational Psychology and Its Four Inferences {#four-paralogisms}

Kant's target is *rational psychology*, the doctrine that tries to derive the nature of the soul from self-consciousness alone: "I think is thus the sole text of rational psychology, from which it is to develop its entire wisdom" (A343/B401). Following the table of categories, rational psychology claims that the soul is a substance, that it is simple, that it is numerically identical across the times it exists, and that it stands in relation to possible objects in space; from these, in combination, it derives immateriality, incorruptibility, personality, spirituality, interaction with bodies and immortality (A344–345/B402–403).

Kant's diagnosis is the same in every case: a logical feature of the "I think" is read as a real determination of a thing. Through the "I", "nothing further is represented than a transcendental subject of thoughts = x" (A346/B404). The A edition (1781) runs the diagnosis against four syllogisms, each with a middle term that shifts meaning between premises:

1. **Substantiality** (A348–351). The absolute subject of all judgements is a substance; the thinking I is that subject; so it is a substance. Being the logical subject of every thought, Kant replies, yields no intuition of anything persisting, so the soul is a substance in idea only, not in reality.
2. **Simplicity** (A351–361). A thing whose action cannot be the concurrence of many agents is simple; the thinking I is such a thing; so it is simple. Kant ranks this the strongest of the four, then denies that its key premise can be proved: the unity of a thought made of many representations is *collective*, and as far as concepts go it fits the collective unity of cooperating substances as easily as the absolute unity of one subject (A352–353).
3. **Personality** (A361–366). What is conscious of its numerical identity at different times is a person; the soul is so conscious; so it is a person. Kant replies that identity of consciousness across time is "only a formal condition of my thoughts and their connection, but it does not prove at all the numerical identity of my subject" (A363). His footnote imagines elastic balls: a series of substances "of which the first would communicate its state, together with its consciousness, to the second" (A363–364n), and so on, until the last is conscious of every earlier state as its own without having been the same person throughout.
4. **Ideality of outer relation** (A366–380). Outer objects, inferred only as causes of perceptions, have doubtful existence. Kant answers with transcendental idealism.

The A edition names the underlying error as mistaking a unity that belongs to the synthesis of thoughts for a unity perceived in their subject (A402).

The B edition (1787) replaces the four syllogisms with one — "What cannot be thought otherwise than as subject does not exist otherwise than as subject, and is therefore substance" (B410) — and classes it as a *sophisma figurae dictionis*, a fallacy of equivocation (B411): the major premise speaks of a being in every respect, even as it might be given in intuition, the minor only of the subject of thought. Kant adds a reply to Mendelssohn's proof of the soul's permanence (a simple substance could still cease through a gradual remission of all its powers, B413–415) and moves the fourth paralogism's topic to the Refutation of Idealism.

Kant states the stakes plainly: "It would be a great, or indeed the only stumbling block to our entire critique, if it were possible to prove a priori that all thinking beings are in themselves simple substances" (B409). The verdict cuts both ways: "if materialism will not work as a way of explaining my existence, then spiritualism is just as unsatisfactory for it" (B420), and the chapter is a discipline against both "a soulless materialism" and "a spiritualism that must be groundless for us in life" (B421). What survives is "I exist thinking", of which Kant says "that proposition is empirical" (B420).

## How the Chapter Is Read {#readings}

The scholarship divides over what the chapter achieves.

**The deflationary line.** From Strawson's *The Bounds of Sense* (1966) to Patricia Kitcher's recent work, the mainstream analytic reading takes each paralogism to mistake a feature of how "I" functions in thought for a property of a thing. Strawson's 1997 chapter describes a systematic liability, in anyone studying the self in abstraction from empirical consciousness, "to confuse the unity and simplicity of his or her consciousness with a consciousness of unity and simplicity" (Strawson 1997, chapter abstract). The first paralogism's exact fallacy is disputed within this line. On Ian Proops's reading, Kant's point is that the grammatical distribution of the first-person pronoun "entails nothing about how the self can be conceived" (Proops 2021, quoted in Kitcher 2024); Kitcher locates the confusion instead in "the fact that the I-representation is both empty and necessary for cognition", while agreeing with Proops that the chapter's task is "less to refute the claims of Rational Psychology than to explain why they seem so seductive" (Kitcher 2024).

**The modest reading.** Karl Ameriks's *Kant's Theory of Mind* (1982; 2nd ed. 2000) concludes, by its publisher's abstract, that Kant's final position on the mind is far nearer to rationalism than is usually recognised; it is commonly summarised as having Kant reject rational psychology's proofs while keeping modest, deflated versions of some of its conclusions about the thinking self. On that reading the B420 agnosticism is meant literally: the critique removes an argument without establishing the denial of its conclusion. The deflationary line reads the chapter as deflationary throughout, and the dispute is live.

**The historical target.** Corey Dyck's *Kant and Rational Psychology* (2014) reconstructs the chapter's most proximate target as Christian Wolff's rational psychology, which began from empirical self-observation rather than pure concepts; Proops (2021) builds on Dyck, and Kitcher reports as one of Dyck's central claims that "'Rational Psychology' is a misnomer" (Kitcher 2024). This is the Wolff–Baumgarten milieu in which, as [[eighteenth-century-influx-debate|the eighteenth-century influx debate]] records, the pre-Critical Kant had argued for physical influx against the conception of grounds held by Wolff, Baumgarten and Meier (Watkins 2003). The Kant who argued for influx in 1755 is the Kant who, in 1781, denied that self-consciousness could prove the simple, persisting soul that debate had taken for granted.

## Dualist Replies {#dualist-replies}

In the texts examined, contemporary substance dualists seldom engage the chapter by name. The one book-length defence located is Stephen Priest's *A Priori Subjects: Kant's Attack on Cartesian Dualism*, a manuscript dated 2007 and hosted by the Oxford philosophy faculty, with no publication record found. Priest offers "a defence of the conclusions of 'the rationalist psychologist' against Kant's attack". He judges Kant's argument against the second paralogism's derivation "unsound", holds against A363 that "Kant is wrong to think the thesis of the transcendental unity of apperception is only formal, because it entails synthetic propositions", and concedes that "Kant's objection to the validity of the Fourth Paralogism is conclusive". His is a minority reading.

William Hasker and E. J. Lowe, in their chapters of *After Physicalism* (Göcke, ed., 2012), each mention Kant once and the Paralogisms not at all. Hasker locates the emergent dualist's principal objection to emergentist materialism in "the unity-of-consciousness argument which is derived from Leibniz and Kant", an argument Kant states at A351–352 and rejects at A352–353. Lowe grants that a Cartesian, wholly mental substance "is vulnerable to certain skeptical arguments to be found in the writings of numerous philosophers during the past three hundred years, including Locke and Kant" — chiefly the worry that a different substance had "my" thoughts yesterday, the substitution case of A363–364n — and defends a non-Cartesian substance dualism instead. Whether Richard Swinburne's books engage the chapter was not established, so the absence claim is narrow: Priest apart, neither substance-dualist chapter examined names the Paralogisms.

## A Posit Commits No Paralogism {#posit-not-paralogism}

From here the analysis is the Map's own reconstruction.

Each paralogism infers a property of the soul from the "I think". The Map's [[tenets/background-commitments|first background posit]] — "a numerically determinate subject that persists through time", combining primitive subject individuation with diachronic persistence — is reached by no such inference. The [[tenets]] page endorses the non-deflationary "I" "on independent grounds", because [[agent-causation|agent causation]] needs a persisting subject, and concedes under Tenet 5 that "the indexical question is meaningful" "is itself a posited claim, not a neutral datum". The [[positions/individuation-and-subjecthood|individuation register]] records the same structure in P-I2: Tenet 4 "is stated unconditionally but argued conditionally". A posit infers nothing from the "I think", so it cannot commit this defective inference. On the historical-target reading the Map stands further still from Kant's target, since it derives its subject neither from self-observation nor from the "I think".

The immunity has a price: a posit escapes the fallacy only as long as the corpus never argues for it from materials Kant showed to be inadequate. It does not escape Kant's rule for transcendental hypotheses, which explain nothing in nature (A772/B800) and are allowed "only as weapons of war, not for grounding a right but only for defending it" (A777/B805). The posit may answer a dogmatic denial of the subject. Used to explain agency and selection before the empirical route [[#what-escapes|below]] gives it a footing, it parts from Kant at the framework boundary rather than escaping him.

## Three Exposures {#three-exposures}

**Felt mine-ness used as evidence.** Wherever an article lets the felt unity, this-ness or ownership of experience count as evidence that a determinate subject exists, Strawson's diagnosis applies: the unity of consciousness is confused with a consciousness of unity. His 1966 book locates the source: when a subject ascribes a current experience to herself, "no use whatever of any criteria of personal identity is required", which is "the fact that lies at the root of the Cartesian illusion" (Strawson 1966, pp. 164–165) and the ground of what later work calls [[immunity-to-error-through-misidentification|immunity to error through misidentification]]. [[mine-ness|Mine-ness]] and [[haecceity|primitive thisness]] are features the Map ascribes to experience; they cannot double as evidence for the subject it posits. The register already concedes this. P-I1 grades its external evidence D because "felt this-ness is compatible with both the primitive-thisness and the perspectival-illusion readings, so no independent evidence discriminates". The Map's rule that a phenomenological datum is not by itself a metaphysical one restates Kant's point. Felt mine-ness is compatible with the Map's subject and gives it no support over rivals that predict the same datum. The same holds for felt unity of agency: the akratic's two pulls are present in one field, which is compatible with divided control, so their co-presence gives a single selector no support over a [[the-divided-will|partitioned will]].

**Unity read as simplicity.** The [[unity-of-consciousness]] article records William Hasker's [[emergent-dualism|emergent-dualist]] argument that no physical composite can be the single subject a unified experience requires, and describes phenomenal unity as "not assembled from parts but... intrinsic to consciousness itself"; the [[apex/mereology-of-mind|mereology of mind]] draws on composition programmes whose criteria cluster, after abstraction, where consciousness occurs. Read as establishing that the subject is *simple*, any of this runs the second paralogism's inference. Kant does not deny that a thought is unified; he denies that its unity can be shown, from concepts, to require one simple bearer rather than a collective of cooperating ones (A352–353). An article that wants unity to *establish* a simple subject owes a premise excluding the collective reading, and Kant holds that none is available a priori or from experience (A353). Until one is supplied, unity is compatible with a simple subject without showing one.

**The diachronic clause.** The third paralogism bears on the second half of the posit. The [[personal-identity]] article's reply to the replica case is the natural candidate here: "if *this* consciousness makes *these* quantum selections, the selector has a unique causal history a replica could not inherit." The reply does not meet Kant's case as stated. A causal history individuates a subject only if its selections were made by one persisting selector, and the elastic-ball series is the case where they were not: each substance contributes its share and passes on its states with its consciousness of them, so the record of selections runs unbroken while its bearer changes. To block the series the Map must hold that the later selector *is* the earlier one, which is the [[haecceity]] posit again, stated rather than argued; the article's own clarification grants that causal history individuates only "the already-posited particular". Butler (1736) had excluded this transfer by premise: even if a person is a property of a substance, consciousness of being the same person proves the same substance, "since the same property cannot be transferred from one substance to another". Kant denies that inference at A363, and the elastic balls treat the transfer as conceivable. Parfit, citing Locke and Kant, imagines memories passed along such a series "like a baton" (1984, p. 223, n. 17). If [[quasi-memory-and-the-circularity-objection|quasi-memory]] is coherent, as the Map accepts, no memory can tell a persisting subject from such a relay. Parfit concludes that, without other reasons, belief in such a subject should be rejected (§81); the Map's other reason is agent causation's need for a persisting subject, which is why its subject is posited rather than remembered. Lowe answers that substances which can be seen and touched are no more plausibly swapped undetectably than a table, while allowing that conclusive refutation "may be an open question"; the Map's subject cannot yet borrow it. A signature that tracks conscious state would pass along the relay with the state, so the empirical route [[#what-escapes|below]] could expose a swap only through a law sensitive to bare identity; the register grades persistence's empirical discriminability *none* ([[positions/subject-census|P-SC3]]).

This bears on a tension the Map has already named. Its [[bi-aspectual-ontology|aspect ontology]] is silent on what makes a subject one subject over time; the persisting, substance-leaning subject enters downstream, where agent causation and the indexical objection draw on it ([[where-the-substance-commitment-enters]]), and whether an aspect can supply it is recorded as open. The third paralogism shows that continuity of consciousness cannot underwrite that downstream entry. Whether something else does remains open, and the Kantian analysis sharpens the question without settling it either way.

## What Escapes the Dialectic {#what-escapes}

Kant's critique targets knowledge of the soul drawn from the "I think" alone, and leaves the empirical determinability of my existence in time where it was (B420). Kant denies that any empirical determination reaches the subject in itself: the intuition it depends on presents the subject only as appearance, never as noumenon (B429–430).

The Map has one commitment in a form Kant never faced; he weighed soul–body interaction only as physical influence and its two rivals, all resting on one subreption (A389–390). The [[positions/subject-census|subject-census register]] holds (P-SC1) that once conscious states condition quantum outcomes — the interface law written *P(O | C, X)* rather than *P(O | X)* — which subjects exist, and which system each is paired to, become part of the physical state description. A subject index inside a selection law would be determined, if at all, through its effects on outcome statistics: empirically, and so beyond the paralogisms' reach.

The escape is real but promissory. P-SC1 depends on [[tenets#^tenet-3-standing|Tenet 3 at its registered standing]] — the interface argument "shows Tenet 3's downward causation to be *available* … without showing it to be *actual*" — and inherits that limit. Its census trilemma sets how far the escape could go. Subject-conditioned effects either show up in conditional outcome statistics, giving the index a signature; or change nothing observable at any conditioning grain, so the index does no identifiable work; or differ but cancel in the aggregate, leaving the Map owing a subject-sensitive balancing law it has not written. Identifying a signature would also need an independent handle on each candidate subject's conscious state, so that the conditioning is not fixed by the hypothesis under test.

Three grades of standing follow. Today the subject index is *compatible* with the evidence and no more. It would become *suggestive* if a conditioned-register signature were reported, meaning an outcome dependence on conscious state visible only once the data are conditioned on that state. It would become *discriminating* only if the signature were subject-indexed, two subjects leaving two distinguishable signatures, since only that would fix *which* subject is paired with which system. Until then the Map's subject is a posit whose individuation could become an empirical determination, a standing Kant's chapter neither rules out nor grants.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The paralogisms leave the Map's tenets standing and narrow the arguments the Map may offer for them. Two costs follow, and one limit on what they cost.

First, [[positions/individuation-and-subjecthood|P-I1]] and the first background posit can never be presented as *following from* the unity or continuity of consciousness; they are primitive posits. An article that moves from felt unity, felt mine-ness or continuity of memory to a determinate persisting subject repeats, inside the Map, the inference Kant diagnosed.

Second, [[tenets#^no-many-worlds|Tenet 4]] loses any appearance of independence from subjecthood: its indexical objection presupposes the posited subject rather than supplying its ground, a dependency P-I2 already registers. The tenets page says the indexical argument "is as strong as the non-deflationary 'I' it presupposes, and no stronger", and that a deflated, fragmented or process-only subject would weaken it "correspondingly". The paralogisms are the classic argument that nothing in self-consciousness prevents that deflation, including the Madhyamaka analysis of the "I" as an aggregate of conditions that the tenets page leaves open at the framework boundary ([[buddhism-and-dualism]]).

The limit is that the posit is not refuted. Kant's B420 verdict is agnostic in both directions, and on Ameriks's modest reading Kant retained deflated forms of the rationalists' conclusions. "Kant refuted rational psychology, so the Map's subject is refuted" needs the premise that the Map derives its subject as the rationalists did, and it does not. The converse, from Kant's agnosticism to a permission to posit, needs [[tenets#^occams-limits|Tenet 5]] as a named premise, which stops parsimony counting against the posit without counting for it. The Map reads Kant's refusal to let either materialism or spiritualism win by default as a near relative of its own refusal to let parsimony settle what incomplete knowledge leaves open.

[[tenets#^dualism|Tenet 1]] is untouched in its bare form, since the irreducibility of consciousness is not inferred from the "I think"; the exposures concern only the substance-leaning reading that enters downstream. [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Tenet 3]], through [[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Tenet 2]]'s selection-level interface, supplies the one empirical route by which the subject could stop being a bare posit, at the standing Tenet 3 actually has. If a subject-indexed signature were found, the Map's subject, in its individuation though not its persistence, would pass from bare posit into the empirical psychology Kant left open, known there only as it appears. If the census trilemma resolves on its no-signature horn, the subject remains what the register says it is: a posit the Map chooses, holds openly, and cannot prove.
