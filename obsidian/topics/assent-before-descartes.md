**Assent** (Greek *synkatathesis*; Latin *adsensio*, later *assensus*) entered philosophy as a Stoic technical term for the act by which a rational mind accepts an impression as true. Long before Descartes made judgment an act of the will, thinkers argued over whether that acceptance is up to us, in six movements traced here. The Stoics held assent both in our power and compelled by the evident. Chrysippus reconciled that power with fate, and Epictetus made it a discipline of testing impressions. The Academic and Pyrrhonian sceptics made withholding assent a way of life. Augustine defined belief as thinking with assent and called the consent voluntary. Aquinas confined the will's part to cases the evidence leaves open. And in Oxford in the early 1330s the Dominican Robert Holcot argued against voluntary belief on both grounds the modern literature distinguishes, the introspective and the conceptual, six centuries before Alston and Williams gave those grounds their standard forms.

The Unfinishable Map's [[assent-void]] uses the word in its narrower modern sense, the acceptance of a proposition as true. This history follows the Greek and Latin line, with [[#buddhist-ascertainment|one Buddhist comparison]] set beside it and other accounts left aside, and is reconstructed from translations and encyclopedia scholarship: Holcot is known here only through the translations in Mark Boespflug and Elizabeth Jackson's *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry, and whether Stoic assent was "voluntary" in any modern sense is disputed. The record carries no evidence for the Map's tenets. Its one use, set out in [[#map-reading|What the History Shows]], is that thinkers with very different views of the mind all recognised a limit on believing at will, which suggests the limit belongs to belief rather than to any metaphysics of mind.

## The Stoic Double Claim {#stoic-double-claim}

Zeno of Citium introduced assent into his theory of sensation around 300 BCE, and Marie-Odile Goulet-Cazé (2011) credits the concept with having "founded both Stoic epistemology and Stoic ethics". Cicero preserves Zeno's illustration (*Academica* 2.145, Yonge's translation). Holding out an open hand, Zeno said perception "is a thing like this"; closing the fingers a little, "Assent is like this." The closed fist was comprehension, and the fist gripped by the other hand was knowledge, "what none but a wise man possessed".

On the reconstruction in the *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry on Stoicism (Durand, Shogry & Baltzly 2023), rational creatures answer for their acts of assent but not for the impressions they receive: assent is "'in our power' or 'up to us' in a way that receiving an impression is not".

The doctrine has a second half. In Cicero's *Lucullus* the spokesman for Antiochus, voicing the dogmatist case rather than Cicero's own, calls assent "wholly in our own power". In the next breath he compares the mind to a scale that must sink under a weight: "so the mind, too, must yield to what is evident". Then: "For he who perceives anything, assents immediately" (*Acad.* 2.37–38).

The two claims sit together because the sage's freedom is a freedom to withhold. The sage "is entirely free of opinion, since she withholds assent on any impression that is non-cognitive" (Durand, Shogry & Baltzly 2023). A cognitive impression compels assent; everything else waits on the agent. What is up to us is never assent against the evident, only the refusal to assent beyond it.

Goulet-Cazé finds the double claim inside the Stoic vocabulary. Some testimonies call assent *eixis*, "submission", and she argues that *eixis* and *synkatathesis* "express one and the same act, envisaged according to two different modalities": the passivity of a ruling part "which lets itself be swept along by a representation", and the same act "once subject to the control of reason" (2011, published abstract). Stoic assent was also wider than belief, issuing either in a doxastic commitment or in a "rational impulse" to act (Durand, Shogry & Baltzly 2023).

## Fate, the Cylinder and the Testing of Impressions {#cylinder}

Chrysippus, the school's third head, had to reconcile responsibility for assent with a world ruled by fate. His answer, preserved by Cicero (*De Fato* 42–43; Long & Sedley 62C, in their translation), is the cylinder, which a push sets rolling but which rolls because of its shape: "just as the person who pushed the cylinder gave it its beginning of motion but not its capacity for rolling, likewise, although the impression encountered will print and, as it were, emblazon its appearance on the mind, assent will be in our power." The impression is the push; the character of the mind receiving it settles the assent. The cylinder is an early compatibilism about assent: "in our power" means flowing from the agent's own nature, not exempt from causes.

Epictetus, more than three centuries later, made assent a daily discipline. "In our power are opinion", movement towards things, desire and aversion (*Ench.* 1, Long's translation). His third field of training "relates to the faculty of assent", and its rule is to examine an impression before admitting it: "Stop, let me see what you are and whence you come" (*Discourses* 3.12, Higginson's translation). No one, he adds, can compel us to assent to what appears false, or prevent us from assenting to what appears true (*Discourses* 1.17).

These passages say that assent cannot be forced from outside and that an impression can be held for inspection, which is less than believing at will. The *Stanford Encyclopedia* history of doxastic voluntarism (Boespflug & Jackson 2024) nonetheless reads *Discourses* 3.12.14 as treating assent "as subject to voluntary control", while calling this side of Epictetus "largely undeveloped". Michael Frede (2011) located the first notion of a will in Epictetus; on Thomas Blackson's (2025) report of him, Frede's Epictetus holds that adults choose to assent to impulsive impressions. Blackson replies that this "gets the object of choice wrong": the choice is "to exercise their ability to use their impressions", and the early Stoics probably believed the same.

## The Sceptics: Withholding as a Way of Life {#sceptics}

Hellenistic discussions, Katja Vogt writes in the *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry on ancient skepticism, "envisage three attitudes that cognizers take to impressions": assent, rejection and suspension of judgment, *epochē*. Zeno had studied in Plato's Academy, and Arcesilaus, who turned the Academy sceptical, argued against the Stoic criterion of truth from about 275 to 240 BCE. Without a criterion, Arcesilaus's sceptic "suspends judgment about everything" (Vogt 2022, citing Sextus, *PH* 1.232). The Stoic reply in the *Lucullus* was that a being who never assents has lost its agency: "although some things may be in our power, yet they will not be in the power of that man who assents to nothing" (*Acad.* 2.37–39).

Carneades, as his pupil Clitomachus reported him (*Acad.* 2.104), split suspension in two. In one sense the wise person "assents to nothing". In the other he refrains from committing himself yet remains "able to answer yes, or no, following probability".

Sextus Empiricus describes the assent he allows himself in three ways: forced (*PH* 1.13, 1.23–24), involuntary (*PH* 1.19) and non-doxastic (*PH* 2.102). The sceptic "does assent to the experiences forced upon him", and would not say, when warmed, that he seems not to be warmed (*PH* 1.13, Burnyeat's translation with changes, as Vogt quotes it). Vogt marks the distance from the Stoic concept: "Non-doxastic assent is, from the point of view of the Stoics, a contradiction in terms, just like forced and involuntary assent." For the Stoics assent was in our power by definition.

The Pyrrhonist does not suspend by an act of will. Facing opposed views of equal weight, sceptics "find themselves unable to decide things", and tranquillity follows; they "do not describe themselves as making an active effort at doubting what ordinarily they would believe, as some philosophers in the Cartesian tradition have it" (Vogt 2022). The Map's [[suspension-void]] already treats this suspension that arrives rather than being performed; the Stoic–Academic dispute is its antecedent.

## A Buddhist Comparison: Ascertainment Without an Act {#buddhist-ascertainment}

Buddhist epistemologists in India reached the Stoic two-stage picture, perception first and determination second, and left the act out. For Dharmakīrti (sixth or seventh century; the date is debated) perception is "purely non-conceptual" (Tillemans 2026). Judging what has been seen falls to ascertainment (*niścaya*), "the conceptual cognition that follows a perceptual cognition" (Nakasuka 2020, 283). Ascertainment is caused rather than performed. Its causes include acuity, habituation and context, and when they are deficient it fails to arise (Nakasuka 2020, 285). In John Dunne's illustration, a bright colour across a field may be a fire, but where the perception lacks acuity one knows it is fire only by inferring it from the smoke (Dunne 2004, 321). Nor need action wait for it: the commentator Śākyabuddhi lets doubt, as well as determination, move a judicious person to act (Dunne 2004, 291). The Stoic sage withholds assent; Dharmakīrti's account has no act of assent to withhold, only an ascertainment that arises or does not. Dharmottara's commentary on the opening sentence of Dharmakīrti's *Nyāyabindu* raises compulsion only for action and denies it there: right knowledge points out an object but lacks "the power forcibly to incite a man (against his will)" (Stcherbatsky 1930; the parentheses are his).

The Theravāda Abhidhamma makes the same point by inventory. "Decision" (*adhimokkha*) is a factor that arises together with a cognition rather than an act taken upon it. The Dhammasaṅgaṇī's list of the states in the first wholesome consciousness omits it, and Buddhaghosa's commentary reads it in, as "resolve", through the list's closing clause, "or whatever other incorporeal, causally induced states there are on that occasion" (Rhys Davids 1900, §1; Pe Maung Tin 1920, 174). It is absent from bare sense-consciousness and from the one type of consciousness "accompanied by 'Doubt'" (Nārada 1987, ch. II), so determinacy and doubt exclude each other as co-arising states rather than as options. The account of a single act of seeing keeps the order: "eye-consciousness seeing that very form" comes before "determining consciousness determining it" (ch. IV). Both traditions supply the ordering the Cartesian picture relies on, understanding first and determination second, and neither supplies the agent whose act the second stage would be.

## Augustine: Belief as Thinking With Assent {#augustine}

The Christian line made assent a matter of faith, and faith a matter of will; the *Stanford Encyclopedia* history quotes Clement of Alexandria calling faith "preconception by the will, an act of consenting to religion" (*Stromateis* II.2). Augustine supplied the definition the Latin Middle Ages used. In *On the Predestination of the Saints* (428–429), in a chapter headed "To Believe is to Think with Assent", he puts thought first, "no one believes anything unless he has first thought that it is to be believed", and defines belief: "belief itself is nothing else than to think with assent" (ch. 5 [II], Holmes and Wallis's translation).

In *On the Spirit and the Letter* (412) he had asked "whether faith is in our own power" and called the contrary absurd: "for what is believing but consenting to the truth of what is said? And this consent is certainly voluntary". He concluded that "faith, therefore, is in our own power", then at once credited it to grace: "it is God who gave us even to believe" (ch. 54 [XXXI]).

Augustine's "voluntary" belongs to an anti-Pelagian argument about merit and grace. It marks consent as an act the believer answers for and grace can give, not a power to believe arbitrary propositions on demand. His ordering, thought first and assent after, is the order Descartes's Fourth Meditation would state as a rule.

## Aquinas: The Will Commands Where Evidence Does Not {#aquinas}

Aquinas adopted Augustine's formula as the definition of believing (*Summa Theologiae* II-II q.2 a.1, Dominican Fathers' translation) and placed belief on a ladder. The doubter inclines to neither side, the suspecter inclines on slight grounds, and the opiner inclines "to one side yet with fear of the other". The believer "cleaves firmly to one side", without the clear sight of science. Where evidence does not decide, "the intellect of the believer is determined to one object, not by the reason, but by the will" (a.1 ad 3). Faith is "an act of the intellect assenting to the Divine truth at the command of the will moved by the grace of God" (a.9).

*De Veritate* 14.1, in the *Stanford Encyclopedia*'s translation, extends the point beyond faith. When neither the terms nor first principles settle a question, "our understanding is determined by the will, which chooses to assent to one side definitely and precisely because of something which is enough to move the will, though not enough to move the understanding". Where evidence compels, the will has no part: knowledge "compels assent—depriving the will of a choice in assenting", and so excludes belief (Boespflug & Jackson 2024).

Aquinas's voluntarism thus covers only the underdetermined case, which the Map notes is the territory the modern [[doxastic-voluntarism#rivals|permissivist voluntarists]] claim. Boespflug (2018) describes medieval voluntarism, which he defines as "direct voluntary control over their beliefs", as "pervasive": "It was held by Augustine, Aquinas, Scotus, Ockham, and Buridan, among many others."

## Holcot and the Fourteenth-Century Dissent {#holcot}

Robert Holcot, an Oxford Dominican who "belonged to the first generation of scholars to absorb and develop the views of William Ockham", lectured on Peter Lombard's *Sentences* at Oxford "in the years 1331–1333" (Gelber & Slotemaker 2017). He also disputed epistemology with fellow students in the *Sex articuli*, which Fritz Hoffmann edited among the Oxford disputations of 1330–1332, and died of the plague in 1349. Against the dominant voluntarism, the *Stanford Encyclopedia* reports, Holcot "would give both psychological and conceptual arguments against the possibility of voluntary belief".

The psychological argument appeals to introspection, and it targets exactly the case Aquinas reserved for the will: "every man, who does not want to obstinately deny sense, experiences in himself (*experitur in seipso*) that he cannot by command of the will determine himself to assent to one side of a contradiction, when both are open to question for him" (*Sex articuli* ii.91, trans. Boespflug & Jackson 2024). In such cases, the encyclopedia glosses, "one is compelled to suspend judgment."

The conceptual argument runs through appearance. One cannot believe a proposition true unless it appears true, and it is not in one's power to make things appear as a proposition says; so believing at will would need a power to make things appear as one wishes. The supposition, Holcot concludes, yields a contradiction: "I believe that this proposition is true, and that proposition does not appear to me to be true" (*Quaestiones super Sententiarum* I.1, trans. Boespflug & Jackson 2024).

Holcot also "presciently maintains that we may exercise some voluntary control over belief 'indirectly'" (*Sex articuli* ii.92; Boespflug & Jackson 2024). Gelber and Slotemaker record that he passed on "a version of what has come to be called 'Pascal's wager.'", in which a heretic's intention to believe in immortality is rewarded with the grace to believe.

### Who Stated the Arguments First {#credit}

Contemporary epistemology associates the two routes to involuntarism with Alston (1988), whose test asks one to try believing at will, and Williams (1973), whose argument turns on belief's aim at truth (see [[doxastic-voluntarism#two-grounds|Two Grounds for Involuntarism]]). Holcot's arguments are not theirs: Williams's turns on a believer's knowledge of having the power, Holcot's on belief's dependence on how things appear. But arguments of both kinds stand in the record six centuries before the modern formulations. Boespflug (2018) calls Holcot "somewhat neglected" and argues that his rejection of voluntarism shows "a coherence and plausibility that reflects and anticipates much contemporary thought on the issue". The Map credits the arguments to Holcot, the case for their significance to Boespflug, and the modern formulations to Williams and Alston.

## What Descartes Inherited {#descartes}

René Descartes, "like the Stoics he admired", would "treat assent as a species of action" (Boespflug & Jackson 2024). The Fourth Meditation traces error to a will "of much wider range than the understanding" that extends itself to what it does not understand, and rules that "the knowledge of the understanding ought always to precede the determination of the will" (Veitch's translation; AT VII:56–62). On the Map's reading the two claims have separate antecedents: the order of thought before assent appears in Augustine, the freedom of assent in the Stoa. Whether Descartes held every belief to be chosen is disputed; Boespflug (2023) argues that, given his compatibilism, "we may take Descartes at his word when he maintains that everything we believe, we believe freely – even if we cannot believe anything we wish." The objections of Hobbes and Spinoza belong to a later history.

## What the History Shows {#map-reading}

What follows is the Map's reading of the record, not a thesis any of these authors advanced.

**Pre-modern "voluntary" mostly meant answerable.** The Stoics put assent in our power and in the same breath made the mind yield to the evident. Augustine called consent voluntary and credited it to grace. Aquinas gave the will a role only where evidence fell short. Boespflug (2018) counts the same authors as direct voluntarists; the Map's narrower reading rests on the qualifications quoted above. The dominant medieval voluntarism and the dominant modern involuntarism are therefore less opposed than their labels suggest, an instance of the caution the [[doxastic-voluntarism]] article draws from the *Stanford Encyclopedia*: verdicts on whether belief is voluntary depend on which notion of "voluntary" is in play.

**The double claim prefigures evaluative control.** Hieronymi (2006) describes evaluative control: one changes a belief by changing one's answer to whether it is true. It has the shape of the Stoic sage's freedom, agency exercised through attention to the evident, with no power to assent against it. Goulet-Cazé's *eixis* gives the same act a passive description, the structure the [[assent-void]] calls agency without a handle, and on Blackson's reading Epictetus's testing governs the approach to assent rather than the act. The lineage is the Map's construction; neither Hieronymi nor the Stoics drew it.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]** faces an objection: if consciousness can cause physical events, why can it not make itself believe? The [[assent-void]] answers that the limit on believing at will comes from belief's answerability to truth, not from causal impotence. The history adds a consideration in that reply's favour: the limit was recognised by Stoics who described assent as a body, "a tensional motion of the pneuma" (Goulet-Cazé 2011), by Augustine and Aquinas within a theology of grace, and by Holcot within an Ockhamist one. A limit acknowledged under such different accounts of the mind is unlikely to be a consequence of any one of them. This removes an objection to interaction and supplies no evidence for it.

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** gains nothing from the record. The Stoic doctrine of assent is a corporealist psychology, and the Map does not borrow its authority. Chrysippus's cylinder is an early compatibilism, while the Map's [[free-will]] pages defend agent-causal libertarianism; the cylinder is a rival's ancestor, not an ally. None of **[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]]**, **[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]]** or **[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]** bears on the history.
