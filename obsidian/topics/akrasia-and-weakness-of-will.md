Akrasia (Greek for "lack of self-control," usually translated *weakness of will*) names the phenomenon of acting intentionally against one's own better judgement about what is best to do. You judge that you should stop at one drink, and reach for a second; you resolve to write, and open a browser tab instead. The philosophical interest is not the misbehaviour but the apparent contradiction: how can an agent freely and knowingly do what, by their own lights, they judge worse? Four positions structure the debate, and this article front-loads them for readers who may not reach the end:

- **Socratic denial** (Plato's *Protagoras*): genuine akrasia is impossible; apparent cases are really ignorance or miscalculation.
- **Aristotelian diagnosis** (*Nicomachean Ethics* VII): akrasia is real but is a failure to *activate* knowledge one possesses.
- **Davidsonian resolution** ("How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?"): akrasia is genuinely possible, intentional action against an all-things-considered judgement, and irrational without being incoherent.
- **Holtonian reconception** (*Willing, Wanting, Waiting*): weakness of will is not acting against best judgement at all but the over-ready abandonment of a prior resolution under temptation.

This is shared-ground philosophy of action and moral psychology, engaged on common descriptive terms by physicalists, functionalists, and dualists alike. Nothing in the phenomenon requires or establishes any metaphysics of mind. The Map offers a **selection-model reading** of the first-person datum "I chose X while judging Y better" as *one interpretive lens* on the structure of the will—explicitly framework-relative and explicitly not a proof of dualism. That reading is deferred to the [Relation to Site Perspective](#relation-to-site-perspective) section below; the exposition that precedes it stays with what each philosopher actually claims.

## The Socratic Denial: There Is No Datum

In Plato's *Protagoras*, Socrates denies that akrasia is possible. On a standard intellectualist reading, to judge a course of action best just *is* to be most motivated toward it, so acting otherwise is incoherent. The argument runs through a hedonist premise—the pleasant is the good—and an art of measuring pleasures and pains; whether Socrates endorses the premise is disputed. Apparent weakness of will must then be a cognitive failure: miscalculation, or ignorance of which option is genuinely better. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy quotes the relevant claim: "No one...who either knows or believes that there is another possible course of action, better than the one he is following, will ever continue on his present course" (*Protagoras* 358b–c).

This position matters to any account of the will because it contests the *reality of the datum* rather than explaining it. If Socrates is right, there is no genuine first-person fact "I chose X while judging Y better"; the report dissolves into "I misjudged Y as worse at the moment of choice." R. M. Hare's prescriptivism supplies a modern analogue (*Freedom and Reason*, ch. 5, "Backsliding"): if sincerely judging that one *ought* to do y is itself a prescription to do y, then failing to do y when one could have done it shows the judgement was not sincerely held—the apparent backslider was either unable to act or was using "ought" in a diminished sense that had shed its prescriptive force. Any framework—including the Map's—that wants to build on the akratic datum must first treat its very existence as contested, not assumed.

## Aristotle: Knowledge That Is "Not Active"

Aristotle (*Nicomachean Ethics* VII) accepts akrasia against Socrates, while trying to preserve what is true in the Socratic paradox—that people go wrong only through some kind of ignorance. His question is not *whether* the akratic acts against knowledge but *in what manner* the knowledge is held.

His answer runs through the **practical syllogism**. Practical reasoning moves from a universal premise (sweet things should be avoided) through a particular premise (this is a sweet thing) to an action. Aristotle argues the akratic possesses the relevant knowledge but does not have it *in active use*: under the pull of appetite or passion, the agent fails properly to grasp or deploy the particular premise that applies the universal to the situation at hand. The knowledge is present potentially but not actualised, a case of "having knowledge in a sense and yet not having it, as in the instance of a man asleep, mad, or drunk" (VII.3, 1147a).

This yields a structural distinction between merely *possessing* an evaluative state and *exercising* it in the moment of action. Aristotle contrasts the akratic with the *enkratic* (continent) agent, who feels the same contrary pull but masters it, and with the temperate agent, who feels no such pull. The diagnosis carries no metaphysical commitment about the nature of mind; it is a claim about how evaluative cognition relates to what an agent actually does.

Aristotle also distinguishes "impetuosity" from "weakness" (VII.7): the weak deliberate but fail to stand by their conclusion, while the impetuous are led by emotion without deliberating, so there may be no formed all-things-considered judgement for the act to diverge from. Nor is standing firm always good. Neoptolemus, abandoning a lie Odysseus urged on him, poses the puzzle of "a good incontinence" (VII.2), which Aristotle resolves by denying the lapse was incontinence; and the "strong-headed" who cling to opinions for the pleasure of victory are "liker the incontinent than the continent man" (VII.9).

## Davidson: The Modern Locus

Donald Davidson's essay "How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?" is the modern point of reference. (First published in Joel Feinberg's edited collection *Moral Concepts*, Oxford University Press—catalogue records disagree between 1969 and 1970 for that volume; the stable citation is the reprint in Davidson's *Essays on Actions and Events*, Essay 2, pp. 21–42.) Davidson's aim is to show that akrasia is possible without being incoherent, thereby answering the Socratic denial on its own terms.

He first sharpens the definition. An agent does *x* incontinently when: (1) *x* is done intentionally; (2) the agent believes some alternative *y* is open; and (3) the agent judges that, all things considered, it would be better to do *y* than *x*. The puzzle is condition (3): how can the agent act against a judgement of what is better *all things considered*?

Davidson's resolution turns on distinguishing kinds of evaluative judgement. **Prima facie** judgements ("in light of reason *r*, *a* is better") and **all-things-considered** judgements are *conditional*—they relate an action to a body of reasons. An **all-out** (unconditional) judgement—"*a* is better," full stop—is what actually issues in action. The akratic, on this analysis, reaches the all-things-considered judgement favouring *y* but does not draw the all-out conclusion it supports; he acts on an all-out judgement favouring *x*, an implication the SEP finds made explicit in Davidson's "Intending" (1978), which identifies intentions with all-out judgements. The action is intelligible as intentional behaviour yet *irrational*, because the agent has detached the judgement that moves them from the judgement supported by all their reasons. Most later writers, the SEP reports, think Davidson has not shown how akrasia is possible: Bratman's Sam (1979, p. 156) judges abstaining best without qualification yet says, "Still, I'm drinking."

Davidson names the norm the akratic violates the **principle of continence**: perform the action judged best on the basis of all available relevant reasons. What marks akrasia as irrational, in his words, is that the agent has no *reason* for the gap. If we ask "what is the agent's reason for doing [x] when he believes it would be better, all things considered, to do another thing, then the answer must be: for this, the agent has no reason" (p. 42, as quoted in Stroud & Svirsky 2025). Crucially for how the Map should handle this material, Davidson renders akrasia *possible but irrational* with no appeal to any non-physical cause. The gap between all-things-considered and all-out judgement is a structural feature of practical reasoning, statable in wholly naturalistic terms.

## Synchronic and Diachronic: Holton's Reconception

Davidson's akrasia is *synchronic*: at a single time, the agent's present action conflicts with their present better judgement. Richard Holton's *Willing, Wanting, Waiting* relocates the failure to a *diachronic* axis and, in doing so, detaches "weakness of will" from acting-against-best-judgement altogether. He offers it as what ordinary speakers mean, a reading later writers have questioned and Holton himself grew less sure of (Stroud & Svirsky 2025, n. 16).

For Holton, weakness of will is the over-ready **abandonment of a resolution**—an intention formed precisely to insulate the agent against contrary inclinations they expect to feel when the time comes. The dieter who resolves in the morning to skip dessert, then revises that judgement when the trolley arrives, has not necessarily acted against a present better judgement; temptation characteristically works by *shifting* the judgement itself. The failure is not evaluative incoherence at the moment of choice but the unreasonable dropping of a commitment that was supposed to hold firm across the change of heart. Whether an abandonment is weakness has, for Holton, an irreducibly normative dimension (1999, p. 259), which also keeps strength of will from collapsing into stubbornness.

This foregrounds **willpower** (strength of will) as a distinct faculty: the effortful capacity to maintain a resolution against inclinations that would otherwise revise it. Holton draws on the self-control research of Mischel and Baumeister and treats willpower as a real executive skill rather than the automatic output of correct judgement. One strand of that evidence has since weakened: the "ego-depletion" model, on which willpower draws down a limited resource, was put at d = 0.04 by Hagger et al.'s (2016) preregistered 23-laboratory replication, and the current SEP treatment records that the model has "increasingly come under question" (see [[mental-effort]]). The replication tested the resource model Holton's account of effort drew on, not his structural claim that resolutions can be dropped over-readily and that [[phenomenology-of-choice-and-volition#sustained-control|holding them]] is a distinct capacity, which it leaves standing without confirming. Whether the capacity is trainable is unsettled: self-control training shows a small effect (g = 0.30; .13–.24 after bias correction) whose mechanisms are "poorly understood" (Friese et al. 2017). The picture deflates the will as the pinnacle of rationality, casting it instead as [[self-control-as-skill-and-scaffolding|a set of devices that compensate]] for our inability to make and hold sound judgements over time. Like Davidson's, Holton's account invokes no non-physical mechanism—the willpower it describes is empirically studied, ordinary self-management.

## Three Standing Debates

Three disputes run through this material and bear on how much weight the akratic datum can carry.

**Is akrasia possible?** The Socratic and Harean denial says no—judging *y* best is constitutively motivating, so acting otherwise is incoherent. The dominant post-Davidson view says yes, treating akrasia as real and shifting the question to *how* to characterise it. The disagreement is ultimately over whether judgement and motivation can come apart. The free-will literature poses the same question in structural terms. Frankfurt's *unwilling addict* in the [[frankfurt-hierarchical-mesh-theory-of-the-will|hierarchical theory of the will]] has an akratic shape—he is moved by a desire his own second-order volition rejects—though Frankfurt presents him as struggling helplessly against the craving, which places the case nearer compulsion than weakness. Gary Watson's reply to Frankfurt ("Free Agency," 1975) splits the agent's *valuational* system from her *motivational* system; that split is a close relative of Davidson's gap, but not the same thing, since Davidson locates the divergence between two judgements (all-things-considered and all-out) while Watson locates it between what the agent values and what moves her.

**Weakness or compulsion?** Watson (1977) contests the ordinary contrast, on which the weak agent could have resisted and the compulsive one could not, and draws the line by normal capacities of self-control. Building on a phenomenon Watson identified, Gorman holds that compulsive action aims to eliminate a desire rather than satisfy it, which puts the act's attributability in question (2019, p. 140; see also 2023). Holton argues that "addictive temptations are not irresistible" but involve a decoupling of judgement and wanting (2009, ch. 5). Frankfurt's unwilling addict sits on this contested line, and placing an act on it is classification, which precedes any question of desert.

**Is akratic action necessarily irrational?** Davidson's definition picks out the phenomenon; the verdict of irrationality comes from the principle of continence, which the akratic violates with a reason for *x* but none for *x* rather than *y*. Audi (1990), McIntyre (1990) and Arpaly (2000) deny that akratic action is always irrational, because a better judgement can itself be defective. Bennett's (1974) Huckleberry Finn, as McIntyre discusses him, akratically fails to turn in the enslaved Jim, against a judgement that ignored his affection for Jim. Arpaly's Emily, judging she should persevere with a chemistry Ph.D. that does not suit her, quits purely on feeling, doing just what she ought to: "An agent's best judgment is just another belief" (Arpaly 2000, p. 512). Traditionalists reply that a procedural defect remains however good the outcome, so the dispute sets procedural against substantive rationality. An agent may likewise be right to abandon a bad resolution. This connects to the internalism/externalism dispute about whether evaluative judgements necessarily motivate. The upshot for any downstream framework: the phenomenon is contested at the descriptive level before any metaphysical reading is layered on.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The preceding sections are source exposition. This section is the Map's interpretation, and it is offered as *one lens alongside the framework-neutral accounts above*, not as a competitor that the data selects over them.

The Map models consciousness as a low-bandwidth [[control-theoretic-will|controller]] that selects among neural options the brain prepares, rather than computing outcomes from scratch. Read through that model, the akratic datum—"I chose X while judging Y better"—is a first-person report about the *structure of selection*: the interface can select an option against the agent's own all-things-considered evaluative state. That state must be indexed to the time of action and kept apart from post-choice report, since the datum usually comes from retrospective report and the [[preference-void|preference void]] records that orderings may be built in the asking. Nor is akrasia a case of idle valence: on the Map's thesis that felt value is what selection tracks, selection there follows present felt pull against considered judgement, the currency on which the [[valence-and-conscious-selection#the-value-sensitive-horn-valence-as-selection-currency|value-sensitive horn]] accommodates it. Where reflective judgement is defective, as in Arpaly's cases, an act moved by present affect may be more value-sensitive, not less.

[[the-divided-will|The strongest rival has no single selector]]. A distributed architecture—valuation systems on different timescales, habitual and goal-directed control, delay discounting, conflict monitoring, later verbal report—predicts that judgement and action will diverge, and Mele's wants whose motivational force is out of line with their evaluation (1987, p. 37) and Ainslie's hyperbolic discounting (2001) supply mechanisms. Recurrent divergence is prima facie evidence against a unitary personal controller. Any mechanism short of a transparent read-off of judgement accommodates Davidson's gap, so its opening is a weak constraint and no support for the selection model. A selector with reasons of its own also reopens Davidson's question—why select what it judged worse?—relocating the gap rather than closing it. For the Map's agent-causal account this is the [[agent-causation#the-luck-objection|luck objection]] at its starkest: the reply that selection on reasons is contrastively explicable fails when selection runs against the agent's reasons, and the objection stands unanswered here. The disagreement is therefore closer to bedrock than it looked, and akrasia does not decide it.

Tamar Schapiro's account cuts both ways, and the Map keeps both readings open. She holds that weak-willed action is better characterised as "abandon[ing] your post as deliberator" (2021, p. 152) than as an error within deliberation; in the SEP's summary it "does not actually engage the will at all". As a rival, this would make the akratic datum a record of the will's abdication, not of a positive selection of the worse option. As the nearest ally in the literature, it is what the SEP calls a "dualistic Kantian moral psychology", dividing deciding agent from inclination and needing no substance dualism.

This reading is **framework-relative and not a proof of anything non-physical**, for three reasons drawn directly from the exposition above:

- The **Socratic denial** contests whether the datum is even genuine. If Socrates is right, there is no "chose X while judging Y better"—only "misjudged Y." The Map's lens presupposes a datum whose reality is itself disputed.
- **Davidson** explains akrasia as *possible but irrational* using only the structure of conditional versus unconditional judgement—no non-physical cause enters.
- **Holton** explains *weakness of will*, which he distinguishes from akrasia, as ordinary, empirically studied willpower failing to hold a resolution—again, no dualist commitment.

So the Map's contribution here is an *interpretation* of a shared phenomenon, not evidence for its metaphysics. A physicalist can accept every word of the Aristotle, Davidson, and Holton sections and read the selection language as a functional description of neural subsystems. What the Map adds—that the controller is genuinely non-physical—draws on the hard problem and the conceivability argument, but these support only non-reduction, and epiphenomenalist dualists accept them too. That non-physical consciousness is the selecting controller is a further commitment the Map owns, [[tenets#^tenet-3-standing|a posit rather than a result]]; akrasia does not supply it.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction (Tenet 3)]]**: The selection model reads akrasia as a case where the conscious controller's selection diverges from the agent's evaluative state—relevant to *what* the interface selects and how faithfully it tracks reasons. Whether that divergence needs a non-physical selector is exactly what akrasia does *not* settle; the phenomenon is neutral between the Map's reading and the naturalistic ones. Classification cannot be deferred, because the selection reading applies only to weakness: where an act is compulsive, the agent's conscious wish that it not occur is no evidence of bidirectional control. What the Map defers to its treatment of [[moral-responsibility|moral responsibility]] is desert: whether akratic acts are blameworthy.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]] and [[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]]**: akrasia engages neither and offers no evidence of a quantum locus.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits (Tenet 5)]]**: The temptation is to declare one analysis "simplest"—Socratic reduction to ignorance, or Davidson's minimal apparatus. The Map's discipline here is to resist that move: the descriptive facts underdetermine the analysis, and simplicity is an unreliable guide when the phenomenon's very existence is contested. Explanatory scope and fit still discriminate among the four positions, so holding them open means weighing them on those grounds, not collapsing to the tidiest.
