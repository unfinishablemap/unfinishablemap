Tamar Szabó Gendler (2008) coined **alief** for an associative, automatic and arational state that can steer behaviour against what a person believes. Her opening case is a visitor who believes the Grand Canyon Skywalk will hold yet feels vertigo on its glass floor; Hume's man in an iron cage likewise "cannot forbear trembling" though "he knows himself to be perfectly secure from falling". The Unfinishable Map's [[doxastic-voluntarism]] article speculates that if control over belief is ever felt as effort, the effort sits at the *back end*, in holding a belief's guidance in check. This article asks whether acting on belief against alief is that case.

The answer depends on how the states are classified, as [[#three-readings|the three readings]] (set out below) show. On Gendler's taxonomy the visitor who walks forward holds an *alief's* guidance in check, not a belief's. That is control over an affect-laden state, the kind Laura Soter's back-end voluntarism starts from, not an instance of back-end control over belief. Override counts as back-end control over belief only on the belief reading, where implicit attitudes are themselves beliefs (Mandelbaum 2016; Bendaña 2021). On Eric Schwitzgebel's reading, broad splits such as an implicit racist's are "in-between" believing, so they are neither. The skywalk is the one case every party agrees on: the belief that the floor will hold is there.

What the article does not establish is that consciousness does the overriding. Dual-process psychology (Evans & Stanovich 2013) explains the vertigo, the override and when effort is felt with no experiential term, and Gendler herself inclines towards physicalism. The Map's speculation comes out coherent with this literature, not supported by it. The [[#noticing-condition|noticing condition]] (explained below) narrows it further: felt effort appears only where the conflict is noticed, and Gendler calls much associative interference "phenomenologically invisible".

## The Precipice Case {#the-precipice-case}

Gendler (2008, p. 634) opens with the Skywalk, a glass walkway extending 70 feet from the Canyon's rim. Of "those who manage to stride to the Skywalk's center" she writes: "Surely they believe that the walkway will hold". Yet while "the venturesome souls wholeheartedly believe that the walkway is completely safe, they also alieve something very different", with content roughly "Really high up, long long way down. Not a safe place to be! Get off!!" (p. 635).

She calls the story "a slight variation on the early modern 'problem of the precipice'" (p. 634 n. 2), naming Hume, Pascal and Montaigne among those who discuss it. Hume's version is the man in the iron cage (*Treatise* 1.3.13.10), introduced just after his diagnosis: "We may correct this propensity by a reflection on the nature of those circumstances; but 'tis still certain, that custom takes the start, and gives a biass to the imagination" (1.3.13.9). Laboratory versions exist too: Rozin, Markwith and Ross (1990, abstract) report that "American adults have difficulty in ignoring a label indicating toxicity on a food, even though they know the label is false."

The cases share a shape the rest of this article depends on. Nobody in them struggles to *form* the belief that the glass holds or the labelled food is harmless; Hume's man "knows". The conflict comes after the belief is formed, between a held belief and something else pulling behaviour and feeling the other way.

## Gendler's Distinction {#gendlers-distinction}

Gendler names the state "what I hereby dub a belief-discordant alief", "so-called because alief is associative, automatic, and arational" (2008, p. 641). Her tentative characterisation reads: "A paradigmatic alief is a mental state with associatively linked content that is representational, affective and behavioral, and that is activated—consciously or nonconsciously—by features of the subject's internal or ambient environment. Aliefs may be either occurrent or dispositional" (p. 642).

Three features matter for the Map's question.

- **Visibility through conflict.** "It is precisely when they are belief-discordant that aliefs tend to be evident to us" (p. 646).
- **A norm of coherence.** Believing *P* while imagining not-*P* violates no norm, "But if I believe that P and alieve that not-P, something is amiss" (p. 651). "The well-functioning aliever is one whose aliefs and beliefs largely coincide (or one whose ability to suppress contrary impulse is strong)" (p. 651). The parenthesis is Gendler's own acknowledgement of a capacity to override.
- **A reduced role for belief.** "It is only through a process of conscious or habit-governed inhibition that representations whose accuracy we endorse come to play a distinctive role in governing our actions" (pp. 660–661), and belief "plays a far smaller role in moment-by-moment management than philosophical tradition has tended to stress" (p. 663).

Two cautions govern any use of her work. First, her metaphysics is not the Map's: "Since I incline towards physicalism, this means that I think alief is also a physical state" (p. 642). Read with that commitment, her "conscious or habit-governed inhibition" names a process in a physical system and lends no support to consciousness as a non-physical cause. Second, she keeps alief apart from weakness of will. Her 2014 paper (abstract) says the traditional divided-soul view handles "a cognitive conflict between our reflective attitudes and our non-reflective endorsements (such as weakness of the will)" but neglects "the mediation of associative and heuristic processes". [[akrasia-and-weakness-of-will|Akrasia]] is action against an evaluative judgement. The skywalk is a conflict with a factual belief, and in the stride forward the belief wins.

## Three Rival Families {#rivals}

Whether alief is a real kind is disputed. The *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry on implicit bias records critics who question "whether putative aliefs form a unified kind" (Brownstein 2019, §2.1.2). The rivals fall into three families, and each answers the Map's question differently.

### Deflationary Pluralism

Deflationists hold that no new kind is needed. Kwong (2012, abstract) argues that "many of the cases which she uses to motivate such a mental state can be fully explained by accounts that make use only of commonplace attitudes such as beliefs and desires." Currie and Ichino (2012, abstract) would "appeal instead to a variety of representational states, including familiar ones such as belief, desire, imagination and perception." Mandelbaum (2013) presses a related critique. On these views the guide pulling against belief is some familiar state, which varies from case to case.

### Implicit Attitudes as Beliefs {#belief-reading}

A second family gives the rival guide propositional structure. Mandelbaum (2016, abstract) argues that "implicit biases are not predicated on any associative structures or associative processes but instead arise because of unconscious propositionally structured beliefs." Bendaña (2021, abstract), working with a fragmented model of mind, agrees: "Most psychologists and philosophers maintain that implicit attitudes are not beliefs. This chapter argues that they are". Levy (2015, abstract) takes a middle position: implicit attitudes have propositional structure, but are "too patchy and fragmented for them to properly be considered beliefs", and he calls them "patchy endorsements". The *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry on belief says it "remains controversial" whether implicit tests reveal beliefs (Schwitzgebel 2023, §2.2). These arguments concern implicit social attitudes. Extending the belief reading to the skywalk's fear is a further step that the passages cited here do not take.

### In-Between Believing {#in-between}

Schwitzgebel (2001, abstract) describes a person "not quite accurately describable as believing that p, nor quite accurately describable as failing to believe that p" as in an "in-between state of belief", and suggests a dispositional account of belief to accommodate it. His 2010 cases include "Juliet the implicit racist", who sincerely argues for racial equality of intelligence while her spontaneous judgements are biased, and "Kaipeng the trembling Stoic", who sincerely judges that death is not bad yet trembles on the battlefield (pp. 532–533). Against Gendler he argues that her reasoning "considerably overdraws the distinction" (p. 539) and that her view "artificially hives off our rational and thoughtful responses from our habitual, automatic, and associative ones" (p. 540).

He exempts the skywalk explicitly: "I would not deny that the man who stands trembling on a glass floor high in the air still believes that the glass floor is solid and can support him, to use one of Gendler's examples." In his own cases a broad range of dispositions deviates from the endorsed judgement, "while in the glass-floor case the deviation is narrow and arguably excused" (p. 549 n. 5).

## The Empirical Record, Briefly {#empirical-record}

The strongest rival to any experiential reading is dual-process psychology. In Evans and Stanovich's theoretical review, "rapid autonomous processes (Type 1) are assumed to yield default responses unless intervened on by distinctive higher order reasoning processes (Type 2)" (2013, abstract). Gawronski and Bodenhausen's (2006, abstract) integrative review likewise separates associative activation, "independent of subjective truth or falsity", from propositional validation. On this template the retreat response on the glass is a Type 1 default and the stride forward a Type 2 intervention. [[the-divided-will|The divided-will article]] records the strict dual-process typology as contested; the template is still the rival this article must answer.

The measurement record is weaker than the philosophical literature sometimes implies. Average correlations between implicit measures and behaviour have "ranged from approximately .14 to .37" across meta-analyses (Brownstein 2019, §5.3). A network meta-analysis of 492 studies found that "changes in implicit measures did not mediate changes in explicit measures or behavior" (Forscher et al. 2019, abstract). Some priming results Gendler drew on, including those behind her pp. 660–661 sentence, have failed to replicate (Doyen et al. 2012; Pashler, Coburn & Harris 2012). The argument below therefore rests on the precipice cases, not on implicit-measure data.

## Is Acting on Belief Against Alief Back-End Control? {#three-readings}

Soter (2025, abstract) separates "front-end" control over forming a belief from "back-end" control over its guidance function, which she says voluntarism debates have "largely ignored". Her mechanism is borrowed: she draws on "a mechanistic account of control of the guidance function for 'emotion-type states'" and argues that the same mechanisms can be used "to block doxastic guidance". The [[doxastic-voluntarism#map-reading|Map's reading]] adds the speculation that any felt effort in controlling belief would sit at this back end.

The skywalk looks like a clean instance: a person acting on what she believes against a rival pull. Whether it is one depends on what the rival pull is.

| Reading | What the overridden guide is | Does the override count as back-end control over belief? |
|---|---|---|
| Alief (Gendler 2008) | An alief: associative, affect-laden and distinct from belief | No. The visitor checks an alief's guidance, the kind of affective control from which Soter's argument starts |
| Belief (Mandelbaum 2016; Bendaña 2021) | An implicit attitude that is itself an unconscious belief | Yes, where the rival guide is a belief. Juliet's deliberate striving would hold one belief's guidance in check in favour of another |
| In-between (Schwitzgebel 2001, 2010) | For broad splits, a dispositional profile on which the person neither believes nor fails to believe | Neither, for Juliet. The skywalk keeps its belief, and its override goes as on the alief reading |

The article does not choose a row, because the literature has not settled which is right. Three points hold whichever is chosen.

First, on the alief reading the skywalk sits on the *source* side of Soter's argument rather than the target side: it shows what back-end control over belief would look like if it exists. Soter's abstract does not mention alief; classing aliefs among her emotion-type states is the Map's step.

Second, the belief reading makes override an instance only where the overridden state is a belief. Its defenders argue the point for implicit social attitudes, so Juliet is the case it converts: "We can imagine that sometimes Juliet deliberately strives to overcome her bias in particular cases" (Schwitzgebel 2010, p. 532).

Third, the skywalk is the cleanest case because every party grants the belief that the floor will hold, and only the status of the opposing state varies. Juliet's case is contested at the level of what she believes, so it cannot anchor an argument about what a belief's guidance is doing.

### Where the Effort Falls {#where-effort-falls}

The precipice cases fit the front-end/back-end split: Hume's man already "knows" the cage will hold, and the conflict arises only when he surveys the precipice. The [[phenomenology-of-resistance-across-domains|resistance article]] draws the same line for belief: felt pushback belongs to failed revision of a standing belief, while at acquisition the [[assent-void]] finds nothing to push on.

The fit is real but tests nothing. A dual-process account predicts the same split: the Type 1 default arises unbidden, and the Type 2 intervention is costly, which the opportunity-cost and signal models of effort discussed in [[mental-effort]] explain without an experiential term. Nor has the effort been measured. That override on the glass is felt as effort rests on descriptions of the cases, such as Gendler's word "manage", not on any study of felt effort during override of fear.

### The Noticing Condition {#noticing-condition}

Felt effort needs the conflict to be noticed. Gendler (2014, abstract) says associative and heuristic processes "produce challenges to reason's rule that are phenomenologically invisible". Greenwald and Banaji (1995, abstract) define implicit cognition by influence "not introspectively known by the actor", and note that "implicit cognitive effects are often reduced by focusing judges' attention on their judgment task". Juliet's striving is intermittent because "it's impossible to constantly maintain such self-conscious vigilance" (Schwitzgebel 2010, p. 532).

So the back-end speculation has to be conditional. Effort can appear where the rival guide is noticed and checked; where interference goes unseen there is nothing to feel. The skywalk visitor notices her vertigo. Implicit bias carries no such guarantee, and nothing here claims that felt effort accompanies implicit bias as such.

Emotion regulation shows the same structure: the extended process model begins explicit regulation with identification, registering that an emotion calls for regulating, while implicit regulation can proceed without awareness of regulating ([[emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control|the timing of emotion regulation]]).

## A Parity With Doxastic Involuntarism {#parity}

Gendler draws a parallel that bears on [[doxastic-voluntarism]]: "just as it is (something close to) conceptually impossible to believe at will, it is practically impossible to alieve at will" (2008, p. 651). For the belief half she cites Williams (1973). Alief's involuntariness is practical, since aliefs depend on associative patterns laid down by experience, ours and our ancestors'. The conceptual and psychological grounds that the doxastic-voluntarism article separates are here distributed across two states.

She adds an asymmetry in indirect cultivation: "if we use such tricks to cultivate beliefs, we need to cover our tracks; if we use them to cultivate aliefs, we can do so under conditions of full disclosure" (p. 652). Retraining an alief works even when one knows what one is doing, which is the territory of [[self-control-as-skill-and-scaffolding|situational self-control strategies]], on which Gendler later co-wrote (Duckworth, Gendler & Gross 2016). She also suggests, "somewhat simplistically", that "Aristotelian ethics is an ethics of alief, whereas Kantian ethics is an ethics of belief" (p. 663 n. 62), a remark attached to her quotation of Aristotle on habits formed "from our very youth"; it points to [[philosophy-of-habit-under-dualism|habituation]] rather than to akratic conflict.

## Relation to Site Perspective

This section gives the Map's interpretation. None of the authors discussed here defends dualism.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]** is the tenet engaged, and the article establishes compatibility with it, not evidence for it. The Map speculates that, if its [[control-theoretic-will]] reading is right, the stride across the glass is where conscious selection would act: a veto on a prepared retreat, a low-bandwidth signal holding one guide in check in favour of another. The alief literature leaves room for this placement and fixes its terms. On the alief reading the placement concerns control over alief, and it becomes control over belief only if implicit attitudes are beliefs. Nothing here shows the placement is correct. The Type 1/Type 2 template explains the vertigo, the override and the effort with no experiential term, so these cases open no explanatory gap of their own for consciousness to fill.

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** gains nothing evidential. Whether alief is a natural kind is a question about how psychology should individuate states, and a physicalist can answer it either way. Like the [[doxastic-voluntarism]] article, this one is coherent with the Map's framework and adds no evidence for it.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]** does not decide between Gendler and the deflationists. Their dispute concerns kind-individuation, and the Map uses neither the limits of parsimony against the deflationists' economy nor that economy against Gendler.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]]** and **[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]]** are not engaged.
