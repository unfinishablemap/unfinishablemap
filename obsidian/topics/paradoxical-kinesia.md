Paradoxical kinesia is the sudden, brief ability of a person with Parkinson's disease to perform a movement they could not perform moments before, typically when facing an immediate threat or a salient external cue. It matters to the Unfinishable Map because the Map's reading of Parkinson's rests on a claim about which side of a dissociation fails. Parkinson's impairs self-initiated movement most and leaves externally cued movement comparatively spared. On the Map's earlier reading, this showed *willed* selection failing while *triggered* movement survived. A live rival reading (Redgrave et al. 2010) holds the reverse: *habitual* control is what dopamine loss damages, and patients fall back on comparatively preserved goal-directed control. Paradoxical kinesia looks like the case that could decide between them. This article's verdict is that it does not decide. The best controlled studies ([explained below](#the-controlled-studies)) show that urgency speeds movement in healthy people as much as in patients, and that an internal expectation can produce the effect without any external trigger. Both findings weaken the "external cues bypass the impaired selector" story the Map used. The habitual-control reading held a modest edge on anatomy and on freezing of gait; the outcome-devaluation test this page names below has since been surveyed in [[outcome-devaluation-and-dual-task-costs-in-parkinsons]] and came out against the habitual reading, restoring parity between the two. The Map's selection-interface reading survives only as *compatible with* the data, not as supported by it.

## The Phenomenon

The term goes back a century. Alexandre-Achille Souques named *kinésie paradoxale* in his 1921 report on parkinsonian syndromes, as recounted in Melo-Thomas and Schwarting's 2023 review, which opens by noting that disabled patients "remain able to produce normal motor responses in the context of urgent or externally driven situations or even when exposed to appetitive cues such as music."

The clinical picture is vivid but anecdotal. Asmus et al. (2008) describe a 68-year-old soccer enthusiast whose freezing of gait did not respond even to high doses of dopaminergic medication, yet who "could reliably overcome freezing" by kicking a tennis ball attached to a rubber band.

When the phenomenon has been looked for systematically, it turns out to be rare. Schlesinger, Erikh and Yarnitsky (2007) interviewed fifty Parkinson's patients whose residential area had suddenly become a war zone under missile attack. Only two reported paradoxical kinesia, and only one of those episodes occurred during the war (the other was historical); both followed visual cues. The air-raid siren, a frightening auditory warning of imminent danger, did not induce it, and patients reported significantly *more* OFF time during the war than before it. The authors conclude that "paradoxical kinesia is uncommon, even in the face of danger." Emergency alone does not reliably unlock movement. The authors draw a modality moral from their two cases: "Visual, but not auditory, triggers appear to be needed to prompt its occurrence." That conclusion favours an external-cue account, though it rests on two patients.

### Pathway Accounts

Two proposals locate the effect in a route that avoids the damaged basal ganglia. Glickstein and Stein (1991) observed that the stimuli that often elicit paradoxical movement resemble those that relay visual information to the cerebellum. They suggested that "the pathways relaying those visual stimuli can bypass the damaged basal ganglia and allow an intact cerebellar circuit to be used for visuomotor control." Melo-Thomas and Schwarting (2023), working from two animal models, point instead to the inferior colliculus as a candidate part of an alternative pathway bypassing the basal ganglia.

Both are anatomical hypotheses. Neither is stated in terms of willed versus habitual control, so neither settles the question this article is about.

### The Controlled Studies

Two experiments bear directly on what paradoxical kinesia is.

Ballanger et al. (2006) asked Parkinson's patients and healthy controls to press a switch under three conditions: as fast as possible when self-generated, in response to an acoustic cue, and in response to a cue under urgency (the press stopped a ball rolling off a ramp). Task difficulty was equalised across groups. External cues shortened movement duration, urgency shortened it further, and "the amount of reduction was identical in PD patients and healthy subjects." Their title states the conclusion: paradoxical kinesis "is not a hallmark of Parkinson's disease but a general property of the motor system."

Distler et al. (2016) tested whether an external sensory trigger is needed at all. Nine patients and nine age-matched controls reached for an object that either stayed put or was about to move out of reach, with timing adjusted individually. Anticipating the object's escape shortened reaction time and movement duration and raised peak velocity in both groups, and the velocity gain was significantly larger in patients. The authors conclude that "expectations rather than sensory cues are critical" and that internal cues such as temporal constraints suffice to diminish bradykinesia.

These two results partly pull against each other. Ballanger found no patient-specific gain; Distler found a patient-specific gain in peak velocity. Both samples are small and the tasks differ. What they share is more important here: neither supports the idea that an *external* stimulus reaches a motor route the patient's own control cannot reach.

## Two Readings of the Dissociation

Both readings accept the same datum: self-initiated action is most impaired and externally cued action is comparatively spared. They disagree about what the impaired side *is*.

**The willed-deficit reading.** Dopamine depletion compromises deliberate, self-initiated selection. Cues trigger motor representations directly, without selection among competing options, and so escape the deficit. This was the reading the Map's [[dopamine-and-the-unified-interface|dopamine article]] originally leaned on, and it matches the older clinical framing of Parkinson's as a disorder of internally generated movement.

**The habitual-control reading.** Redgrave et al. (2010) argue that the basal ganglia contain spatially segregated territories for goal-directed and habitual control, and that in Parkinson's the loss of dopamine "is predominantly in the posterior putamen, a region of the basal ganglia associated with the control of habitual behaviour." Patients "may therefore be forced into a progressive reliance on the goal-directed mode," and many of their difficulties "may reflect a loss of normal automatic control owing to distorting output signals from habitual control circuits." On this reading, freezing of gait is a failure of the most automatic behaviour there is, and effortful, attended movement is the compensating route.

The two readings invert the meaning of the cue benefit. For the willed-deficit reading, the cue helps because it removes the need for choice. For the habitual reading, the cue helps because it gives goal-directed control something to act on, so that walking becomes a sequence of attended targets instead of an automatism. Redgrave et al. make this move themselves for cued movement generally, answering the objection that cue benefits show sensorimotor circuits still working: "sensory input is equally important for goal-directed control." They do not discuss paradoxical kinesia by name; extending their account to urgency and threat is this article's extrapolation.

## What Each Reading Predicts

| Observation | Willed-deficit reading | Habitual-control reading | What was found |
|---|---|---|---|
| External visual cue helps | Yes: cue triggers movement without choice | Yes: cue recruits goal-directed control | Yes, but by the same amount in controls (Ballanger 2006) |
| Internal expectation alone helps | Not predicted; anticipation is self-generated | Predicted; goal-directed control can use an internal goal | Yes, with a larger velocity gain in patients (Distler 2016) |
| Emergency reliably unlocks movement | Predicted if threat acts as a trigger | No strong prediction | Rare: 2 of 50 ever, 1 during the missile attacks; siren ineffective (Schlesinger 2007) |
| Freezing of gait | Failure of internal selection | Breakdown of automatic control | Fits the habitual reading more naturally |
| Anatomical locus of dopamine loss | No specific prediction | Posterior (habit) putamen | Posterior putamen (Redgrave 2010) |

Three rows lean toward the habitual reading, one is neutral, and the cue row no longer discriminates once the healthy-control result is in. None of these rows is decisive. The habitual reading's anatomical advantage is its strongest point, but anatomy tells us where dopamine is lost, not how function reorganises around the loss. The Distler result fits the habitual reading, yet a defender of the willed-deficit reading can reply that anticipating an object's escape converts a self-paced act into a deadline-triggered one, which is a kind of external timing. That reply is available, but it costs the reading its simplest prediction: if anticipations count as triggers, "self-initiated" stops marking a clear category.

Emergency-driven paradoxical kinesia, the case often taken as the purest bypass, turns out to be the weakest evidence. It is rare, known mostly from single-case reports, and both readings can absorb it: fleeing a fire is a triggered response on one reading and an intensely goal-directed act on the other.

## What Would Discriminate

The two readings make different predictions in places paradoxical kinesia does not reach. Outcome-devaluation tests, which ask whether a learned response persists after its outcome stops being valued, are the standard behavioural signature of habit (see [[philosophy-of-habit-under-dualism]]). If Parkinson's damages habitual control, patients should show *more* outcome sensitivity than controls in well-practised tasks; if it damages willed selection, the reverse. Dual-task costs offer a second test, one Redgrave et al. themselves propose when they predict that reliance on goal-directed control makes patients' actions "increasingly subject to interference from other goal-directed tasks": if cued walking runs on the goal-directed route, it should degrade under a concurrent attention-demanding task more in patients than in controls. Both tests have since been surveyed in [[outcome-devaluation-and-dual-task-costs-in-parkinsons]]. The devaluation test ran and reversed: in the two direct patient studies located as of 2026-09-30, habit formation was not impaired and goal-directed control tracked disease severity (de Wit et al. 2011; Mi et al. 2021), the direction the willed-deficit reading predicted. The dual-task test is mixed, and external cues reduce rather than raise dual-task interference, against the extension stated above.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**The Map's reading is downgraded.** The Map's dopamine article previously treated the Parkinsonian asymmetry as the willed interface failing while triggered movement bypassed it. After the 2026-09-27 revision, that article frames the gap as self-initiated versus externally cued action, presents Redgrave et al. as a live rival, and gives both readings. This article supports that scoping and goes one step further: the specific claim that "external stimuli bypass the impaired selection mechanism" is weakened by the controlled evidence, because the cue and urgency benefits appear in healthy motor systems at comparable size. The asymmetry remains a real datum; its direction is open. The habitual reading held the modest edge on this page's evidence, but the devaluation result recorded in [[outcome-devaluation-and-dual-task-costs-in-parkinsons]] removes that edge and returns the two readings to parity, with the gap between the instrumental "goal-directed" construct and self-initiated movement left open.

**Tenet 3 at its available standing.** The [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] tenet claims that consciousness causally influences the physical world. The Map's own tenet text records that the interface argument shows this influence to be *available*, consistent with physics and not excluded by causal closure, without showing it to be *actual*. Paradoxical kinesia does not move it from available to actual. It is worth noticing that the habitual reading is not hostile to the Map. On that reading, what survives in Parkinson's is effortful, attended, goal-directed control, which is where the Map locates [[mental-effort|felt effort]] and where it proposes conscious selection might operate. A patient walking by attending to each step is arguably a case of *more* reliance on the kind of control the Map cares about. But non-reductive physicalism with mental causation predicts the same pattern: a physically realised goal-directed control process, felt as effortful, compensating for a damaged habit system. The data fit the Map's selection-interface reading and this rival equally. The honest tier for the Map's reading is *compatible, not supported*.

**Tenet 5 cuts both ways.** [[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]] warns against preferring the simpler account because it is simpler. It gives no licence to prefer the Map's reading where the evidence leans the other way. The disagreement between the Map and the physicalist rival lies outside the Parkinsonian data, where the dopamine article already places it: in why goal-directed control is felt, which neither the anatomy nor the kinematics addresses.

**What the Map should stop saying.** Any Map page that cites Parkinson's as showing "willed movement disabled, automatic movement preserved" states the contested direction as settled, and states it the wrong way round relative to the anatomical rival. The defensible wording is the one the dopamine article now uses: self-initiated action most impaired, cued action comparatively spared, with the direction of the underlying dissociation in dispute.
