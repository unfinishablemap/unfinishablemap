Convergence arguments hold that when multiple independent lines of reasoning arrive at the same conclusion, the conclusion is more likely to be true than any single argument could establish. "Convergence argument" names several distinct things across the literature — Whewell's consilience of inductions, the convergent realism Laudan attacked, Bayesian agreement among partially reliable witnesses — and this article locates the cross-traditional cumulative case among them rather than treating its own usage as the only one. The Map treats convergence as evidence that warrants scrutiny: widely used in philosophical practice yet undertheorised relative to the weight it bears, and subject to a powerful historical objection (Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction) that any defence must meet on its own terms. The case matters especially for consciousness, where traditions separated by geography, language, and centuries independently identify something irreducible about subjective experience. The central move throughout is a distinction the literature makes tractable: convergence on a *diagnosis* (an explanatory gap) survives objections that defeat convergence on a *solution* (a posited mechanism).

## Convergence as Philosophical Method

Philosophy lacks the controlled experiments scientists use to test convergence directly, and the possibility that shared cognitive architecture generates systematic philosophical errors has no analogue in laboratory science. Convergence reasoning pervades philosophy regardless — convergent moral intuitions for moral realism, agreement between mathematical practice and metaphysics for abstract objects, Swinburne's Bayesian cumulative case for theism.

Cross-cultural convergence is the most ambitious application. When Sāṃkhya philosophers in classical India and Descartes in seventeenth-century France both conclude that consciousness is irreducible to the processes constituting the rest of reality, mutual influence cannot explain the agreement: whatever does must be either a feature of reality or a feature of human cognition. The structural claim is that multiple routes to one destination may be better evidence than one, *provided the routes are genuinely independent*. The epistemological challenge lies entirely in that proviso.

## The Convergence Argument in Philosophy of Science

Three bodies of work in philosophy of science bear on that structural claim.

**Whewell's consilience of inductions.** William Whewell's *Philosophy of the Inductive Sciences* (1840) gives the historical articulation: when an induction from one class of facts coincides with an induction from a different class, the *consilience* confers epistemic credit beyond what either earns alone. The cross-traditional convergence on consciousness's irreducibility is a consilience claim in exactly that sense — analytic conceivability arguments, contemplative phenomenology, and Indian metaphysics coinciding on one diagnosis. Whewell never reduced consilience to a calculus, and the Hesse–Laudan debate over whether it formalises as a Bayesian increment or names an irreducibly historical-judgemental virtue remains unsettled; the three conditions below partially regiment his notion for a domain that lacks the instrumental checks he could assume.

**Laudan's confutation and the pessimistic meta-induction.** The strongest objection is Larry Laudan's "A Confutation of Convergent Realism" (1981), which catalogues past theories that were genuinely successful — predictively, explanatorily, and by the convergence of independent investigators — yet are now judged false: crystalline spheres, phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether. If convergence on success has so often accompanied falsity, convergence cannot be the truth-tracking mark realists claim, and the objection has to be met on its own terms rather than deflected by a fresh example. Laudan's cases are convergences on *theoretical posits* — the ether as the medium of light, caloric as the substance of heat — and the catalogue retired the posits, not the explananda they were introduced to handle: light still propagates, heat still flows. The cross-traditional convergence the Map defends is, the distinction suggests, convergence on an *explanandum* — that structural-physical description leaves the qualitative character of experience unaccounted for — not on any mechanism that would explain it. The concession is real: Laudan's history shows convergence on *how to explain* a phenomenon is a poor guide to truth, so the Map disclaims convergence-weight for its own preferred reading (substance dualism with bidirectional interaction) at the solution layer, as the calibration discipline below makes explicit. What survives is narrower — convergent identification of a gap may be evidence the gap is real, even when no convergent verdict on its closure is available. Disagreement with a thoroughgoing anti-realist about explananda persists at the framework boundary: Laudan's pessimism is not refuted by the Map's resources so much as confined to the territory (theoretical posits) where it has its force.

**Bayesian convergence and partially reliable witnesses.** The formal apparatus is most fully developed in Bovens and Hartmann's *Bayesian Epistemology* (2003), which models the question convergence reasoning asks: when several *partially reliable, independent* witnesses agree, how much should agreement raise confidence? Their result sharpens the proviso — agreement appears to raise confidence strongly only when witnesses are genuinely independent and individually better than chance; correlated or biased witnesses may agree without confirming. The convergence-of-opinion theorems (Blackwell–Dubins merging, Gaifman–Snir) point the same way, with merging driven by a shared evidence stream, and traditions do not share an external evidence stream the way investigators reading the same instruments do — which is why the bootstrapping problem below is the binding constraint. The Bayesian literature does not vindicate the cross-traditional case; it specifies the conditions the case must meet and shows it has not met them formally.

## Three Conditions for Evidential Convergence

A convergence argument has three components: **multiple arguments** (two or more distinct arguments each supporting conclusion C); **independence** (they derive from different premises, methods, or features of the subject matter, so the failure of one does not entail the failure of the others); and **convergence** (despite independence, all point toward C rather than distributing across alternatives). Formalising this in Bayesian terms is well-defined in principle but contested at every parameter — priors on dualism, conditional independence given each hypothesis, likelihood ratios for the conceivability argument under the epistemic/metaphysical possibility distinction — and the conditional-independence assumption carries the argument, which is why the conditions below are stated as the ways it can fail.

### Genuine Independence

Independence is the hardest condition to satisfy and the easiest to overestimate. The arguments for moral realism — convergence of moral disagreement over time, moral explanation, the companions-in-guilt argument with mathematics — appear to start from different places, but all presuppose that moral phenomenology is a reliable guide to moral ontology; if it is systematically misleading, all three lose force simultaneously. Independence must therefore be assessed at the level of *deep assumptions*, not surface premises.

The Map's [[common-cause-null|common-cause-null discipline]] regiments Reichenbach's common-cause principle (1956): a correlation demands either a direct causal link or a common cause that screens it off. Salmon (1984) also bounded the principle — his *interactive forks* are cases where a genuine common cause fails to screen its effects off from each other, so correlation surviving the conditioning is weaker evidence of independent contact than the test's clean form suggests. Applied to convergence, the discipline asks whether agreement reflects independent contact with the subject matter or a shared upstream cause — common cognitive architecture, a shared framework — that would produce the agreement whether or not the conclusion is true. Its contribution is a procedure for *applying* the screening-off test where the candidate common cause (homo sapiens cognition) is always present: whether observed agreement is more specific than that common cause alone would predict, distinguishing convergence that earns its N-fold weight from one upstream architecture read N times.

For cross-cultural convergence, independence is never total: all human traditions share a common origin in human cognition, social structures, and embodied experience. This creates a bootstrapping problem parallel to the [[phenomenology-of-intellectual-life|bootstrap problem in belief revision]], and it bites harder than in science, which is partly disciplined by external instruments. It cannot be fully resolved from within the framework, so the argument is suggestive rather than demonstrative. What partially rescues it is the *specificity* criterion below: shared cognitive architecture predicts convergence on *general* tendencies (agent-detection, mind-body separation as a rough heuristic), whereas the consciousness convergence picks out a narrower diagnosis, arguably too specific to be the natural output of generic predispositions. The threshold remains under-articulated as a formal criterion.

### Robustness Under Variation

Convergence depending on a particular school's assumptions is weaker than convergence across rival traditions: agreement among analytic philosophy, phenomenology, and Indian metaphysics is harder to explain by shared methodological commitments than agreement within analytic philosophy alone.

The [[comparative-phenomenology-of-meditative-traditions|comparative phenomenology of meditative traditions]] illustrates the [[common-cause-null|common-cause-null]] audit. Buddhist, Hindu, Christian, Sufi, and Daoist practitioners share an upstream mechanism — trained introspective attention in homo sapiens — enough to generate convergence on *some* pattern, so the audit asks whether the agreed pattern is more specific than that mechanism alone would predict. Reports converge on structural features (self-dissolution under sustained attention to the witness, contentless awareness, collapse of subject-object structure) narrower than generic introspection would license, while the metaphysical *interpretations* diverge informatively (Madhyamaka against Yogācāra's reflexive *svasaṃvedana*, Vedānta's *Ātman*-as-*Brahman*, theistic union). The pattern survives as structural-feature agreement, but the count cannot upgrade from one cross-tradition pattern with shared-mechanism dependency to five independent triangulations, and [[neoplatonist-common-cause-weight-for-cross-tradition-introspection-architecture-parallels|the Neoplatonist sub-audit]] shows the discipline yields *asymmetric* per-tradition discounts rather than uniform ones. The [[phenomenological-evidence#convergence|convergent phenomenological evidence]] therefore contributes structural-feature agreement only.

Robustness also applies across time. Aristotle's puzzlement about soul and body, Avicenna's Flying Man, Descartes' conceivability argument, and Chalmers' zombie argument target structurally similar difficulties despite different intellectual contexts — a diachronic pattern conceded to be at least partly a canon-formation selection artefact rather than independent evidence. The Sāṃkhya parallel illustrates the limit on how far such robustness may be claimed: the *Puruṣa*/*Prakṛti* distinction is sometimes read as tracking phenomenal consciousness versus functional cognition, but O'Brien-Kop (2024) characterises *Puruṣa* as *contentless* — "does not contain sensation, feeling, or experience" — relocating the problem to *the capacity to consciously recognise experience*, which is precisely not phenomenal character in Chalmers's sense; rival scholarship (Larson, Bryant, Whicher) reads it closer to a Kantian transcendental subject. This is a framework-boundary disagreement among interpreters, not a clean convergence on phenomenal consciousness — the readings differ on what *Puruṣa* even is. What survives is narrower and structural: every reading preserves an irreducible witnessing subject that the apparatus of *Prakṛti* cannot constitute or absorb, and the convergence is robust only at that level. The [[cross-traditional-convergence-on-consciousness-irreducibility|dedicated analysis]] identifies three structural features — the asymmetry of acquaintance, the regress of objectification, and the gap between structure and quality — that explain why irreducibility functions as an attractor across traditions.

### Specificity of Agreement

Convergence on vague conclusions carries little weight — all traditions agree the world is puzzling, which tells us nothing. Convergence on specific conclusions carries far more, because the space of possible specific conclusions is large. The consciousness convergence is specific relative to that space: diverse traditions do not merely register consciousness as a curiosity but independently identify a particular structural problem — that accounts of the physical world, however complete, leave something unexplained about subjective experience. Avicenna's Flying Man, suspended in a void with no sensory input, cannot doubt the existence of his consciousness while doubting everything physical — a structural parallel to Descartes specific enough to resist attribution to coincidence.

### Compound Signatures: Convergence Without a Shared Mechanism

The independence condition has a limiting case worth naming, because it appears to be the form in which convergence does its most defensible evidential work. A *compound signature* is convergence among routes that share no mechanism, so the agreement seems unlikely to be the read-out of one upstream cause sampled several times. Where ordinary convergence must survive the [[common-cause-null|common-cause-null]] audit by arguing that the agreed pattern is more specific than a shared architecture would predict, a compound signature may pre-empt the audit at the structural level: there is arguably no candidate *mechanism* to screen off — the difference between three witnesses who all read the same instrument and three witnesses with no instrument in common, and the case where Bovens and Hartmann's model rewards agreement most. Disjointness closes only the mechanism channel, though. Routes individuated under a shared scheme still answer to a common cause of a different kind — shared definitions rather than shared machinery — which is why the Map reads its own [[what-voids-reveal|voids catalogue]] as coherence rather than evidence: the framework individuating the voids is the one their clustering was taken to support.

The clearest worked exhibit is clinical rather than cross-cultural: focal **lesion** ([[neurological-dissociations-as-interface-architecture|what destruction reveals]]), selective **pharmacology** ([[anaesthesia-and-the-consciousness-interface|what reversible chemical interruption reveals]]), and psychogenic **dissociation** ([[clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence|functional decoupling with no structural damage]], sharpest in [[conversion-disorder-as-consciousness-side-fault|conversion disorder]]) converge on one multi-channel taxonomy of the consciousness-brain interface. The three routes appear to share no mechanism, so their agreement may be read as a compound signature rather than one architecture read three times — plausibly the convergence form the cross-cultural case can only approximate, since human traditions always share *some* upstream cognition whereas the clinical routes look disjoint by construction.

The exhibit also shows where a compound signature's evidential reach stops. Convergence appears to underwrite the *architecture* — that consciousness connects to brain mechanisms through separable channels — and not the architecture's dualist *significance*. The dissociation pattern tells strongly against naive identity theory (if consciousness simply *were* the processing, damage would presumably degrade both together), but sophisticated functionalism — Block's access/phenomenal distinction, multi-realisability, integrationist readings — predicts the same modular decomposition by treating the channels as coupled subsystems whose joint operation *constitutes* consciousness. The compound signature is therefore best read as *compatibility*-grade rather than *vindication*-grade for the interface reading: fully compatible with the Map's reading and not adjudicated in its favour over the functionalist rival, because both seem to predict the identical dissociation phenomenology. Per [[project/evidential-status-discipline#compatibility-vs-support-at-the-rival-model-interface|the compatibility-vs-support discipline]], citing it as *support* would require a discriminator the functionalist does not also predict, and the dissociations alone do not supply one.

That two-tier discount governs how a compound signature may be cited anywhere: the architectural claim transfers, while which metaphysics the structure favours inherits none of the convergence's weight. Letting architectural strength bleed into the significance claim is the compatibility-as-vindication error the [[project/evidential-status-discipline|evidential-status discipline]] exists to catch; [[phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence]] applies the same discount in the first-person domain.

## When Convergence Fails

The history of philosophy and science contains cases where convergence pointed toward falsehood — Laudan's catalogue. Each is a convergence on a theoretical posit later retired, and testing the consciousness case against them is the discipline the diagnosis/solution distinction is built to pass.

**[[vitalism|Vitalism]].** Multiple independent lines — across cultures — converged on a non-physical vital force: the irreducibility of biological organisation, the purposiveness of organisms, and the gap between living and nonliving matter all seemed to require something beyond mechanism. The convergence was genuine; the conclusion was wrong. Vitalism collapsed once biochemistry supplied the specific mechanisms. What makes the consciousness case structurally different cannot be that no reductive explanation has *yet* appeared — vitalists could have said the same in 1800 — so the answer must come from the *character* of the arguments. Two features distinguish it.

First, core arguments target **logical relationships rather than empirical gaps**. The conceivability argument holds that a world physically identical to ours but lacking consciousness is *logically coherent* — a claim about what physical descriptions can *in principle* yield, not what science has failed to achieve, where vitalism's arguments were empirical gap arguments that mechanism could and did close. Second, the [[knowledge-argument|knowledge argument]] is explicitly constructed to survive complete scientific knowledge: Mary, who knows every physical fact about colour vision, learns something new upon seeing red. It *grants* that physics is complete and still concludes physical knowledge is not exhaustive, so there is no consciousness analogue of Wöhler's urea synthesis to wait for.

The strongest reply to this contrast comes from the illusionists. Keith Frankish ("Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness," 2016) and Iris Berent (2023, 2024) argue that the apparent logical coherence of the zombie scenario is itself a cognitive artefact — the output of essentialist and dualist biases Berent locates deep in human psychology — rather than a genuine modal datum. If they are right, the logical/empirical contrast collapses: the "logical" character of the conceivability argument would be a psychological appearance, not a structural fact. This is serious, and against the *realist* reading it has real force. The reply — that converting a debunking etiology into a refutation requires a screening-off the illusionist has not supplied — is pressed in the [[#cognitive-universals|cognitive-universals analysis]] below, where Berent's programme is taken in its strongest form and the surviving boundary is [[#scope-of-the-argument|marked against eliminativism]].

**Geocentrism.** The convergence of common sense, astronomical observation, and Aristotelian physics on a stationary earth shows how deep frameworks generate convergent errors: the arguments all presupposed a physics in which natural motion tends toward a center, and once that framework was replaced the apparent convergence dissolved. Surface-level diversity does not guarantee deep independence.

Kyle Stanford's problem of unconceived alternatives (*Exceeding Our Grasp*, 2006) sharpens the test: the history of inquiry is a graveyard of theories whose proponents could not conceive the alternative that later displaced them, so the present unavailability of a rival explanation of the cross-cultural pattern is weak evidence that none exists. The claim that no better explanation than a real explanatory gap is *currently* available must be stated as exactly that — a claim about conceived options, not a demonstration of exhaustiveness. The honest weight is correspondingly bounded: it raises the cost of physicalism without foreclosing an unconceived deflationary account of why the diagnosis recurs.

### The Methodology-Symmetry Test

Defeasibility of the *conclusion* is distinct from falsifiability of the *methodology*. Would the three-condition framework underwrite a counterfactual cross-cultural convergence on the *reducibility* of consciousness, with specific structural agreement and diachronic robustness? It would: the framework tests for evidential convergence of any kind and would deliver the opposite verdict on opposite data. That symmetry checks the framework's integrity.

## The Anti-Realist Challenge

The most powerful objection holds that shared human cognitive architecture explains the patterns better than shared access to truth.

### Cognitive Universals

Humans share neural architecture, embodied experience, Theory of Mind, and agent-detection tendencies, which could generate systematic philosophical tendencies. If humans naturally separate mental from physical — as Paul Bloom's "natural-born dualist" hypothesis suggests — cross-cultural convergence reflects cognitive predisposition rather than discovery.

The empirical evidence complicates this, and is itself live. Barrett et al.'s (2021) cross-cultural study of afterlife beliefs across six populations found the overall pattern does not support intuitive dualism: most participants gave non-dualist responses, and the authors conclude dualist thinking is "a possible mode of thought enabled by evolved human psychology" but "does not constitute a default mode of thought." Barlev and Shtulman (2021) argue dualist conceptions are learned rather than innate; Bering's afterlife-reasoning work and developmental studies on agent-detection point the other way. Taken at face value the result removes the *default-intuition version* of the debunking explanation only. If dualism turns out more innate than those authors conclude, the anti-realist explanation gains traction — the argument's force here depends on an empirical question they opened rather than closed.

Iris Berent's debunking programme (2023, 2024) presses the challenge in a form designed to survive that concession. Where Bloom's hypothesis needs intuitive dualism to be a *default* — the premise Barrett et al. undercut — Berent argues that "problem intuitions" emerge from the *interaction* of two biases: a soft, violable intuitive Dualism (mind seems separable from body) and an intuitive Essentialism (living kinds have hidden essences). The biases need not surface in every afterlife survey; they need only be available dispositions that, jointly engaged when a tradition reflects on subjectivity, generate the sense that experience resists physical description. Because the diagnosis is the *joint output of universal cognitive machinery*, Berent predicts precisely the cross-traditional recurrence the argument treats as evidential — whether or not consciousness is in fact irreducible. The [[common-cause-null|common-cause-null]] concession does not defuse this: Berent supplies a *specific* candidate common cause (Dualism-plus-Essentialism), not the generic "homo sapiens cognition" the specificity criterion is built to discount.

The reply locates the step Berent has not earned. Granting her case, it shows that the *intuition* of irreducibility may have a discoverable etiology in two biases. The inference she needs is from "the intuition is produced by these biases" to "the intuition cannot be trusted to reflect what consciousness is," which requires the premise that an intuition with an identifiable cognitive cause is thereby *unreliable about its object*. Stated without restriction this seems to prove too much — every belief, including the physicalist's conviction that mechanism suffices, plausibly has a cognitive etiology, so an evenhanded application would appear to debunk the deflationary intuition alongside the realist one. To avoid that symmetry the debunker must show the Dualism-and-Essentialism mechanism would generate the irreducibility intuition *whether or not the gap were real* — a sensitivity-failure specific to this belief. Berent's data establish causation, not insensitivity. Until that gap is closed the debunking relocates rather than removes the burden: the specificity of the cross-traditional diagnosis (the same structural feature, not merely "mind is mysterious") is what a generic two-bias story struggles to predict, though whether Berent's biases could encode the structure-versus-quality distinction rather than diffuse separability remains open, the specificity criterion being unoperationalised. Where disagreement persists it is the [[#scope-of-the-argument|eliminativist boundary]]: a thoroughgoing illusionist who refuses experience as a datum reads the biases-explanation as complete. That residue is genuine, not a refutation.

### Cognitive Closure

[[mysterianism]] — McGinn's view that humans may be cognitively closed to the consciousness-brain relationship — offers a subtler anti-realist account: traditions might converge on irreducibility not because consciousness *is* irreducible but because human minds cannot grasp the reductive explanation.

Two observations limit it. Cognitive closure does not favour physicalism — if the solution is beyond us, we cannot know whether it is reductive or non-reductive, so mysterianism entails agnosticism rather than materialism. And a *generic* limitation would produce variable errors, not convergence on a structurally precise diagnosis — though McGinn's *specific* hypothesis, closure about the consciousness-physical relationship in particular, predicts exactly the observed pattern ("there is a gap here we cannot close"). Against generic closure the convergence is at least as well-explained by a real feature of consciousness; against specific closure it is arguably what the closure hypothesis itself predicts, leaving the work to whether one finds closure or irreducibility the better explanation — a comparison the convergence argument alone seems unable to settle.

## What Divergence Reveals

If convergence is significant, divergence should be equally informative. The sharpest divergence — Chinese traditions (Daoism, Neo-Confucianism) largely dissolving rather than generating the hard problem by treating mind and world as aspects of a unified process — is surveyed in [[the-hard-problem-in-non-western-philosophy|the non-Western survey]]. This is no convergence on physicalism: Chinese thought does not reduce mind to matter but works in a framework that dissolves the distinction the hard problem presupposes. It poses a genuine challenge to the realist interpretation: if the hard problem depends on isolating subjective experience as distinct from its physical correlates, convergence among traditions that make this move may track the move rather than a truth about consciousness.

Chalmers takes up this question in "Is the Hard Problem of Consciousness Universal?" (2020), and his graded conclusions both discipline and partially support the argument. He rejects the stronger of four theses: *judgment universality* (everyone explicitly judges there to be a hard problem) he calls "obviously" false — "in some cultures, the issue never seems to have been raised," which places the Chinese case in the *expected* category rather than as an embarrassment to realism — and individual-level *intuition universality* he takes to be "probably false." What he defends, stressing that it all "involves disputable empirical claims," is the weaker pair: *population-level intuition universality* and, above all, *source universality*, a factor present in every normal adult that tends to generate the problem intuitions when "taken up in a certain way," even where explicit intuitions are dormant. The convergence the Map defends is best read at that grain — source or population-level disposition, not the claim that every tradition explicitly thematises the gap. His diagnosis of why the explicit problem is historically scarce sharpens this: it "did not really get a grip" until materialism became a live foil in the 1860s, for "one has to take materialism seriously in order to take the hard problem seriously as a problem." A tradition with a non-dualist process metaphysics is the mirror image — without the materialist framing the demand presupposes, the problem need not surface even if the source is present. That is more principled than a bare "latent everywhere" gloss, though shielded by the materialism-as-foil auxiliary: it predicts the problem should appear wherever a tradition takes a reductive physical account as the thing to be measured against.

Marking a divergence rather than borrowing Chalmers's authority: source universality does *not* by itself vindicate the realist reading, and he says so — it "is especially natural for a realist about consciousness such as myself" but "is also common among illusionists." A universal source disposing humans to find consciousness irreducible is exactly what a debunker like Berent also predicts, so the shared source is common ground, not a tiebreaker: Chalmers's work supports the convergence being *real and deeply sourced* while withholding the claim that the source tracks a genuine feature of consciousness. That contested step is carried not by the universality finding but by the screening-off argument pressed against Berent above. The Chinese case remains the sharpest test of how far source-universality can be pressed before it becomes unfalsifiable.

The realist can respond that the subject-object distinction is itself a philosophical discovery, but the response risks circularity: the features cited ([[qualia]], the unity of experience, perspectival character) are partly *outputs* of the distinction's own vocabulary rather than independent test-data. The honest position is that the distinction is *productive* — it generates vocabulary picking out features Western and Indian traditions take to be real — but productivity is not veridicality, and the Chinese alternative cannot be decisively ruled out on that basis.

Divergence also appears in proposed solutions: Indian traditions lean toward idealism or radical dualism, Islamic philosophy toward emanationism, Western analytic philosophy toward property dualism and neutral monism. The convergence is on the *problem*, not the *solution*. That convergent-diagnosis-with-divergent-treatment pattern appears to mirror scientific contexts where investigators agree on a phenomenon but disagree about its explanation — consistent with, though not establishing, a genuine underlying phenomenon. The same shape appears in dissolved cases (nineteenth-century convergence on a luminiferous medium whose mechanics disagreed about its properties), so the inference from convergent-diagnosis to real-phenomenon is suggestive rather than decisive.

## Convergence Applied to Dualism

The Map's [[the-convergence-argument-for-dualism|convergence argument for dualism]] applies this framework. The [[knowledge-argument]], the conceivability argument via [[philosophical-zombies]], the [[explanatory-gap]], arguments from [[qualia]], and arguments from the unity of consciousness all point toward the irreducibility of phenomenal experience. On **independence**, the arguments fall into two cleanly independent clusters and a third only partially earned: knowledge and qualia arguments depend on phenomenal intuition; conceivability and Kripke arguments exploit logical possibility; the structural cluster (unity, intentionality) is independent for the unity prong — establishable from third-person psychophysics — but contested for intentionality, which the eliminativist collapses into phenomenal report. On **robustness**, it holds across analytic philosophy, phenomenology, and several non-Western traditions, limited by the Chinese counterexample. On **specificity**, the diagnosis is that physical descriptions, however complete, leave the qualitative character of experience unexplained.

### Scope of the Argument

A deeper objection holds that *all* these arguments share a common root — the intuition that there is "something it is like" to have an experience — so that if the intuition is a cognitive artefact, every argument collapses simultaneously. The argument's reach is conditional on the experience-as-datum starting point. But that shared root cannot be reclassified into independence — the count-cannot-upgrade discipline holds here too — so independence rests on the disjoint premise-clusters above, not on recounting the single root as subject matter. Eliminativists and illusionists — Dennett, Frankish (2016), Berent (2023, 2024) — contest even the datum, treating reports of experience as the data and declining any additional ontological item. This is bedrock disagreement the convergence argument cannot resolve; it advances against rival non-eliminativist positions and stands honestly at the boundary against eliminativism rather than pretending the boundary is internal. The [[#cognitive-universals|cognitive-universals analysis]] shows what it *can* do — require a screening-off specific to the realist intuition — and what it cannot: compel an illusionist who refuses the datum from the start. The cumulative case for dualism ([[the-convergence-argument-for-dualism]], the [[tenets#^dualism|Dualism tenet]]) inherits this scope condition.

## Methodology and Framework Commitment Are Different Routes

A calibration issue runs across applications. The framework supplies a methodology — three conditions on independence, robustness, specificity — operating on a body of arguments. The third element in any deployment is the framework that *reads* the output: which conclusion the convergence supports depends on what that framework selects from the irreducibility-respecting alternatives. The methodology and the data are independent of each other; the framework that reads them is not an independent third route. The [[project/evidential-status-discipline#coherence-vs-evidential-status|coherence-vs-evidential-status]] discipline guards this. Success at *layer one* (the arguments earn "consciousness is irreducible" across rival irreducibility-respecting framings) does not license *layer two* (the specific reading — substance dualism, panpsychism, neutral monism, idealism, Madhyamaka emptiness). Layer-two selection runs on the framework's own further arguments — bidirectional interaction, combination strategies, regularity-of-nature accounts, therapeutic deconstruction — which do honest work but do not inherit the convergence's weight; they earn what their separate cases supply.

The pattern operates on every Map application: [[the-convergence-argument-for-dualism|the convergence argument for dualism]] runs the philosophical-arguments cluster, [[cross-traditional-convergence-on-consciousness-irreducibility|cross-traditional convergence]] the contemplative cluster, [[cross-cultural-convergence-on-mental-causation|convergence on mental causation]] the mental-causation cluster. Each earns the cross-rival conclusion and lets the framework's specific reading run on separately-supported arguments; [[topics/animal-consciousness#why-the-inference-is-stronger-for-animals-than-for-ai|the animal-consciousness COGITATE treatment]] is the worked exhibit, holding markers and convergent evolution as two independent routes plus the framework's no-substrate-barrier as the selection commitment, never three independent confirmations. A framework that silently counted its consumers' commitments as additional routes would be unfalsifiable in exactly the failure mode the methodology-symmetry test guards against — it would confirm itself by reading itself into the count.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Unfinishable Map relies on convergence at multiple levels. The [[tenets#^dualism|Dualism tenet]] is supported not by any single argument but by the convergence of *partially* independent routes to irreducibility — extended beyond one tradition by the cross-cultural dimension, whose own independence the common-cause audit discounts rather than assumes.

The [[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits tenet]] connects directly: parsimony is a tiebreaker between theories of equal explanatory power, not a trump card overriding convergent evidence. Dismissing cross-cultural convergence as mere cognitive bias assumes we understand human cognition well enough to explain the pattern away, which the empirical evidence on [[intuitive-dualism]] does not yet support.

The Map treats convergence as genuine evidence while acknowledging its limits. Convergence does not prove dualism — the vitalism precedent shows even genuine convergence can be overturned — and the contrast with vitalism holds only if the conceivability and knowledge arguments are sound, which illusionism contests; the force comes from those arguments' content, not from convergence. The hard problem's framework-dependence, revealed by the Chinese divergence, means the argument requires supplementation by direct arguments for irreducibility and cannot stand alone. Physicalist accounts still owe an explanation of the pattern beyond appeal to shared cognitive architecture: the pattern's specificity, audited against the common-cause null, is what creates the explanatory burden.
