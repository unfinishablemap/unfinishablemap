Temperature is the perceptual modality where felt value is most visibly *indexed to the state of the body*. The same cool water is pleasant when you are overheated and aversive when you are chilled; the same skin temperature is comfort or distress depending on what the body needs—the phenomenon Michel Cabanac named *alliesthesia* (Cabanac, 1971), with temperature its canonical case. This is a different fact from the affective tone the [[tactile-consciousness-and-the-interface|tactile article]] locates in the dedicated affective channel of touch. Affective touch carries a roughly fixed hedonic tone—a gentle caress is pleasant more or less regardless of body state. Thermal valence flips with thermoregulatory need: it is computed against a homeostatic set-point, not read off the stimulus. Touch supplies the cleanest exemplar of [[evaluative-phenomenal-character|constitutively evaluative phenomenal character]] as such—felt experience arriving already valued rather than neutral-then-judged; thermoception supplies the cleanest *state-indexed* case, the valence visibly a function of the perceiver's condition.

Thermoception supplies a second distinctive the other modality articles have not covered. Skin thermoreceptors report *both* the temperature of the external world (an exteroceptive fact) *and* the body's own thermoregulatory condition (an interoceptive fact). Temperature is therefore the boundary case for the asymmetry the [[cross-modal-capability-division|cross-modal synthesis]] argues for—that the capability division is an *exteroceptive* regularity which [[interoceptive-consciousness-and-the-interface|interoception inverts]] inward. If one modality is both at once, the Map reads the outward/inward dichotomy as a gradient with thermoception sitting on the line. As with every article in this series, nothing here *settles* dualism over physicalism. The physical account of temperature—molecular transduction, homeostatic integration, allostatic prediction—is strong, and the Map adopts it as mechanism. What thermoception does is press the [[explanatory-gap|explanatory gap]] in the register of *value indexed to need*, and locate a structural boundary the survey's outward/inward partition cannot place.

This is the thermal companion to [[tactile-consciousness-and-the-interface|the tactile case]], the [[vestibular-consciousness-and-the-interface|vestibular case]], and the inward [[interoceptive-consciousness-and-the-interface|interoceptive case]]—the modality each named as still lacking an interface treatment—scoped tightly to its two non-redundant distinctives. The tactile article covers fixed-valence affective touch and treats temperature as a bound feature of a felt object; the interoceptive article names "thermal" as a convergent channel without developing state-dependent valence or the skin's dual-organ status. The uncovered material is the *homeostatic indexing of thermal valence* and the *boundary status* of the modality.

## Thermal Valence Is Indexed to Body State

The classical somatosensory picture treats temperature as a discriminative cutaneous sense, alongside touch—a sense that reports how warm or cold a surface is, with comfort or discomfort a separate evaluation laid on top. The neuroscientist A. D. (Bud) Craig argued this gets the structure backwards. As the retrospective by Anders Blomqvist puts the reframing: "while we normally regard temperature sensation as a discriminative cutaneous sensory capacity, the valence of the feeling depends on the body's thermoregulatory needs" (Blomqvist, 2023). The example Blomqvist gives is the canonical one: "a glass of cool water is pleasant if you are overheated but aversive if you are chilled" (Blomqvist, 2023).

Craig was systematising a datum Cabanac had named three decades earlier: alliesthesia is documented, on Cabanac's evidence, in three senses—thermal, gustatory, and olfactory—with the internal signals "related to the set points of some regulated variables" of the internal milieu (Cabanac, 1971). The same meal is pleasant when the body needs calories, aversive when sated. The restriction is principled: Cabanac conjectures that alliesthesia exists only where a sensation bears on a regulated internal variable, which no visual or auditory stimulus can modify. So thermoception is not alone in carrying state-indexed value—only the survey's cleanest case—and Cabanac's criterion returns below, at the boundary question.

The structure to notice is that the valence is not a constant property of the stimulus: the felt pleasantness of warmth is a function of how far the body is from its set-point and in which direction (Cabanac, 1971; Craig, 2018). This is what distinguishes thermal valence from the affective tone of [[tactile-consciousness-and-the-interface|cutaneous affective touch]]. The slow C-tactile afferents that carry the pleasantness of a gentle caress respond best to a fairly narrow band of stroking near skin temperature (Ackerley et al., 2014), and the hedonic tone they deliver—itself gated by stimulus temperature—is stable with respect to the perceiver's thermoregulatory state. Thermal valence is *state-dependent by construction*: it tracks a homeostatic comparison, so the very same afferent input can present as relief or as threat.

On Craig's reading, temperature is not really an exteroceptive perception of an object's property but an interoceptive *feeling* about the body's condition—a "feeling from the body" carried by lamina I spinothalamic neurons to the posterior insula (Craig, 2002) and re-represented in the anterior insula, the cortical region Craig identifies with the felt bodily self (Craig, 2009; Blomqvist, 2023). The thermal channel shares this lamina I pathway with pain, which is why the two integrate so intimately—a point the next section develops.

What this gives the Map is a perceptual case of phenomenal character that is constitutively evaluative *and* visibly relative to the subject's state. In thermoception the value cannot be a downstream verdict on a neutral percept, because there is no fixed neutral percept to judge; the phenomenology suggests that what is felt *is* the comfort or distress, with the discriminative magnitude recoverable only by an effort of attention.

## The Thermal Grill: Temperature and Pain Integrate Centrally

The sharpest single datum that thermal experience is centrally constructed rather than read off the skin is the thermal grill illusion. Craig and Bushnell gave the modern analysis: "In Thunberg's thermal grill illusion, first demonstrated in 1896, a sensation of strong, often painful heat is elicited by touching interlaced warm and cool bars to the skin" (Craig & Bushnell, 1994). Their recordings from cold-sensitive spinothalamic neurons supported a central-disinhibition or "unmasking" model: warm stimuli suppress cool-sensitive lamina I activity, releasing inhibition on polymodal nociceptive pathways, so an innocuous combination of warm and cool bars is felt as burning heat. The model "predicted a quantitative correspondence between grill-evoked pain and cold-evoked pain, which was verified psychophysically" (Craig & Bushnell, 1994).

The illusion matters for a precise reason: the felt heat corresponds to nothing on the skin—there is no hot bar—and the burning quality is generated by the central interaction of two thermal channels, not by any peripheral signal that resembles it. This shows that the felt quality is constructed rather than transduced: the skin reports warm-and-cool; the integration reports painful heat. Said plainly, the construction is by a fully specified physical mechanism with a confirmed quantitative prediction—exactly what the mechanism-sufficiency rival expects, no tilt toward the interface. The residue is the one the Rivals section names: the model says which central pattern is produced, not why its production is *like* burning for anyone.

The grill also shows how tightly thermal and nociceptive processing are woven together: cold, warmth, and pain are a system whose outputs depend on how the channels interact. This is the thermal version of the constructed-percept point the [[tactile-consciousness-and-the-interface|tactile]] and [[auditory-consciousness-and-the-interface|auditory]] articles make—here with the further point that the constructed quality is *aversive*, so what is constructed is felt value, not merely felt content.

## The Boundary Case: Both Exteroceptive and Interoceptive

Thermoception's second distinctive is structural rather than affective. Skin thermoreceptors face two ways at once—reporting the temperature of whatever the skin contacts (an exteroceptive fact about the world) while contributing to the monitoring of core thermoregulatory state (an interoceptive fact about the organism). Laura Crucianelli and H. Henrik Ehrsson make this dual status the heart of their case that the skin is a neglected interoceptive organ.

Their premise is that "the skin, given its very nature, is a sensory organ extensively and directly exposed not only to the inside of the body but also to the external environment" (Crucianelli & Ehrsson, 2023). Thermoception is precisely where the two roles fuse: "interoceptive responses to thermal stimuli can refer both to internally generated stimuli but also in response to the application of thermosensory stimuli on the skin" (Crucianelli & Ehrsson, 2023). The dual character is part of why the skin's interoceptive contribution has been overlooked: "one of the reasons why skin signals might have been overlooked so far is the fact that they provide both interoceptive and exteroceptive sensory information, making it difficult to disentangle the two" (Crucianelli & Ehrsson, 2023). For them the difficulty is not inseparability—the passage continues: "Nevertheless, carefully designed and controlled experiments can allow us to manipulate only one component (i.e., the interoceptive one of interest) while keeping the other constant or absent (i.e., the exteroceptive one)". Their thesis is a *reclassification*, proposing "thermosensation as a particularly attractive model of skin-mediated interoception", while recording that "there is no consensus on whether certain skin signals should be defined as interoceptive".

This makes thermoception a test of the [[cross-modal-capability-division|cross-modal synthesis]]. That synthesis contends that the capability division's headline asymmetry—modality-specific brain machinery, supramodal mind-side access boundary—holds for the distance and proprioceptive senses but is *inverted* by [[interoceptive-consciousness-and-the-interface|interoception]], whose channels converge on a shared insular integrator and whose mind-side contribution becomes affect-and-ownership. The synthesis frames the asymmetry as an *exteroceptive regularity* with an inward exception—and its unstated assumption is that each modality falls cleanly on one side: outward-facing or inward-facing.

Thermoception strains the assumption: one receptor sheet, one lamina I pathway, one insular destination serve both reports. The article's two authorities resolve the strain by reclassification, differently drawn. Craig moves the whole modality inward: temperature is not exteroceptive at all but an interoceptive feeling. Crucianelli and Ehrsson keep two components, hold them experimentally separable, and propose studying the neglected interoceptive one as such. The gradient reading—exteroception and interoception as ends of a spectrum, thermoception on the line—is the *Map's* inference, asserted by neither source. The Map prefers it because each reclassification saves the dichotomy by assigning a dual-role signal wholly to one side, while the duality is the datum: separability under controlled design shows the components are distinguishable, not which side the modality lives on, and in ordinary experience the two reports arrive fused. Cabanac's criterion gives the placement a principled basis—thermoception reports an external magnitude that is simultaneously a perturbation of a tightly regulated internal variable, sitting mid-axis by construction.

Read this way, the boundary does not weaken the apex's finding; it locates an edge the finding's terms could not place. The extero/intero distinction becomes a partition of *roles a single modality can play* rather than of modalities, and the apex's outward/inward sorting becomes a spectrum.

## The Rivals, On Their Own Terms

Three physicalist accounts bear on temperature, not in full agreement among themselves, and each must be engaged as a rival the interface reading has to out-accommodate rather than as an ally to be conscripted. The Map adopts the mechanism each describes; it contests only the claim that the mechanism exhausts the phenomenon.

The first and most complete is **molecular transduction**. Thermal sensation begins in temperature-gated ion channels of the transient receptor potential (TRP) family, with distinct channels tuned to distinct temperature ranges: TRPV1 responds to noxious heat (threshold near 43 °C) and to capsaicin, TRPM8 to innocuous cooling and to menthol, with further TRP channels covering warmth and noxious cold (Vriens, Nilius & Voets, 2014). David Julius shared the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for identifying these receptors, TRPV1 and TRPM8 among them. The knockout evidence is decisive at its own level: animals lacking TRPM8 lose much of their ability to sense cold. The Map takes this complete, well-characterised transduction account as established mechanism, and the consequence is locating: the interface claim is not a claim about transduction, which is fully physical and uncontested. The claim, if there is one, lives *above* the receptor—at the level of felt, owned, state-indexed value, where the molecular story specifies how the signal arises but not why its arising is *like* anything.

The second is **Craig's homeostatic-emotion reconceptualization** itself, which is a rival even though it supplies the state-dependent-valence datum the article leans on. Craig's reframing of temperature as an interoceptive homeostatic feeling, integrated in the insula and presented as part of the felt bodily self, is a *physicalist* account of why thermal experience is valenced and self-involving. It asserts almost everything the Map would say about thermal valence being constitutive and state-indexed, and explains it with no non-physical remainder: a homeostatic integrator generating an affective representation of body state. The Map adopts Craig's structural claims as mechanism. Its residue is the familiar one and no larger: the account explains why a representation of thermoregulatory comfort becomes available to the system and guides behaviour, but not why there is a subject *for whom* the comfort or distress is felt. That is an argumentative residue—the [[explanatory-gap|explanatory gap]] and the ownership question—not a place where the thermal data tilt toward the interface.

The third is **allostatic, active-inference regulation**. On Barrett and Simmons's model, agranular visceromotor cortices issue autonomic, hormonal, and immunological predictions that deploy the body's resources for the world "not as it is right now, but as the brain predicts it will be in a moment from now", with felt interoception arising as the sensory consequence of that anticipatory regulation (Barrett & Simmons, 2015). Their worked examples are not thermal, but the programme generalises to any regulated variable and predicts the anticipatory, affective character of thermal feeling. What it does not supply is the set-point: Barrett and Simmons expressly reject reading interoception as comparison against homeostatic set-points—their glossary defines homeostasis as dynamic regulation, "not a single set point"—so the allostatic programme *competes with* the set-point framing this article takes from Cabanac and Craig rather than extending it. The Map adopts the predictive architecture as mechanism, as it does in the [[interoceptive-consciousness-and-the-interface|interoceptive case]] and the [[predictive-processing-and-dualism|interaction framework]]. But the framework does not discriminate between the readings: an anticipatory regulatory loop is what the physicalist expects, and equally what the interface reading expects, since on the Map's picture conscious participation operates through, not against, the brain's predictive machinery.

The honest situation, stated plainly, is that each of these rivals accounts for the thermal data with no non-physical remainder, and the Map says so. What the modality contributes is not evidence that tilts the balance but a structural exhibit: a phenomenal character that is constitutively evaluative and visibly indexed to the subject's state, and a modality that straddles the extero/intero boundary the survey's architecture presupposes.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The thermal distinctives above are framed throughout as phenomena the dualist/interface reading *accommodates*, never as evidence that settles the dispute. The empirical literature is naturalist—Cabanac, Craig, Blomqvist, Crucianelli, Ehrsson, Ackerley, Vriens, Nilius, Voets, Barrett, and Simmons build physicalist models and draw no dualist conclusion—and the molecular-transduction, homeostatic-emotion, and allostatic-prediction accounts are engaged here as the rivals the interface reading must out-accommodate. The Map adopts their mechanics and contests their sufficiency.

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]**: Thermoception is a clean exhibit of phenomenal structure for the [[explanatory-gap|explanatory gap]] in the register of *value indexed to need*. Because the felt valence flips with body state while the stimulus is unchanged, thermal experience makes vivid that what is felt is not a neutral magnitude with an evaluation appended but a constitutively evaluative state. The physicalist's homeostatic-integration reply stands; the residue it does not address is why the state-indexed comfort or distress is experienced *by a subject* at all. The Map reads thermal quality as constituted at the [[mind-matter-interface|interface]], with the molecular and homeostatic stories describing the brain side and the felt, owned valence the mind side.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]**: Thermoregulation is anticipatory, not merely reactive—the system predicts thermal change and acts to forestall it, so thermal feeling is bound up with action. This is consonant with the Map's reading, on which perceiving and feeling involve the perceiver's participation. The alignment is interpretive, not probative: the active-inference theorist re-describes the same anticipatory regulation in wholly physical terms, and the Map's commitment to bidirectional interaction rests on its broader [[predictive-processing-and-dualism|interaction framework]], not on the thermal case alone.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]** enters through the boundary argument. The single label "temperature" flattens two genuinely different roles—outward report and inward report—into one apparently simple modality. Treating the simple label as evidence that there is one simple thing would be the over-trust in apparent simplicity the tenet warns against; the careful experiments Crucianelli and Ehrsson call for are needed precisely because the simple surface hides a dual structure.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]]** and **[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]]** enter only obliquely: no thermal datum establishes the quantum-interface mechanism or bears on branching; the dependence runs from the framework to the reading of the data, not the reverse.

The Map does not claim thermoception settles dualism. It claims this modality is evidence any adequate theory must accommodate, and that it extends the modality survey in two directions its companions leave open: a phenomenal character whose value is visibly indexed to the subject's own state—the survey's cleanest case of Cabanac's alliesthesia—and a sense that straddles the exteroceptive/interoceptive boundary the cross-modal asymmetry presupposes, a straddle the Map reads as a gradient. A framework shaped by the object-presenting senses looks incomplete until it can represent a modality whose felt content is value relative to need.
