Hempel's dilemma is a challenge to defining "physical" by reference to physics. On the reconstruction in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP), physicalism stated over today's physics is false, because no one thinks contemporary physics is complete. Stated over a future or ideal physics it is "trivial", because no one can say what that physics will contain, and it might contain mental items. The conclusion is that there is no concept of the physical "clear enough to do the job that philosophers of mind want the physical to play" (Stoljar 2026, §4.3). The dilemma tells against *theory-relative definitions* of "physical" and nothing more. It is no evidence for dualism, for categorical natures that physics leaves out, or against causal closure. Barbara Montero, The Unfinishable Map's main source on it, judges that "this dilemma, alone, is not fatal to definitions of the physical in terms of physics" (Montero 2003, n. 25).

The Map has a stake of its own. Its [[tenets#^dualism|Dualism tenet]] posits something beyond the material that "cannot be fully explained by physics alone". That wording is physics-relative, so it inherits the second horn: if a future physics wrote consciousness into its laws, the wording would count the Map's dualism false in a world that matches its interactionism. How to read the tenet in that light is an open question, taken up in [[#site-perspective|Relation to Site Perspective]] (below).

## The Dilemma Stated {#dilemma}

Montero's statement of the dilemma keeps that shape but gives different reasons. Physicalism defined by current physics is "a theory we cannot defend", since a particle discovered next week would not count as physical. Defined by future physics it is "impossibly vague; for who knows what future physics will bring" (Montero 2003). Firt, Hemmo and Shenker (2021) compress the result: "the thesis of physicalism is either false or empty".

## Where the Dilemma Came From {#origin}

Carl Hempel raised the problem in 1969 and again in 1980, in comments on Nelson Goodman's *Ways of Worldmaking*; both texts are described here at second hand. Balázs Gyenis (2025) argues that Hempel's argument "was neither mounted against a metaphysical thesis" nor aimed at fixing what is physical by choosing a theory of physics. Its target was linguistic physicalism: the logical empiricists' claim, associated with Carnap and Neurath, that the language of physics could serve as a unitary language of science. [[logical-behaviourism]] covers that programme.

The metaphysical version is a later adaptation, which Gyenis notes is usually credited to Crane and Mellor (1990) and Melnyk (1997). Crane and Mellor argued that physicalism "lacks a clear and credible definition, and that in no non-vacuous interpretation is it true". They criticise physicalism's formulation and defend no dualism. Alyssa Ney (2008b) calls the problem "an important puzzle now frequently referred to as 'Hempel's Dilemma'". Who first gave it that name is unsettled, and this article credits the label to no one.

## Three Charges in the Second Horn {#second-horn}

The first horn is clear. The second carries three different charges.

- **Indeterminacy.** A future-physics physicalism has no content we can fix now. Melnyk makes determinable content a condition on any formulation, and Montero's "impossibly vague" belongs here.
- **Triviality.** If "physical" means whatever the final physics posits, physicalism is true whatever the world contains. The SEP's "trivial" presses this charge, as do Crook and Gillett (2001), following Chomsky, and Crane and Mellor.
- **Admitting fundamental mentality.** Jessica Wilson, herself a physicalist, argues that the first two are not genuine worries, because "physics" names a scientific theory of effectively fundamental entities. The real worry is that a future physics might posit fundamentally mental entities or forces, so a physics-relative definition cannot keep physicalism opposed to dualism (Wilson 2006).

A vague thesis is not thereby trivial. A thesis that admits mental items is neither, but it is no longer physicalism in the sense the mind–body debate needs. An argument that uses the dilemma has to say which charge it means.

## Six Responses and Their Costs {#responses}

Each response buys a definition at a price. None refutes the dilemma, and the dilemma refutes none of them.

### Take the First Horn

Andrew Melnyk (1997) defines the physical by current physics and asks that physicalism so defined be held, like any scientific theory, as more probable than its relevant rivals rather than as probably true. The SEP objects that whether a property is physical should not depend on which physics happens to be true: impetus physics is false, yet impetus is a physical property. Wilson objects that since the Standard Model and general relativity are mutually inconsistent, current physics is strictly false, and a false theory cannot sensibly be called more probable than its rivals.

### The Object-Based Conception

Daniel Stoljar (2001) named the distinction between theory-based and object-based conceptions of the physical. The object-based conception itself is older; the SEP cites Meehl and Sellars, and Feigl, as its best examples. On it, a physical property is "the sort of property had by paradigmatic physical objects and their constituents". Its cost is the panpsychism problem: if the paradigm objects were conscious through and through, physicalism so defined could still come out true. It also counts the categorical properties of physical objects as physical, so that "physical" and "structural" come apart ([[intrinsic-nature]]).

Meehl and Sellars (1956), the SEP's first example, drew a contrast of their own: an entity is physical₁ "if it belongs in the space-time network", and physical₂ if it is definable in primitives adequate to describe the actual states (not necessarily the potentialities) of the universe "before the appearance of life". Feigl (1958) took over the terms but glossed physical₁ as "practically synonymous with 'scientific'" (p. 377); the SEP relates that notion to the theory-based conception. Sellars later classed sensa, recast as absolute sensory processes, as physical₁ but not physical₂ ([[sellars-manifest-and-scientific-images]]).

### The Via Negativa and No Fundamental Mentality

David Spurrett and David Papineau (1999) answered the dilemma by defining "physical" as "non-mental", turning causal closure into closure of the non-mental. Montero (1999) suggested replacing the appeal to the physical with an appeal to the fundamentally non-mental. The label appears in print at least by the title of Gillett and Witmer (2001), a critique. Wilson calls her refinement the "no fundamental mentality" constraint, as the SEP reports. Justin Tiehen (2016), another physicalist, defines the physical as "neither fundamentally mental nor possibly realized by a fundamentally mental property".

The SEP lists the costs. Élan vital is neither mental nor physical, and an identity theory makes pain both. A world whose fundamental properties are both mental and physical "doesn't seem to be impossible", yet the constraint rules it out as a matter of "the definition of the words". Gillett and Witmer add that an appeal to completed physiology brings the dilemma back.

### Structuralism

Structuralists restrict physical theory to logical, mathematical, causal and nomological vocabulary (Alter 2016; the SEP also names Chalmers and Goff). The SEP grants that this "provides an answer to Hempel's dilemma" but finds it "overly stringent", since physics speaks of mass and electrons, not only topic-neutral structure. The proponents are anti-physicalists, and on this definition their structure-and-dynamics argument says physicalism is false. The determinate definition and the refutable physicalism arrive together.

### Physicalism as an Attitude

Ney (2008a) treats physicalism as an attitude: in the SEP's quotation, "an attitude one takes to form one's ontology completely and solely according to what physics says exists". Bas van Fraassen (2002), an empiricist critic, argues that materialism is a stance. The SEP replies that the attitude is neither necessary (Democritus had no physics to defer to) nor sufficient (Ney's own year-3000 scenario, discussed in Relation to Site Perspective). The dilemma also recurs over which physics the attitude defers to.

### Deflation

Stoljar replies that the dilemma shows, "if it shows anything", that "a particular proposal about how to define a physical property … is mistaken". We understand many concepts we cannot analyse (Stoljar 2026, §4.3; Stoljar 2010, ch. 5). The cost, conceded in the same section, is that the theory conception still needs to know "what type of theory a physical theory is", and whether a family-resemblance answer suffices "remains to be seen".

## Does the Dilemma Bind the Dualist? {#dualist-version}

Firt, Hemmo and Shenker (2021), none of whom writes as a dualist, argue that the dilemma "equally applies to any theory that gives a deep-structure and changeable account of our experience". In particular, "it also applies to mind–body dualistic theories", being "a special case of a general sceptical argument against changeable deep-structure theories".

Buzaglo (2024) replies, as his title says, that the dilemma challenges physicalism rather than dualism. On his abstract's account, a general-sceptical construal resting on the pessimistic induction reaches every changeable deep-structure theory. A non-sceptical construal resting on the instability of current physics reaches theories with exclusionary ambitions. Dualism largely escapes both, he argues, because theories of the mental are stable and non-exclusionary.

Gyenis takes a third line. His "empirical structure physicalism", on which the current special sciences supervene on current and future physics in a defined empirical sense, is meant to avoid both horns. He grants that it is logically compatible with metaphysical dualism but holds that believing both runs against an "Occam's Contrastive Razor": low confidence in an entity present in one region and absent from another when no current empirical theory asserts it, or when every theory that does gives both regions the same empirical content.

## What the Dilemma Cannot Do {#limits}

Three inferences from the dilemma fail.

- **To dualism.** If physicalism could not be well stated, whether consciousness is fundamental would be unchanged. A badly formulated rival is not a refuted one.
- **To quiddities or permanent ignorance.** On the object-based conception the categorical natures of physical things count as physical, so the dilemma cannot show that physics omits something non-physical.
- **Against closure.** Closure stated over current physics is probably false, but for reasons unrelated to mind, such as physics' incompleteness about gravity and dark matter. That falsity gives dualism nothing, because the missing causes could be non-mental. The live question is whether the *non-mental* is closed. Montero and Papineau (2005) defend the inductive case that it is, from empirical research's failure to find sui generis mental causes, against the dilemma only; vindicating the induction itself, they write, would be "a substantial empirical task". Montero alone (2003) had found it "not entirely clear" that lack of evidence for the fundamentally mental is evidence against it. The Map denies that the non-mental is closed, and that is a determinate thesis for evidence to decide ([[causal-closure]]; [[causal-closure-debate-historical-survey]]).

## Relation to Site Perspective {#site-perspective}

**Dualism takes the second horn.** The [[tenets#^dualism|Dualism tenet]] says consciousness "is not reducible to physical processes" and posits something "that cannot be fully explained by physics alone". Over current physics the second clause is true but weak, since current physics fails to explain much that nobody thinks non-physical. Over a future physics it is exposed. Ney's scenario, as quoted in the SEP, is the case: "it is the year 3000 AD and physicists have been forced to introduce irreducible mental entities into their theory". So are consciousness-collapse dynamics ([[testing-consciousness-collapse]]) and Chalmers's fourth route out of type-C materialism, a physics that appeals to consciousness itself (Chalmers 2003; [[type-a-type-b-and-type-c-physicalism]]). On a theory-based reading, the tenet's wording would place consciousness inside physics and deny the Map's dualism in exactly the world its interactionism describes.

One repair comes from Montero. She holds that "what is at stake in the debate over the mind-body problem" is "whether the mental is a fundamental feature of the world", which she offers as a framing of the question, not a case for dualism. Stated that way, as the claim that consciousness is a fundamental, non-derivative feature of reality that makes a difference to physical outcomes, the Map's thesis is determinate whatever physics becomes. It is also the contrast Wilson's constraint exists to protect. Whether Tenet 1 should be read, or restated, in those terms is an open question for the Map.

The reading has costs. It classifies panpsychism and emergentism as non-physicalist, which the Map can accept ([[panpsychism]]). It also excludes by definition a world whose fundamental properties are both mental and physical, where dual-aspect and Russellian views live ([[russellian-monism]]). Used honestly, the constraint classifies those views without refuting them.

**The interface does not share bare dualism's escape.** This is the Map's own analysis, drawn by none of the authors cited here. Buzaglo's argument, if it succeeds, spares a dualism whose theories of the mental are stable and non-exclusionary, and the Map's interface claims are neither. Outcome selection at quantum indeterminacy, and the [[psychophysical-laws]] proposed for it, tie the Map to the least settled part of physics, the measurement problem. The interface also has exclusionary ambitions: no strong closure, no many worlds. Bare dualism stated over fundamental mentality may escape the dilemma, while the interface hypotheses face its general-sceptical version as much as any physical theory does. That cost is acceptable because the tenets hold the interface as revisable. [[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]] asks for "the smallest deviation from standard quantum mechanics", using current physics as a benchmark without currentism about the physical.

**Bidirectional Interaction and closure.** At a quantum indeterminacy physics fixes chances, not outcomes, so the Map's selection escapes closure stated as sufficiency. Closure stated as chance-fixing survives wherever the Born measure is exactly preserved, and whether settling an outcome without shifting its chance counts as causing it is part of the Map's open [[positions/quantum-interface#^mechanism-debt|mechanism debt]]. [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] fits this picture, and the dilemma gives it no evidence. Gyenis's razor presses at the same joint. Under unconditioned aggregate tests the Map concedes that selection is indistinguishable from chance, which is the case the razor targets. [[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]] denies that such razors reliably track truth when knowledge is incomplete, but it binds the Map's own parsimony arguments too. The better answer is a deviation conditioned on intention or task, which would differ in empirical content and has not yet been tested at the neural interface.
