Colour is the case the secondary-quality distinction was built around, and the ontology of colour is the cleanest test of what that distinction leaves behind. Every live theory of colour must say where the intrinsic quality — the simple, sensuous redness a tomato seems to have — goes. Reductive physicalism identifies colour with reflectance and hands the quality to a separate theory of experience. Relationalism and dispositionalism define colour by a perceiver-side relatum, and that relatum is a colour experience. Eliminativism and projectivism say outright that the quality is instantiated by nothing and lives in experience alone. Realist primitivism alone keeps the quality in the object, and can do so only by rejecting the premise that physics inventories every property a body has. The result — the Map's reading of the survey, not a finding the colour literature reports — is that the residue the [[galilean-exclusion]] points to is invariant across colour ontologies. Four of five theories put it in experience, which is the [[qualia]] question under another name; the fifth moves it back into the world by denying the exclusion's own premise. No colour ontology dissolves it.

The epistemic status of this claim belongs beside it. The sorting is compatible with the Map's [[tenets#^dualism|first tenet]] and offers no discriminator in its favour: a physicalist theory of experience and a primitivist theory of objects predict the same sorting. Primitivism is the live rival reading, [[#primitivism|treated below]] rather than set aside; and the Map's existing concession that colour's relational *structure* is objectively investigable stands, with a twist developed [[#structure|below]].

## Five Ontologies and One Question {#five-ontologies}

Maund's survey in the *Stanford Encyclopedia* sorts the field into primitivism (realist and eliminativist), reductive physicalism, dispositionalism, projectivism, subjectivism and hybrid views (Maund 2024, §1.5). The Map puts one question to each family: once the theory has said what colour is, where has the quality gone?

### Reductive physicalism

Byrne and Hilbert defend "the view that physical objects (for instance, tomatoes, radishes, and rubies) are colored, and that colors are physical properties, specifically, types of reflectance" (Byrne & Hilbert 2003, abstract). Where does the quality go? Nowhere in colour. The felt redness is handled by a distinct theory of experience — in Byrne and Hilbert's case a representationalist one, which Chalmers describes as a "Russellian physicalism about phenomenal content" (Chalmers 2006, n. 12) and which the Map treats under [[first-order-representationalism]]. The colour thesis and the experience thesis are separable, and only the second conflicts with the Map.

### Relationalism

Cohen holds that "colors are constituted in terms of relations between objects, perceivers, and viewing conditions", and specifically that "colors are identical to certain functional roles involving perceptual effects on subjects" (Cohen 2009, abstract). His Master Argument reaches this from perceptual variation: an ecumenical treatment of the variants is preferable to an unmotivated stipulation in favour of one (Cohen 2009, p. 24, as reported in Maund 2024, §2.6). Where does the quality go? Into the perceiver-side relatum. It is a relation *to a colour experience*, and relationalism is a theory of colour rather than of that experience. The survey names the residue exactly: "On the face of it, only two candidates remain: Color Relationalism and Color Irrealism", and "The crucial issue between these two theories, it would seem, is whether Color Relationalism can account adequately for the phenomenology of visual experience" (Maund 2024, §2.6).

### Dispositionalism

Colours as "powers to appear in distinctive ways to perceivers (of the right kind), in the right kind of circumstances" (Maund 2024, §2.5) is the form the secondary-quality doctrine originally took; the disposition-versus-causal-basis fork is already at work in Boyle, Locke and Reid (Bolton 2022, §§4.2, 5.2.1, 6.1). Where does the quality go? Into the "appear red" clause: a disposition is individuated by its manifestation, and the manifestation is a colour experience. Johnston (1992) pays openly. His constraints on any account of colour include Revelation — that a standard visual experience fully reveals the intrinsic nature of the colour seen — and Maund records that Johnston concedes dispositionalism has difficulty meeting it "but, he thinks, giving this up is a small price to pay" (Maund 2024, §2.5). Dispositionalism was the exclusion's first vehicle rather than an alternative to it.

### Eliminativism and projectivism

Hardin (1988), Maund (1995) and Pautz (2006, 2020) hold that the properties colour experience presents objects as having are instantiated by nothing. The survey is candid that "the Eliminativist is a Color Primitivist, but of a different sort from Realist Primitivists", so that "Most versions of Color Eliminativism/Irrealism commit one to an error theory of visual experience" (Maund 2024, §2.3). Where does the quality go? Explicitly into experience: projectivism posits subjective qualities belonging to the visual experience itself, which the visual system attributes to objects. The survey then splits subjectivism into "(i) dualist subjectivism, according to which subjective qualities are irreducible to physical properties" and a form on which they may be identical to physical properties of the brain (Maund 2024, §2.4). That split is the Map's residue question posed about experience rather than colour: eliminativism about colour is the Galilean position itself, and the tenets answer the second fork.

### Realist primitivism {#primitivism}

Realist primitivism — Hacker (1987), Campbell (1993), McGinn (1996), Allen (2016), Gert (2017) — holds that colours are "simple intrinsic, non-relational, non-reducible, qualitative properties" of bodies (Maund 2024, §2.1), "sui generis properties of objects, irreducible to physical or dispositional properties" (Gert 2017, abstract). Campbell states the argument he resists in three lines: "Physics tells us what is objectively there. It has no place for the colours of things. So colours are not objectively there" — hence colour, if there is such a thing, is mind-dependent (Campbell 1993, abstract). That is the Galilean exclusion written as a syllogism, and Campbell's target is its first premise: the assumption "that the view of colour as mind-independent has been refuted by science". Where does the quality go? It stays in the object — the one ontology on which the quality survives in the world rather than the mind. The price is the completeness premise: Chalmers notes that a primitivist may answer the arguments against instantiation "by accepting that physicalism about ordinary objects is false" (Chalmers 2006).

## The Sorting Result {#sorting}

Set the five answers side by side and a pattern appears that no single theory states, because each is a theory of colour and the pattern concerns experience. Four of five accounts leave the quality in experience — the territory the [[qualia]] page maps and the [[phenomenal-concepts-strategy]] contests — and the fifth refuses the relocation by denying the premise that justified it. The intermediate positions the Map's critics have urged on it relocate the residue rather than remove it, relationalism with unusual precision, since the survey makes the phenomenology of visual experience the crucial issue in its contest with irrealism.

The Revelation debate makes the same point from the inside. Byrne and Hilbert (2007) use it against primitivism; its defenders keep a moderate form (as reported in Maund 2024, §§1.4, 2.1). What no party disputes is that a qualitative character is *given* in experience; they disagree about whether the giving is veridical. The residue is common ground and only its address is in dispute. The survey's recorded criticism of primitivism — that its arguments may depend "on a questionable form of the doctrine of Revelation" (Maund 2024, §2.1) — doubts whether the given character licenses a conclusion about objects, not whether there is a given character.

## Eden as the Formal Statement {#eden}

Chalmers's "Perception and the Fall from Eden" gives the relocation its most exact expression, in the vocabulary of perceptual content. What colour experience presents, he argues, is primitivist in form: the apparent redness seems to be "a simple qualitative property, with a distinctive sensuous nature", which he names "perfect redness: the sort of property that might have been instantiated in Eden" (Chalmers 2006). He then holds that "There is good reason to believe that the relevant primitive properties are not instantiated in our world", and builds a two-stage view on which "our experience presents an Edenic world, and thereby represents an ordinary world". His conclusion is that "the most fundamental content of perceptual experience is its Edenic content".

Chalmers's footnotes locate the view within the sorting: he records the primitivist split over whether the primitive properties are instantiated, and allows that Byrne and Hilbert's Russellian physicalism "may be particularly close to this view" (Chalmers 2006, nn. 4, 12). The paper is deliberately neutral on the metaphysics of phenomenal character. The exclusion left perfect redness out of the object; Eden says it is still what experience presents; the open question is what a presenting of an uninstantiated quality *is*. That question belongs to the [[qualia]] and [[phenomenal-concepts-strategy]] debates, and the bridge from Chalmers's content thesis to a phenomenal residue is one the Map builds, not one the paper asserts.

## Structure Is Tractable; Quality Is Not {#structure}

The [[galilean-exclusion]] page concedes that colour as a relation between surface and visual system is a legitimate scientific target, measured by psychophysics and colorimetry for a century; the concession is correct. What the colour literature adds is that the same measurable structure cuts against reflectance physicalism as sharply as against any dualist residue. Pautz's structure argument (Pautz 2006) holds that the similarity structure of the colours as experienced has no counterpart in the structure of reflectances. Kuehni's comparison of unique-hue data from "ten different experiments involving nearly 600 observers" found that "one observer's unique blue can be another's unique green and vice versa, and the same for yellow and green" (Kuehni 2004, abstract) — the empirical basis on which "Cohen and Hardin argue that there is no non-arbitrary way to pick out one group of perceivers as identifying the 'real' color" (Maund 2024, §2.1).

Gert draws the same lesson from the primitivist side: his objective colours are "insufficiently determinate in their nature to allow them to be associated with precise points in standard color spaces", and instead "standard color spaces are appropriate for the description of color appearances" (Gert 2017, abstract). A physicalist critic and a primitivist defender thus agree that the structure colorimetry measures is the structure of appearance. Structure being tractable does not make the quality that carries the structure tractable — the split the [[primary-secondary-quality-boundary]] page calls structure described against quality undergone.

## Objections {#objections}

**Primitivism at its strongest.** Hacker's reply to the causal-role objection is that colours are no worse placed than solidity and liquidity (as reported in Maund 2024, §2.1). Campbell's diagnosis is that the mind-dependence of colour was assumed rather than shown, resting on the treatment of physics's inventory as complete — an assumption the Map's own [[galilean-exclusion]] page calls a posit rather than a discovery. If the Map may reject the completeness premise for experience, why not for objects?

The Map's reply concedes the shared premise and disputes the address. Both deny that physical description exhausts the properties there are; they disagree about whether the surplus belongs to the tomato or to the experience of it. Three considerations tell against the object address without settling the matter. If the same chip is unique blue for some normal observers and unique green for others, a determinate sui-generis property in the chip is hard to reconcile with the data — which is why Gert retreats to indeterminate objective colours, conceding much of what the sorting claims. Primitivism makes physicalism about ordinary objects false, on Chalmers's reading, a larger revision than the Map's. And primitivism does not remove the experience-level question: a veridical experience of a primitive quality is still an experience with a qualitative character, and what that is remains to be said. These are costs rather than refutations; the dispute is open, and primitivism remains the live alternative reading of the residue.

**The physicalist theory of experience.** The reply from the other direction grants the sorting and denies that it matters: a physicalist theory of experience then explains the relocated quality — Byrne and Hilbert's representationalism, or the [[phenomenal-concepts-strategy]]. The reply is right about the sorting. The colour debate delivers the residue to the doorstep of the theory of experience and stops there; whether it survives physicalist treatment beyond that door is the hard-problem debate, on which the sorting takes no side. What the sorting rules out is the cheaper move the Map's critics had proposed — dissolving the residue with a theory of colour alone.

**Relationalism can answer the phenomenology.** Maund reports that Cohen has a reply to the phenomenological challenge, running parallel to Levin's defence of colour dispositionalism (Maund 2024, §2.6). The Map does not contest that reply here; it notes where any such reply must be made. A reply about the phenomenology of visual experience is a claim about experience, and a relationalism that succeeds by it has detached the phenomenology of colour experience from the ontology of colour — the separation the sorting result asserts.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The sorting result is compatible with the Map's [[tenets#^dualism|first tenet]] and gives it no independent support. A physicalist theory of experience predicts the same sorting the Map's dualism does, and a primitivist theory of objects predicts it too, with the quality returned to the world. Nothing in the colour literature discriminates among these three. The conclusion that the residue survives physicalist treatment is argued in the [[explanatory-gap]] and [[hard-problem-of-consciousness]] pages, and this page inherits rather than supplies it. Removing a proposed defeater — the suggestion that relational theories dissolve the residue — does not upgrade the residue into evidence for dualism; it restores the standing of a question the colour debate was never positioned to close.

[[tenets#^occams-limits|Tenet 5]] is engaged at one point. The Map reads Cohen's Master Argument as reaching relationalism by a parsimony-style judgement made under acknowledged incomplete knowledge of what fixes veridicality, and registers it as a reasonable procedural preference rather than a discovery about colour. The same limit binds the Map's own preference for the experience address over the primitivist's object address.
