The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*'s dualism entry records that "some distinguished neuroscientists, such as Charles Sherrington (1951), Wilder Penfield (1975) and John Eccles (1994) continued to defend dualism throughout the second half of the twentieth century." The lineage behind that sentence is genuine and unusually tight: Penfield trained under Sherrington at Oxford from 1915, and Eccles took his doctorate under Sherrington in 1929, co-authoring eight papers with him. Read carefully, though, the sentence flattens a gradient. Sherrington's *Man on His Nature*—his Gifford Lectures of 1937–38, published in 1940 and revised in 1951—defends an agnostic, epistemic dualism: natural science resolves the world into forms of energy, mind does not appear among them, and their relation remains bare correlation. He asserted a limit of natural science and stopped there. His students went further. Penfield built a clinical case for an interacting mind; Eccles built mechanisms for it, ending in quantum physics and, quite openly, in theology.

Two calibrations govern everything that follows, and they matter more than the lineage itself. First, the lineage is presented here as history—the provenance of images, data, and arguments the Map still draws on—never as an authority count: three eminent dissenters against a consensus is sociology, not evidence, and their eminence lay in synaptic physiology, not in adjudicating metaphysics. Second, a provenance caveat: no primary copy of Sherrington's texts could be directly consulted in preparing this article, so every Sherrington quotation is given as carried by the cited secondary sources. Relatedly, the two most-quoted "dualist Sherrington" sentences belong to his 1947 foreword to *The Integrative Action of the Nervous System*, not to *Man on His Nature*—a standing misattribution in the secondary literature that this article corrects.

## The Two-Ultimates Argument

Charles Scott Sherrington (1857–1952) coined "synapse," wrote the foundational *The Integrative Action of the Nervous System* (1906), shared the 1932 Nobel Prize with Edgar Adrian, and held the Waynflete Chair of Physiology at Oxford from 1913 to 1936. *Man on His Nature* closed his career: Gifford Lectures on natural theology's remit, staged as a running dialogue between modern physiology and the sixteenth-century physician Jean Fernel.

Its central argument starts from the physics of Sherrington's day, which resolved the natural world into forms of energy. Mind, he argued, is not found among them: "The energy-concept of Science collects all so-called 'forms' of energy into a flock and looks in vain for the mind among them" (as quoted in Pepperell 2018). He pressed the question whether mind is in any strict sense energy and answered that thoughts and feelings "are not amenable to the energy (matter) concept" (again as carried by Pepperell 2018). The thinking subject therefore confronts *two ultimates*—energy and mind—that natural science cannot reduce to one.

The companion claim concerns what science can say about their relation: nothing beyond correlation. In chapter 9 of the 1940 edition (p. 290, as located by quotation collections), Sherrington maintained that as followers of natural science we know of no relation between thoughts and the brain except a gross correlation in time and space. Mind, he wrote elsewhere in the book, goes in our spatial world "more ghostly than a ghost" (ch. 11, p. 357, same provenance caveat). No mechanism connects the two ultimates—on his verdict, not even the beginning of one.

The book also gave neuroscience its most famous image. Describing the waking brain, Sherrington pictured the head-mass becoming "an enchanted loom where millions of flashing shuttles weave a dissolving pattern, always a meaningful pattern though never an abiding one" (1940 ed., p. 225, as carried by quotation collections). He elsewhere characterised the brain as a million-fold democracy of cells (paraphrase; exact wording unverified)—integration without a central executive. The loom is an ancestor of the imagery the Map itself uses for the brain-side of the mind–brain relation, which is one reason the provenance of these lines is worth keeping straight.

## The 1947 Foreword and a Standing Misattribution

Sherrington's two most explicit late dualist sentences appear in the foreword, dated June 1947, that he wrote for the Physiological Society's reissue of *The Integrative Action of the Nervous System*. Secondary sources routinely cite them to *Man on His Nature*; they belong to the foreword.

The first is a parsimony refusal: that our being should consist of two fundamental elements offers, he supposed, no greater inherent improbability than that it should rest on one only (foreword, p. xx, as transcribed by quotation collections; punctuation varies across carriers). The sentence declines to let monist simplicity settle an open question—an early, clean statement of the discipline the Map's fifth tenet codifies.

The second is a concession stated at full strength: the relation of mind to brain remains "not merely unsolved, but still devoid of a basis for its very beginning" (as echoed across the scholarly literature). Sherrington was not claiming a mechanism and hedging it; he was asserting that no basis for a mechanism existed at all. Any interactionist descended from him inherits that gap as a debt.

## The Agnostic Pole: What Sherrington Did Not Claim

The verdict-direction of Sherrington's dualism is the point most often blurred, so it should be stated plainly: his position is agnostic and epistemic, not confessional or ontological. He asserted that natural science runs out—two ultimates, correlation only, no basis for a mechanism—and he declined to convert that limit into either a theology or an interaction theory. The book reads as reverent agnosticism throughout; the fair summary of his stance is that he regarded dualism as no less reasonable an assumption than monism, never as demonstrated.

In particular, Sherrington did not assert that mind causally steers the brain. The closest *Man on His Nature* comes to an efficacy claim is a chapter 6 remark (p. 201, aggregator-carried) that the influence of mind on the doings of life makes mind an effective contribution to life—but its context could not be checked against print, it may concern the evolutionary reality of mind rather than any mechanism of interaction, and nothing in this article rests on it.

This makes Sherrington the lineage's agnostic pole, and it opens a debate *within* the lineage that is usually papered over: does "science finds only correlation" license asserting a second, causally active element—or only suspension of judgment? Sherrington took the second option. His students took the first.

## The Lineage: One Teacher, Two Students, a Quantum Terminus

Sherrington's Oxford school produced a generation of eminent physiologists—John Fulton, Derek Denny-Brown, Howard Florey (Nobel 1945), Ragnar Granit (Nobel 1967) among them. Only some became public dualists. The dualist lineage is a strand within the school, not the school itself, and that distinction already deflates any argument from the school's collective prestige.

**Penfield is the clinical middle node.** A Rhodes Scholar from 1914, he arrived at Merton College in January 1915, was drawn to neurophysiology by Sherrington and William Osler, returned for a final Rhodes year as Sherrington's graduate student, and then turned to neurosurgery to study the living human brain. Six decades later, *The Mystery of the Mind* (1975)—the "Penfield (1975)" of the SEP sentence—argued from his cortical-stimulation series that the deciding, believing mind is not localizable to any stimulable site. Where the teacher had a conceptual gap, the student supplied clinical evidence; the Map's full treatment, including the comparator-model reply that keeps that evidence framework-relative, is at [[penfield-interactionist-dualism]].

**Eccles is the mechanistic terminus.** He came to Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar in 1925 specifically to work with Sherrington, served as his research assistant from 1928 to 1931, took his DPhil in 1929, and shared the 1963 Nobel Prize with Hodgkin and Huxley for work on the ionic mechanisms of synaptic excitation and inhibition. His interactionism escalated across four decades. Book-length statements began with his Waynflete Lectures, *The Neurophysiological Basis of Mind* (1953). *The Self and Its Brain* (Popper & Eccles 1977) argued for a self-conscious mind acting on the "liaison brain" within Popper's three-worlds scheme—Eccles described himself as having moved from dualist to "trialist." *How the Self Controls Its Brain* (1994)—the SEP's "Eccles (1994)"—paired each cortical *dendron* (a bundle of apical dendrites of pyramidal cells, some forty million of them) with a mental unit he called a *psychon*, with willed action raising the probability of synaptic vesicle release in the corresponding dendron and perception running the reverse way (details as carried by secondary transcriptions). The physics beneath this is the Beck–Eccles proposal (1992): a quantum-tunnelling model of exocytosis triggering on which a mental influence biases release *probability*, so that no energy is injected and no conservation law is violated.

Eccles also made the lineage self-conscious: with William Gibson he wrote the teacher's biography, *Sherrington: His Life and Thought* (1979). And he was candid about where his own dualism rested. In *Evolution of the Brain: Creation of the Self* (1989) he wrote that we are spiritual beings with souls existing in a spiritual world as well as material beings with bodies and brains (p. 241), and that each soul is a divine creation implanted in the growing fetus between conception and birth (p. 237)—passages cited here as transcribed in secondary discussions. These sentences are exactly what critics of the lineage point at, and they should be displayed rather than laundered.

Two consequences follow from displaying the gradient honestly. First, the lineage runs from agnostic limit-claim (Sherrington) through clinical interactionism (Penfield) to confessional, mechanised substance dualism (Eccles): the teacher's suspension of judgment became the student's confession, and citing "the lineage" as if it held one position misrepresents all three. Second, the Beck–Eccles mechanism and Eccles's theology are separable, and should be separated in both directions. The 1992 proposal is a physical model of probability-biasing at the synapse; nothing in it requires divinely created souls, so refuting the theology leaves the mechanism standing—and adopting the mechanism family, as the Map's quantum-interface programme in effect does, commits no one to the theology.

## The Critics at Full Strength

The dualist reading of this material was contested from the beginning, and the strongest replies deserve full statement.

**Ritchie (1941).** Reviewing *Man on His Nature* in *Nature*, A. D. Ritchie answered the two-ultimates argument with flat naturalism: "There does not appear to be anything incorporeal about living organisms as such"—life being special arrangements of ordinary physics and chemistry. The book's own first scientific audience declined the dualist verdict.

**Pepperell (2018).** The modern direct answer to the energy argument—a philosophical argument drawing on neuroscience, published in *Frontiers in Psychology*—accepts Sherrington's premise that mind is not a *form* of energy and blocks his conclusion: consciousness, Pepperell argues, is a physical process constituted by the *organization* of energy in the brain. If mind is energy's structure rather than a further item on energy's inventory, the two ultimates collapse into one, and the "looks in vain" observation loses its force.

**Smith (2001).** The historicising reply. Smith places Eccles in a Cartesian tradition running through British neurophysiology, on which the lineage's dualism was transmitted intellectual culture as much as individual conviction, and argues that Eccles's quantum-microphysical move did not solve the problem Descartes posed so much as relocate it. (Access caveat: Smith's argument is reported here from abstract and listing level; the full text was not read.)

**Arshavsky (2006).** Argues that a *covert* dualism—an unwillingness to treat consciousness as a tractable biological problem—pervades neuroscience well beyond the overt dualists, and treats dualism of both kinds as an obstacle to be overcome. The same access caveat applies, and a stronger framing often attributed to this paper—that the lineage's dualism had specifically religious roots—could not be confirmed at abstract level and is not asserted here.

The Map's replies to this family live elsewhere—the parsimony discipline against organization-style dissolutions in [[parsimony-case-for-interactionist-dualism]], and the interface-problem treatment of the "relocated, not solved" charge in [[the-interface-problem]]—but the critics' positions stand as stated, and Sherrington himself would have conceded the third and fourth critics' starting point: he said in 1947 that no basis for a mechanism existed.

## Is Distinguished Dissent Evidence?

No. The SEP sentence establishes that dualism retained scientifically literate, indeed eminent, defenders deep into the twentieth century—a fact about the sociology of the debate that corrects a common assumption that no serious neuroscientist ever held the view. It carries no evidential weight beyond that. Eminence in synaptic physiology confers no privileged access to metaphysics; the same school produced physicalists of equal eminence; and counting authorities is exactly the move the Map's own methodology disallows when it runs the other way.

The honest use of the lineage is as provenance. The enchanted loom is the ancestor of a family of brain-side images; Penfield's stimulation series is a real clinical dataset whose interpretation remains contested; the 1947 parsimony sentence is an argument that stands or falls on its content; Beck–Eccles is a mechanism proposal assessable on its physics. Each of these earns whatever weight it has independently of who produced it. That rule governs the Map's own citation of the SEP sentence in [[penfield-interactionist-dualism]] as much as anyone else's use of the lineage.

## Relation to Site Perspective

Sherrington's two-ultimates argument is an ancestor of the Map's first tenet, and the relation should be stated without conscription. His verdict was epistemic—natural science finds two irreducibles and no bridge—whereas the Map's Dualism tenet is an ontological commitment. The Map goes beyond Sherrington, and it does not claim him for the further step: on the evidence surveyed here he asserted the limit and suspended judgment.

The 1947 foreword sentence is the cleanest early anticipation of **Tenet 5** (Occam's razor has limits): a refusal to let monist parsimony decide a question the evidence leaves open. This is also the Map's principled reply to Pepperell-style dissolutions—the organization-of-energy reading is simpler, but by Tenet 5 its simplicity is not by itself a reason to believe it, a point developed in [[parsimony-case-for-interactionist-dualism]].

On **Tenets 2 and 3** Sherrington is silent, and the Map records that silence rather than papering over it. He asserted no interaction; his "devoid of a basis for its very beginning" names precisely the gap the Map's quantum-interface programme works on. The mechanism side of the Map's framework descends instead from the lineage's terminus: Beck–Eccles probability-biasing at the synapse is the direct ancestor of Tenet 2's minimal quantum interaction. The Map adopts that mechanism family minimally—stripped of psychon bookkeeping and entirely decoupled from Eccles's theology, which the tenets neither need nor assert. The Map's architecture is fairly summarised as taking Sherrington's gap seriously and Eccles's mechanism minimally.

Finally, the lineage confers no authority on any of this. Following [[evidential-status-discipline]], the Map treats Sherrington, Penfield, and Eccles as sources of arguments and data to be assessed, never as a roll-call of eminent support—and it accepts the mirror-image discipline: the far longer roll-call of eminent physicalists is not evidence against the tenets either.
