Between 1927 and 1940 four writers built the first "quantum free-will" position and a dozen contemporaries wrote against or around it. Ralph Lillie (1927) proposed that the nervous system amplifies quantum indeterminacy into voluntary action; Arthur Eddington (1928, 1935) announced that physics had withdrawn its "moral opposition to free-will"; Arthur Compton (1931, 1935) built the photocell-and-dynamite apparatus that made amplification vivid; Pascual Jordan (1932–1938) turned it into a theory of organisms. Three objections were on the table by 1936, each with a first stater: the magnitude objection (Eddington himself, in 1928, against the few-atom "switch"), the randomness objection (Schlick in 1930 in generic form, Schlick in 1935 against Jordan in quantum form, Schrödinger in 1936 as a physicist's dilemma) and the interface objection (Jeans, 1933). Page locators are checked against the raw texts where they could be reached, and figures known here only through carriers are marked.

For the Map the finding is uncomfortable in a useful way: its selection posit and the strongest objection to it, [Schrödinger's 1936 dilemma](#randomness-objection), are both older than it is. Eddington's 1928 chapter draws the line the Map's [[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]] tenet draws, between directing one atom among outcomes "neither of which would be improbable" and steering "a crowd of atoms", and then takes the horn the Map declines (see [the magnitude objection](#magnitude-objection), explained below). None of the four builders is a witness for the Map's dualism, and the critics name debts the Map still carries (see [Relation to Site Perspective](#relation-to-site-perspective)).

## The Wave {#the-wave}

The physiological amplifier image is Lillie's. His *Science* paper of August 1927 is known to the Map only through later carriers (Compton's 1931 citation of it, Popper's note 13, Davis 2009), and every carrier agrees it was in print before Eddington's book and four years before Compton's note. Compton's contribution was the apparatus and the extension. In *Science* (August 1931) and in *The Freedom of Man* (1935, the Terry Lectures of November 1931) he imagined a photon diffracted toward one of two amplifier-wired photocells, one firing dynamite and the other opening a cut-out switch, and argued, citing Lillie, that deliberate acts are events of the same kind. He added, in 1931, that the apparatus does not by itself show organisms free, and in 1935 "very tentatively accepted" a selector, minds acting "by influencing or selecting some quantum jumps" (Popper's report, p. 233). The Map's fuller account of Compton is in [[popper-three-worlds-interactionism#penny-toss|the penny-toss section of the Popper article]]; no Compton page was read in the primary for either article.

Eddington gave the wave its public form. Chapter XIV of *The Nature of the Physical World* (1928) announces that "science thereby withdraws its moral opposition to free-will" (p. 295) and defines volition inside indeterminism: if the laws are not strictly causal, the conscious brain's behaviour is one of a mechanical brain's possible behaviours, "and the decision between the possible behaviours is what we call volition" (p. 311). In the same breath he refused to find volition in the atom: an atom deciding between quantum jumps is "scarcely" volition, "the analogy is altogether too remote" (pp. 311–312). *New Pathways in Science* (1935) restates the position with the volume turned down: "Although the door of human freedom is opened, it is not flung wide open; only a chink of daylight appears" (p. 87). Anyone who says the indeterminist attributes something like free will to atoms is answered flatly: "I deny this altogether. We do not discard one rash generalisation only to fall into another equally rash" (p. 86).

Jordan's *Verstärkertheorie* ("amplifier theory"), stated in *Die Naturwissenschaften* in 1932 and restated in 1938, held that organisms amplify single quantum events in control centres into macroscopic behaviour; his 1934 *Erkenntnis* paper extended it to psychology and the will and offered it, in Mach's name, as positivism. None of Jordan's physiology was read for this article, so his content is given from titles and Howard's summary only. Jeans belongs to the cautious wing: *The New Background of Science* (1933) allows that "crevices have begun to appear" in the closed cycle of physics (p. 280) and goes no further, for a reason given [below](#interface-and-relocation).

## Eddington Against the Switch {#magnitude-objection}

The sharpest early objection to amplification came from inside the wave, in the chapter that launched it, three years before Compton's reply. Eddington considered "a possible though difficult hypothesis": that "very few atoms (or possibly only one atom)" have direct contact with the conscious decision and "serve as a switch to deflect the material world from one course to the other", and judged it "physically improbable that each atom has its duty in the brain so precisely allotted that the control of its behaviour would prevail over all possible irregularities of the other atoms" (pp. 312–313). So, it seems, "we must attribute to the mind power not only to decide the behaviour of atoms individually but to affect systematically large groups—in fact to tamper with the odds on atomic behaviour", which "has always been one of the most dubious points in the theory of the interaction of mind and matter" (p. 313).

He then faced the cost. Granting the mind "power to set aside statistical laws which hold in inorganic matter" is needed if its interference is to produce what mental decisions observably produce, "but the admission involves a genuine physical difference between inorganic and organic (or, at any rate, conscious) matter. I would prefer to avoid this hypothesis, but it is necessary to face the issue squarely" (p. 313). The hypothesis "that the mind operates through two or three key-atoms in the brain is too desperate a way of escape for us, and I reject it for the reasons already stated. It is one thing to allow the mind to direct an atom between two courses neither of which would be improbable for an inorganic atom; it is another thing to allow it to direct a crowd of atoms into a configuration which the secondary laws of physics would set aside as 'too improbable'" (p. 314). His positive posit is unity rather than amplification: in the brain region a decision affects there is "some kind of interdependence of behaviour of the atoms which is not present in inorganic matter", so the statistics of that region need not "agree with those appropriate to structures of haphazard origin" (pp. 314–315).

Seven years later the position is restated, not softened. Attributing bodily movement to "the 'trigger action' of the unpredetermined behaviour of a few key atoms in our brain cells" would make the problem simple, "but it would be rash to suppose that the physical controlling cause is contained in the configuration of a few dozen atoms" (1935, p. 88). He conjectures that the smallest unit in which volition's physical effects originate "contains many billions of atoms", that an inorganic system of that mass would have too little indeterminacy for appreciable freedom, and that the "conscious unit" differs from an inorganic system "in having a much higher indeterminacy of behaviour—simply because of the unitary nature of that which in reality it represents, namely the Ego" (p. 88). In the conscious cell "the behaviour symbolises a single volition of the spirit and not a conflict of billions of independent impulses" (p. 89). The reading in Davis (2009, n. 68) that Eddington stressed consciousness amplifying microscopic uncertainties is not supported by either chapter; in both he names the few-atom route in order to reject it. The Lillie–Compton reply, that the nervous system already amplifies, so no crowd needs steering, is the reply Eddington did not take.

## Randomness Is Not Freedom {#randomness-objection}

The objection the Map now calls the [[quantum-indeterminacy-free-will|luck objection]] has two separate datings in this period. The generic form is in Schlick's *Fragen der Ethik*, whose preface is signed in Vienna in September 1930, eleven months before Compton's note; the book never mentions quantum physics, Heisenberg, Compton or Eddington. In the English *Problems of Ethics* (1939): "Freedom means the opposite of compulsion; a man is free if he does not act under compulsion" (p. 150), and a decision "utterly without any cause" would be "entirely a matter of chance, for chance is identical with the absence of a cause; there is no other opposite of causality. Could we under such conditions make the agent responsible? Certainly not" (p. 156). R. E. Hobart (Dickinson S. Miller), in *Mind* for January 1934, argued that free will involves determination and is inconceivable without it; his text, read here only through the Information Philosopher's reproduction, contains no physics.

The quantum-specific form is Schlick's reply to Jordan, not to Compton or Eddington: a note in *Erkenntnis* 5 (1935), among the Vienna Circle's responses to Jordan's 1934 paper. This is the passage Popper quotes in "Of Clouds and Clocks", in his own translation, as freedom and responsibility stopping "where chance begins", with "a higher degree of randomness" meaning "a higher degree of irresponsibility" (Popper 1972, pp. 226–227, citing *Erkenntnis* 5, p. 183). The slogan that "the only alternative to determinism is just sheer chance" is Popper's gloss on Schlick and Hume, not Schlick's wording (p. 227); Schlick's own phrase is "there is no other opposite of causality".

Schrödinger supplied the physicist's version in *Nature* for 4 July 1936, prompted by F. G. Donnan's sketch of a physics of life rather than by Eddington or Compton, neither of whom the note names. As the Information Philosopher's reproduction carries it, Schrödinger calls the hope of founding free will on quantum indeterminacy an illusion and poses a dilemma: if the agent can alter the odds of the quantum event a decision rides on, the laws of nature are at the agent's mercy; if not, Heisenberg's principle fixes the frequency of the agent's acts and there is no reason to feel responsible. The first horn is the cost of Eddington's choice: a mind that tampers with the odds overrides the statistics.

Susan Stebbing's *Philosophy and the Physicists* (1937) reaches the same conclusion in the ordinary-language register and adds a charge of overreach. The book could be reached only as OCR fragments of the 1958 Dover reprint plus Davidson's 1942 digest, so her wording is not reproduced. Chapter X, "Human Freedom and Responsibility" (pp. 222–240), treats freedom as the opposite of compulsion, denies that all causation is a form of compulsion, and presses Eddington on how he knows what he claims to know by immediate self-knowledge. Davidson reports her charging the physicists with misleading theologians, preachers and philosophers who should have known better.

## Relocation and the Interface {#interface-and-relocation}

A third group of critics accepted none of the wave's framing. Russell's *The Scientific Outlook* (1931), reached here only through Eddington's quotations of it (1935, pp. 306–307), called Eddington's free-will argument one based on ignorance, weaker than the entropy argument for a Creator, based on knowledge, which Eddington declined. Eddington's reply fixes the scope of his claim: "I have not suggested that either religion or free will can be deduced from modern physics; I have limited myself to showing that certain difficulties in reconciling them with physics have been removed" (p. 306). Planck, in *Where Is Science Going?* (1932), kept causality universal, "even to the highest achievements of the human soul" (p. 155), and relocated freedom to the one point where causal research is inapplicable "not only on practical grounds but also on logical grounds": "This point is the individual ego" (p. 161). We could in principle trace the causes of our own conduct but never can in practice, "for no eye can see itself" (p. 163).

Jeans stated a different objection, the one the Map's interface programme inherits. Free will "lies beyond the province of physics" (p. 280), and even granting the crevices, "Whether the volitions of the human mind can pass through these and affect the operations of nature must in the last resort depend on whether the two are sufficiently alike to interact—a keyhole is useless unless we have a key of the same nature as the lock. It may still be, as Descartes maintained, that mind is too dissimilar from matter ever to be able to influence it" (p. 280).

Jordan drew his own critics. In *Erkenntnis* 5 (1935) Zilsel accused him of trying to rescue vitalism quantum-mechanically, Neurath wrote on Jordan, quantum theory and freedom of the will, Schlick added the note above, Frank asked whether modern physics showed a tendency toward a spiritualist outlook, and the plant physiologist Erwin Bünning asked whether organisms are microphysical systems at all; Jordan replied in the same volume.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Map's reading of this history is its own, and the four builders would not have signed it.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]].** Eddington's p. 314 sentence states the tenet's distinction a century early: influence confined to outcomes "neither of which would be improbable for an inorganic atom" against influence that steers "a crowd of atoms" into a configuration physics would set aside. He then chose the second, odds-tampering horn and paid its price, a "genuine physical difference" between conscious and inorganic matter. The Map's default reading takes the horn he did not: selection within the Born distribution, with the aggregate statistics exactly preserved. Schrödinger's 1936 dilemma shows what that choice buys and costs. The Map escapes his first horn as he stated it, since no publicly measurable odds are altered, at the price of making each outcome depend on the agent, which is what his second horn then asks about: a selection that leaves the statistics untouched must say what distinguishes it from chance. The Map records that as its [[positions/quantum-interface#^mechanism-debt|mechanism debt]] rather than conceding epiphenomenalism: the per-trial-versus-ensemble distinction and the indexical character of which outcome a subject actualises are its candidate answer, held as a framework-internal coherence result until a worked model exists ([[apex/born-preserving-causal-efficacy]]). The dilemma was stated and chosen on in 1928 and its cost named independently in 1936; the Map's position is the road not taken rather than a discovery.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]].** The wave supplies the opening the tenet uses and the critics supply the debt. Eddington's magnitude objection is why the Map pairs minimal interaction with neural amplification, the Lillie–Compton reply, and why the [[amplification-mechanisms-consciousness-physics|amplification problem]] and Popper's grain complaint remain open against it. Jeans's keyhole is the interface problem itself; the Map's answer, a non-physical selector acting where physics leaves outcomes open, meets his question rather than dissolving it, since "sufficiently alike to interact" is exactly what a selection posit has yet to show.

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]].** None of the four builders can be enlisted. Eddington's metaphysics is a structuralist idealism of "mind-stuff" in which the physical world is a symbolic representation; his conscious unit is a unity of that stuff, not a second substance acting on a first. Compton argued for the efficacy of consciousness from natural selection and placed the knowledge of freedom in inner awareness, outside what physics can decide. Jordan offered his amplifier theory as Machian positivism, which is why the Vienna Circle answered it in its own journal. Lillie's paper is unread here, and nothing its carriers report makes him a dualist. The Map takes from them a structure, selection among physically permitted outcomes, and an objection, and takes no authority.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]].** Schlick's "there is no other opposite of causality" is a definitional partition, and the Map rejects it with Popper, who called the thesis that nothing can lie between perfect chance and perfect determinism "clearly absurd" (p. 228). A dichotomy adopted for its tidiness, under incomplete knowledge of what a selecting mind would be, is no evidence that the middle is empty; rejecting it does not fill the middle, which is the debt above. Tenet 4 is not engaged by this history.
