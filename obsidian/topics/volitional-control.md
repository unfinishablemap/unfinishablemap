When you decide to raise your hand, something happens that neuroscience has spent four decades trying to explain away. A readiness potential appears in your motor cortex hundreds of milliseconds before you report the conscious intention to move. Since Benjamin Libet's landmark 1983 experiments, this temporal gap has been treated as evidence that consciousness arrives too late to cause anything — that the feeling of willing is an afterthought to neural processes already underway. The Unfinishable Map argues this conclusion was premature. Both the phenomenological structure of volitional experience and converging empirical evidence — clinical dissociations, brain stimulation, implicit timing measures, neuroplasticity — show that the sense of agency tracks identifiable causal architecture in the nervous system rather than being confabulated after the fact. That finding removes the principal empirical case for epiphenomenalism and constrains any adequate theory of agency. It does not by itself settle whether that architecture is physical or non-physical: the strongest current physicalist rival, predictive processing and active inference ([discussed below](#predictive-processing-the-strongest-physicalist-rival)), predicts the same evidence. The case for the Map's [[mental-causation-and-downward-causation|interface reading of conscious causation]] rests on its broader framework, not on this evidence taken alone.

## Reassessing the Libet Interpretation

Libet et al. (1983) asked subjects to flex their wrist whenever they felt like it while watching a clock. The readiness potential (RP) — a slow negative EEG shift in premotor cortex — began approximately 550 milliseconds before movement, while subjects reported conscious awareness of their intention only around 200 milliseconds before. The resulting 350-millisecond gap was widely interpreted as proof that the brain decides before consciousness knows about it.

This interpretation rested on treating the RP as an unconscious decision signal. Schurger, Sitt, and Dehaene (2012) undercut that assumption. Their accumulator model showed that the RP's shape and timing are exactly what would be expected from ongoing stochastic fluctuations in neural activity rather than from a goal-directed preparatory process: in Libet's paradigm — subjects must eventually move but choose the moment — premotor activity hovers near threshold, and random noise drifting toward that threshold determines when it is crossed. Their "Libetus interruptus" variant confirmed the prediction: when subjects were unexpectedly interrupted, neural activity was already near threshold on trials where they would have moved soon.

Sjöberg's 2024 essay in *Brain* on the Eccles–Libet debate points the same way from clinical data, though more narrowly than it might first appear. It reflects on his group's earlier surgical cohort — patients who underwent resection near the supplementary motor area (SMA), widely regarded as the generator of the readiness potential. Patients with transient post-operative akinesia reported still willing, and making full effort to move, even when initiation or execution failed; automatic movements like walking were comparatively spared. Three qualifications matter. First, this is a single essay reflecting on a prior cohort, not an independent replication. Second, Sjöberg is himself no dualist: he argues the resection findings show Libet's paradigm is largely *irrelevant* to the free-will question and that the philosophical issue cannot be settled from the clinical data — so the cases remove a defeater rather than confirming conscious causation. Third, SMA-complex lesions are not clean execution failures. Bala et al. (2025) document that they impair executive function — planning, initiation, inhibition, conflict resolution — and the SMA proper (motor execution) is functionally dissociable from the pre-SMA (higher-order control). Read carefully, the cases support only that reported effort can survive impairment of an SMA-linked execution gateway, so felt willing is not identical to activity in that one gateway. That the *feeling* of effort persists alongside degraded executive *capacity* is at least as readily read as phenomenology coming apart from the machinery of volition as it is read the Map's way.

Alfred Mele's philosophical critique reinforces this from a different angle. What subjects become aware of at the 200-millisecond mark may be an urge to flex that has already arisen, not a decision already made. More fundamentally, spontaneous wrist flicking in a laboratory bears almost no resemblance to voluntary action involving deliberation over minutes, hours, or days: Libet's experiments test impulse timing, not [[topics/free-will|free will]].

Braun, Wessler, and Friese's 2021 meta-analysis is more mixed than a single quotation conveys. Its headline finding is that the overall temporal pattern is "largely consistent with the one found by Libet and colleagues"; its caveat is that one crucial comparison rested on only six studies and carried "a high degree of uncertainty." The defensible reading is that a specific effect size is fragile, not that the Libet record has collapsed wholesale. What the 2020s literature has done is level the playing field — the eliminativist inference from RP timing is now underdetermined — rather than settle the question in any direction. Newer probe-based work sharpens the point without favouring either side: Gavenas, Schurger, and Maoz (2025) interrupted subjects at random and found that pre-probe RP buildup was *not* related to reported awareness of motor preparation, which instead tracked beta desynchronization over contralateral motor cortex. That cuts against the classical decision-signal reading of the RP, but it equally severs the RP from conscious intention — constraining the Map's picture as much as its opponents'. The temporal-ordering puzzle admits multiple interpretations; that several of them are compatible with the Map's framework is not, on its own, evidence for it.

## The Phenomenological Structure of Willing

The phenomenological tradition, particularly the work of Shaun Gallagher and Dan Zahavi, reveals an internal structure to voluntary action that simple timing studies cannot capture. (For extended treatment of the agency-passivity axis, see [[phenomenology-of-agency-vs-passivity|Phenomenology of Agency vs Passivity]].) Two components are distinguishable in first-person experience:

**Sense of ownership** — the feeling that *my body* is moving. This is present in both voluntary and involuntary movement. When a doctor taps your knee and your leg kicks, you experience ownership ("that's my leg") without agency.

**Sense of agency** — the feeling that *I am initiating* the action. This is present only in voluntary movement. When you deliberately raise your hand, ownership and agency coincide.

Gallagher further distinguishes motor-level agency (SAm), the feeling of controlling specific bodily movements, from intentional-level agency (SAi), the feeling of pursuing goals through action. The readiness potential relates to motor preparation; it says nothing about the intentional level where agents form plans, weigh reasons, and select among possible courses of action.

## Clinical Dissociations: Agency Tracks Causal Structure

The strongest empirical evidence that the sense of agency tracks real causal structure comes from clinical populations where agency and ownership dissociate.

**Schizophrenic delusions of control.** Patients with first-rank symptoms report that their bodies move but that someone or something else is causing the movements. Gallagher (2000) analysed this phenomenology: ownership remains intact — "this is my arm" — while agency vanishes — "I am not the one moving it." The comparator model developed by Frith (1992) and refined by Blakemore, Wolpert, and Frith (2002) explains why. Voluntary movement generates an efference copy predicting its own sensory consequences; when feedback matches prediction, the movement is experienced as self-generated. In schizophrenia the comparator malfunctions: predicted and actual feedback diverge, and the movement is attributed to an external source. The same attenuation mechanism explains why healthy subjects cannot tickle themselves (Blakemore, Wolpert, & Frith, 1998), and why patients with auditory hallucinations and passivity experiences fail to down-rate self-produced tactile stimulation the way controls do (Blakemore, Smith, Steel, Johnstone, & Frith, 2000). The effect is symptom-specific, not a blanket property of everyone with a schizophrenia diagnosis.

**[[anarchic-hand-and-action-ownership|Anarchic hand syndrome]].** Patients with medial frontal or callosal lesions experience a hand that performs goal-directed actions — reaching for objects, unbuttoning clothes — that the patient does not intend and cannot stop. Ownership is preserved: "that is my hand." Agency is absent: "I am not doing this." Della Sala, Marchetti, and Spinnler (1991) documented cases where the anarchic hand actively opposed the patient's voluntary actions with the other hand.

**Anosognosia for hemiplegia.** After right-hemisphere stroke, some patients deny that their paralysed limb is impaired, insisting they have just moved it when they have not (Berti et al., 2005). Here agency is falsely present while execution is absent — the mirror image of anarchic hand.

These three dissociations triangulate a common architecture:

| Condition | Ownership | Agency | Movement |
|-----------|-----------|--------|----------|
| Normal voluntary action | Present | Present | Present |
| Schizophrenic delusion of control | Present | Absent | Present |
| Anarchic hand | Present | Absent | Present |
| Anosognosia | Present | Falsely present | Absent |

If agency were mere confabulation — a story generated after every movement regardless of its cause — these selective dissociations would be unexplained. That tells against a crude narrator that indiscriminately attributes or denies agency; it does not tell against the sophisticated predictive and hierarchical-Bayesian models — the comparator model among them — built precisely to explain selective, context-sensitive agency errors. That agency tracks the integrity of specific circuits indicates causal architecture rather than narrative convenience. This constrains the space of viable accounts of agency, but no further: the comparator model doing the explaining is itself physicalist computational neuroscience, so the dissociations do not by themselves establish that the causal architecture is non-physical — a question taken up under [predictive processing](#predictive-processing-the-strongest-physicalist-rival) below.

## Brain Stimulation: Intention Without Execution

Desmurget et al. (2009) provided direct evidence that conscious intention and motor execution occupy distinct neural territory. During awake neurosurgery, electrical stimulation of the posterior parietal cortex produced conscious intentions to move — and at higher intensities, the false belief of having moved — without any muscle activity. Stimulation of premotor cortex produced actual movements that patients denied initiating.

This double dissociation is significant. If the experience of willing were simply the brain's interpretation of detecting its own motor output, stimulating the motor system should produce that experience. It does not. The two come apart cleanly.

What this refutes is the narrow view that the feeling of willing is nothing but the brain detecting its own completed muscle output. It does not, on its own, favour a non-physical interface. The parietal cortex is an integrative hub that multiplexes attention, sensory expectation, and motor planning, and a physicalist model can read the stimulation-induced "urge" as an artificially evoked sensory prediction with no movement to cancel it. The sample was also seven surgical patients, and direct stimulation disrupts tissue as much as it activates it. The clean dissociation is real and theory-constraining; it does not select between physicalism and dualism.

## Conscious Veto: The "Whether" Component

Libet himself recognized that his experiments did not eliminate conscious causal power. He proposed "free won't": the brain may initiate action unconsciously, but consciousness retains the power to veto prepared movements. Marcel Brass and Patrick Haggard (2007, 2008) provided neural evidence through their "what, when, whether" model of intentional action:

- **What** (which action to perform) — lateral prefrontal and premotor areas
- **When** (the timing of action) — supplementary motor area
- **Whether** (to act or refrain) — dorsal fronto-median cortex (dFMC)

The "whether" component is the most philosophically significant. When subjects intentionally inhibited a prepared action, Brass and Haggard observed specific activation in dFMC. This is a candidate correlate, identified from a correlational fMRI contrast, of the decision to withhold — not a demonstrated source exerting inhibition, and later work depicts inhibition as a distributed process spanning medial and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, right inferior frontal cortex, and pre-SMA. The activation is consistent with an active neural process of cancellation, with its own circuitry, rather than merely the absence of a go signal. In the [[control-theoretic-will|control-theoretic]] reading of will, this veto is an emergency-stop operation — a low-bandwidth signal that overrides a prepared trajectory — one of several distinct control operations consciousness performs over neural dynamics.

Critics have objected that the veto itself must be unconsciously initiated, generating an infinite regress. But this objection assumes what it needs to prove — that every neural event must have a prior neural cause sufficient to explain it. The Map's framework offers an alternative it hypothesises rather than something it has shown empirically: the "whether" decision may be where [[consciousness-selecting-neural-patterns|consciousness selects among neural patterns]], biasing quantum-indeterminate outcomes toward one resolution rather than another at the [[attention-as-interface|interface]] where conscious selection meets neural preparation. This is the *volitional modulation* mechanism in the Map's hypothesised four-mechanism architecture (see [[consciousness-and-causal-powers#Volitional Modulation]] for the framework-level calibration: the mechanism is offered at the speculative-integration evidential tier, with the underlying neural-timescale quantum-coherence requirement an open and contested empirical question). So read, the veto requires no prior unconscious veto. Identifying causal closure as the disputed premise reframes the regress rather than empirically refuting it, and the Map owes — and does not yet have — a test that would discriminate its interface reading from a physicalist account of the same inhibitory circuitry. For how this mechanism might work through the quantum Zeno effect applied to motor selection, see [[motor-control-quantum-zeno|Motor Control and the Quantum Zeno Framework]].

### What the Felt Data Can and Cannot Settle

A calibration boundary should be marked here, because volitional control is a prime site for an inference that runs faster than the evidence allows. The dissociation and stimulation data above constrain accounts of agency, but the felt phenomenology of willing does not by itself discriminate the interface reading from a physicalist one. The libertarian reading (a non-physical self initiating among open possibilities), the compatibilist reading (willing as a higher-order physical process), and the hard-determinist reading (the felt initiation as the brain's representation of a settled outcome) are very nearly empirically equivalent on the introspective data: each predicts that voluntary action *feels* self-initiated, effortful, and revisable. The move from "we experience willing" to "therefore non-physical volition" is not licensed by the phenomenology alone. Removing the epiphenomenalist defeater, which the levelling of the Libet debate arguably does, is not the same as upgrading the positive evidence for non-physical volition; the two should not be conflated.

## Predictive Processing: The Strongest Physicalist Rival

The clinical dissociations, the comparator account, intentional binding, and the parietal-stimulation result share a common physicalist home that this article has to meet head-on: predictive processing and active inference (Friston, Clark, Hohwy, Seth; see [[predictive-processing-and-dualism|Predictive Processing and Dualism]]). On that framework the brain is a hierarchical prediction engine that minimises the mismatch between top-down expectations and incoming signals. The sense of agency is what the system computes when the sensory consequences of an action match the forward model's efference-copy prediction; delusions of control, anarchic hand, and altered intentional binding are what happens when that predictive machinery is perturbed. Active inference is the generalisation of the very comparator model the clinical sections above rely on — so the evidence marshalled here is, in the first instance, evidence *for* a physicalist theory of agency, not against one.

This sets the real shape of the dialectic. Active inference predicts the dissociation table, the binding effect, the stimulation double dissociation, and the effort–plasticity covariance with no non-physical remainder. What the Map can defensibly say is narrower than "the evidence favours dualism." It can say three things. First, removing the Libet defeater and showing that agency tracks specific circuitry is fully compatible with the interface reading and no longer counts against it. Second, active inference helps itself to the felt character of agency without earning it: it specifies the *functional* signatures of agency in fine detail but stipulates rather than derives why any of that processing is experienced from the inside — the hard problem is untouched, and that gap is where the Map locates its opening. Third, on present data the interface reading and active inference are very nearly empirically equivalent, so the choice between them turns on the broader framework — the [[the-epiphenomenalist-threat|reportability argument]], the [[tenets]], the quantum opening — and not on the volitional evidence alone. This last point runs counter to active inference's own commitments only at the framework boundary, and is honestly noted as such rather than presented as a refutation from inside it.

The Map's case, then, does not rest on the brittle opponents that are easy to knock down — an RP that is a completed unconscious decision, a narrator that confabulates agency after every movement, a will localised to one cortical region. Those are strawmen. The live rival is active inference, and against it the volitional evidence is a draw, not a win. Presenting the clinical and stimulation data as though it discriminated the two would mistake compatibility for positive confirmation — the very move the calibration boundary above warns against.

## Intentional Binding: An Implicit Measure

The sense of agency is not only available through introspective report. Haggard, Clark, and Kalogeras (2002) discovered intentional binding — a temporal compression between a voluntary action and its outcome. When subjects press a button voluntarily and hear a tone 250 milliseconds later, they perceive the action as occurring later and the tone as occurring earlier than their actual times, so the perceived interval shrinks. This compression does not occur for involuntary movements produced by transcranial magnetic stimulation.

Moore and Obhi (2012) reviewed a decade of binding research and concluded that the effect tracks the operation of agency mechanisms — particularly the predictive (efference-copy) component — rather than post-hoc inference about authorship. The effect is reduced when outcomes are unpredictable (Wolpe et al., 2013) and altered in schizophrenic patients (Voss et al., 2010), consistent with the comparator dysfunction that produces delusions of control.

That 2012 verdict should now be held with lower confidence. A substantial body of construct-validity work published since 2020 argues that "sense of agency" measures conflate several distinct constructs, that implicit measures like binding often fail to correlate with explicit agency reports, and that binding may index temporal or causal prediction rather than the phenomenal feeling of authorship — with some registered work reading the effect as most compatible with an illusionist account. This is precisely the article's own second disconfirmer (that binding is an artefact of timing or motor preparation rather than a measure of agency), and it can no longer be listed as merely hypothetical: the current literature has made it a live possibility. Binding is treated here as suggestive and contested, not as a settled non-introspective measure of agency.

## Directed Effort and Neuroplasticity

A tempting argument runs: if conscious effort were epiphenomenal, it should be possible to produce the same neural changes without the phenomenology of effort. That inference does not go through. An epiphenomenalist can hold that a single neural control process produces *both* the experienced effort and the plastic change, so the two covary perfectly while the experience does no causal work. Distinguishing that common-cause model from one in which effort genuinely drives plasticity would require an intervention that varies conscious experience while holding the neural control variables fixed — a design the clinical studies do not supply. What the neuroplasticity evidence establishes is more modest but still real: cognitive therapies and internally guided tasks have measurable neural implementations, and the felt effort and the neural changes track each other closely.

Schwartz et al. (1996) found that OCD patients who learned to cognitively reframe their intrusive thoughts showed decreased caudate nucleus activity on PET scans—though the study included only 18 participants, of whom 9 were new, and has not been independently replicated (see [[clinical-neuroplasticity-evidence-for-bidirectional-causation|clinical neuroplasticity evidence]] for honest assessment). The treatment requires patients to recognise obsessive urges as brain-generated and redirect attention away from them. Patients consistently report this as effortful, requiring sustained concentration against compelling impulses. Classical experience-dependent plasticity — Hebbian re-weighting driven by prefrontal executive control — fully accounts for these changes, and the Map does not claim otherwise here; any stronger quantum-selection reading is routed to its explicitly speculative-tier siblings. The case supplies covariation, not a demonstration that the feeling does the causal work.

This finding generalises across therapeutic contexts. Paquette et al. (2003) showed CBT for spider phobia changed dorsolateral prefrontal and parahippocampal activity. Goldapple et al. (2004) found CBT for depression altered prefrontal and limbic metabolism in patterns distinct from pharmacological treatment — same symptom improvement, different neural signatures, suggesting the cognitive intervention operates through a different causal pathway.

Rajan et al. (2019) identified a neural signature that distinguishes willed from instructed attention. Frontal theta power and fronto-parietal coherence are *greater* in willed than in instructed attention — a relative increase, not a signal present in one condition and absent in another — emerging from roughly 500 milliseconds after the cue. The authors read the difference as reflecting the additional decision and conflict-processing demands of self-directed attention. The heavier fingerprint marks the functional load of the willed condition rather than settling whether its phenomenal character does causal work.

## Against the Illusion Theory

Daniel Wegner's *The Illusion of Conscious Will* (2002) is the strongest case for treating volitional experience as entirely epiphenomenal. Wegner argued that the experience of willing is produced by a brain system separate from the one that produces action, drawing on cases where the two dissociate: automatisms, hypnotic behaviour, the ideomotor effect, alien hand syndrome.

Eddy Nahmias (2002) identified the central weakness: showing that the feeling of willing *can* be miscalibrated does not show it is *always* an illusion, any more than optical illusions show that vision is always illusory. The cases Wegner cites are precisely the *pathological* exceptions against which normal operation is defined; that the sense of agency can malfunction is evidence that it normally tracks something real.

The Map's [[the-epiphenomenalist-threat|reportability argument]] applies with particular force here. If conscious will were genuinely illusory — if the experience of willing never causally influenced action — then our reports about the experience of willing would be causally disconnected from the experience itself. Wegner, writing a book about conscious will, would have to be producing those words through neural processes that happen to correlate with his experience of willing, without the experience playing any causal role. This is a serious objection to strong epiphenomenalism rather than a knockdown. Its force is conditional on a causal-connection premise about self-knowledge, and a physicalist can resist it: the same neural state can realise the experience and produce the report, or a reliable common cause can underwrite both. The [[phenomenal-concepts-strategy|phenomenal-concept strategy]] (Frankish, Papineau) presses exactly this point, and the dispute then relocates — to whether the first-person mode of presentation under which such concepts refer can be exhaustively physically characterised — rather than ending. The reportability argument raises the cost of consistent epiphenomenalism; it does not by itself close the position off. This matches the conditional formulation now carried by the Map's [[concepts/bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] treatment, and supersedes the stronger "cannot be rationally held" phrasing this article once used.

## What Would Challenge This View?

The convergence argument would be weakened if: (1) a clinical population demonstrated intact volitional phenomenology with completely disrupted causal architecture — agency feeling normal while the comparator, the parietal intention circuits, the dFMC, and the neuroplasticity pathways were all damaged, so that the phenomenology floats free of every known causal substrate simultaneously; (2) intentional binding turned out to be an artefact of motor preparation timing rather than a genuine measure of agency, collapsing the implicit evidence line — a disconfirmer the post-2020 construct-validity literature has already made partly active, as the intentional-binding section above records; (3) neuroplasticity from CBT proved achievable without any phenomenology of effort, patients improving through purely automatic processes with no subjective sense of trying; (4) a system without phenomenal consciousness replicated the full pattern of volitional behaviour including appropriate dissociations under damage conditions, demonstrating functional sufficiency without experience.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Unfinishable Map's [[tenets]] predict the pattern the evidence reveals. The **[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** tenet holds that consciousness is irreducible to physical processes — and the phenomenological structure of volitional experience (the agency-ownership distinction, the multi-level nature of intentional action) resists reduction to neural timing data. Desmurget's stimulation studies show that conscious intention and motor execution occupy dissociable neural territory, and Sjöberg's resection cases show that reported effort can survive impairment of an SMA-linked execution gateway. Neither result adjudicates between physicalism and dualism on its own — both are consistent with the multi-level, non-reducible structure the tenet describes, and the physicalist can read them as dissociations within the brain rather than between brain and non-physical mind. What the tenet supplies is the reason to prefer the interface reading; the evidence supplies the constraint the reading must fit.

The **[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]** tenet holds that consciousness causally influences the physical world. Brass and Haggard's "whether" component provides a plausible neural locus for this influence, and the neuroplasticity evidence is suggestive: Schwartz's OCD patients showed measurable brain changes correlated with directed mental effort, tracking the phenomenology of straining to redirect attention—though the small sample and lack of independent replication counsel caution. The Map interprets this as a site where consciousness modulates quantum-indeterminate processes, consistent with the **[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]]** tenet.

The **[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]** tenet is directly relevant to the Libet debate's history. For decades, the "simpler" interpretation — that the RP marked consciousness as epiphenomenal — was treated as settled science. Schurger's stochastic model, the meta-analytic mixed record, and the clinical resection cases have levelled that conclusion — the eliminativist inference is now underdetermined rather than settled science. The rush to eliminate conscious causation in favour of a parsimonious neural account illustrates how simplicity can mislead when understanding is incomplete.
