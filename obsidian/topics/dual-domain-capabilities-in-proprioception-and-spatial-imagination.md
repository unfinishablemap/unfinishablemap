The Unfinishable Map's [[episodic-memory|dual-domain framework]] argues that memory requires contributions from both physical mechanisms (engrams, reconsolidation) and non-physical consciousness (autonoesis, selectional guidance, phenomenal colouring). Memory is not unique. Proprioception and spatial imagination display the same architecture: physical substrates that encode position data or generate imagery, paired with phenomenal dimensions that the substrates alone cannot explain. The recurrence of this pattern across three cognitively distinct capabilities strengthens the case that dual-domain architecture is a general feature of the mind-body relationship rather than an artefact of how memory happens to work.

## Proprioception: Body Schema and Phenomenal Embodiment

Proprioception tracks the body's position, posture, and movement through muscle spindles, Golgi tendon organs, and joint receptors. The physical mechanisms are well understood: receptor signals travel via afferent neurons to the somatosensory cortex, where they integrate with vestibular and visual data to maintain an internal body model. Neuroscience distinguishes two components: the **body schema**—a non-conscious sensorimotor model that guides action automatically—and the **body image**—the conscious perceptual representation of one's body as an experienced presence. Merleau-Ponty (1945/2012) anticipated this distinction phenomenologically, contrasting the body as an object among objects with the lived body, the "vehicle of being in the world," whose character exceeds any objective description. His aim was to undercut the mind–body dualism the Map defends, not to support it; the Map borrows the distinction, not the conclusion.

This distinction maps directly onto dual-domain architecture. The body schema is physical: it operates without phenomenal awareness, adjusting posture and coordinating movement below the threshold of consciousness. The body image is phenomenal: it constitutes the felt sense of *having* a body, of inhabiting a spatial form from the inside. Oliver Sacks (1985) documented the case of Christina, who lost proprioception to a rare, almost purely sensory polyneuritis. Her motor nerves and her intellect were spared, yet at first she could barely sit up; over months she relearned to move by watching her body, vision substituting for the lost sense. The felt sense of embodiment did not return. She described herself as "disembodied," her body an object to manage rather than a medium to inhabit, and Sacks reports the feeling as severe years later as on the day it began.

Christina's case displays the dual-domain structure. Physical proprioceptive processing can be lost while consciousness continues; the phenomenal body can stay lost while physical function is partly rebuilt by another route. Function and feel come apart—the pattern a dual-domain architecture expects. A purely neural account of schema and image expects it too, so the case fits the framework without deciding for it.

### The Phantom Body as Evidence

Phantom limb phenomena provide the complementary dissociation. After amputation, the physical receptor channel is severed at the felt site, yet patients experience vivid sensations of the missing limb—position, movement, even pain. The canonical treatment of these phenomena and their underlying neuroscience belongs to [[phantom-limb-phenomena]]; what matters here is the dissociation they instantiate. Persistent cortical representations, peripheral and dorsal-root-ganglion contributions, spinal sensitisation and predictive body-model dynamics together specify the production conditions: the brain continues to generate a body image when the body part it represents is gone. The Map accepts that multilevel mechanistic picture. What survives the mechanism is the explanatory-gap question — why production conditions of this kind should be accompanied by *felt* embodiment at all. Phantoms expose the gap between neural body maps and lived bodily presence; they sharpen the dual-domain question without by themselves establishing a non-neural bearer of embodiment.

Many patients also report that the phantom can be moved at will, and a mirror reflection of the intact limb can evoke vivid felt movement in it (Ramachandran & Hirstein, 1998). Whether this is genuine voluntary control or reactivation of motor representations is debated. The richness and interactivity of the experience add pressure on any account that treats the felt body as a transparent read-out of peripheral signal, but the phantom remains a brain-mediated phenomenal state whose conditions of existence are neural even when the limb is not. The dual-domain reading is that those neural conditions specify the production of bodily experience without closing the question of why such production is felt — the same pattern the article tracks across memory and spatial imagination.

## Spatial Imagination: Mental Rotation and the Aphantasia Spectrum

Spatial imagination generates, manipulates, and explores spatial structures without sensory input. The classic demonstration is Shepard and Metzler's 1971 mental rotation study: participants judge whether two three-dimensional objects are identical despite differing orientations, and response time increases linearly with the angle of rotation. The spatial structure of the task is preserved in the phenomenal experience—mental images appear to rotate continuously through intermediate positions.

### The Physical Domain

Neuroscience has identified extensive physical correlates. Mental imagery activates many of the same brain regions as perception—the parietal cortex for spatial transformation, motor cortex for imagined movement, early visual areas for vivid imagery (Kosslyn, 1994). This overlap supports the view that imagery reuses perceptual and motor machinery. Functional imaging shows that mental rotation activates regions overlapping those involved in physical object manipulation.

### The Phenomenal Domain

Physical overlap between imagery and perception does not dissolve the [[hard-problem-of-consciousness|hard problem]] for imagination—it sharpens it. If imagination is neural reuse, why does it generate phenomenal experience at all? A neural network that reactivates visual cortex patterns could compute rotation without *experiencing* it as the smooth turning of a three-dimensional form in an inner space. Read alongside subjects' reports, the linear relationship between rotation angle and response time suggests that, for those who have the experience, it tracks the structure of the computation. Tracking does not by itself show that the experience does the work: aphantasics produce the same linear function without visual imagery (see below). What stays unexplained is why a computation that can run without imagery is, in most people, accompanied by it.

The [[explanatory-gap]] is especially visible here. Physical accounts explain *that* the brain computes spatial transformations and *how* neural populations encode angular relationships. They do not explain *why* these computations are accompanied by a phenomenal three-dimensional workspace in which imagined objects have apparent solidity, orientation, and spatial extent.

### Aphantasia as Natural Experiment

The aphantasia-hyperphantasia spectrum provides a natural experiment in dissociating the two domains. Aphantasics—around 1% of the population in the extreme form (Zeman, 2024)—cannot voluntarily generate visual mental imagery, a condition named by Zeman et al. (2015). Hyperphantasics experience imagery so vivid it approaches perception. Crucially, aphantasics can still perform spatial reasoning: they navigate, plan movements, and complete classical mental rotation tasks slower but more accurately than controls, using analytic rather than object-based strategies (Kay, Keogh & Pearson, 2024). Both groups show the standard linear relationship between angular disparity and response time, suggesting that the underlying spatial computation can be solved through phenomenally distinct architectures.

This dissociation constrains two simple pictures. If phenomenal visual imagery were what does spatial reasoning, aphantasics should fail at spatial tasks. They do not: Kay and colleagues conclude that visual imagery "is not crucial for successful performance in classical mental rotation tasks." Nor is imagery idle. The two groups reach the answer by different strategies with different speed–accuracy profiles, and when drawing scenes from memory aphantasics recall fewer objects and less colour than controls while matching them on spatial accuracy and making fewer memory errors (Bainbridge et al., 2021). Having imagery changes how a task is done and what is retained, without simply making performance better. A physicalist reads these as differences between neural strategies, and nothing in the data rules that out: the findings do not show that phenomenal character, over and above its neural correlate, does the work.

The dual-domain reading: physical spatial computation provides the substrate for both populations. Phenomenal spatial character, when present, goes with a distinct way of solving the task—object-based rather than analytic—but is not necessary for the underlying computation. The Map reads this as two distinguishable contributions; the evidence fits that reading without selecting it over a purely neural one. Viewed from the introspective side, the same dissociation opens the function-phenomenology face of the [[imagery-void|imagery void]]: if classical mental rotation can be solved with and without phenomenal imagery, introspection cannot determine from inside which mode is doing the cognitive work.

## Imagination as Mental Causation

Imagination generates novel phenomenal content without external input, and imagining shapes physical outcomes. A sculptor mentally rotates a form before cutting stone. An athlete rehearses a movement sequence and measurably improves physical performance—a meta-analysis of mental practice found a positive and significant effect on performance (Driskell, Copper & Moran, 1994). An architect walks through an unbuilt building and revises the design based on what the imagined walkthrough reveals.

In each case, on the Map's reading, phenomenal content generated by consciousness (the imagined form, the rehearsed movement, the virtual walkthrough) causally influences physical action. The causal chain runs: consciousness generates spatial content → content is evaluated phenomenally → evaluation guides physical action. This chain poses a challenge for epiphenomenalism, which must say that how the imagined form looked made no difference to where the sculptor cut, though she would give exactly that as her reason. The challenge is not decisive. Mental practice shows that imagery *processes* improve performance; an epiphenomenalist or physicalist credits the improvement to the neural simulation and treats the experience as its accompaniment, and the aphantasia findings show that rotation, at least, can proceed without visual imagery. The cases show what conscious causation would look like if the Map is right. They do not discriminate it from the neural-simulation account.

The Map's [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] tenet provides a framework: consciousness does not merely observe neural computations but contributes directional input that shapes which computations become action. In spatial imagination, this contribution is easiest to picture: the phenomenal content (an imagined spatial form) and the physical outcome (a shaped stone, an improved movement) would be connected through a causal chain that runs through conscious experience.

## The General Pattern

Three capabilities—memory, proprioception, and spatial imagination—display the same dual-domain architecture:

| Capability | Physical Domain | Phenomenal Domain | Key Dissociation |
|-----------|----------------|-------------------|------------------|
| Memory | Engrams, reconsolidation | Autonoesis, phenomenal colouring | Amnesia vs. vivid, confident recall of events that never occurred |
| Proprioception | Receptor signals, body schema | Felt embodiment, body image | Christina (no feel, some function) vs. phantom limbs (feel, no receptor) |
| Spatial imagination | Neural reuse, parietal computation | Phenomenal spatial workspace | Aphantasia (rotation succeeds without visual imagery) vs. typical imagery (same task, object-based strategy) |

In each case:
1. **Physical mechanisms are necessary but not sufficient** for the full capability
2. **Phenomenal contributions are dissociable** from the listed mechanisms (not, on this evidence, from neural activity)
3. **The phenomenal dimension goes with functional differences** in observable behaviour, which the Map reads as functional work and a physicalist reads as the work of its neural correlate
4. **The [[explanatory-gap]] persists** between mechanism and experience even after the physical side is well understood

The convergence of this pattern across cognitively distinct domains suggests it reflects something about the *architecture* of mind-body interaction rather than the peculiarities of any single cognitive function. One qualification keeps the count honest: the three capabilities share a single upstream — the [[explanatory-gap|explanatory gap]] itself, which each dissociation presupposes — so they are not three fully independent confirmations of dual-domain architecture so much as one explanatory-gap pattern recurring across three sites. What the recurrence genuinely adds is *substrate divergence*: memory engrams, proprioceptive receptors, and parietal mental-rotation machinery cannot share capability-specific mechanism, so a deflation that explained away the gap in one would not automatically explain it away in the others. That partial independence is real and worth the weight of one cross-substrate pattern, not three; the [[capability-division-problem]] anticipated the question it bears on: if consciousness and physical processes make distinguishable contributions to cognition, where exactly does the division fall? Proprioception and spatial imagination provide two further sites where the same division appears.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** is supported by the persistent explanatory gap in each capability. Physical accounts of proprioception explain receptor signals and cortical maps but not the felt sense of inhabiting a body. Physical accounts of imagination explain neural reuse and computational performance but not the phenomenal three-dimensional workspace. From the Map's perspective, the gap is not diminishing as neuroscience advances—it is becoming sharper, because the physical mechanisms become better understood while no physical account explains *why* these mechanisms produce felt experience. The more precisely we map receptor pathways and cortical representations, the more conspicuous it becomes that mechanism and experience are different kinds of thing.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]** finds its most intuitive illustration in spatial imagination. The sculptor's chisel follows the imagined form. The athlete's body executes the mentally rehearsed movement. On the Map's reading, phenomenal content generated internally by consciousness causally shapes physical outcomes—the non-physical acting on the physical through the neural interface. The behavioural evidence (mental-practice gains, strategy differences across the imagery spectrum) is equally what a neural-simulation account expects, so it illustrates the tenet without adding evidential weight to it.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]]** gains no support from these cases; it is only compatible with them. If consciousness biases quantum outcomes in neural systems, nothing confines that interface to cortex—microtubules are found in neurons throughout the nervous system, proprioceptive ones included—so it could reach bodily awareness as well as higher cognition. This is speculative; the dual-domain argument rests on the explanatory gap, not on any specific mechanism.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]** applies directly. Materialist accounts of proprioception and imagination appear parsimonious: receptors fire, cortex computes, behaviour results. But this parsimony purchases simplicity by ignoring the phenomenal dimension entirely. The apparent economy of "it's all neural computation" dissolves once the question shifts from *how* to *why there is something it is like*.
