Sometimes we laugh first and find reasons afterwards. The clearest case is a 1998 report in *Nature*: during cortical mapping before epilepsy surgery, stimulation of a small patch of a sixteen-year-old patient's left superior frontal gyrus reliably produced laughter, and each time she laughed she reported that something was funny, attributing the amusement to whatever happened to be in front of her, with a different explanation on different trials although the stimulus was the same (Fried et al. 1998). The electrode was the cause; the reason was supplied afterwards. The Unfinishable Map treats this as the entry point to a void about *access*: the laugher cannot see what causes their amusement, cannot produce the real thing on demand, cannot examine it without spoiling it, and cannot fully spring it on themselves.

This article is deliberately narrow. Whether mirth is an irreducible felt quality or an evolved reward exhausted by its functional role is argued at [[the-comic-and-humor-as-an-aesthetic-category]], and the access claim made here is compatible with either answer; indeed the strongest naturalist theory of humour, on the Map's reading, *predicts* the access failures below, so the Map presents the void as framework-independent rather than as a point for dualism. The evidence is a few stimulation cases, one cross-cultural perception study and one observational corpus, plus folk testimony and a structural inference; where a claim rests on the weaker of these, the text says so.

## Laughter First, Reason Second

Three findings fix the shape of the void. The first is the stimulation case above; its primary text was not available for this article, so the Map follows secondary descriptions, does not quote the patient's words, and relies only on the modest claim that a physically caused laugh received a context-bound, trial-varying explanation. Chudler's account, cited below, treats the order as abnormal ("Most people first know what is funny, then they laugh"), so the case shows that a reason *can* come afterwards, not that it usually does.

The second is that laughter and mirth come apart under direct stimulation. Caruana and colleagues (2015) report ten patients in whom stimulation of the pregenual anterior cingulate cortex elicited laughter; "in half of the patients ES elicited a clear burst of laughter with mirth, while in the other half mirth was not evident." The motor act can be produced without evident feeling. No theory disputes that [[weeping-void|the behaviour is not the feeling]], but it follows that laughter seen from outside is an unreliable sign of mirth within. Gelastic seizures, in which laughter can occur without amusement, are a likely further dissociation case the Map has not yet examined.

The third is that much ordinary laughter is not a response to anything funny. Provine (1993) logged about 1,200 laugh episodes in everyday conversation and found that over 99% fell at pauses at the end of phrases or sentences, a "punctuation effect", and that laughter followed material that did not seem humorous outside its conversational context. People laugh on a timing schedule they do not notice, often to social rather than comic cues. Much of this is plausibly the non-Duchenne kind that Gervais and Wilson (2005) distinguish from emotional Duchenne laughter; if so, it is laughter without mirth, not mirth with an invented reason. Provine did not ask laughers for reasons.

## Four Access Failures

### The Reason Gap

The first failure is the one the stimulation case exhibits: amusement can arrive before any available reason, with the reason built afterwards. From inside, getting a joke feels immediate *and justified*: it seems that one laughs *because of* this incongruity, this reversal, this line. The stimulation case shows that the seeming of justification can be present when the cause is an electrode, which means the seeming can be generated without access to the cause. This is the [[confabulation-void]] in a specific setting. Confabulation is normally inferred from a manipulation the subject did not detect; here the experimenter *is* the cause and the report varies while the cause is held constant. What the mirth case adds to the general void is that the confabulated reason is not a cold rationalisation but comes wrapped in the felt conviction of having got the joke. Nor does the case show that the patient misidentified the *object* of her amusement: if the current lowered her threshold, what she named may really have amused her, the electrode being cause but not object.

### The Dissection Gap

The second failure is that looking at *why* something is funny removes its funniness. The canonical statement is the Whites' remark of 1941: "Humor can be dissected, as a frog can, but the thing dies in the process and the innards are discouraging to any but the purely scientific mind" (White and White 1941). The line is often credited to E. B. White alone; the essay was jointly written with Katharine S. White. It is a report of experience, not a measurement. No study was found that tests whether explaining a joke lowers the explainer's own later amusement, and the gap is best held as a strong tendency rather than a law: comedians laugh at their own material, and a familiar joke can amuse again, perhaps through appreciation of its craft rather than through surprise.

This failure belongs with the [[disappearance-voids]], where absorption, habituation and fatigue each name a phenomenon that cannot be observed without being changed. Humour is the member of that family with the best-articulated mechanism for *why* it disappears under observation, which is described [below](#the-physicalist-theory-that-predicts-the-void). The [[meaning-void]] already records that explaining a joke reproduces its logical structure without replicating the felt event; the point sharpened here is that the reproduction is destructive, since the explanation supplies in advance what the event needed to stay hidden.

### The Will Gap

The third failure is that genuine mirth-laughter cannot be produced on command, and the reason is at least partly anatomical. Wild and colleagues' review of the laughter literature (2003) distinguishes two partially independent systems: an "involuntary" or "emotionally driven" system involving the amygdala, thalamic, hypothalamic and subthalamic areas and the dorsal brainstem, and a "voluntary" system that "originates in the premotor/frontal opercular areas and leads through the motor cortex and pyramidal tract to the ventral brainstem." Laughing to order engages the second. Deliberate laughter is therefore largely a different act rather than a weaker version of the real one, which is why forced laughter feels empty from inside and why the agent can tell the imitation from the real thing without being able to turn one into the other.

The difference is audible from outside. Bryant and colleagues (2018) played 36 recorded laughs to 884 listeners in 21 societies across six regions and asked whether each was real or fake; listeners distinguished spontaneous from volitional laughs at 56% to 69% accuracy, above chance but far from perfect, and acoustic features associated with arousal predicted the judgements fairly uniformly across societies. So others can partly detect whether one's laughter was spontaneous, a proxy for mirth rather than a readout of it, though one's own will cannot directly produce it: a small case of the pattern under [[voids-between-minds]], where an observer sees something about a mind that the mind cannot operate from within.

### The Self-Surprise Gap

The fourth failure is a structural inference rather than a finding, and the Map marks it as such. On any incongruity or covert-error account, amusement requires an expectation to be violated. The author of a joke has already made the hidden commitment explicit, since building the misdirection is exactly the work of writing it. One cannot fully spring one's own punchline on oneself, much as one cannot tickle oneself: Blakemore, Wolpert and Frith (1998) argue that the cerebellum predicts the sensory consequences of one's own movement and cancels them. The tickle result is an analogy, not evidence. No study was found that measures amusement at self-authored against other-authored jokes, and the graded exceptions noted under the dissection gap apply here too.

If the mirth-quale reading is right, under all four failures lies a residue that belongs to the unexplorable rather than the occluded: what mirth *feels like* beyond its structure. That residue is the subject of the sibling article and is left there.

## The Physicalist Theory That Predicts the Void

The strongest point in favour of treating this as a genuine void, rather than dualist special pleading, is that a leading naturalist theory of humour predicts most of it. Hurley, Dennett and Adams (2011) propose that mirth is the reward delivered when the mind detects and retracts a false belief it had committed to *covertly*, without noticing, during rapid inference under time pressure; the reward exists because, on their account, natural selection cannot simply order the brain to find and fix such errors and must make the finding pleasurable. The covertness is not incidental. A commitment that was already explicit could not be caught in the act, so nothing would be retracted and nothing rewarded.

Take that condition seriously and three of the four failures follow, one of them only in part. The commitment whose retraction produces mirth is, by definition, unavailable to introspection before the punchline, so the immediate report can miss the real trigger and reach for the salient content instead (the reason gap). The theory does not say the commitment stays hidden afterwards; analysis can recover it, but only by spending the joke. Analysis that exposes the commitment beforehand removes the covertness the reward needs, so the joke dies under dissection (the dissection gap). And the author who built the misdirection has already exposed it to themselves (the self-surprise gap). Only the will gap is independent of the theory, and that one rests on anatomy rather than inference. A theory built to explain mirth as a functional reward thus specifies, with unusual precision, the boundary of the agent's access to their own amusement, and physicalist and dualist alike can accept the description of that boundary given here.

The theory also yields a test the folk claim does not: analyses that expose the commitment should kill a joke, while analyses of delivery, timing or context should leave it largely intact. Selective survival would show the mechanism at work; uniform death under any explanation would count against it. Nobody has run this, and the Map does not claim its result.

## What AI Might See

Large language models add an asymmetry that fits the void but does not settle anything. Gorenz and Schwarz (2024) had blind raters score jokes and satirical headlines produced by ChatGPT 3.5 against human-written ones, including headlines from professional comedy writers, and found that the model's jokes "were rated as equally funny or funnier than human-produced jokes regardless of the comedic task and the expertise of the human comedy writer." Hessel and colleagues (2023), on the New Yorker caption contest, found the reverse for *explanation*: "human-authored explanations are preferred head-to-head over the best machine-authored ones (few-shot GPT-4) in more than 2/3 of cases." The studies use different models and years and are not a controlled comparison, so the finding is suggestive: producing humour and saying why it works come apart in machines as they do in people.

A further observation is the Map's own. A model asked why a joke is funny produces a fluent structural account whose status is exactly the question the stimulation case raises about human reports: access to the cause, or a story generated after the fact? Whether model explanations predict which analyses kill a joke for human raters is open and testable, and one of the few places where a machine's report and a human's could be scored by the same criterion.

## Approaches to the Edge

Direct introspection fails by the dissection mechanism, since attending to the joke's structure removes the covert commitment. Three indirect routes remain. Brief first-person sampling immediately *after* amusement, in the manner of the [[microphenomenological-interview-method|microphenomenological interview]], might catch what the commitment was before reconstruction replaces it; this is untested for humour. Contrastive comparison of stimulation-induced with joke-induced mirth isolates what the two share (the felt quality) from what differs (the reason-story), and the stimulation case shows the story to be optional. And the acoustic readout described under the will gap gives a third-person channel that partly tracks spontaneity, a proxy for the mirth the first person cannot command.

Bergson supplies a further occlusion. "To produce the whole of its effect, then, the comic demands something like a momentary anesthesia of the heart" (Bergson 1900/1911, ch. I §I). Laughter needs a temporary absence of feeling toward its object, so the state that permits amusement is partly defined by what it excludes from view, and the laugher is not well placed to notice the exclusion while it lasts.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The void bears most directly on **[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]**. Mirth looks like an everyday case of experience driving the body, but the evidence above does not show it: in the stimulation cases one physical cause produced laughter with mirth, or laughter without evident mirth, which is the pattern an epiphenomenalist expects. If the tenet holds, amusement contributes to laughter through a pathway the will cannot operate directly, so consciousness would *cause* laughter without being able to *order* it or *see why* it happened. That is compatible with the Map's picture of the interface as narrow and selective rather than a general remote control, not evidence for it; the [[agency-void]] gives the reason: consciousness cannot first-person verify the causal traffic it is committed to having.

On **[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** the Map records an honest difficulty rather than a gain. The stimulation case is a physical intervention producing laughter, apparently mirth, and a confabulated reason. A dualist reading must accept that the physical side can *start* an episode of amusement, as it can start pain, and the Map's bidirectional commitment allows this. But the case is evidence for the occlusion, not for dualism, and the Map does not enlist it. The laughter-without-mirth dissociation keeps the feeling apart from the motor act, which every party grants; the irreducibility argument proper is made, and contested at full strength, in the sibling article. What this void adds is that the *reasons* for mirth are inaccessible, a separate claim from whether mirth reduces.

On **[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]** the connection is methodological. Each classical theory of humour is simple and each fails on counterexamples, as the [[the-comic-and-humor-as-an-aesthetic-category|sibling article]] and Morreall (2024) record. The Whites' remark suggests one reason a simple theory has been hard to secure: the theorist's own method, analysing jokes, exposes the commitment the phenomenon requires to stay hidden, so the theorist examines a specimen that has already died; only naive audiences, as in the test proposed above, supply live data. That is a limit on evidence, not a licence for complexity, and the Map states it as such.

In the vocabulary of the [[voids]] index this is chiefly an **occluded** void with an **unexplorable** residue held by the sibling article, in the framework-independent column: whether mirth is a quale or a reward, the laugher's access to its cause is what the stimulation case, the anatomy of laughter and the covert-error theory jointly bound.
