We experience ourselves as causes. When you raise your arm, you feel yourself to be the author of that movement. When you choose coffee over tea, something in you—call it will, intention, decision—seems to make the choice happen. Yet we cannot observe *how* any of this works. The mechanism by which consciousness interfaces with physical causation is invisible from every angle we have.

This is the causal interface void: we are certain we are agents (or at least the experience of agency is undeniable), but we cannot access the interface that makes agency possible.

The opacity is not peculiar to mind: the [[causal-impression-void]] argues that physical causation is never observed either, only a constructed impression of it, so this void is a special case of a general one.

## The Double Opacity

The causal interface escapes both modes of human access.

**From the inside**: Introspection reveals intentions and actions but not the connection between them. When you move your arm, you can attend to the intention ("I want to raise my arm") and to the movement (the arm rising). Between them is nothing phenomenologically distinct. The connection is felt as immediate, but there is no "feeling of connecting." The bridge is crossed without experiencing the crossing.

**From the outside**: Neuroscience observes neural correlates and behavioural outputs but not consciousness causing anything. We see brain states that correlate with decisions, we see muscles contracting—but we do not see a non-physical mind entering the causal chain. The interface remains beyond third-person observation.

The gap between willing and doing has no observable content. Closer looking sharpens the two ends and still finds nothing between them.

## Evidence for the Void

Multiple lines of evidence converge on the same conclusion.

### The Philosophical Tradition

Since Descartes, no philosopher has successfully explained how mind and body interact. In May 1643 Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia asked Descartes how a soul that is only a thinking substance can move the bodily spirits to produce voluntary action, when moving anything seems to require contact and the Cartesian soul has no extension (see [[history-of-the-interaction-problem|the interaction problem's history]]). Descartes answered that soul–body union is a basic notion, not to be grasped through the notions proper to bodies, and elsewhere placed the interaction at the pineal gland (*The Passions of the Soul*, 1649). Neither supplied a mechanism.

Four centuries later, no satisfying answer exists. Interactionists have proposed various interfaces—quantum effects, information-theoretic bridges, emergent causation—but each leaves a placeholder at the crucial point: consciousness → [?] → physical effect. One diagnosis is that the placeholder resists filling because filling it would require showing causal relations between physical and non-physical realms using concepts derived from physical causation.

That diagnosis would make the difficulty structural, but the persistence is consistent with it without favouring it: a long failure to find a mechanism is equally expected given no interaction, a misspecified search target, too-coarse measurement, or conceptual confusion.

### Cognitive Science: The Process/Product Asymmetry

Nisbett and Wilson's classic 1977 review argued that there may be "little or no direct introspective access to higher order cognitive processes": reports of what influenced a choice draw on implicit causal theories, and are accurate mainly when the real influence is salient and plausible. We confuse accessible mental *contents* with inaccessible mental *processes*. You know what you decided, but not how you decided it.

The pattern is general. We access the products of cognition—formed intentions, completed perceptions, reached conclusions—far more reliably than the processes generating them. Mental causation fits this pattern: we access *that* we caused something, but not *how* we caused it.

Daniel Wegner's research takes this further. The felt experience of willing is not direct observation of causation but *inference* from cues: the thought appeared before the action, was consistent with it, and no competing cause was salient. The inference feels like observation, but it isn't. Wegner himself left open whether intentions are causes (Wegner 2004), so his model explains the feeling without supporting the Map's claim that consciousness is one.

### Temporal Slippage: Libet's Experiments

Benjamin Libet reported that, for spontaneous unplanned acts, awareness of wanting to move came about 200 milliseconds before the movement, while the readiness potential, a slow cortical build-up he took to mark the act's unconscious initiation, began about 550 milliseconds before it (Libet 1985).

That reading is contested: Schurger, Sitt and Dehaene (2012) modelled the build-up as spontaneous fluctuations averaged back from the moment they happen to cross a decision threshold, not as planning already under way. The awareness reports are fragile too. When a beep implied that participants' movements came later than they did, their reported time of decision moved later with it, and Banks and Isham (2009) concluded that the report "is largely inferred from the apparent time of response". Even the felt time of willing is partly reconstructed after the act.

Libet held that conscious will does not initiate the act but can still veto it in the 150 milliseconds or so that remain, which preserves some causal role but concedes that the interface for initiation is hidden.

### Phenomenology of Agency

Philosophers disagree about how rich agentive experience is. Terry Horgan finds it pervasive—raising your hand has "the what-it's-like of self as source of the motion"—yet argues that introspection alone cannot settle what that experience requires of the world: agent causation in the libertarian's heavyweight sense, or ordinary causation by one's mental states. He defends the compatibilist answer himself (Horgan 2011). Even on a rich account, the experience presents oneself as source without presenting how the sourcing works.

Failures of agency are more conspicuous than its successes. Patients with alien hand syndrome experience actions without authorship—their hand moves but they do not feel themselves to be the source. The dissociation reveals no mechanism; it is consistent with the normal sense of agency being an attribution rather than an observation of a causal process.

## The Physicalist Objection: Opacity Is Normal

A physicalist might object that most bodily mechanisms are opaque to introspection. We do not experience how our liver metabolizes alcohol or how our immune system identifies pathogens. Why should the opacity of "mental causation" be any more mysterious?

The Map's reply is that consciousness *is* our mode of access. Liver function is opaque because no sense is directed at our organs, whereas introspection is directed at our mental lives, and this void concerns the very thing we are closest to: our own agency in action. That combination—intimacy without transparency—would distinguish it from ordinary biological opacity. But the reply holds only if willing is itself a conscious act. The physicalist can answer that the process/product asymmetry above already places the mechanism of decision among subpersonal processes as opaque as the liver's, and the language-model case [[#what-ai-might-see|below]] shows self-report missing its own mechanism with no experience presumed. Whether this opacity is special or ordinary therefore turns on whether conscious will is a cause, the point in dispute.

## The Frustration of Introspection

Turn your attention to the moment of causation—the exact instant where intention becomes movement. It slips away. You can attend to the intention. You can attend to the initiation of movement. But you cannot attend to the connection between them.

Contemplative reports sharpen the point rather than close the gap. In Mahasi Sayadaw's Burmese insight practice, the advanced meditator notices the intention to bend an arm distinctly, before the bending, and "experiences directly that a bodily process takes place after a preceding intention". Attention sharpens both terms and their order; between them it finds succession, not a connecting. The author drops out too: apart from the paired mental and bodily processes, the meditator comes to reflect, "there is no separate person or being".

What fails here differs from forgetting, where an observation fades: no observation of the connecting was ever made. The interface shows no phenomenal signature. The gap between willing and doing, when examined, is not a gap but an absence.

## Why the Void?

What explains this systematic invisibility?

### Structural Inaccessibility

If consciousness interfaces with physics at the quantum level, as the [[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]] tenet proposes, it would bias which of several individually random outcomes occurs while leaving long-run Born statistics intact. Any unconditioned aggregate test would then see only chance, by construction rather than because quantum scales defeat measurement. That explains the outside half of the void without counting in the tenet's favour, since the opacity follows from how the tenet is framed (the [[amplification-void]] makes the same point). Nor is it total: a deviation conditioned on intention or task could still show up, and no such brain-internal test has yet been run.

If the interface has a bodily locus rather than a purely cortical one, the [[interoceptive-void]] marks a natural region to look. Most of the body's interior never reaches awareness, and the opacity that keeps visceral state below the threshold of experience would also conceal any consciousness-physics contact occurring there. The interoceptive void thus names a candidate locus while explaining why, if the interface lies there, it would remain undetectable from inside.

### The Self-Reference Problem

The causal interface may be inaccessible for the same reasons that consciousness cannot fully model itself (see [[self-opacity|self-reference paradox]]). The interface is not an object *for* consciousness but the very means *by which* consciousness acts. Observing how you cause things would require stepping outside the causal process while remaining within it—grasping your own grasping.

The eye cannot see itself seeing. On this reading, the interface cannot be observed because observing would itself use the interface.

### Defended Territory?

Could the interface be actively hidden rather than merely inaccessible? The [[self-maintained-cognitive-limits|defended territory]] article examines limits that minds maintain against themselves, through self-deception and motivated avoidance, alongside the possibility (in simulation scenarios) that some are imposed from outside.

If consciousness's causal efficacy is real but hidden, perhaps the interface must remain obscure for consciousness to function: full visibility of the mechanism might destabilise the sense of agency that coherent action needs. This is speculation, perhaps untestable, since active hiding and passive inaccessibility may be indistinguishable from within.

## What AI Might See

AI systems presumably lack phenomenal consciousness yet make decisions and execute actions, so they offer a comparison case if the route of access is held fixed. From outside, a model's computations are in principle inspectable, but so are a brain's. From inside, the model fares no better than we do. Asked how it had computed 36+59, Claude 3.5 Haiku said it had carried the 1, while attribution graphs showed parallel pathways for an approximate sum and an exact ones digit; the researchers call this "a simple instance of the model having a capability which it does not have 'metacognitive' insight into" (Lindsey et al. 2025). The process/product asymmetry thus appears, for at least this capability, without presumed experience (see [[chain-of-thought-as-verbal-protocol]]), which tells against reading the inside half of this void as specific to consciousness. A discriminating comparison would need a feature of our opacity about willing that experience-free self-modelling systems lack.

## What Would Challenge This View?

The causal interface void would be undermined if:

1. **Phenomenology of causation becomes articulate.** If contemplative practice or careful attention reveals the felt quality of *connecting* intention to action—not just having connected, but connecting.

2. **Neuroscience locates the interface.** If brain imaging finds a process that corresponds to consciousness entering the causal chain, observable from the third person.

3. **Libet-style timing becomes fully explained.** If the gap between neural preparation and conscious awareness proves to be methodological artifact, and the timing of causation becomes introspectively accessible.

4. **Mechanism is articulated.** If philosophers or physicists explain *how* something non-physical biases quantum probabilities without leaving the placeholder unfilled.

**Where these conditions stand:** Four centuries of philosophy and decades of neuroscience have met none of them, and the contemplative reports above describe succession rather than connecting. The persistence is consistent with a structural limit but does not by itself favour one over the rival explanations noted under [[#the-philosophical-tradition|the philosophical tradition]].

## Relation to Site Perspective

The causal interface void connects directly to The Unfinishable Map's [[tenets]]:

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]** is the most relevant. The tenet holds that consciousness causally influences the physical world. This void concerns why we cannot observe *how* that influence operates. The Map asserts causal efficacy while acknowledging that the mechanism is opaque. This is coherent: one would expect an interface between consciousness and physics to be at the edge of what either mode of access can reveal. The [[agency-void#the-verification-circularity|verification face of the agency void]] reveals a deeper layer: not only is the mechanism hidden, but *whether* consciousness causes anything at all cannot be verified from within. The Map holds Bidirectional Interaction as a starting commitment rather than a derived conclusion—together, the two voids explain why, for now, that commitment is adopted rather than discovered. The [[preference-void]] adds a question about what selection selects from: if a preference ordering is assembled during the choosing, any conscious contribution would select among physically prepared candidate orderings, and whether it selects or merely receives the result cannot be told from inside.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]]** entails the outside half of this void: an influence that preserves Born statistics leaves no signature in unconditioned measurements. Since that opacity follows from the tenet by construction, and rivals with no interaction predict it too, observing it adds nothing in the tenet's favour (compare the [[amplification-void]]).

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** makes the void intelligible. If consciousness is genuinely distinct from physical processes, then the interface between them is where two ontologically different domains meet. Such an interface would resist reduction to either domain's methods of investigation. The void marks the boundary between what physics can explain and what consciousness can observe.

This void appears in the [[tenet-generated-voids|tenet-generated voids]] framework as the "Mechanism Void"—the characteristic gap generated by accepting that consciousness interfaces with physics while acknowledging we cannot explain *how*. The causal interface void is the experiential face of that structural limit.
