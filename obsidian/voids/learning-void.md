While a person is practising, nothing in view shows how much of what they are doing will last. Memory researchers separate *performance*, which can be watched during practice, from *learning*, the change that shows later in retention and transfer. The judgement people make during practice follows how easy the practice feels. Under three studied arrangements (spacing, interleaving and self-testing) that judgement has been shown to favour the method that served the learner worse. In the first experiment of Kornell and Bjork (2008), with 120 UCLA undergraduates learning painters' styles, "78% of the participants did better with spaced presentations than they did with massed presentations, but 78% of the participants said that massing was as good as or better than spacing." The learning void is this limit on monitoring one's own learning while it is under way.

The limit is narrow and has a plain remedy: test yourself later, with the answer out of sight. A meta-analysis found that delaying the judgement brings "robust benefits to relative accuracy (g = 0.93)" (Rhodes & Tauber 2011). The misjudgement can outlast a person's own contrary results, and it gave way for most participants in one experiment that separated the two schedules and explained the difference ([Persistence and Its Exit](#persistence-and-its-exit)). The feeling is sometimes right, and some difficulties do not help ([Limits](#limits)). Most of the evidence is laboratory work with university students in the United States. In the terms of [[three-kinds-of-void|the three kinds of void]] this is an occluded limit of a practical sort, with a known mechanism and a known way round, and it is unexplorable only in the time-bound sense that later retention is not yet there to inspect. Ordinary cognitive psychology predicts it, so it supports none of the Map's tenets.

## What the Learner Has to Go On

Three parts of the limit can be separated. The split is this article's and appears in none of the sources.

The first is a matter of timing. If learning is what remains later, nothing present during practice can be it. This part is close to definitional.

The second is a substitution. In place of the missing quantity, the judgement uses ease of processing and current performance. Koriat and Bjork (2005) give the structural reason in their abstract: "Judgments of learning (JOLs) are made in the presence of information that is absent but solicited during testing." The learner judges with the answer on the page and will be tested with it gone. Kornell et al. (2011), in three experiments with single words, report that "Type size affected predicted but not actual memory, and future study opportunities affected actual memory but scarcely affected predicted memory." Kirk-Johnson et al. (2019), a separate laboratory running four studies on interleaving and retrieval practice, report that "participants who perceived a strategy as more effortful rated it as less effective for learning and, in turn, were less likely to choose it for future study"; that analysis is correlational.

The third is persistence, taken up [below](#persistence-and-its-exit). Only the second and third are findings about people, and both are conditional.

## Where Feeling and Outcome Part

The abstract of Soderstrom and Bjork's review (2015; only the abstract was read for this article) states the general point: "certain manipulations can have opposite effects on learning and performance." Four results show the learner's judgement following the performance side.

**Category learning.** In Kornell and Bjork (2008), participants studied six paintings by each of 12 artists, massed for some artists and interleaved for others, and then had to name the painter of new paintings. Spacing won in the within-subjects Experiment 1a (n = 120; proportion correct .61 against .35), and the abstract reports that "Participants rated massing as more effective than spacing, even after their own test performance had demonstrated the opposite." Two cautions travel with the figure. The second 78% includes people who said the two schedules were about the same, and the test came shortly after study, so the result concerns learning a category and says little about long retention. The authors had predicted that massing would win, and wrote of themselves that they may have fallen for "the illusion that a sense of ease or fluency accompanies effective learning, whereas a sense of difficulty signifies ineffective learning."

**A motor skill.** Simon and Bjork (2001) assigned 48 UCLA undergraduates to blocked or randomly interleaved practice of three timed keystroke patterns and asked them during practice to predict a test 24 hours later. Their abstract reports that "blocked practice enhanced acquisition but harmed retention. Participants, though, predicted better performance given blocked practice." The figures were read from a scanned copy and are given here as approximate: the blocked group predicted a timing error near 8% and produced one near 20%, while the random group predicted about 12% and produced about 11%. The group whose practice was hard predicted well. This is one experiment, between subjects, with one laboratory task.

**Prose.** Roediger and Karpicke (2006) had students either restudy passages or take recall tests on them. Their abstract reports that "When the final test was given after 5 min, repeated studying improved recall relative to repeated testing." On tests two days and a week later testing won, "even though repeated studying increased students' confidence in their ability to remember the material".

**A classroom.** Deslauriers et al. (2019) ran a randomised crossover in two introductory physics courses at Harvard (149 students with midterm scores). Each student had one topic taught by active instruction and another by a polished lecture. Students in active classrooms reported a feeling of learning 0.56 standard deviations lower and scored 0.46 standard deviations higher on a test of 12 multiple-choice questions. The test came at the end of the same class. The study has no delayed measure and shows nothing about retention; it is one institution, one subject and two class meetings per course. Students with more prior expertise showed a stronger positive correlation between feeling and score, so the mismatch was largest among novices.

## Persistence and Its Exit

Yan, Bjork and Bjork (2016) ran six experiments on the painting task, in the same laboratory as Kornell and Bjork, with UCLA undergraduates and online workers. They extend the earlier result and do not independently replicate it. They report that "Only 13% of participants in Experiments 1A and 1B judged interleaving to be more effective than blocking." Telling participants that interleaving works better for 90% of people did little: in the conditions given that information, most placed themselves in the remaining tenth.

The same paper reports the exit. In Experiment 6 (197 online participants), blocked and interleaved study were placed in two separate study-and-test cycles. With a full explanation added, "75% (59/79) of the participants reported interleaving to be more effective than blocking"; without it the figure was 46% (35/76). The misjudgement resisted explanation and experience while the evidence was mixed together, and gave way for most when it was separated out.

## Limits

**The feeling is sometimes right.** Brunmair and Richter's meta-analysis (2019), based on 59 studies, found a moderate overall benefit of interleaving (Hedges' g = 0.42), largest for paintings (g = 0.67), and an advantage of blocking for studies based on words (g = −0.39). The painting task that anchors this literature is its most favourable case, and for word materials the learner who prefers blocking is correct.

**Not every difficulty helps.** Whether hard-to-read print aids learning is disputed. Xie et al. (2018) report null effects in a meta-analysis that was not read for this article; Weissgerber et al. (2021) found errors in it, judged its transfer results "(rather) robust" and doubted its recall results. Ease during practice is a poor guide to retention, and difficulty is no better a guide in the other direction.

**The judgement can be accurate.** Rhodes and Tauber's figure comes from 112 effect sizes "across 4,554 participants". Tullis et al. (2013) found that judgements made with the cue alone in view reflected the benefit of testing, and that learners "generalize that knowledge to future learning only with considerable guidance". Koriat and Bjork (2006) report that the foresight bias they describe "can be alleviated by study-test experience".

**The evidence base.** Simon and Bjork and Deslauriers et al. are single studies. Much of the core work comes from one extended research group; Kirk-Johnson et al., Deslauriers et al. and the two meta-analyses come from others. Except for Kornell and Bjork, Yan et al., Simon and Bjork and Deslauriers et al., the papers cited were read as abstracts, which give no sample sizes beyond those quoted. One search for failed replications found none, which is weak evidence that none exist.

## The Remedy

The limit is on monitoring during practice and leaves finding out untouched. A delayed test with the answer covered measures the thing the feeling was standing in for. People already use it this way: in Kornell and Son (2009), "Participants (incorrectly) judged that the pair mode resulted in the most learning, but chose the test mode most frequently", and the abstract adds that they chose it to diagnose their learning. Keeping predictions and later results side by side, sorted by study method, follows the logic of Yan et al.'s sixth experiment.

## Relation to Site Perspective

None of the Map's [[tenets]] is supported by the learning void.

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** gains a reading and no evidence. The datum is that the judgement of learning made during practice follows ease, and misleads where ease and retention are driven apart. Cue-utilisation accounts of metamemory, on which such judgements are inferences from available cues such as fluency, predict this, as does the mismatch between conditions at study and at test. The datum therefore does not discriminate between dualism and physicalism, and none of the authors cited argues for dualism or should be read as lending it support. The Map can say that experience during practice is a poor witness to change in one's own capacities, in line with [[self-opacity]]. That reading is the Map's. As a catalogue entry the void adds no weight either: the voids register has [[positions/voids-as-evidence#^p-v3|suspended the cumulative step]].

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]** is illustrated. "I can tell when I am learning" is a simple self-description that covers a missing quantity and a substituted cue. A physicalist psychology draws the same distinction, so the illustration is no evidence for the tenet.

**Bidirectional Interaction** is not engaged. The findings concern monitoring. They do not show that conscious effort causes learning, since the effort at issue belongs to the task arrangement and a physicalist reads it as more demanding processing. They do not show that conscious judgement is idle either: the remedy, choosing to test oneself later, is a deliberate act. The Map's case for a causal role is made on other grounds in [[consciousness-and-skill-acquisition]]. **Minimal Quantum Interaction** and **No Many Worlds** are not engaged.
