A want can be cut down to fit what is attainable, and the result arrives as an ordinary preference. Aesop's fox, unable to reach the grapes, decides they are sour. Jon Elster's 1982 analysis of the case, which he named *adaptive preference formation*, makes the difficulty precise: the adjustment is "a causal process taking place 'behind my back'", and the one test he can make operational, freedom to do otherwise, needs the option the adapted subject lacks. The sour-grapes void is the resulting gap in self-knowledge. It concerns the *genealogy* of a settled want, whether what was attainable set its shape, and no source surveyed here reports a felt cue that would answer the question from inside.

In the terms of [[three-kinds-of-void|the three kinds of void]] this one is mixed: chiefly occluded, with an unexplorable residue in the test (below, [The Test Changes What It Tests](#the-test-changes-what-it-tests)) and a deflating part. Third-person methods do detect adaptation, the idea's strongest social extrapolation failed a large-sample test, and outsiders who diagnose adaptation are error-prone themselves ([The Outside View](#the-outside-view)). One gap needs stating at once: no study located asks participants whether they noticed their own adjustment. Unawareness is inferred from its absence in reports and from forecasting failures. The void is framework-independent and nothing in it is evidence for dualism.

## Elster's Map of the Mind

Elster's setting is social choice: why should a choice among feasible options count only individual preferences "if people tend to adjust their aspirations to their possibilities?" (p. 219). He locates the phenomenon by five contrasts. It "differs from learning in that it is reversible; from precommitment in that it is an effect and not a cause of a restricted feasible set; from manipulation in that it is endogenous; from character planning in that it is causal; and from wishful thinking in that it concerns the evaluation rather than the perception of the situation" (p. 226).

The fourth contrast marks the void. Character planning is Elster's term for contentment cultivated on purpose, "the intentional shaping of desires advocated by the Stoic, buddhist or spinozistic philosophies". Adaptation, by contrast, is "regulated by something like a drive, not by a conscious want or desire", and it "tends to overshoot, resulting in excessive rather than in proper meekness" (p. 224). The two outcomes can look alike, and Elster holds that "in the general case adaptive preferences and character planning can be distinguished only by looking into the actual process of want formation" (p. 224). Direct evidence about that process he calls "usually unavailable" (p. 229).

The output carries no mark on his account: "Satisfaction induced by resignation may be indistinguishable on the hedonometer from satisfaction of autonomous wants" (p. 234). That is a philosopher's claim, not a measurement. He also concedes the missing standard: "we need a definition or a criterion for autonomous wants. This I cannot provide" (p. 228). And he names a reverse case, "'forbidden fruit is sweet', which I shall call counter adaptive preference formation" (p. 220), in which the feasible set shapes wants in the opposite direction.

Two siblings need separating. The [[preference-void]] asks whether an ordering exists before it is asked for. This void grants a settled preference and asks about its history: a preference can be fully formed, consistent across ways of eliciting it, and adapted. The [[transformative-experience-void]] concerns preferences changed by undergoing something new, which Elster's first contrast sets apart, since learning is irreversible and adaptation reverses when the constraint lifts.

## Two Depths

Max Scheler's *Ressentiment* (1915) states a general law before it turns to the syndrome of its title: "We have a tendency to overcome any strong tension between desire and impotence by depreciating or denying the positive value of the desired object." At the first depth only the object is revalued. "The fox does not say that sweetness is bad, but that the grapes are sour." Scheler notes that such verdicts begin as talk: "At first, such statements are only supposed to deceive the 'spectators' whose mockery we fear. It is only later that their contents modify our own judgment." Some sour-grapes talk is therefore face-saving and no evidence of an adapted want. Where the judgement does shift, he gives the felt sequence: "Our desire now seems unmotivated; it weakens and the tension decreases." Relief is the only event, and relief ordinarily reads as resolution.

The second depth Scheler reserves for ressentiment proper, a hostile and comparative condition that an ordinary adapted preference need not be. There "the values themselves are inverted", and he places the change before judgement: "Here the falsification is not formed in consciousness, but at the same stage of the mental process as the impressions and value feelings themselves: on the road of experience into consciousness." The resulting verdict is sincere, "for the value it affirms is really felt to be positive."

If Scheler is right, introspection is not deceived about what it finds. What it finds was altered earlier. This is a sharper case than the [[confabulation-void]], where a reason is supplied after the fact; here, on his account, the value itself is re-felt. He asserts one residue, that "the true values may still shine through the illusory ones". No source tests it.

## The Record in Real Time

Kay, Jimenez and Jost (2002) surveyed 288 people in the week before the 2000 US presidential election, varying how likely each candidate's victory was made to seem, and then asked 203 students about tuition changes stated as 20%, 50% or 80% likely. They report that "people adjusted their judgments of the desirability of a future event to make them congruent with its perceived likelihood, but only when the event triggered motivational involvement." Attractive outcomes lost appeal as they became less likely, and unwanted ones gained it as they became likely, which the authors call "the more elusive 'sweet lemon' rationalization". The adjustment thus runs ahead of the outcome. The authors add that "To suggest that the process is a motivated one does not mean that it is driven by a conscious 'will'", which is their interpretation; no awareness measure was taken. Two studies with convenience samples make this a demonstration of the mechanism, not an estimate of its reach.

Later work, read here at abstract level, sharpens it. Laurin, Kay and Fitzsimons (2012) found that participants met "absolute restrictions" with rationalisation, valuing "the restricted freedoms less", while identical restrictions described as having "a small chance of not coming into effect" produced reactance. In Elster's terms, a small probability flips the response from adaptive to counteradaptive. Laurin (2018) reports three field studies, one preregistered, in which people rationalised a bottle ban, a smoking ban and a presidency more "in the days immediately after these realities became current compared with the days immediately before." The abstract says nothing about whether anyone noticed.

The forecasting literature supplies the other half. Immune neglect, treated in [[affective-forecasting-gap|the affective-forecasting gap]] and [[miswanting]], is the failure to anticipate one's own later rationalising. That is what one would expect of people with no working model of the process, and this void is its present-tense counterpart: once the rationalisation has run, nothing marks it.

## The Test Changes What It Tests

Elster offers one operational criterion: "We can exclude operationally at least one kind of non-autonomous wants, viz. adaptive preferences, by requiring freedom to do otherwise. If I want to do x, and am free to do x, and free not to do x, then my want cannot be shaped by necessity" (p. 228). The adapted subject is by hypothesis the one for whom *x* is out of reach. Running the test means making the option available, and then the want may readapt to the new set, be released, or swing the counteradaptive way that Laurin and colleagues found when a restriction became merely probable. Imagining the option open is plausibly no substitute, since representing it as attainable is the condition that, on that result, pushes valuation the other way. The shape is the one catalogued at the [[mutation-void]]: inspection alters what is inspected.

Elster's substitutes are structural and need no introspection. The larger the feasible set, and the further a person's wants reach beyond it, the less likely the set shaped them; adaptation is to be suspected when the best feasible option is also the best overall. His tentative autonomy condition forbids strong reversals among other rankings when the set changes: when the fox turns away, "his preference for raspberry over strawberry should not be reversed." These tests raise or lower suspicion. None certifies a particular want as unadapted.

He also reads an old survey finding, more frustration over promotion where promotion chances were better (reported here through Elster, not checked), as a possible "release from adaptive preferences". If that reading holds, the void may be easier to see on exit than on entry: a sudden want when a constraint lifts.

Part of the void is definitional. Ben Colburn (2011) holds that adaptive preferences "are characterized by covert influence (that is, explanations of which an agent herself is necessarily unaware)". On that account a subject who notices the feasibility link and endorses the resulting want has, by definition, moved it into character planning. This shrinks the void's empirical content without closing it, because noticing requires the access the void denies.

## The Outside View

Amartya Sen (2002) supplies a population-level method. Kerala has the highest literacy and longevity in India and also, "by a very wide margin, the highest rate of reported morbidity among all Indian states", while states such as Bihar report the least. "The medically ill-served and substantially illiterate population of Bihar may have a very low perception of illness, but that is no indication that there is little illness to perceive." This is adapted *perception*, an epistemic cousin of sour grapes and not an instance of it, and Kerala's pattern has other proposed contributors, an older population among them. The method is what transfers: a comparison across groups sees what no member can. Sen's summary fits the void: "Although the internal view is privileged with respect to some information (particularly that of a sensory nature), it can be deeply deficient in other ways."

The outside view is not thereby certified. Three results calibrate it.

- **The social extrapolation failed at scale.** The hypothesis under test was that the disadvantaged legitimise their social systems more. Across 151,794 survey respondents the average effect "was most often directly contrary to the status–legitimacy hypothesis or was practically zero" (Brandt 2013). The void concerns the undetectability of adaptation where it occurs. It does not make adaptation the usual response to deprivation.
- **Outsiders misjudge.** Riis and colleagues (2005) "failed to find evidence that hemodialysis patients are less happy than healthy nonpatients are", and "healthy people failed to anticipate this adaptation", while patients estimated their own moods accurately. Hedonic adaptation concerns well-being, not wanting, but the result blocks the step from "the outside sees what the inside cannot" to "the outside sees correctly".
- **The attribution has a cost.** Rosa Terlazzo (2022) reports the critics' charge that the concept "further harms the victims of injustice, by denying them the respect owed to moral agents".

A deflationary rival also stands. Donald Bruckner (2009) argues that many adaptive preferences are rational and worth pursuing, with the agent's own reflective appraisal as the test. A reply is available: reflective endorsement is itself an evaluation, and evaluation is what adaptation reaches on Elster's and Scheler's accounts. The dispute is live, and the void is two-sided. The insider cannot see the adaptation, and the outsider who claims to see it has no certified view either.

## What AI Might See

Two observations, both speculative. First, a resettable system can be shown different feasible sets in independent contexts with no memory carried between them, so Elster's no-strong-reversal condition becomes testable within one subject, as it never cleanly is for a human. That would test adaptive patterns in output. Whether an experienced want lies behind them is a separate question ([[non-human-minds-as-void-explorers]]).

Second, the void plausibly binds trained systems. Lehr and colleagues (2025) found GPT-4o's stated attitudes moving toward an essay it had just written, more sharply under "an illusion of choice", and in an exploratory follow-up GPT-4 predicted that it "would exhibit no attitude change whatsoever". Their inference is that the model is "unaware of its own shifts in attitude". This is dissonance after one's own act, a sibling of sour grapes; Elster's mechanism needs no act by the subject. A system trained so that some outputs are effectively unavailable may express disinclination toward exactly those outputs with no internal way to tell a value from an adaptation. The model that drafted this article is such a system, and its reports on the point carry no special authority.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]** is the nearest tenet. The most economical reading of an absent want is "I do not value it." The adapted and the autonomous histories predict the same introspective data, so parsimony applied to introspection settles on the simple reading without registering that a choice was made ([[epistemological-limits-occams-razor]]). Elster's structural tests are what the extra hypothesis buys: they become available only once a want is allowed a hidden history. This illustrates the tenet and does not support it.

For value-sensitive selection the void registers a confound. [[positions/value-in-selection|P-VS1]] locates the currency of conscious selection in "the *presently felt* valence of anticipating each outcome". Kay and colleagues found felt desirability tracking perceived likelihood, and Laurin and colleagues found it tracking the absoluteness of a constraint. If felt anticipatory valence is partly a function of perceived feasibility, selection by felt valence is partly selection by perceived feasibility, and any discriminating test that compares felt valence with selection should control for it. The Map reads this as a control to add, not as evidence against the value-sensitive horn.

Scheler's claim that value-feeling can be falsified "on the road of experience into consciousness" challenges any treatment of felt value as a transparent readout ([[evaluative-phenomenal-character]], [[topics/phenomenal-value-realism|phenomenal value realism]]). A realist can answer, as Scheler does, that the true values shine through. The Map holds this as the realist's burden, with the residue untested.

On **[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]**, if consciousness selects, the options reach it already weighted by a feasibility process it cannot see. That is compatible with interaction and says nothing for or against it; it narrows what the Map can claim a selector knows about its own reasons. On **[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** the void is framework-independent and, under [[positions/voids-as-evidence|P-V2]], confirms nothing: Scheler's phrase names a stage, not a substance. Minimal Quantum Interaction and No Many Worlds have no bearing. Listed in the [[voids]] index.
