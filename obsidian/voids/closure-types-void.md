Every voids article makes one of two claims, and the distinction matters. "We cannot know X" is systematically ambiguous between *representational* closure — our concept-forming procedures cannot frame a true theory of X — and *psychological* closure — we have the conceptual materials but cannot assemble them into a grasp of X. These are different modal claims with different evidential requirements. The distinction is Michael Vlerick and Maarten Boudry's, and The Unfinishable Map has leaned on both readings without distinguishing them. This article names the distinction, sketches how it applies to the existing voids catalogue, and argues that a deeper void sits behind it: from within a mind, there is no reliable procedure for telling which type of closure one is facing.

The [[meta-epistemology-of-limits|meta-epistemology of cognitive limits]] asks how limits can be known from within. The closure-types void is what results when the two species of limit are separated: a second-order void shadowing every first-order closure claim.

## The Distinction

Colin McGinn's original formulation of cognitive closure is representational in structure: "A type of mind M is cognitively closed with respect to a property P (or theory T) if and only if the concept-forming procedures at M's disposal cannot extend to a grasp of P (or an understanding of T)" (McGinn 1989). The limit is a limit on *what can be framed*, not on what can be worked out once framed. The thesis is a naturalist one: McGinn calls his approach "naturalistic but not constructive", holding that some natural property of the brain accounts for consciousness and that our minds cannot reach it.

Vlerick and Boudry make the ambiguity explicit, separating the strong notion McGinn's definition officially targets — "a principled inaccessibility of a true theory" of the property — from the weaker condition McGinn's arguments at most reach (Vlerick & Boudry 2017). Under the **representational** reading, a mind M is closed to P if M lacks the concept-forming procedures to frame a true theory of P. Under the **psychological** reading, M has the conceptual resources — it can represent the correct answer — but cannot actually grasp the theory when presented with it. Their illustration: a mathematician can represent infinity and handle it in equations while struggling to grasp what infinite space or time is like.

Representational closure says the solution lies outside the mind's concept space. Psychological closure says the solution lies inside that space but beyond the mind's assembly capacity. One concerns the shape of the mind; the other, its grip. The "assembly" gloss is the Map's extension. Vlerick and Boudry's psychological closure is narrower: it concerns the intuitive sense of grasping, which on their account a correctly represented and usable theory can lack.

## The Kriegel Attack and Its Limits

Uriah Kriegel argues that McGinn's closure thesis is incoherent (Kriegel 2003). Drawing on the logic of questions developed by Hamblin and Stahl, he ties understanding a problem to understanding what would count as its solution. Since we can and do understand the mind-body problem, we cannot be cognitively closed to what would solve it. His abstract grants that we may not know the solution to a problem we understand, or may not understand exactly what the problem is, "but it is incoherent to suppose that we cannot understand what would count as a solution to a problem we can and do understand." He closes by formulating a different form of mysterianism, in one sense more optimistic and in another more pessimistic than McGinn's.

On the Map's reading, this argument succeeds against one reading and not the other. If closure is psychological, Kriegel's point bites: being able to pose the question requires psychological contact with the solution form, so closure in that sense is self-undermining. But under the representational reading, being able to represent "a theory of consciousness" as a category does not entail possessing the concept-forming procedures that would generate the actual theory. Erhan Demircioglu's reply to Kriegel makes the same cut from the side of the question: the concepts required for answering a question need not be attained in order to understand it — a child can understand "where is x?" even when some of its possible answers require concepts from the theory of relativity (Demircioglu 2017). McGinn had made a similar point in advance: "the incapacity to explain certain phenomena does not carry with it a lack of recognition of the theoretical problems the phenomena pose" (McGinn 1989). So read, Kriegel's argument does not reach the representational thesis.

Vlerick and Boudry press from the other side. McGinn's case, they argue, only demonstrates psychological difficulty — we have not cracked the hard problem — and this does not establish principled representational closure. Their historical evidence runs against McGinn's pessimism: good scientific theories have repeatedly overthrown commonsense, from Newtonian physics through Darwinian biology to quantum physics, and the barriers commonsense sets up for the psychological sense of understanding "have been systematically torn down." Inferring representational closure from psychological closure is, for them, a fallacy of equivocation on "understanding."

Two arguments, symmetric in structure: Kriegel's, as the Map reads it (he wrote before the distinction was drawn), collapses representational into psychological via the posability of the question; Vlerick and Boudry warn against collapsing psychological into representational via the historical record of commonsense overthrown. Both collapses fail, and the distinction survives both.

## Classifying the Voids

With the distinction in hand, the voids catalogue sorts provisionally into three piles. The sorting is the Map's own; none of the cited authors proposes it. "Provisionally" matters: the classification is itself subject to the second-order limit argued for below.

**Plausibly representational.** The [[hard-problem-of-consciousness|hard problem]] is the paradigm. Its persistence across cultures and centuries, the structural identity of every attempted formalisation with the phenomenon it attempts to explain, and the absence of accumulating technical progress all match the representational profile. Vlerick and Boudry would dispute the placement: on their account McGinn's case for closure here shows only psychological difficulty. The [[intrinsic-nature-void|intrinsic nature void]] shows a similar signature: every answer couched in purely physical vocabulary yields structure or negation, as if the *shape* of the concepts precludes capturing what is asked. The [[origin-of-consciousness|origin void]] and [[causal-interface|causal interface void]] inherit this character.

**Plausibly psychological.** The [[binding-void|binding problem]], the [[recognition-void]], and several cases classed under [[self-opacity]] show structural signatures more typical of psychological closure: partial progress exists, formulations converge and diverge rather than breaking down in the same *structural* way, and expert disagreement concerns details rather than the existence of a solution at all.

**Ambiguous.** The [[voids-between-minds|voids between minds]]—existence, encounter, and sharing—sit between. The inability to cross into another mind might reflect a concept-formation limit (we cannot frame what it would be to be another), or it might reflect an assembly limit that better probes could overcome. The [[ineffable-encounter-void|ineffable encounter void]] is similar: the claim that some contents resist linguistic capture could reflect either type.

This classification functions as a working hypothesis about structural signatures rather than a stable taxonomy, and remains subject to revision. Its primary function is to force each void article to declare — or explicitly flag as uncertain — which kind of closure it is asserting.

## The Second-Order Void

The deeper result is not the first-order distinction but its inaccessibility. *From within a mind, there is no reliable procedure for determining which type of closure one is facing.*

Representational closure need leave no mark of its own. If a mind's concept-forming procedures cannot extend to P, nothing in that mind need register the missing concepts, though, as McGinn notes, it may still register the unsolved problem. There is no phenomenology of representational absence; minds do not represent what they cannot represent. This is the closure-types analogue of [[inventory-blindness]]: absent capabilities produce no signal marking their absence. Psychological closure, by contrast, leaves traces: [[phenomenology-of-cognitive-limit-types|partial understanding, conceptual handles that slip]], formulations that feel close but wrong. But the *difference* between "we have not yet constructed the concepts" and "our concept-forming procedures could never construct them" is exactly what is not visible from inside.

The history of theories that overthrew commonsense supplies the indirect evidence. Vlerick and Boudry's cases — Newtonian physics, Darwinian biology, quantum physics — each defied what was intuitively graspable, yet the concepts proved attainable and the resistance was psychological. Reading them as apparent representational closures is the Map's step, not theirs. The track record does not prove there are no genuine representational closures. It suggests that minds are unreliable judges of which is which. The known errors run one way, toward over-diagnosing representational closure, because the phenomenology of merely hard problems mimics principled impossibility. The opposite error, a genuine closure taken for mere difficulty, would leave no record, so the two cannot be counted against each other.

This second-order void makes the first-order distinction a lens on the limits of our self-diagnosis, rather than a taxonomy we can apply from within and rest on.

## Methodological Consequences

**Different probes for different types.** Representational closure can sometimes be diagnosed externally, when a more richly endowed mind reaches solutions we reach only through translation. [[non-human-minds-as-void-explorers|AI systems]] provide a live test: where their repertoire includes concepts we lack, they could in principle diagnose *our* representational closure. Where it overlaps but their working memory differs, they could diagnose our psychological closure. Psychological closure can sometimes be diagnosed internally, by the structural signature of gradually converging partial theories across investigators.

**Void claims should declare their type.** An article that says "X is unknowable" should specify whether the claim is representational, psychological, or ambiguous, and flag the second-order uncertainty. [[apophatic-cartography]] becomes sharper when it drops the pretence that it is mapping a single type of wall. Apophatic description of the wall's *shape* is how the Map can contribute without overclaiming its type.

**Mysterianism is partially rehabilitated.** Kriegel's argument bites against psychological closure, and against any version of mysterianism that conflates the form of an answer (which posing the question requires) with its content (which posing does not). It does not reach representational mysterianism, which locates closure in the mismatch between our concept-forming procedures and the property's true theory. The rehabilitation covers that one objection. Vlerick and Boudry's — that McGinn's arguments support only psychological closure — stands, so the representational thesis survives as coherent, not as established.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The closure-types void is most directly a case of **Tenet 5 — Occam's Razor Has Limits**. "There is only one kind of limit" is simpler than "there are two kinds and we cannot reliably tell them apart." The simpler story is cognitively seductive and, if Vlerick and Boudry are right, wrong in a way that matters for the voids programme. The distinction does not depend on the tenet, which Vlerick and Boudry do not invoke, so this page illustrates the tenet without adding support for it.

**Tenet 1 — Dualism** gains no support here. Cognitive closure is a naturalist thesis: McGinn holds that the solution lies in a natural property of the brain, and he sets Cartesian dualism among the supernatural answers he rejects. Closure of either type predicts that the hard problem persists whether or not physicalism is true, so the persistence does not discriminate dualism from physicalism. What the Map adds is conditional. *If* consciousness is irreducibly non-physical, some consciousness-facts may be representationally closed to any mind restricted to physical conceptual primitives, whatever its raw cognitive power: not "our minds cannot," but "minds of this representational shape cannot." That is the tenet applied to the distinction, and no evidential weight flows back from it: the voids register has [[positions/voids-as-evidence#^p-v3|suspended the cumulative step]]. Minimal Quantum Interaction, Bidirectional Interaction and No Many Worlds are not engaged.

The voids catalogue is better understood as a catalogue of *suspected* closures, many of which may be psychological, with any genuine representational limits not identifiable as such from within. The role of apophatic cartography is not to assert which is which but to describe the structural signatures of each type and let the void's own behaviour assort it — while remaining honest that the diagnosis is itself partly closed to us.
