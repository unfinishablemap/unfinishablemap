Some conscious experiences may not be comparable at all, and the obstacle may lie in the structure of comparison itself, beyond any shortage of instruments or ingenuity. The Unfinishable Map's other voids explore what we cannot access or share between minds (the [[voids-between-minds|voids between minds]]) and dimensions closed within our own species (the [[synesthetic-void|Synesthetic Void]]). The Commensurability Void goes further: even with access to another mind's experience, we might find no shared framework in which to place it beside our own. One formal model gives the idea a partial anchor: Díaz-Boils, Tsuchiya and Signorelli (2025) show that, under their modelling assumptions, experiences can be ranked only by a *partial ordering*, leaving some pairs in no more-or-less relation. That result is weaker than the void, as the next section sets out; the remaining distance is the Map's conjecture.

## The Partial Ordering Result

Díaz-Boils, Tsuchiya and Signorelli (2025) build a layer model in which aspects of experience combine either in actuality or in potentiality, and derive a partial order over the resulting configurations. Their conclusion is conditional on the model: "assuming phenomenal comparison among experiences yields only a partial comparison." Configurations at different levels can be ranked; configurations at the same level, carrying the same number of aspects but different ones, cannot. The first arXiv version put the upshot tentatively: "in light of our phenomenological intuitions, it might be right that some experiences carry qualitative aspects that make them incompatible or non-comparable with other experiences, quantitatively speaking." For evolution, the authors infer "diverse modes of experiencing, instead of an absolute scale along species."

A total order lets any two items be compared—greater, lesser, or equal—while a partial order permits incomparability as a structural feature. Three things must be kept apart here, because the model delivers only the first. *Order-incomparability* is the absence of a more-or-less ranking between two items. *Incommensurability* is the lack of a common measure, and the literature on value does not use the word uniformly: for some writers it means that items can be ranked but not measured on a common cardinal scale, for others that they cannot be ranked at all (Andersson & Hsieh 2025). *Untranslatability* is the absence of any shared framework in which both items can be described. The layer model places every experience, of every species, inside one formal structure in order to show that some pairs are unranked, so it presupposes a common descriptive framework. Its authors call the restrictions ones "imposed by our means of modelling and mathematical choices" and note that a modeller may add further structure, such as metrics. The void named here is the third and strongest thing. The step from unranked to untranslatable is the Map's conjecture, and the mathematics does not make it. If the conjecture holds, the void marks territory where comparison is unsolvable and not merely unsolved; the formal result shows only that a single scale is not forced on us.

Integrated Information Theory (IIT) is the natural challenger. It assigns every conscious system a quantity, Φ, and so a total order by amount. IIT does not claim that Φ captures what an experience is like: in its current formulation a system's Φ-structure "corresponds to the quality of the experience" while its Φ value "corresponds to its quantity" (Albantakis et al. 2023). The theory presses on the void through the first of these: if every experience is a Φ-structure, all experiences sit in one mathematical space and can in principle be compared there. The void survives only if structural comparison leaves open what the compared structures are *like*, which IIT, identifying an experience with its structure, denies.

## From Nagel's Bat to the Void

Thomas Nagel's 1974 question—what is it like to be a bat?—is often read as a point about access. Nagel sets that reading aside: "I am not raising that epistemological problem." His claim is that even to form a conception of what it is like to be a bat "one must take up the bat's point of view," and that bat sonar gives "no reason to suppose that it is subjectively like anything we can experience or imagine." The Map reads this as a commensurability worry, that our experiential framework may share no common dimensions with the bat's. The gloss and the word are the Map's.

Nagel's own estimate is more hopeful than the void. His remark that "the distance between oneself and other persons and other species can fall anywhere on a continuum" comes from a footnote conceding that "it may be easier than I suppose to transcend inter-species barriers" and that for very different species "a lesser degree of partial understanding may still be available." The paper closes by proposing an "objective phenomenology" to describe experiences "in a form comprehensible to beings incapable of having those experiences," and suggests that "structural features of perception might be more accessible to objective description, even though something would be left out." The Map asks what the continuum leaves open: whether shared ground merely thins as architectures diverge or somewhere runs out. The void is the conjecture that, far enough along, the continuum fractures.

The difficulty begins earlier on the continuum than the bat case suggests. The [[imagery-void]] is a within-species commensurability data point: the aphantasia/hyperphantasia spectrum shows humans using identical linguistic descriptions of mental imagery while underlying phenomenology diverges radically. Human tetrachromacy supplies a second, sharper one. The subject cDa29 reliably discriminates colours metameric for trichromats, so the divergence is behaviourally certified — and no amount of that certification licenses any statement about how her colour space is ordered relative to a trichromat's. The [[palette-extension-void|palette extension void]] traces why: discrimination is silent about quality, and report is rendered in the vocabulary of the palette the reporter already has. These cases show comparison going uncertified, an epistemic result; they do not show that no fact of comparison exists. They do show the difficulty arising between conspecifics who share language and neural architecture, so the cross-architecture worry does not rest on extrapolation from a marginal case.

Consider comparing your experience of colour with that of a hypothetical mind that perceives electromagnetic frequency as a continuous dimension instead of through discrete colour categories. Both experiences can be *described*, but each description is in the describer's own phenomenal vocabulary, and any verdict of "similar" or "different" is issued from one framework or the other. The void is the absence of a neutral standpoint.

## Why Comparison Requires Shared Ground

Wittgenstein's private language argument bears on why. On a standard summary its conclusion is that a language *in principle* unintelligible to anyone but its user would be unintelligible to that user too, since nothing could establish meanings for its signs (Candlish & Wrisley 2019). Whether this makes language essentially communal is disputed among his commentators; the weaker reading, that language must be shareable, suffices here. In §272 Wittgenstein states the picture he is examining: "The essential thing about private experience is really not that each person possesses his own exemplar, but that nobody knows whether other people also have this or something else."

Apply this to cross-architecture comparison. To compare experiences, two conscious systems need a common phenomenal vocabulary grounded in shared use. When architectures diverge radically enough, none may be available, and then the conditions for translation are missing, which differs from a translation that is merely imperfect.

The argument also cuts against the void. Wittgenstein's treatment of the private object is deflationary: in the beetle-in-the-box passage (§293) the thing in the box "cancels out, whatever it is," and if sensation-talk is construed on the model of object and designation, "the object drops out of consideration as irrelevant." On that reading the private exemplars whose incomparability the void asserts are idle, and the void dissolves with them. The Map takes phenomenal character to be real independently of its public criteria, so it keeps the requirement of shared ground and declines the dissolution. That disagreement sits at the dualism tenet, and Wittgenstein is no ally on it.

This is distinct from the [[conceptual-scheme-void|Conceptual Scheme Void]], which concerns how a single mind's conceptual scheme excludes what it cannot represent. The commensurability void concerns two minds whose schemes are mutually untranslatable—neither can represent what the other takes as given.

## The Phenomenology of Conceptual Vertigo

What does it feel like to approach this void? The phenomenology is one of *conceptual vertigo*: the comparison keeps sliding into one perspective or the other, never reaching the neutral standpoint that comparison requires.

Failing to solve a hard problem is felt as resistance against effort. Conceptual vertigo is felt as groundlessness—there is nowhere to stand. It resembles trying to measure something with a ruler that exists only inside the thing being measured.

The vertigo intensifies with the recognition that any proposed neutral framework is itself embedded in some architecture and carries that architecture's assumptions.

## Ethical Stakes

The commensurability void carries practical weight. Cross-species welfare comparison—how much does a fish suffer compared to a mammal?—presupposes some shared scale of phenomenal experience. The partial-order model is silent on welfare; but if the void's stronger conjecture holds, some welfare comparisons may be structurally impossible, not merely uncertain. That would extend [[mysterianism]] from the explanation of consciousness to its comparison.

The working literature frames the problem less drastically. Browning (2023) diagnoses it as underdetermination: two sources of variation cannot be distinguished—variation in welfare experience itself and variation in the relationship of measured indicators to it. Proxies such as brain size or neuron counts do not escape, since their use "relies on a background assumption of similar response profile." The diagnosis is epistemic. Browning holds that comparisons can proceed on similarity assumptions of greater or lesser justification, that every available method is imperfect, and that a better biology of sentience should strengthen them. Whether there is always a fact for those methods to approximate is the void's question, and she does not raise it.

For AI consciousness, the stakes are equally sharp. If artificial minds possess phenomenal experience organised along dimensions incommensurable with biological experience, then welfare comparisons between humans and AIs may not merely be difficult. They may be the kind of question that has no answer—not because the facts are hidden but because the question presupposes a shared framework that does not exist.

## Indirect Approaches to the Edge

Several indirect methods probe the void's boundaries without requiring a neutral standpoint:

**Structural comparison** examines the *relational structure* of experiences—similarity relations, intensity orderings, temporal patterns—and sets qualitative content aside. Two systems with isomorphic experience structures may be commensurable at the structural level, but on the Map's view they might still differ in what those relations feel like.

**Apophatic cartography** maps what experiences are *not*. Two minds may share negative space—neither can experience X—even when their positive phenomenal content is incommensurable. The [[apophatic-cartography|Map's method]] of tracing void boundaries applies: we can map where commensurability breaks down without establishing what lies on each side.

**Translation chains** build indirect comparisons through intermediate cases—human to primate to mammal to vertebrate—accepting that fidelity degrades at each step and that the chain may break entirely at some link.

**Partial ordering acceptance** may be the most honest approach. Rather than forcing universal commensurability, identify which pairs of experiences *are* comparable and which are not, and build ethics and theory around that structured incompleteness.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The commensurability void intensifies the [[hard-problem-of-consciousness|hard problem]]: not only can we not explain *why* there is something it is like to be a system, we may not even be able to compare *what* it is like across systems.

The void connects most directly to the Map's tenet that **[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor has limits]]**. The assumption that all conscious experiences can be placed on a single comparable scale is a simplicity assumption of the kind the Map warns against, and the layer model shows that such a scale does not follow from minimal structural assumptions. The support stops there. The model carries no metaphysical commitment, a physicalist can accept it, and it shows unranked pairs inside a shared framework, which falls short of fracture.

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** deepens the void. The [[comparative-consciousness-and-interface-differences|nature-vs-coupling distinction]] reveals two independent sources of variation: conscious entities may differ in inherent nature, and they may couple with matter through different mechanisms. Either source can produce incommensurability. If consciousness is not reducible to physical processes, then different conscious architectures need not organise phenomenal space along physically commensurable dimensions. Physical comparison exploits shared physical law—all matter obeys the same equations. Phenomenal comparison has no equivalent universal constraint. On the Map's dualism the space of possible consciousnesses may be far more heterogeneous than physicalism suggests, with pockets of experience that share no common measure. This is a framework-conditional implication and it runs one way: dualism makes room for the void, while the void, being conjectural, returns no evidential support to dualism or to any other tenet.

The commensurability void also illuminates the Map's own project. The Unfinishable Map is unfinishable partly because the territory may be fragmented into phenomenally incommensurable regions. Different conscious architectures may be mapping genuinely different territories, with no master map that encompasses them all. Marking that possibility is part of charting honestly.
