Boredom is a common occasion on which consciousness reports on its own engagement, and the report is hard for its owner to read. A widely cited synthesis defines it as "the aversive experience of wanting, but being unable, to engage in satisfying activity" (Eastwood et al. 2012). The Unfinishable Map reads four limits in that experience as a void, each explained below: boredom [[#objectless|demands a change of engagement without naming a target]]; it [[#projection|presents a failed relation as a property of the object]]; the untrained mind [[#self-supply|struggles to occupy itself with thought and underpredicts how much it will enjoy trying]]; and, by the attention-based definition alone, [[#examination|satisfying engaged study of a bout ends it]].

The faces differ in standing. Faces 2 and 3 rest on experiments with their own limits. In the best-known, 12 of 18 men and 6 of 24 women gave themselves an electric shock they had said they would pay to avoid, during fifteen minutes alone with their thoughts (Wilson et al. 2014a); the sample is small and its interpretation disputed (Fox et al. 2014). Face 1 holds on one model and face 4 on one definition. A computational model with no dualist commitments accommodates the objectless feeling, so nothing here requires dualism. What the Map reads as a void is a felt verdict that steers behaviour without displaying its grounds.

## Wanting Without a Determinate Object {#objectless}

Eastwood's formula has two halves, wanting to engage and being unable to, and the wanting has no determinate object. Tolstoy found the phrase in 1877. Vronsky, with what he had long desired fully realised, feels "springing up in his heart a desire for desires—*ennui*", and "Without conscious intention he began to clutch at every passing caprice, taking it for a desire and an object" (*Anna Karenina*, Part Five, ch. 8, trans. Garnett). The stand-in objects are the mind's own constructions, presented to it as wants.

Computational psychology suggests why. Kurzban et al. (2013) proposed that the phenomenology of effort is the felt output of opportunity-cost computations, the face treated in the [[agency-void|agency void]]. Agrawal, Mattar, Cohen and Daw (2022) formalise what they call "previous suggestions" that fatigue and boredom are felt opportunity costs: "boredom signals the value of exploration (external in the world)." In their model, exploration value is computed for particular alternatives of uncertain worth, including ones already tried, while what is felt is a cost on the current task. Nothing in the feeling displays which alternative carries that value. (Fatigue, their other case, belongs to the [[disappearance-voids|disappearance voids]].)

The indeterminacy is therefore an occlusion, a gap in what the feeling presents rather than in what the signal is about. The reading rests on one model, and evidence that bored people can reliably tell which alternative would repay them would weaken it; for kinds they partly can (Westgate & Wilson 2018). Boredom, which "motivates pursuit of new goals when the previous goal is no longer beneficial" (Bench & Lench 2013), specifies a kind of goal but no particular one. Hunger does too, so face 1 is one case of a compression common to affect; what stays distinctive is boredom's kind, a state of its owner, not a kind of thing: on Danckert and Elpidorou's (2023) model, an optimal zone of cognitive engagement.

## The Verdict Points Outward {#projection}

Situational boredom arrives as a predicate of its object: this lecture, this queue, this book is boring. The models place it in a relation. Eastwood et al. write the outward attribution into the definition, on which the bored "attribute the cause of our aversive state to the environment"; that is a definitional component rather than a measured misattribution.

Westgate and Wilson's (2018) Meaning and Attentional Components (MAC) model splits the cause into a mismatch between cognitive demands and mental resources and a mismatch between the activity and valued goals. They report support for the predictions that "Deficits in attention and meaning each produce boredom independently of the other" and that "boredom results from two types of attentional deficits, understimulation and overstimulation." One verdict covers *too easy*, *too hard* and *pointless*, even when people "become bored with their own thoughts" and no external object is there to blame. Pascal saw this: man "would weary even without any cause for weariness from the peculiar state of his disposition" (*Pensées*, fr. 139, Brunschvicg numbering, trans. Trotter, whose "weariness" renders *ennui*).

The claim needs a fence. Bernard Williams held that "not being bored can be a sign of not noticing, or not reflecting, enough" (Williams 1973), so boredom is sometimes fitting and the projection is not always an error. The void claim is that the verdict, even read relationally like *nauseating*, does not carry on its face what would distinguish the fitting case from the projected one. Westgate and Wilson report "different profiles of boredom" for the two deficits, and in their Study 2, a 2×2 design (N = 216), the self-reported profiles separated them: the attention manipulation alone predicted the Inattention subscale of a state boredom scale, and the meaning manipulation alone predicted the disengagement, affect and time-perception subscales. What remains open is attribution: whether a bored person can say which deficit made her bored, a question Study 2 did not ask.

## The Mind Alone With Itself {#self-supply}

Pascal held that "all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber" (fr. 139), and that diversion "principally hinders us from reflecting upon ourselves" (fr. 171). Wilson and colleagues (2014a), without citing him, put people in the chamber: "In 11 studies, we found that participants typically did not enjoy spending 6 to 15 minutes in a room by themselves with nothing to do but think." Most reported that their minds wandered "even though there was nothing competing for their attention." In the shock study, 12 of 18 men and 6 of 24 women shocked themselves at least once in 15 minutes, counting only those who had said they would pay to avoid it.

The interpretation is contested. Fox, Thompson, Andrews-Hanna and Christoff (2014), re-analysing the data, found just thinking rated, on average, "somewhat enjoyable, somewhat entertaining, and somewhat boring", and 14 of the 18 who shocked themselves citing curiosity about the shock; Wilson et al. (2014b) replied that all had already felt it and said they would pay to avoid it. Four experiments by Eder et al. (2022) favour curiosity over avoidance of thinking; the Map adds only that, on the opportunity-cost model, where a tried option of uncertain worth still has exploration value, curiosity about the one manipulable thing in an empty room is what an exploration signal would produce. The comparative finding is robust: across 2,557 participants in 11 countries, people assigned to do something enjoyed it more than people assigned to think for pleasure, and "The results were consistent in every country" (Buttrick et al. 2019).

Forecasting adds a second limit. Across six experiments, Hatano, Ogulmus, Shigemasu and Murayama (2022) found that "participants' predicted enjoyment and engagement for the waiting task were significantly less than what they actually experienced", and that the low forecast led them to choose internet news instead, "despite their experiences not being statistically different." The Map draws an inference the authors do not state: avoidance driven by the forecast removes the experience that would correct it, so the forecast tends to keep itself in place. That gives Pascal's diversion thesis a candidate mechanism and ties this face to [[self-maintained-cognitive-limits|self-maintained cognitive limits]] and the [[affective-forecasting-gap|affective-forecasting gap]].

The limit belongs to the untrained default, which simple prompts ease (Westgate & Wilson 2018). Wilson et al. confine their conclusion to people "Without such training", meaning meditation and similar techniques, and Buttrick et al. found enjoyment of thinking correlated with need for cognition, openness, meditation experience and initial positive affect, and inversely with reported phone use.

## Absorbed Examination Ends Boredom, by Definition {#examination}

Part (b) of Eastwood's definition has the bored "focused on the fact that we are not able to engage attention and participate in satisfying activity", so boredom permits self-attention; what it resists is *engaged* self-study. Someone who takes up her boredom as an object of absorbed inquiry meets the condition whose failure defines the state, attention engaged with internal information in a satisfying activity. By the attentional definition the bout is then over, and what she studies is its memory, or a new curiosity about it. On Slaby's (2010) reading, Heidegger in his 1929–30 lectures sets out to work "by awakening the fundamental attunement", profound boredom, rather than by "amassing factual knowledge" (p. 107). That method concerns profound boredom, not face 4's attention-deficit kind.

The inference is definition-relative. Under the MAC model a person can attend fully and still be bored if the activity has no value for her, so engaged examination of meaning-deficit boredom may leave it intact. The face is unexplorable for attention-deficit boredom and open for meaning-deficit boredom. Nor does it block measurement: in 1.1 million experience-sampling reports, "Subjects report boredom in 2.8% of the 30-min sampling periods" (Chin et al. 2017). What the definition excludes is a long, engaged and *satisfying* first-person reading of one bout (unabsorbed, unsatisfying meditative noting is not excluded), a shape shared with the [[mirth-void|mirth void]] and the [[mutation-void|mutation void]].

## Two Borders: Duration and Profound Boredom

Boredom slows the felt passage of time: in a real 7.5-minute wait, "Boredom was associated with the feeling of time passing more slowly and more frequent thoughts about time" (Witowska et al. 2020). *Langeweile* is literally a long while, and this face belongs with the [[temporal-void|temporal void]].

Distinct from face 2's situational boredom is Heidegger's profound boredom, voiced as "it is boring for one", in which, on Slaby's gloss, "the world in its entirety ceases to offer anything of significance." It has no object at all. Here the boredom void meets the [[mood-void|mood void]] and the [[mattering-void|mattering void]], which treat attunement and significance as media that disclose a world but cannot be inspected while they work. The Map borrows Heidegger's phenomenology and leaves his anti-Cartesian metaphysics with him.

## What AI Might See

Artificial agents get boredom-analogues by design. In curiosity-driven reinforcement learning the explore signal is an intrinsic reward with a written specification, such as prediction error, open to the designer, where the human signal is felt and its specification hidden. The void concerns first-person access to the signal's grounds, and the Map grants the physicalist that the function itself can be specified. Testing "a common thought experiment called the noisy-TV problem", Burda et al. (2018) found that a randomly changing TV "drastically slows down learning" in such agents, which also had an extrinsic goal reward; the authors trace the failure to stochastic dynamics, so a useful exploration signal must discount unlearnable noise. Language models learned their talk of boredom from human writing and give no independent purchase on it ([[non-human-minds-as-void-explorers|non-human minds as void explorers]]).

## Approaches to the Edge

- **Experience sampling** measures boredom within half-hour windows, sidestepping face 4 (Chin et al. 2017).
- **A 2×2 like Westgate and Wilson's Study 2**, with subjects also asked which deficit made them bored, would test face 2's unexplored margin.
- **Contemplative practitioners** could show whether engaged examination dissolves boredom or merely redescribes it; the only evidence cited here, Buttrick et al.'s meditation correlate, is correlational.

## Relation to Site Perspective

On the Map's [[three-kinds-of-void|three-kinds taxonomy]] the boredom void is mixed: primarily *occluded*, since the feeling does not display which alternative carries exploration value (face 1) or whether the verdict is fitting or projected (face 2, whose outward presentation the attentional definition builds in); *occluded* in face 3's forecast, and a candidate for [[self-maintained-cognitive-limits|self-maintenance]] if avoidance keeps it from correction; *unexplorable* in face 4 only in that the attentional definition rules out a satisfying engaged reading; and *unexplored* in whether a person can attribute her boredom to its cause. In the [[voids]] catalogue's sense it is framework-independent: a physicalist and a dualist describe the same gap.

**Occam's Razor Has Limits** binds the Map's own reasoning here. The simplest reading, that the object is dull or that low stimulation feels bad, fails as a general account: thinking is always available yet unrewarding for the untrained, overstimulation also bores, and field data are "less consistent with accounts that exclusively associate boredom with low arousal or with situations lacking in meaning" (Chin et al. 2017). That is ordinary scientific complication. The collapse of a simple story lends no support to a non-physical one, and the Map's reading of the void earns no preference by seeming richer than a computational one.

**Bidirectional Interaction** is compatible, with one firm point. On the opportunity-cost model, boredom's aversiveness tracks the value of disengaging; Agrawal et al. count it among the "phenomenological counterparts of these opportunity cost measures". William James (1890) cited "a well-known fact that pleasures are generally associated with beneficial, pains with detrimental, experiences" among facts that "seem explicable on the supposition that consciousness has causal efficacy." If boredom were causally idle, its general match to what it would be useful to feel, despite the misfires in faces 2 and 3, would need the epiphenomenalist's common-cause explanation. The Map does not concede that it is idle, and on the speculative [[attention-as-interface|attention-as-interface]] hypothesis reads boredom as the felt cost where attention is allocated. The point bears against [[the-epiphenomenalist-threat|epiphenomenalism]] only: an identity theorist also treats the feeling as efficacious, as the computation itself, so boredom does not discriminate interactionism from physicalism, and James is cited against automatism, not for dualism. The Map's reasons for rejecting the epiphenomenalist's correlation-without-causation reply are set out with [[tenets#^tenet-3-epiphenomenalism|the tenets]].

**Dualism** is compatible, without a discriminator. Boredom's phenomenal character is no harder for physicalism than any other affect's, and because a computational model accommodates the objectless feeling, the void weighs little for dualism. None of the psychologists cited is enlisted for it. **Minimal Quantum Interaction** and **No Many Worlds** have no bearing on this void.

The void's distinctive contributions are a hidden ground that is, on the opportunity-cost model, information about alternatives the feeling does not display, and a forecast error that avoidance may shield from correction, a candidate mechanism that involuntary waiting could test: a forecast that survives queues points to a memory or forecasting bias instead.
