## Executive Summary

**The thesis.** A self-ascription is IEM relative to its grounds when the subject cannot know, on those grounds, that something is F and judge herself F by wrongly taking that thing to be herself.

**Origin.** Wittgenstein's "use as subject" (*Blue Book*, 1933–34); named by Shoemaker (1968); memory only "de facto" immune (1970); explained by Evans (1982) as identification-freedom; *de re* vs *wh* split by Pryor (1999).

**Counterexamples.** Thought insertion is the standard case. The SEP holds it shows other-misidentification, not the self-misidentification IEM excludes; Lane and Liang (2011) take such cases to refute it.

**Significance.** The literature converges: IEM *generates* the Cartesian picture of the self rather than evidencing it (Wittgenstein, Strawson, Anscombe, Child). Bodily self-ascriptions are identification-free too, and "an immaterial ego could not have crossed legs" (Child). Anscombe's dualist-friendly horn yields at most "a stretch of" a Cartesian ego.

## Key Sources

### Wittgenstein, *The Blue and Brown Books* (Blackwell 1958), pp. 66–69 [F]

The distinction:
- "There are two different cases in the use of the word 'I' (or 'my') which I might call 'the use as object' and 'the use as subject'" (p. 66).
- "It is possible that, say in an accident, I should feel a pain in my arm, see a broken arm at my side, and think it is mine, when really it is my neighbour's" (p. 67).
- "To ask 'are you sure that it's you who have pains?' would be nonsensical" (p. 67).

The diagnosis: "this creates the illusion that we use this word to refer to something bodiless, which, however, has its seat in our body. In fact this seems to be the real ego, the one of which it was said, 'Cogito, ergo sum'" (p. 69).

Disputed: Wiseman 2019 **[A]** finds no IEM thesis in the passage ("a deep challenge to IEM"); Child 2026 **[F]** defends the standard reading.

### Shoemaker 1968 and 1970 (reprinted in *Identity, Cause, and Mind*, CUP 1984) [S]

**1968** (reprint pp. 6–18):
- The thesis: "it cannot happen that I am mistaken in saying 'I feel pain' because, although I do know of someone that feels pain, I am mistaken in thinking that person to be myself" (p. 8). Pryor 1999 p. 272 quotes the same sentence **[F-img]**.
- No identification: "My use of the word 'I' as the subject of my statement is not due to my having identified as myself something of which I know, or believe, or wish to say, that the predicate of my statement applies to it" (p. 9).
- Two kinds of immunity: "absolute" versus "circumstantial" (p. 8).

**1970**, "Persons and their Pasts" (reprint pp. 19–48):
- Develops quasi-memory (the SEP says he "introduces" it; the open quasi-memory research task tracks a Penelhum 1970 coiner lead, so do not call him the coiner).
- Gives memory claims only "de facto immunity to error through misidentification" (p. 46; the SEP cites the same page).
- Quotes Strawson's criterionless passage (p. 25).

### Strawson, *The Bounds of Sense* (Methuen 1966), pp. 164–166 [F]

The mechanism:
- "And now we come to the fact that lies at the root of the Cartesian illusion" (pp. 164–165).
- "When a man (a subject of experience) ascribes a current or directly remembered state of consciousness to himself, no use whatever of any criteria of personal identity is required to justify his use of the pronoun 'I' to refer to the subject of that experience" (p. 165).

The credit to Kant: "it is because Kant recognized this truth that his treatment of the subject is so greatly superior to Hume's" (p. 165).

Why reference survives: "'I' can be used without criteria of subject-identity and yet refer to a subject" (p. 165), because the links with public criteria "are not in practice severed".

The illusion that follows:
- "the illusion of a purely inner and yet subject-referring use for 'I'".
- Its referent then seems "an object of singular purity and simplicity - a pure, individual, immaterial substance" (p. 166).
- Kant's insight "explains why that notion, so used, is really quite empty of content" (p. 166).

Strawson includes "directly remembered" states. Shoemaker 1970 limits memory to de facto immunity, so the two disagree on the diachronic case.

### Anscombe, "The First Person" (1975; *Collected Papers* vol. 2, Blackwell 1981, pp. 21–36) [F]

Anscombe grants the immunity: "could he take the lady herself to be himself?" (p. 30).

The dilemma:
- "Thus we discover that if 'I' is a referring expression, then Descartes was right about what the referent was" (p. 31).
- "Nothing but a Cartesian Ego will serve. Or, rather, a stretch of one" (p. 31).
- "How do I know that 'I' is not ten thinkers thinking in unison?" (p. 31).

Her diagnosis: "Getting hold of the wrong object is excluded, and that makes us think that getting hold of the right object is guaranteed. But the reason is that there is no getting hold of an object at all" (p. 32).

She names the Map's move as one of the positions in an "endless" dispute: "Others in effect treat selves as postulated objects for 'I' to be names of in different people's mouths" (p. 32).

Her solution: "'I' is neither a name nor another kind of expression whose logical role is to make a reference, at all" (p. 32).

The p. 31 running head is missing from the OCR, so the p. 30/31 assignments are by line position.

### Evans, *The Varieties of Reference* (OUP 1982), ch. 7 [2nd via SEP supplement; Google Books ids dead to a control query]

- **Identification-dependent vs identification-free**: a judgement is identification-dependent when knowledge of it rests on "b is F" plus "a = b".
- **"I" modelled on "here"**: introspection, bodily awareness and self-locating perception are channels for identification-free thought, so "I" is modelled on "here" rather than "this". This answers Anscombe's sensory-deprivation tank.
- **Reference can fail**: "I"-thoughts fail to refer when the channels derive from different objects (§7.6).

### Pryor 1999, *Phil. Topics* 26(1–2): 271–304 [F-img pp. 271–273]

- Several importantly different sorts of misidentification exist, and immunity comes in two main sorts.
- On memory he sides with Shoemaker against Evans (p. 272, paraphrase).
- Section II is "*De Re* Misidentification".
- SEP: after Pryor, "wh-IEM" is treated as the more fundamental notion.

### Smith, "Self-Consciousness", SEP (rev. 14 June 2024), §2.3 and supplement "The Scope of IEM" [F]

- **Grounds-relativity**: "IEM is always relative, then, to the grounds on which a judgement is based."
- **Significance**: identification-freedom "gives rise to the idea that they pick out a private object, a soul". Smith cites Wittgenstein 1958, Strawson 1966 pp. 164–166 and Anscombe.
- **Proposed counterexamples**: thought insertion, alien limb, anarchic hand, "anonymous memory" and "anonymous vision". But "IEM, as typically understood, concerns an immunity to self-misidentification", and these cases show other-misidentification. So "none obviously challenge the claim that when these various information sources do ground self-ascriptions, those first-personal thoughts are IEM."
- **The live threat**: fantasy cases (quasi-memory, quasi-proprioception), which turn on contested conditions for *de re* thought.

### Campbell 1999b and Coliva 2002 [Campbell via Coliva's opening paragraph]

**Campbell's claim** (as Coliva reports it): the patient "is introspectively aware of a certain thought and yet she is wrong in identifying whose thought it is".

**Coliva's reply**: thought insertion "is not a counterexample to the view that introspection-based mental self-ascriptions are logically IEM" (opening paragraph, via Semantic Scholar) **[A]**.

### Lane & Liang 2011, *J. Phil.* 108(2): 78–99 [A]

- "we deny that this relationship is tautological; instead, we treat Shoemaker's principle (IEM) as a hypothesis."
- Their conclusion: "not only can the what of conscious experience be misrepresented, so too can the who."

## Major Positions and Key Debates

**RQ1: variants.**
- *Grounds-relative IEM* (standard): a judgement is IEM relative to grounds G if one cannot know via G that *a* is F and judge oneself F through a false belief that *a* = oneself.
- *Absolute / circumstantial* (Shoemaker 1968).
- *Logical / de facto* (Shoemaker 1970; Coliva).
- *De re / wh* (Pryor 1999). Wh-IEM entails IEM but not conversely (SEP).
- *Identification-free / -dependent* (Evans): the explanation, not a variant.
- "Wide vs strict" (the brief's label) occurs in **no source read**; do not use it.

**RQ2: does thought insertion refute IEM?**

| Verdict | Who | Argument |
|---|---|---|
| Refutes | Campbell 1999 (per Coliva); Lane & Liang 2011 | Introspection does not guarantee whose thought it is |
| Does not refute | Coliva 2002; Gallagher 2000; SEP; de Vignemont 2012 [M] | The patient withholds "I think that p" or attributes the thought outward. That is other-misidentification, never a false "I = *a*" |
| Reframes | García-Carpintero 2024 | The answer varies with the kind of thought |

The debate is open. For the Map, the case separates *ownership* from *self-identification*: mineness can be disturbed while the "I" goes unmisidentified. This fits the Guillot split that `concepts/thought-insertion` L72–80 already uses.

**RQ3: what IEM implies about the subject.**
- *Cartesian genealogy* (Wittgenstein, Strawson, SEP, Child): identification-freedom makes "I" seem to name "something bodiless". Bodily IEM is the control case.
- *No reference* (Anscombe): a referring "I" with guaranteed reference needs a Cartesian ego, and only "a stretch of one". She denies that "I" refers. Evans and Campbell (2012, "On the thesis that 'I' is not a referring term" [M]) deny the dilemma's premise.
- *Kantian* (Strawson; Longuenesse): the abstracted "I" is "empty of content".

**Mapping onto the Kant page's three exposures** [Map reconstruction]:
- *Felt mine-ness as evidence*: Strawson states the mechanism.
- *Unity read as simplicity*: Anscombe's "ten thinkers thinking in unison" is the collective-bearer objection (A352–353) put in the first person.
- *The diachronic clause*: quasi-memory leaves memory only de facto immune, and "a stretch of one" is the Cartesian horn's ceiling.

The Kant page's "posited, not inferred" verdict is untouched. Anscombe even lists the posit ("selves as postulated objects").

## Map Relevance (RQ4, P-M1 tiers)

**The phenomenon** is the field's standard view, with its scope beyond introspection contested. It is *established* as a description of the debate's consensus, not of any metaphysics.

**Bearing on the posited subject: compatible, no support.** Every reading predicts IEM (Child's embodied person, Anscombe's non-referring "I", Kant's formal "I", the Cartesian ego), so the datum discriminates nothing, matching P-I1's grade D for felt this-ness. IEM is **not** evidence that the subject is non-physical.

**Defeater-removal: none.**

**Defeater for Map inferences: yes.** The ownership page infers irreducibility from the absence of "whose" in physical description (L84, L126). The IEM literature predicts that absence from how "I"-thought works, with bodily cases as control. That is the Kant page's first exposure at a named locus.

**P-I1's would-shift clause.** IEM debunks an appearance about *reference*, not the felt this-ness of experience. It is a partial candidate, not a trigger. Record it as "weighed, not met" if a positions-evolve pass touches P-I1.

**Tenet 4 and the vertiginous question.** Anscombe's "no getting hold of an object at all" implies that "why am I *this* one?" asks for an identification "I"-thought never makes. `topics/vertiginous-question` L84–88 treats deflation without this form. P-I2 already concedes the conditional, so no new concession is owed.

**Tenet 3: no bearing.**
