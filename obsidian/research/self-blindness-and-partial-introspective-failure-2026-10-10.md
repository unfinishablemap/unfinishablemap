**Headline findings.**
- (a) **Anton syndrome, anosognosia for hemiplegia and DID inter-identity amnesia are not counterexamples to the impossibility of self-blindness.** Each fails at least two of the five conditions a counterexample needs ([[#clinical|below]]).
- (b) **Shoemaker's own texts provide for local failures.**
  - He grants, for the sake of discussion, that error about one's own mental states "of whatever kind" is logically possible (1988, p. 25).
  - He holds that "Failures of rationality, such as those involved in self-deception, can bring with them failures in access" (1990, p. 71).
  - He allows repressed beliefs that are sincerely denied (1995, p. 80).
  - He confines self-intimation to *available* beliefs (1995, p. 92).

  What the thesis forbids is total, capacity-level blindness in a rational subject, and failure as "the rule rather than the exception" (p. 71).
- (c) **The live issue is the one the Anton page already prices for Chalmers.** Can "rational" be specified independently of the self-knowledge failure? Gertler (2011, ch. 5, as reported in her SEP entry) argues that a definitional reading makes "No rational creature is self-blind" a de dicto truth that inner-sense theorists can accept. In the local cases Shoemaker allows, the irrationality he cites (self-deception, unconscious belief) is diagnosed largely through the access failure itself. Shoemaker concedes the cost: "All of this, I realize, puts a rather heavy burden on the concept of rationality" (1988, p. 49).
- (d) **The review's Chalmers parallel holds in shape only.** Both thinkers restrict first-person reliability to rational subjects. They differ in direction: Chalmers is concerned with false positives, Shoemaker with omissions. They differ in modality: Chalmers makes a plausibility claim, Shoemaker a claim of conceptual impossibility. And they differ in their primary object: experience for Chalmers, attitudes for Shoemaker.
- (e) **Dualism (Tenet 1): orthogonal. Compatible, no support, in both directions.** Shoemaker is a functionalist who holds that "there are lots of good reasons for rejecting Cartesian dualism" (p. 24). Both sides of the self-blindness debate are physicalist-compatible.

## Executive Summary

Sydney Shoemaker argued, in "On Knowing One's Own Mind" (1988) and Lecture II of the Royce Lectures "Self-Knowledge and 'Inner Sense'" (1994), that a rational subject who has the concepts of mental states cannot be *self-blind*. He defines the self-blind creature as one that could "frame and understand ascriptions to itself of various mental states, but would be incapable of knowing by self-acquaintance whether such self-ascriptions were true" (p. 31). His target is the "broad perceptual model" of introspection, which pairs a causal condition with an independence condition. On that model the introspective faculty could fail as sight fails, leaving rationality intact. His argument, the George reductio, uses Moore's paradox to show that such a creature's rationality would make it behave exactly like someone with ordinary self-knowledge. He takes that result as a reductio of the supposition that self-blindness is possible.

The main critics deny that the argument reaches inner sense:
- Kind (2003) says Shoemaker "conflates introspective awareness and self-knowledge".
- Gertler (2011) argues the inner-sense theorist can accept the conclusion de dicto.
- Kimble (2013) defends the broad perceptual model directly.

Others grant the impossibility and dispute its explanation. Peacocke (1998) derives it from possession conditions for the concept of belief. Parrott (2017) derives it from rational cognition while keeping states and beliefs about them distinct.

Nichols and Stich (2003) supply the empirical counter-strategy: domain-specific breakdowns of a self-monitoring mechanism, with schizophrenia as their candidate.

Measured against the thesis itself, the Map's clinical record supplies no counterexample. Anton is a false *positive* about perceptual experience, so it runs in the wrong direction. Anosognosia concerns the *body*, and its implicit registration is not shown to be a belief available to the subject. DID is either two "occupants", which Shoemaker himself calls "not self-blindness" (p. 234), or a local, motivated failure of availability, which he allows. What the record does show is local, domain-specific, sometimes reversible failures of access in subjects whose rationality is otherwise intact or contested. That is the shape monitoring accounts expect. It is not discriminating, because Shoemaker's allowance for local failures absorbs it.

For the Map the topic is orthogonal to Tenet 1. It bears only on how much weight first-person *attitude* reports can carry, a domain the Map's layered authority does not yet cover.

### The definition

> "self-blind creature could frame and understand ascriptions to itself of various mental states, but would be incapable of knowing by self-acquaintance whether such self-ascriptions were true. Only if self-blindness were a conceptual possibility would it be appropriate to think of the capacity for self-acquaintance as a quasi-perceptual capacity, which is something over and above the capacity to have and conceive of the mental states in question. And it is the appropriateness of so thinking of it that I am anxious to deny." (1988, p. 31) [S, three overlapping snippets, consistent at the joins]

The 1994 definition (p. 226) is outside the searchable preview. The Byrne & Paul handout H7 transcribes it [2nd]. On that transcription the creature "must have the ability to conceive of those facts and phenomena", and it lacks only introspective access. It "should learn of them in the way others might learn of them, i.e., by observing her own behavior, or by discovering facts about her own neurophysiological states." Quote this only as a transcription, or replace it with the 1988 wording.

SEP "Introspection" glosses the definition this way [F]: "a rational creature with a conception of the relevant mental states, and who can entertain the thought that they have this or that belief, desire, intention, or pain, but who nonetheless utterly lacks introspective access to the type of mental state in question."

### The target: the broad perceptual model

Lecture I rejects the *object*-perception model: "There is no organ of introspection that the self directs either to itself or to mental entities of any kind" (p. 207) [S]. Lecture II then takes on a weaker model defined by two conditions [S, p. 224]:
- one "(call it the causal condition) saying that our beliefs about our mental states are caused by those mental states, via a reliable belief-producing mechanism, thereby qualifying as knowledge of those states and events";
- the other "(call it the independence condition) saying that the existence of these states and events is independent of their being known in this way, and even of there existing the mechanisms that make such knowledge [possible]". The bracketed word is supplied from Parrott's quotation of the same sentence, at PPR p. 271 [F].

Parrott notes that the self-blindness argument engages only the independence condition, since Shoemaker calls the possibility of self-blindness a "consequence of the independence condition" (PPR p. 273, as quoted by Parrott) [2nd].

**Shoemaker opposes Cartesianism as well.** "Although I am equally opposed to Cartesianism and the broad perceptual model, it is only the latter that I shall be arguing against here" (p. 225) [S]. On the same page he lists data "equally damaging to the Cartesian conception":
- self-deception;
- Nisbett and Wilson's confabulation findings;
- "various phenomena that have been observed in people who have suffered brain damage. One of the most striking is 'blindsight.'"

So Shoemaker himself treats the clinical record as evidence against *Cartesian* transparency, which he also rejects, and not against his own thesis. This matters for the clinical assessment below.

### The George argument (belief)

"George" is a "self-blind man who has human intelligence and conceptual capacity. My aim will be to reduce to absurdity the supposition that he is self-blind" (1988, p. 39) [S]. The 1994 statement names the conditions as "normal intelligence, rationality, and conceptual capacity" (p. 239) [S]. The steps, from 1988 pp. 34–42 and 1994 p. 237, with SEP "Introspection" [F] as the reconstruction:

1. George's rationality and conceptual grasp make him appreciate "the logical impropriety of affirming something while denying that one believes it, or denying something while affirming that one believes it" (1994, p. 237) [S]. He therefore avoids Moore-paradoxical assertions.
2. He answers "Do you believe that p?" as he answers "p?", and he prefixes "I believe" where it is pragmatically apt (Parrott's reconstruction, pp. 5–6 [F]).
3. So there would be nothing in his behaviour "that would give away the fact that he lacks self-acquaintance" (1988, p. 36). The snippet [S] begins at "give away"; Parrott quotes the full clause (p. 6) [F].
4. If that were so, how could we be sure self-blindness is not "the normal condition of mankind"? "But rather than conclude that self-acquaintance provides no benefits that would not be available without it, and that it is questionable whether we have it, it seems better to take the considerations" as a reductio of the possibility (p. 36) [S to "considerations"]. The rest of the sentence was not verified; H7 gives "[above] as a reductio ad absurdum of the view that self-blindness [with respect to beliefs] is a possibility" [2nd].
5. The 1994 criterion: "If self-blindness is possible with respect to a given mental phenomenon, one would think that there should be something that would show, or at any rate provide good evidence, that someone was afflicted with such self-blindness" (p. 233) [S, and quoted identically by Parrott, p. 6, F].

### Extensions: pain, perceptual experience, the will

**Pain.** Creatures whose "only access to their pains is a third-person access i.e., observing their own behavior, or their own inner physiology" (p. 227) [S] would have pains that hurt "but they don't hurt him" (`06Shoe` handout transcription [2nd]). Shoemaker continues: "But this of course is nonsense. We have seen that if you take away the link with introspective beliefs from the total causal role of pain, you take away a lot else" (p. 228) [S]. Hence "self-blindness with respect to pain seems to be an impossibility" (p. 229) [S], and the same goes "for itches, tingles, and the like", and for "having a ringing in one's ears" (p. 229) [S].

**Perceptual experience.** This extension contains the move that matters most for the clinical record [S, p. 231]:

> "I do not deny that this case is possible, but I do deny that it should count as a case of self-blindness – for I deny that the person, as described, has perceptual experiences in the sense we do. By hypothesis, she has states that play some of the functional role of perceptual experiences."

The `06Shoe` handout reads "in the sense *that* we do". The Google Books text has no "that". Use the Google Books form.

**The will: the agent/agnostic case** (pp. 234–235). Imagine a body whose verbal professions of "introspective ignorance of what is going on" are interspersed with purposive behaviour. Shoemaker offers two readings.
- **Two persons.** There may be two persons or "personalities", one controlling the action and one making the professions. "In that case, of course, the blindness is not self-blindness, and not introspective blindness; it is a matter of one of the occupants of the body being blind to what the other occupant is up to" (p. 234) [S].
- **One person.** "If anything would make it reasonable to say that the agent and the agnostic are one and the same person, it would be the fact that what the agent is doing fits with, and is 'rationalized' by, beliefs and desire that can independently be ascribed to the agnostic" (p. 234) [S].

### What the thesis permits {#scope}

This is the part of Shoemaker the clinical question turns on. Each item is [S]:

- **Fallibility, granted for discussion.** "In the present essay I shall assume, at least for the sake of discussion, that the first of these denials is correct – that it is logically possible for a person to be mistaken about his own mental states, of whatever kind" (1988, p. 25). What Shoemaker resists is the *second* denial: that it is not "in any way constitutive or definitive of mental states … that these states intimate their existence to their possessors in a special and direct way" (p. 25).
- **Local failures travel with failures of rationality.** The 1988 essay ends on "failures of rationality that manifest themselves in failures of self-knowledge. And such I assume we have in cases of unconscious belief. All of this, I realize, puts a rather heavy burden on the concept of rationality. Fortunately, that is a matter for another essay, which I haven't the slightest idea of how to write" (p. 49).
- **The rule/exception line.** "[A]n appreciable degree of rationality, and the first-person access that comes with it, is required for the very existence of mental states of the sort we have. It is less than perfect because our rationality is less than perfect. Failures of rationality, such as those involved in self-deception, can bring with them failures in access. But if we try to suppose such failure the rule rather than the exception, we overstep the bounds of intelligibility" (1990, p. 71).
- **Sincerely denied belief.** "self-deception in which someone believes that P and yet, when the content P is presented as a candidate for assent, sincerely denies that she believes it. Presumably this happens in cases of repressed belief. And I think that there are more ordinary cases, not involving even garden variety self-deception, in which this can happen. For lots of the beliefs that one can be said to have at a time are not available to one at that time" (1995, p. 80).
- **Two-stage self-intimation.** "The first stage is the transition from the mere possession of a belief to its being available." The second "is the transition from a belief's being available to the subject's having the available second-order belief that she has it" (1995, p. 92).
- **Idealisation.** Full self-intimation of belief is "tantalizingly close to being true. But I think the most we can claim is that it is true in some conceptually central class of cases, ones blessed with more rationality than is usual in human affairs" (1995, p. 93).

**Scope statement for the page.** The impossibility thesis concerns the *capacity* for first-person access to a *kind* of state, in a subject whose rationality and conceptual competence are normal. Parrott states it cleanly [F, p. 6]:

> "Even if we can conceive of someone lacking first-person access to some of her beliefs, this is not self-blindness. We all lack that kind of access to some of our beliefs some of the time, but none of us are self-blind."

His note 17 [F, p. 10] adds: "the question is whether, insofar as one is a rational agent, one must have a capacity for first-person access. If so, this is compatible with one lacking first-person access to a range of attitudes."

### The positive view

Shoemaker's explanation is functionalist. The relevant passage reads: "When we speak of the realization, or implementation, of a mental state, we should distinguish between what I call the core realization of it and what I call the total realization of it" (p. 242) [S]. SEP "Self-Knowledge" [F] summarises: "in rational creatures, the causal role defining a mental state are also sufficient for belief that one is in that mental state (Shoemaker 1994: 287–9)." Parrott [F, n. 4] quotes the two 1994 proposals. On the first, the first-order state and the self-belief have different core realizations but "their total realizations overlap in a certain way". On the second, they share a core realization, and the first-order state's total realization "is a proper part of the total realization of the first-person belief that one has it" (PPR p. 288). Shoemaker 2009 (*Erkenntnis* 71(1), 35–51, doi 10.1007/s10670-009-9172-z [M]) restates the view. Parrott quotes "he acts as if he believes that p, when he does so believe, which seems contrary to the supposition that he is self-blind with respect to his beliefs" (2009, p. 37) [2nd].

**Author stance, to be carried into any article.** Shoemaker is a functionalist. He questions whether qualia that are not introspectively accessible are even coherent. Of blindsight he asks: "do we think that they have sensory states that have qualia, but that these are not introspectively accessible to them? Is it even coherent to think that?" ("Qualia and consciousness", reprint p. 123) [S]. These are posed as questions, and his answer was not read. He also says "there are lots of good reasons for rejecting Cartesian dualism" (p. 24) [S]. He must never be cited as supporting the Map's subject, its dualism, or its Layer 1 near-incorrigibility. His 1988 essay *grants* fallibility "of whatever kind".

## Critiques {#critiques}

**Kind (2003)** [A; publisher page and OpenAlex]. Amy Kind, "Shoemaker, Self-Blindness and Moore's Paradox", *The Philosophical Quarterly* 53(210), 39–48, doi 10.1111/1467-9213.00294. From the abstract:
- "I show how the 'inner–sense' (quasi–perceptual) view of introspection can be defended against Shoemaker's influential 'argument from self–blindness'."
- "But because Shoemaker's discussion conflates introspective awareness and self–knowledge, he cannot establish his conclusion. There is third–person evidence available to the self–blind which Shoemaker ignores, and it can account for the considerations from Moore's paradox that he raises."

SEP "Self-Knowledge" [F] glosses the paper: "Even if Shoemaker is right that rational creatures will generally be capable of self-awareness, this conclusion is silent as to how such awareness occurs." The full text was not read, so characterise Kind no further than this.

**Gertler (2011, ch. 5)** [2nd: SEP "Self-Knowledge", Gertler's own entry]. Brie Gertler, *Self-Knowledge*, Routledge (doi 10.4324/9780203835678; Crossref online date 2010-11-25) [M]. The SEP entry reports: "Gertler (2011a: ch. 5) argues that the inner sense theorist can block Shoemaker's objection by stipulating that no creature qualifies as rational if it is self-blind. 'No rational creature is self-blind' would then be a de dicto truth and, as such, is compatible with the inner sense account. Shoemaker's challenge to inner sense views requires a stronger thesis, namely that the capacity for self-knowledge is a de re necessary characteristic of rational beings." **This is the philosophical form of the independent-markers demand** on the Anton page (L84).

**The SEP dilemma for rationalist accounts** [F, SEP "Self-Knowledge" §3.6]. "if a conception of rationality is too demanding, then it is questionable whether it applies to cognitively flawed creatures like us (Kornblith 2012; Cassam 2015)." Success "depends on whether there are conceptions of rationality, consciousness, belief, etc. that are robust enough to exclude the possibility of self-blindness yet psychologically realistic enough to explain the cognitive achievements of actual human beings." The same section reports Stoljar's (2018, 2019) "introspective principle" and Smithies's (2019) Moorean argument as successors to Shoemaker. These were not checked at source.

**Parrott (2017)** [F]. Matthew Parrott, "Self-Blindness and Self-Knowledge", *Philosophers' Imprint* 17(16), August 2017, 22 pp. (CC BY-NC-ND). Abstract: "I shall argue that even if self-blindness is impossible, the best explanation for this does not deny that a person's psychological states are wholly distinct from her beliefs about them … One advantage of this account over constitutive theories of self-knowledge is that it is better placed to explain our fallibility and ignorance." Asked whether a self-blind person is possible, Parrott answers "I tend to agree with Shoemaker that it is not" (p. 6), and he names dissenters: "See, for instance, Kind (2003) and Finkelstein (1999)" (n. 11). His scope note excludes judgement-insensitive states: "if there are any attitudes that are insensitive to judgment — perhaps implicit attitudes or what Gendler (2008) calls 'aliefs' — these will fall outside the scope of this essay" (p. 5). He also flags "a kind of dissociation, or alienation" as a distinct irrationality, "clearly possible even in cases where a subject's judgments about reasons for belief are epistemically flawless" (n. 25, p. 15). **Parrott is the best published route from the debate to the clinical cases.** His fallibility-friendly account is the one a page should set against Shoemaker's constitutivism.

**Peacocke (1998)** [M + 2nd]. Christopher Peacocke, "Conscious Attitudes, Attention, and Self-Knowledge", in C. Wright, B. C. Smith & C. He accepts the impossibility but explains it through the possession conditions for the concept of belief, so that "Anyone who lacked this sort of immediate access to her own beliefs would fail to have the concept of belief, and so would not even qualify as a candidate for self-blindness." Those are Speaks's words, not Peacocke's. **Not read in primary; do not quote Peacocke.**

## The Clinical Record, Case by Case {#clinical}

### What a counterexample would need

The following conditions are a **Map reconstruction** from the texts above. A clinical case refutes the impossibility thesis only if it shows:

- **(C1) Omission.** A first-order state is present and the subject has no first-person access to it. A false self-ascription (commission) does not qualify, because Shoemaker grants fallibility (p. 25).
- **(C2) Object.** The state is of a covered kind: belief, desire, intention, pain, or perceptual experience in the full sense. A bodily condition or a capacity does not qualify.
- **(C3) Scope.** The failure is capacity-level, across a kind of state. A local or occasional lapse does not qualify (pp. 71, 80; Parrott p. 6).
- **(C4) Rationality.** Normal rationality and conceptual competence are certified by markers independent of the failure itself. Gertler's de dicto point and Anton L84 both require this.
- **(C5) One subject.** The case is not two "occupants" (p. 234).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anton, reading A ([[anton-syndrome-and-the-sincere-report-of-seeing]]) | **Fails**: false positive ("I can see") | Perceptual experience (modal grain) | One modality | Mostly impaired on independent markers (L84) | ✓ | Outside. It tests Chalmers's commission clause, not Shoemaker's |
| Anton, reading B | **Fails**: a seeming is present and self-ascribed; its *source* is misjudged | Imagery taken for perception | One modality | As above | ✓ | Outside |
| Inverse Anton, blindsight ([[blindsight]]) | Omission-*shaped* | Whether a visual *experience* is present is the contested point | A field region or one modality | Cognition generally intact | ✓ | Outside on Shoemaker's p. 231 classification. Whether that move is principled is open |
| Anosognosia for hemiplegia ([[anosognosia-and-the-reversible-self-monitoring-channel]]) | Commission about the limb, with implicit registration under explicit denial | **Fails**: the paralysis is a *bodily* state; the implicit state is not shown to be an available belief | Domain-specific and reversible | Two-factor accounts posit impaired belief evaluation | ✓ | Outside |

### Anton syndrome

**Shows.** Under pathology, a sincere first-person report of seeing can occur with no visual seeming behind it (reading A), or with imagery misclassified as perception (reading B). The Map already draws the right conclusion: "The case also fixes where authority attaches: to *seemings*, not to *reports*" (L44).

**Does not show** that a rational subject can lack first-person access to a state it has. Three independent reasons:
1. The error runs in the wrong direction: a false positive, where self-blindness is an omission.
2. Shoemaker grants fallibility about one's own states "of whatever kind" (p. 25).
3. Rationality is mostly impaired on markers independent of the error: "dementia nearly always coexists"; Levine's "severe intellectual impairment"; confabulation that resists correction (all on L84).

The pressure cases are Swartz and Brust's "otherwise mentally normal" patient and Seneca's Harpaste. They press on Chalmers's clause, which is about massive error regarding experience. They do not press on Shoemaker's.

### Inverse Anton and blindsight

These cases do run in the omission direction. Hartmann et al.'s patient named objects in a preserved field while "typically stating 'I feel it'" (Anton L54). Shoemaker anticipated the type, citing blindsight at p. 225, and his classification move at p. 231 absorbs it: states that "play some of the functional role of perceptual experiences" are not "perceptual experiences in the sense we do".

**What the case exposes is the cost of the thesis's protection.** Any unaccessed state can be reclassified as not a full-blown state of the kind. That keeps the thesis safe from clinical refutation, and it also means clinical data cannot confirm it. Two separate questions follow:
- Whether the reclassification is principled. It needs a criterion for "in the sense we do" that does not mention introspective access.
- Whether a visual experience is present at all, which the Anton table lists as "contested".

### Anosognosia for hemiplegia

**The object of the error is the body.** A false belief that one's arm moves is a false belief about the world. Shoemaker's thesis concerns mental states. The mental-state candidate is the implicit registration of the deficit. Fotopoulou et al. 2010 found slowing on deficit-related sentences "despite their explicit denial of the self-relevance" (anosognosia L68), in 6 of 7 patients. For this to count as self-blindness, that registration would have to be a belief in Shoemaker's sense: available and rationalising action. The data show interference effects, not use in deliberation. It therefore falls under his availability distinction (1995, pp. 80, 92) and Parrott's exclusion of judgement-insensitive states (p. 5).

On two-factor accounts the second factor is "impaired belief evaluation" (L80), a rationality failure of the kind p. 71 allows.

**Shows.** Self-monitoring of *bodily* deficits is domain-specific and lesion-dissociable. Vocat et al. report "distinct monitoring mechanisms for each of these domains" (L44). It is also reversible by a spared route, as in the video case. Rival monitoring accounts expect exactly this shape (Nichols & Stich make the parallel argument for schizophrenia). That expectation is a **Map reconstruction**.

**Does not show.** It is not evidence about introspection of attitudes, because the monitored object is the body. It does not discriminate between inner-sense and constitutive accounts of how we know our beliefs.

### DID inter-identity amnesia

Read at the corrected level the Map's page now uses: identities report no memory of each other's material. Yet they perform like healthy controls on feigning-resistant tests (Huntjens et al. 2003), and on a concealed-information task (2012). On easily feigned recall and recognition tests the profile is amnesia-like, "with some leakage", and simulators match it (Marsh et al. 2021; L68, L76). The authors' inference is that it is "not possible to determine whether DID participants presented a true inability to access information experienced in another identity" (L78).

**Why it falls outside the thesis:**
- **(C2)** The retained, used information is a memory trace, not an occurrent belief. The self-knowledge error is a false belief about a capacity: patients are "holding incorrect beliefs about their own memory functioning" (Huntjens et al. 2006, L51).
- **(C5)** Shoemaker's dilemma applies directly (p. 234).
  - If the identities are distinct "occupants", "the blindness is not self-blindness".
  - If the patient is one person, that is so because the agent's doings are "rationalized" by beliefs and desires ascribable to the agnostic. The professions of ignorance are then local, motivated failures of availability, of the self-deception kind he allows (pp. 71, 80). The control-level reading the Map lists, Dimitrova et al.'s "inter-identity avoidance", is this shape.
- **(C4)** DID patients show no general cognitive impairment. The only irrationality Shoemaker can cite is the motivated non-search, and that is diagnosed through the access failure plus a motive. **This is where the de dicto worry bites hardest.** DID is the most informative case for whether the restriction is independent, and it is still not a counterexample.

**Note.** On DID the clinical record meets Shoemaker's own detectability criterion ("something that would show … that someone was afflicted", p. 233). Indirect tests do show the access failure. But they show a *local* one, which the thesis permits.

### Thought insertion

Nichols and Stich's schizophrenia candidate is described in SEP as patients who "struggle to report, or deny the existence of, ongoing thoughts" [F]. On the Map's page, four of five readings keep the inserted thought "still accessible to the patient alone" (L36). The patient reports the thought and disowns its authorship, so access is intact and attribution fails. Fails C1. The case belongs to the ownership and agency questions the wing already treats.

### Alexithymia

This case is not named in the task. It is the strongest rational-subject case the Map holds. It is "a normally-distributed personality trait — elevated in roughly 10% of the general population" ([[alexithymia]] L23), so C4 is met on any reading. The page's "honest headline" is that "present-but-inaccessible versus absent is unresolved" (L51).

If the affect is present but inaccessible, alexithymia is a partial, domain-specific degradation of first-person *classification* in rational subjects. It is still not self-blindness:
- The difficulty is graded.
- It concerns identifying and describing a state, not first-person access to whether a state is occurring.
- Emotions are not among Shoemaker's paradigm kinds.

**Use on a page.** Alexithymia is the honest pressure case for *any* claim that rationality guarantees accurate self-classification. It shows the guarantee cannot extend that far. It leaves the impossibility of total blindness standing.

### What the record does and does not establish

- **Established (clinical, at the Map pages' own grades):** local, domain-specific failures of first-person report and access occur. Some occur in subjects with intact general cognition (DID, alexithymia, the pressure cases of Anton and blindsight), and some are reversible by a spared route (anosognosia).
- **Map reconstruction, suggestive at most:** the pattern looks like what monitoring (inner-sense-type) accounts predict. It is non-discriminating. Shoemaker's allowance for local failures (pp. 49, 71, 80, 92–93) accommodates the pattern, and the clearest dissociations concern the body or memory, not attitudes.
- **Not established:** a rational, conceptually competent subject who lacks the *capacity* for first-person access to a kind of state. Nothing in the record touches the impossibility thesis as Shoemaker states it.
- **Open and worth a page:** whether "rational" can be specified without mentioning the self-knowledge failures it is meant to explain. This question links Gertler's de dicto point, the SEP dilemma and Anton L84.

## Is Chalmers's Restriction the Same Shape? {#chalmers}

The optimistic review called the parallel "the Map's reconstruction and must be checked at source". Checked against consc.net [F]:

| | Chalmers (1995), "Absent Qualia, Fading Qualia, Dancing Qualia" | Shoemaker (1988, 1994) |
|---|---|---|
| Restriction | "The plausible claim is not that no system can be massively mistaken about its experiences, but that no rational system whose cognitive mechanisms are unimpaired can be so mistaken." | Self-blindness is impossible for a subject of "normal intelligence, rationality, and conceptual capacity" (p. 239) |
| Direction | **Commission**: Joe "will even judge that he has all these complex experiences that he in fact lacks" | **Omission**: having a state without being able to know it first-personally |
| Modality | **Plausibility**: "To be sure, Fading Qualia may be logically possible"; "We have little reason to believe that consciousness is such an ill-behaved phenomenon" | **Conceptual**: "Only if self-blindness were a conceptual possibility…" (p. 31) |
| Object | Conscious experience | Primarily belief, desire and intention; pain and perceptual experience by separate arguments |
| Scope | Massive, systematic error ("so systematically out of touch with its experiences") | Total lack of the capacity; local failures allowed |
| Independent markers offered | "any unusually poor inferential connections between his beliefs, or any systematic psychiatric disorder"; "none of the confabulatory symptoms of those with blindness denial" | For local failures: self-deception, unconscious belief, unavailability. These are largely identified through the failure |

**Verdict.** The review's "same shape" is accurate for the restriction: both guarantees hold only for unimpaired rational subjects. The two thinkers stand behind *different theses*. The consequence matters for routing:
- **Anton** (commission, about experience) is a test of Chalmers's clause. The Anton page already engages it there.
- **DID and implicit anosognosia** (omission-shaped) would be tests of Shoemaker's clause, if they were not local.

A page should not write "Anton tests Shoemaker" or "Shoemaker and Chalmers make the same claim". The shared liability is real. Both clauses escape circularity only to the extent that impairment is identified by independent markers. Chalmers names markers. One of them, "confabulatory symptoms", sits close to the error itself. Shoemaker names fewer, and concedes the "heavy burden".

## Presentation Is Not Certification: Relation and Overclaim Check {#certification}

The wing's unnamed rule, as the review states it: a first-person state can present a fact without certifying it. The rows are felt ownership (quasi-memory), felt pastness (episodic memory), a sincere report of seeing (Anton), felt initiation (thought insertion) and reported absence (metacognitive access).

**How self-blindness relates.** It is orthogonal in three respects:
1. **Different direction.** The rule governs the step from a presentation to the fact presented: does the seeming certify? Self-blindness governs the step from a state to its availability for self-ascription: must the state be knowable? Shoemaker does not claim that self-ascription certifies its object. He grants error "of whatever kind" (p. 25). He confines even belief's self-intimation to cases "blessed with more rationality than is usual in human affairs" (p. 93).
2. **Different object.** The rule's rows are *presentations*: felt or phenomenal ways a state appears. Shoemaker denies that self-knowledge of attitudes involves any presentation: there is "no organ of introspection" (p. 207), and Lecture I denies an introspective sense-experience. His topic is a non-presentational self-ascription of attitudes.
3. **No new row, no counter-row.** Self-blindness neither instantiates the rule nor counts against it.

**Overclaims a page must avoid:**
- (i) Listing self-blindness, or Shoemaker, as another instance of "presentation without certification".
- (ii) Inferring from the convergence that the clinical cases refute Shoemaker. They fall outside his thesis on independent grounds.
- (iii) Treating Shoemaker's thesis as a certification claim that supports Layer 1 near-incorrigibility. He grants fallibility, and Layer 1 rests on [[self-stultification]], not on his argument.
- (iv) Treating the wing's convergence as evidence. The review itself says convergence on a restraint "cannot inflate a tier".

**One compatible junction.** [[metacognitive-access-versus-retention]] Rule 2 says "Read reported absence as evidence about access." Shoemaker would agree that reported absence is evidence about access, and that access fails locally where availability or rationality fails. Nothing in his view lets reported absence reach experience, so Rule 2 stands as written.

## Relation to Site Perspective {#map-relevance}

These grades use the vocabulary of [[evidential-status-discipline]]. Philosophical claims carry the four editor-internal labels. Tenet bearings use the compatible / suggestive / discriminating ladder.

- **Claim labels.**
  - Shoemaker's thesis and its scope are a *documented textual claim* (pages above).
  - Whether the thesis is true is contested: Kind, Gertler and Kimble against; Parrott, Peacocke and Stoljar for impossibility, with rival explanations.
  - The five-condition test, the routing of Anton to Chalmers, and "the clinical cases fall outside" are each a *Map reconstruction*.
- **[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]: orthogonal. Compatible, no support, in either direction.**
  - Both live accounts are physicalist-compatible: Shoemaker's constitution through core and total realization, and inner-sense monitoring (Armstrong, Lycan; Nichols & Stich's Monitoring Mechanism).
  - If self-blindness is impossible, a functionalist explains that natively, as a fact about the causal roles of attitudes in rational systems.
  - If it is possible, a monitoring mechanism is a physical detector.
  - Neither outcome bears on irreducibility, and the debate supplies no discriminator.
  - Author-stance firewall: Shoemaker rejects Cartesian dualism (p. 24) and opposes Cartesian transparency (p. 225).
- **Where it bears: the access/report layer for attitudes.** The question is how much weight first-person reports of belief, desire and intention can carry. The Map's layered authority (phenomenal-authority L98–106) is framed for phenomenal states and does not cover this domain. That is the page's reason to exist.
- **Tension, recorded rather than adjudicated.** Shoemaker's classification move at p. 231 and his doubt about inaccessible qualia (p. 123) run against the Map's openness to phenomenal states without report: the overflow reading on [[introspection]], and [[covert-consciousness-and-cognitive-motor-dissociation]]. This is a framework-boundary disagreement. Neither side refutes the other with the clinical data.
- **[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]: compatible, no support.** The Map argues from reports against epiphenomenalism, and that argument already survives Anton only "as a claim about report reliability in unimpaired systems" (Anton L113). Shoemaker's account explains why rational subjects' self-ascriptions are reliable through functional constitution, without phenomenal causation. That gives the rival a mechanism of the kind the correlation reply invokes. The Map already treats the argument as suggestive, not decisive, so nothing is lost and nothing is gained.
- **[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]].** Parsimony cannot choose between inner sense and constitution. Neither choice raises any Map claim.
- **[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]], [[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]]:** not engaged.

## Key Debates

- **Does the George argument reach inner sense?** Shoemaker says yes, since the independence condition implies the possibility of self-blindness. Kind (2003) and Gertler (2011) say no. Kind: awareness and self-knowledge are conflated, and third-person evidence suffices. Gertler: the conclusion is acceptable de dicto. Kimble (2013) defends the broad perceptual model. Ongoing.
- **If self-blindness is impossible, why?** Shoemaker answers constitution (core and total realization). Peacocke answers concept possession (via Speaks). Parrott answers rational cognition with distinct existences. Stoljar answers an "introspective principle" of rationality (via SEP). Ongoing. Parrott's account is the most hospitable to clinical fallibility.
- **Can rationality be specified without circularity?** SEP's dilemma: demanding conceptions do not fit actual humans, and realistic ones may not exclude self-blindness. Unresolved. This is where the clinical record could bear, through cases with intact general cognition.
- **Do clinical dissociations refute impossibility?** Nichols and Stich argue that schizophrenia shows a breakdown of self-monitoring (via SEP). On the analysis above, their case (thought insertion) preserves access. No paper was found using Anton, anosognosia or DID against the thesis (see [[#gaps|Gaps]]).
