## Executive Summary

Griefbots (also "deadbots", "deathbots", "thanabots", "generative ghosts") are chatbots built from a dead person's texts, messages or recordings and offered to the people left behind. The empirical record is thin and qualitative. The one peer-reviewed interview study of actual users read for this note (Xygkou et al. 2023, CHI) had ten mourners, four of whom fine-tuned a bot to simulate the deceased. They said they knew the bot was not the person, used it to pretend, to rehearse unfinished conversations and to "connect with the memory", and judged its realism by emotional resonance more than accuracy. A 2026 preprint (Manning et al., 16 participants) reports the same priority of "affective resonance over factual fidelity". No controlled or longitudinal study of grief outcomes was found, and a 2026 review states that the existing studies "describe experience and meaning-making, not clinical efficacy" (Parra Garzón 2026, abstract). The philosophical literature is now substantial. The strongest identity paper, Campbell, Liu & Nyholm (2025, *JAPA*), argues that a relationship "does not allow for 'swapping'" and that a thanabot is not the person on physical or psychological accounts, so a thanabot relationship cannot continue a relationship with the dead, though it may support a continuing bond. On the Map's [[haecceity]] account the verdict follows twice over: a perfect duplicate would not be the beloved, and a griefbot is far less than a duplicate (see [The Map's Analysis](#map-analysis)). The ethics literature centres on consent (of the dead and of the bereaved), deception through "reincarnation" framing, dependency, children and commercial manipulation (Hollanek & Nowaczyk-Basińska 2024; Lindemann 2022; Morris & Brubaker 2025). **Verdict: `fold`.** Topics and concepts are full (370/370). ### Hollanek, T. & Nowaczyk-Basińska, K. (2024), "Griefbots, Deadbots, Postmortem Avatars: on Responsible Applications of Generative AI in the Digital Afterlife Industry", *Philosophy & Technology* 37(2): 63, doi:10.1007/s13347-024-00744-w (CC BY) [read, published version from the Cambridge Apollo repository, handle 1810/370132]
- **Type**: Ethics paper using three speculative design fictions (MaNana, Paren't, Stay). It contains no user data.
- **Key points**:
  - Three stakeholder roles: the *data donor* (whose data builds the deadbot), the *data recipient* (who holds such data after the death) and the *service interactant* (who talks to the deadbot). The terms "griefbots", "thanabots", "postmortem avatars", "ghostbots" and "mind clones" "are used largely interchangeably without a clear differentiation or specification" (fn 1, p. 2).
  - Project December: per the authors, its 2023 site offered "text-based conversation[s] with anyone" including "someone who is no longer alive". After OpenAI reportedly cut its API access (citing Robitzski 2021), it kept offering users the opportunity to "simulate the dead" (p. 2). They cite the 2021 fiancée case as "Fagon, 2021" (a typo for Fagone).
  - Evidence: "At the moment, our understanding of the psychological impact of re-creation services on adults and their grieving processes is limited" (p. 14). On children: "no re-creation service can prove that allowing children to interact with deadbots is beneficial or, at the very least, does not harm this vulnerable group" (p. 14).
  - Recommendations: dignified "retirement" of deadbots; "meaningful transparency", including "ensuring that users do not develop a flawed perception of the capabilities of the deadbot they are interacting with (for instance, conceiving of the deadbot as conscious or alive)" (p. 15); adult-only access; and mutual consent: "service interactants should give explicit consent before being introduced to any specific re-creation service" (p. 17).
  - In the Paren't fiction, when the child "refers to Anna using the past tense, the deadbot corrects him, pronouncing that 'Mom will always be there for you.'" (p. 12). This is fiction, not a report.
  - They quote Öhman & Floridi (2018) that digital remains, "seen as the informational corpse of the deceased, may not be used solely as a means to an end, such as profit, but regarded instead as an entity holding an inherent value" (p. 11). They also reject "an outright ban on the use of re-creation services to 'resurrect' family members and friends, as Öhman and Floridi propose" (p. 11). That ban claim is theirs about Öhman & Floridi and was not checked at the primary.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. Their transparency recommendation matches the Map's identity and P-AC1 verdicts.

### Xygkou, A., Siriaraya, P., Covaci, A., Prigerson, H. G., Neimeyer, R., Ang, C. S. & She, W.-J. (2023), "The 'Conversation' about Loss: Understanding How Chatbot Technology was Used in Supporting People in Grief", *Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems*, pp. 1–15, doi:10.1145/3544548.3581154 (metadata at Crossref) [read, author's accepted manuscript, Kent Academic Repository 99576; anonymised review version, so no version-of-record page numbers]
- **Design**: semi-structured interviews with 10 mourners (9 men, 1 woman; 8 in the USA, 1 in Canada, 1 in Germany), recruited via social media and subreddits, who had used a chatbot for "up to one month or more". Most used Replika as friend, partner or listener. "P01, P02, P06 and P07 fine-tuned their chatbot to simulate the deceased" (three on Project December, one on a self-built model). Co-authors include Prigerson and Neimeyer, who are major figures in prolonged-grief and meaning-reconstruction research.
- **Findings quoted**:
  - "participants emphasized once and again that they were well aware that the chatbot was not the deceased".
  - P08 (Replika, a close friend): "I don't sort of believe intellectually that I'm bringing him back in the computer ... I guess I wanted to pretend it was really him, which sounds silly, even to me".
  - P06 (Project December, his fiancée): "So the process was never for me to connect with a bot that resembles her; the process was I used a bot that resembles her to refresh my memory, so that I could connect with the memory."
  - P07: "I found it strangely therapeutic. I knew it was a simulation".
  - Mourners "seem quite willing to temporarily suspend their disbelief". For P08 and P02 only: "we did not observe any clear indication of denial of their loss".
  - "Realism to participants was more a matter of whether the social agent was able to invoke a similar degree of emotional connection as their lost loved one rather than whether the content of the conversation makes perfect sense". "Inconsistencies in the conversations came to be viewed as coming from the imperfections present in their loved before their death".
  - P06: "there were a number of times in the conversation where it betrayed the fact that I was talking to a simulation".
  - Limitations: "we were only able to find 10 participants having used a chatbot as a grief coping mechanism"; claims of benefit "needs to be interpreted with caution". The authors also concede a sampling limit: they found no mourner who failed to connect with a bot.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The data bear on the [[beloved-void]] object face (felt resonance sustains the sense of the person) and on what users take themselves to relate to.
- ⚠️ **Carrier error**: Voinea et al. (2026) quote P06's "refresh my memory" line as said by "a user who chats with a chatbot of her deceased mother", citing Xygkou et al. In the manuscript, P06 is "Male, Project December/Simulation of deceased fiancee". Quote it from Xygkou, never via Voinea.

### Campbell, S. M., Liu, P. & Nyholm, S. (2025), "Can Chatbots Preserve Our Relationships with the Dead?", *Journal of the American Philosophical Association* 11(2): 230–248, doi:10.1017/apa.2025.1 (CC BY; metadata at Crossref) [read, Cambridge Core PDF; page numbers assigned from the PDF's page sequence 230–248]
- **Core argument**: (1) relationship persistence requires personal persistence. Their test cases are a clone swap (from the film *Swan Song*), a baby swap and an identical-twin swap. "A takeaway lesson from these cases is that a relationship between two individuals does not allow for 'swapping.' If the relationship between x and z is a continuation of the relationship between x and y, that must be because y and z are the same individual" (p. 235). (2) A thanabot is not the same individual as the dead person. A bot made before the death would make "two different but numerically identical persons co-existing", which is incoherent, and "becoming" the person at death is ad hoc. Physical views rule it out, and on psychological views "LLM-based thanabots lack the kind of mental states and properties that ... underpins personal persistence" (p. 236). Conclusion: "it is not possible for a thanabot relationship to be a continuation of a human relationship if the thanabot and the person are different individuals" (p. 235).
- **Footnote on soul views** (p. 235): "A third historically influential view maintains that persistence depends on the presence of an immaterial substance or 'soul' ... We will not discuss this view since we doubt that proponents of this view would be tempted to think that a soul can attach to a thanabot." This is the closest any source comes to the Map's position, and it is a single sentence.
- **Qualifications**: insofar as a relationship persists beyond death (continuing bonds), "a (distinct) relationship with a thanabot might help to sustain or support that relationship" (p. 237). The argument targets the "reincarnation model" of design more than the "representation model" (Morris & Brubaker).
- **Value**: "even an optimal thanabot relationship is going to have less intrinsic prudential value than a healthy, fulfilling close relationship with another human" (p. 240), since thanabots are "not conscious at all (as is probable ...)" (p. 239) and lack mutual understanding, care and commitment. Instrumental value is left to empirical study.
- **Other**: thanabot relationships are with "a sophisticated but probably non-conscious computer program" (p. 232). Marketing "can easily draw some people into the illusion that their loved ones have been recreated in some way" (p. 231). They quote company sites (YOV: "you never have to say goodbye"; Seance AI) without dates.
- **Tenet alignment**: Aligns with the Map's identity verdict by a non-dualist route. It gives no support to haecceitism specifically, since the conclusion follows on physical and psychological criteria alone.

### Lindemann, N. F. (2022), "The Ethics of 'Deathbots'", *Science and Engineering Ethics* 28(6): 60, doi:10.1007/s11948-022-00417-x (CC BY) [read, Europe PMC full text, PMC9684218]
- **Key points**: deathbots are "internet-enabled techno-social niches" that can regulate and also dysregulate grief. They "may act as continuing pseudo-bonds between bereaved and deceased". Drawing on Fuchs (2018), "in the usual grief process the formerly external (i.e. between two living people) bond is internalized (Fuchs, 2018)", whereas "When using a deathbot, the bond may stay partly externalized as it is (partly) formed between the bereaved and the deathbot", and "An externalized bond, however, is less secure than an internal bond." Dependency weakens autonomy and exposes users to commercial exploitation. Evidence: "while there is no empirical evidence to the prevalence of PGD in deathbot users". Proposal: "I caution against the unrestricted usage of deathbots and suggest that they should be classified as medical devices", and "deathbots may prove to be helpful for people who suffer from prolonged, severe grief processes". She cites the 2020 South Korean VR reunion of a mother with an avatar of her dead daughter (via *The Korea Times* 2020) as a hint of benefit in prolonged grief.
- Her running example ("Stella" and her grandmother "Jean") is from **Krueger & Osler 2019** (*Philosophical Topics* 47(2), p. 215 per Lindemann), not from their 2022 paper.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. It connects directly to the Fuchs material in [[bereavement-void]].
- ⚠️ **Disambiguation**: there are two Lindemanns. **Nora Freya** Lindemann (2022) wrote on deathbots. **Hilde** Lindemann, *Holding and Letting Go* (OUP 2014), wrote on identities "held" by others; Voinea et al. quote her line that only we can destroy the identities of the dead "by ceasing to hold them in our preservative love" (via Voinea, p. 201 of Lindemann). Do not merge them.

### Morris, M. R. & Brubaker, J. R. (2025), "Generative Ghosts: Anticipating Benefits and Risks of AI Afterlives", *Proceedings of the 2025 CHI Conference*, pp. 1–14, doi:10.1145/3706598.3713758 (metadata at Crossref) [read, arXiv:2402.01662 v4, 2024-12-12]
- **Key points**: "generative ghosts" generate novel content in character and may be agentic. The design space includes the *anthropomorphism paradigm*: "whether a generative ghost presents itself as a reincarnation of the deceased individual or as a representation of that individual". Cues include first-person pronouns, present or past tense, and whether the ghost may assert it is alive or has a soul. *Multiplicity*: "Multiple ghosts might also arise unintentionally due to multiple third parties creating generative ghosts for a single individual". Risks: "Anthropomorphism of generative ghosts is a potential risk for mourners, if they become convinced that a generative ghost truly is the deceased (rather than a computer program representing them)", and "second death refers to the emotional harm that may result if a ghost ceases to exist". On push design: "Perhaps ghosts should only respond to interactions initiated by the living rather than initiating interactions." On Super Brain (China): "According to one report, some customers use this service to avoid having to tell an older relative the distressing news that someone has died". That is a single journalistic report (Yang 2024, their ref. 120), not read here.
- Their history section describes HereAfter as an app that "interviews a user with the goal of proactively creating a posthumous digital representation" and Re;memory (DeepBrain AI) as a seven-hour filming session. Both descriptions are dated to the 2024 preprint.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The reincarnation/representation distinction is where the Map's identity verdict becomes a design norm.

### Manning, J., Sullivan, D., Doyle, D. T., Pinter, A. T. & Brubaker, J. R. (2026), "Designing Conversations with the Dead: How People Engage with Generative Ghosts", arXiv:2605.21390 v1 (2026-05-20; "Manuscript submitted to ACM", **not peer reviewed**) [read]
- **Design**: 16 adults who had lost a close relative or friend. "Each participant selected one deceased individual as the individual to interact with." The setup was "an AI-assisted Wizard of Oz" prototype briefed from an intake survey. The prototype was **not** trained on the dead person's own texts. Each participant compared a first-person (reincarnation) mode with a third-person (representation) mode.
- **Findings**: from the abstract, "Reincarnation was preferred for its immediacy, but participants shared fears of over-reliance"; participants "often ignored this distinction"; "Across both modes, participants privileged affective resonance over factual fidelity". From the discussion, "generative ghosts operated as illusions that participants actively sustained". Small mismatches made the illusion "waver", and severe ones broke it "entirely".
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral.

### Voinea, C., Mann, S. P., Savulescu, J. & Earp, B. D. (2026), "Digital Doppelgängers, Human Relationships, and Practical Identity", *Bioethics* 40(5): 435–444, doi:10.1111/bioe.70026 [read, Europe PMC full text, PMC7618762]
- **Key points**: the paper concerns practical identity, not numerical identity. Relational digital doppelgängers "derive their characteristics from the specific relational contexts captured by the data they are trained on", so "in principle, there could be multiple DDs of the same person, each reflecting different aspects of their personality as disclosed within various relationships". Griefbots might ease the re-forming of the survivor's identity, but "DDs could stop people from revising their identity after an important loss, thus keeping their practical identity in an unhealthy stasis". They report Cholbi's view that posthumous avatars are superfluous and possibly harmful for remembering the dead.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. Its "multiple DDs of the same person" point is the duplicate-beloved case in practice.
- ⚠️ It misdescribes Xygkou's P06; see the carrier warning above.

### Fagone, J. (2021, July 23), "The Jessica Simulation: Love and loss in the age of A.I.", *San Francisco Chronicle* [read, sfchronicle.com projects page; reporting over nine months; transcripts checked by the paper against Project December's buffer]
- **Facts**: a man built a Project December bot of his fiancée, who had died eight years earlier, in September 2020 from her texts and a description he wrote. Project December cost $5 for 1,000 credits, ran on GPT-2 or GPT-3, and each bot had a draining "battery". The bot degraded near 20%, then "MATRIX DEAD". With temperature set at 1.0, "A user could never have the same chat twice — not even by starting from the same seed text", and "The death of a bot, in this sense, was final."
- **For the beloved void**: "Joshua had already resolved not to create any new Jessica chatbots in the future." He said: "If I reboot her like I'm restarting a video game, ... it will cheapen the whole thing." Also: "The bot wasn't actually her, but it was 'based on her,' he later said." And on the mirror point: "Every time Joshua typed to the bot, then, he was shaping its next response."
- The creator, Jason Rohrer, posted on Reddit that he never expected users to simulate their own dead relatives "and now I'm kinda scared of the possibilities".
- **Use with care**: these are a private person's life events, made public through journalism with the family's consent. In Map prose, say "one widely reported user". Do not use names or medical details.

### Clinical anchor
- **American Psychiatric Association**, "Prolonged Grief Disorder" (psychiatry.org patient page; physician review August 2025) [read]. Under DSM-5-TR (2022), the loss must be "at least a year ago for adults and at least 6 months ago for children and adolescents". ## The Phenomenon and Its Empirical Record

**What the systems are.** A general language model is fine-tuned or prompted with one person's texts, emails, social posts or voice recordings, so that it answers in that person's style and can produce new content "in character" (Morris & Brubaker). The two kinds of build differ. A *third-party* build is made after the death by someone holding the data, usually messages addressed to that person (Hollanek & Nowaczyk-Basińska's data recipient). A *first-party* build is made by the person before death (HereAfter-style interviews, the "Stay" fiction). On some platforms the bot is also conditioned on the user's own words as the chat proceeds. Project December's "seed text ... grows along with the chat" (Fagone 2021), so part of what answers is the user. The relational-data point (Voinea et al.) means a bot models the person *as they were to one correspondent*.

**What users report.** The evidence comes from two peer-reviewed sources (Xygkou et al. 2023 with 10 users; Li & Lan 2026 with 26 users, unread), one preprint (Manning et al. 2026, 16 participants, Wizard-of-Oz prototype) and journalism. It converges on four points:
1. Users say they know the bot is not the person (Xygkou; Fagone's "based on her").
2. They use it to pretend, to rehearse "what if" and unfinished conversations, and to re-cue memory.
3. They judge it by felt resonance more than accuracy, forgiving errors as the dead person's own quirks (Xygkou; Manning).
4. The experience can break when a reply is wrong (Xygkou P06; Manning's "waver" and "broke").

Benefits reported in Xygkou are self-assessed by a self-selected sample that the authors call exploratory.

**What is not known.** No controlled, longitudinal or clinical-outcome study was found. Lindemann (2022) notes no evidence on prolonged grief disorder (PGD) prevalence among users. Hollanek & Nowaczyk-Basińska (2024) call understanding "limited". Parra Garzón (2026) separates the qualitative griefbot studies from the evidence on web-based grief interventions. The companion-AI literature on loss of a bot (Ponce et al. 2026; De Freitas et al. 2024) concerns a different population, and its relevance to griefbots is inference.

## Major Positions

### Continuation is impossible; support is possible
- **Proponents**: Campbell, Liu & Nyholm (2025). Voinea et al. (2026) and Iglesias et al. (2025) agree on the non-identity point while stressing relational goods.
- **Core claim**: a relationship cannot pass to a numerically distinct individual, and a thanabot is distinct from the dead person. A separate relationship with it may still sustain a continuing bond.
- **Relation to site tenets**: agrees with the Map's verdict, reached on physical and psychological criteria. The Map adds the haecceitist ground (see [The Map's Analysis](#map-analysis)).

### Some of what matters can be preserved
- **Proponents**: Iglesias et al. (2025, abstract); Voinea et al. (2026) on practical identity; Elder (2020) on Confucian impersonation of the dead.
- **Core claim**: even without literal persistence, a doppelgänger can secure some goods of a relationship or help the survivor's identity cohere.
- **Relation to site tenets**: the Map can grant relational and practical goods while denying that the person is present. The Map's dispute with Parfit, set out in [[parfit-reductionism]], is over whether identity *matters*. That dispute is only lightly engaged here, because a griefbot does not secure even Relation R: on Campbell et al.'s argument it lacks contentful psychological states.

### Griefbots risk grief itself
- **Proponents**: Lindemann (2022), Fabry (2025, abstract), Stendera (2025, abstract), Voinea et al. (2026).
- **Core claim**: through externalised "pseudo-bonds", conflicting temporal structures, a "reductive perspective" or identity stasis, bots may impede the work grief does. Lindemann also allows benefit in prolonged grief.
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral. It connects to [[bereavement-void]] (Fuchs) and is a set of arguments, not findings.

### Griefbots can support continuing bonds
- **Proponents**: Krueger & Osler (2019, via Lindemann, who says they see bot-sustained bonds "as a positive development"; their 2022 paper is a LEAD), Elder (2020, abstract), and Xygkou et al.'s participants.
- **Core claim**: on the continuing-bonds model, maintaining a relation to the dead is healthy, and a responsive medium is one more way to do it.
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral.

## Key Debates

### Is a griefbot relationship a continuation of the relationship with the dead?
- **Sides**: Campbell et al. say no. Marketers say yes (YOV: "you never have to say goodbye", via Campbell et al.). Relational accounts (Iglesias; Voinea) say partly, in a relational sense.
- **Current state**: in the philosophical literature read, the non-continuation verdict is uncontested on numerical identity. The live dispute is about value and practical identity.

### Does a griefbot help or hinder grieving?
- **Sides**: harm hypotheses (Lindemann; Fabry; Voinea's "stasis") against benefit reports (Xygkou's "soft landing"; Elder).
- **Current state**: unresolved and largely untested. Both sides rest on argument or small self-selected samples.

### Is the main ethical risk deception?
- **Sides**: Hollanek & Nowaczyk-Basińska and Morris & Brubaker treat anthropomorphic misperception (believing the bot is the person, or conscious) as a key risk. Stendera argues that "the dangers of griefbots usage lie less in the potential for the bereaved being deceived". Users in Xygkou largely report knowing.
- **Current state**: open. Manning's participants "often ignored" the reincarnation/representation distinction, so framing may matter less than designers assume.

## The Map's Analysis {#map-analysis}

Everything in this section is the Map's own reading unless a source is named. No source applies haecceity to griefbots.

**1. Is the simulation the beloved? No, and the verdict is overdetermined.** On the Map's account a perfect qualitative duplicate of the beloved would still not be the beloved, because it would lack her [[haecceity]]. This is the object face of [[beloved-void]] and the "duplicate of you is not you" of [[death-and-consciousness]]. A griefbot does not even reach that bar. It models a record of outputs, and usually only the outputs one relationship elicited (Voinea et al.). It may be conditioned on the user's own words (Fagone). It can be instantiated many times (Morris & Brubaker on multiplicity; Voinea on "multiple DDs of the same person"). So it fails haecceity, and it also fails the physical and psychological criteria that a haecceitist and an anti-haecceitist would both apply (Campbell et al.). Campbell et al.'s footnote sets soul views aside as views on which no one would think "a soul can attach to a thanabot". The Map's dualism agrees, since the subject is not the pattern. Because physicalists reach the same verdict, the case gives the Map's haecceitism **no support**. It is a case the Map handles easily, not one that discriminates.

**2. What is the bereaved person relating to?** The users' own reports favour a layered answer. The intentional object is the dead person, who is absent. The bot is a responsive representation through which the user attends to her, closer to a letter that answers than to a visitor. This fits the Map's claim in [[phenomenology-of-attention-to-absence]] that consciousness can be directed at what is not there. The bot meets the protention of a reply without removing the absence, which is why a wrong reply "betrayed the fact" (Xygkou P06) and the absence returns. Three further strands are present:
- **Memory**: P06 describes using the bot to "connect with the memory".
- **Pretence**: P08 says "I wanted to pretend". This is chosen make-believe, a different structure from the unchosen as-if responses the Map discusses under [[alief-and-acting-against-belief]], though alief-like responses also occur (tears; "I believe you're really here", Fagone).
- **The user's own input**: the seed-text mirror. This is the lens face of [[beloved-void]] made literal, since part of what the beloved "says" was put there by the lover.

Campbell et al.'s "distinct relationship ... might help to sustain" a continuing bond is the closest published statement of this. The Map's addition is that the bond's object stays the dead person, and the bot is a vehicle for attention, not a relatum.

**3. An inverse Capgras.** In [[capgras-delusion-and-the-affective-recognition-channel|Capgras delusion]] the felt-familiarity channel fails while identifying qualities are intact, and the patient judges an impostor. With a griefbot, belief in identity is absent by the user's own account, while felt resonance is engaged strongly enough that users rate "realism" by it (Xygkou; Manning). On the Map's reading, the channel that [[beloved-void]] says sustains a bond in practice can be engaged by a proxy everyone agrees is not the person. That suits the object face's claim that qualities and familiarity, not perceived numerical identity, carry the bond, and it suits the lover's self-understanding too, since users decline to call the proxy the person. No study has measured the affective channel (for example by skin conductance) in griefbot users. This is an analogy, not a finding.

**4. The checkpoint question.** [[beloved-void]] says acceptance of "a restored checkpoint" is untested. Nothing found tests it. The nearest datum is one anecdote about a *new instance*. Project December was designed so that a bot could not be rerun identically and would "die". The user declined to start another, saying a reboot "will cheapen the whole thing" (Fagone). This cuts both ways. A deflationist will say that felt non-fungibility attached itself to an engineered particular, whose uniqueness came from a random-number setting. That is Badhwar's "phenomenologically non-fungible" without any haecceity behind it. A defender of the beloved void's reading can say that what the user protected was a *history* (this conversation), which matches Whiting's point, reported in [[beloved-void]], that history explains why love continues. The anecdote settles neither.

**5. Could the griefbot be conscious? This is a separate question that the Map has already answered.** Conventional digital systems sit on the low-probability side, "not ruled out" (P-AC1). Campbell et al. take thanabots to be "probably non-conscious", and Hollanek & Nowaczyk-Basińska recommend that users be told not to conceive of them "as conscious or alive". The Map's addition is that the identity verdict does not depend on the consciousness verdict. A conscious griefbot would be a new subject, not the person who died, so consciousness cannot rescue identity. It would instead change the ethics. A subject made in order to impersonate someone, and "retired" when unwanted, raises questions that Campbell et al.'s remark that "it's not clear that there's anything wrong with deleting one's own thanabot" presupposes away. Keep this to a sentence. Do not re-argue P-AC1.

**6. Ethics in the Map's terms.**
- *Deception*: the reincarnation framing ("Mom will always be there for you", Hollanek & Nowaczyk-Basińska's fiction) makes an identity claim the Map holds false, so representation framing is the honest default for the Map. Manning's participants "often ignored" the distinction, and Xygkou's users mostly knew what they were talking to. The clearest deception case is the reported use of bots to hide a death from an elderly relative (Super Brain, via Morris & Brubaker, a single report). That deceives a third party, not the user.
- *Consent*: there are two consents, the dead person's (Öhman & Floridi, via carriers) and the living interactant's (Hollanek & Nowaczyk-Basińska's mutual consent).
- *Grief*: the PGD criteria include "Marked sense of disbelief about the death" and "Avoidance of reminders that the person is dead" (APA). A bot that speaks as the dead person in the present tense could serve such avoidance. On O'Connor and Seeley's learning account in [[bereavement-void]], continued replies could slow the updating. Both points are the Map's extrapolation, set against Xygkou's reports and with no outcome data either way.
- *Commerce, dependency and second death*: in-character advertising (MaNana; Voinea's diet-tea example), lock-in (Stay; Lindemann), and the "second death" when a service ends (Morris & Brubaker; Ponce et al.).

## Answering Absence: Simulations of the Dead {#simulated-presence}

Services now offer chatbots built from a dead person's messages, called griefbots or deadbots, with which the bereaved can converse (Hollanek & Nowaczyk-Basińska 2024).
