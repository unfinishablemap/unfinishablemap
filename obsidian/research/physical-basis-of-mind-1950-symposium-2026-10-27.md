## Executive Summary

The sentence the Map attributes to Sherrington's 1950 broadcast — the relation of mind to brain is "still not merely unsolved but still devoid of a basis for its very beginning" — is **not from the broadcast**. It is from the 1933 Rede Lecture *The Brain and Its Mechanism*, p. 32, verified this run by Google Books search-within-volume on the 1933 Cambridge printing, control-checked. In the 1950 volume it occurs once, at p. 69, where Viscount Samuel introduces it and the 1947 "two fundamental elements" sentence with "Sir Charles Sherrington has written **elsewhere**" — Samuel marks both as imports, then separately quotes the broadcast's actual closing words. So the Sherrington page's L43/L47/L112/L116 carry a misattribution introduced on 2026-10-26 (half right: the sentence is not in the 1947 foreword, re-grepped this run; but the 1950 volume only *quotes* it). Sherrington's own 1950 chapter is the four-page "Introductory" (pp. 1–4), recorded 29 December 1948 and broadcast 11 May 1949. He was born 27 November 1857 and died 4 March 1952; Dale's on-air preface calls him "in his ninety-second year". "Last published statement" is right; "1950" is the book and the repeat broadcast, not the talk.

## Sherrington's chapter (pp. 1–4) and who replied

Sherrington asserts no dualist thesis. p. 2: "Mind is always an inference from behaviour… though there is matter which exists apart from mind, we know of no instance where mind exists apart from matter… That the brain is the bodily organ of the mind we have to accept as an established fact." p. 3: "there remain mental events which seem to lie beyond any physiology of the brain"; pp. 3–4: "It is a far cry from an electrical reaction in the brain to suddenly seeing the world around one, with all its distances, its colours and chiaroscuro." p. 4: Mill versus Tyndall, "so the mind is a secretion of the brain", then the Aristotle close.

- **Adrian** (p. 5) adopts the "misgivings": "The part of our picture of the brain which may always be missing is, of course, the part which deals with the mind, the part which ought to explain how a particular pattern of nerve impulses can produce an idea"; (p. 6) resolves to "go on trying".
- **Zuckerman** (p. 25): "In his introduction Sir Charles Sherrington reminded us that physiology, as a natural science, is only concerned with physical matters, and that mind is an inference from behaviour. Both he and Professor Adrian also indicated that there may be mental events which lie outside any possible explanation of the way the brain functions. While this is a question essentially for philosophers…"; presses whether "mind" names "something which is distinct from, and which animates the body" or "a generic term".
- **Penfield** (p. 64) closes on a hedge Samuel seizes: "something else finds its dwelling-place between the sensory complex and the motor mechanism, that there is a switchboard operator as well as a switchboard" — 1949 Penfield, a suggestion, not the 1975 position.
- **Samuel** (pp. 65–69) is the only voice pulling toward two orders: reducing "matter into mind is the outcome of what T. H. Green called 'the philosophic craving for unity'" (p. 66); "Some meeting-place there must be to account for the brain-mind relation" (p. 68); then the Sherrington quotations and "standstill".
- **Ayer** (p. 70) replies to Sherrington by name — "Sir Charles Sherrington remarks that 'it is a far cry from an electrical reaction in the brain to suddenly seeing the world around one…'; and Professor Adrian confesses to the same 'misgivings'" — and dissolves the gap: nothing about "causal connections between what we choose to describe respectively as mental and physical occurrences implies anything by which we need to be perplexed" (p. 74).
- **Ryle** (p. 74): "mind and body are not to be conceived as two disparate entities between which we have to make, or find, some sort of amphibious bridge, but… talking about minds and talking about bodies are different ways of classifying and interpreting our experiences"; (p. 76) "Ayer would like to reject it, but does not see how to do so"; (p. 78) "In saying that a person is not to be described as a mind coupled with a body I am not saying, with some truculent thinkers, that people are just machines."

Bearing for the Map: Ayer and Ryle answer Sherrington's "far cry" by dissolution, not refutation; only Samuel treats the gap as evidence for two orders; Sherrington and Adrian stay agnostic. The optimistic review's "an interactionist and his critics in one volume" overstates it — the critics reply to a gap-claim, not to an interactionist thesis.

## Tenets and the house rule

Tenet 5 work is done by the 1947 sentence; nothing in the 1949 talk adds to it, and Samuel's "craving for unity" line is the same move in a non-scientist's mouth and must not be enlisted. The 1933 sentence is a gap-claim, not a Tenet 3 claim; keep its present role as the debt the lineage inherits. House rule (d) survives: Sherrington stays history; Penfield 1949 is a hedge; Samuel's standing is nil for the Map; the two professional philosophers reply by dissolution and are not to become a "critics' count" either.
