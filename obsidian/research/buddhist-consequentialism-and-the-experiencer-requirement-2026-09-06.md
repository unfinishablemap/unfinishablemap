If the momentary locus is a genuine owner, the ownerless premise is false at exactly the grain where the argument needs it, and the tradition's stated route to impartiality is cut. ## The Core Text

### Bodhicaryāvatāra 8.90–103 — chapter **eight**, not nine

⚠️ **Citation trap, verified.** The SEP *Śāntideva* entry's prose refers to "the argument of BCA IX.94–96" and labels the second quotation block "IX.97 … 99 … 101 … 102", but the verses it quotes are from **chapter 8** and its own page citations (C&S: 96–97) are chapter-8 pages. Independent confirmations that the chapter is 8:

- Priest, Garfield & Jenkins title the chapter **"The Śāntideva Passage: *Bodhicaryāvatāra* VIII: 90-103"** and number every verse VIII throughout [raw].
- Harris's article is titled **"Does Anātman Rationally Entail Altruism? On *Bodhicaryāvatāra* 8:101-103"** [metadata].

**An article must cite BCA 8.101–102.** Copying the SEP's roman numeral would import an error at the exact locus the article is about.

### The argument, verbatim

Crosby & Skilton (1995) translation, as quoted in Goodman's SEP *Śāntideva* entry [raw — grep-verified]:

> **8.97.** If I give them no protection because their suffering does not afflict me, why do I protect my body against future suffering when it does not afflict me?
>
> **8.99.** If you think it is for the person who has the pain to guard against it, a pain in the foot is not of the hand, so why is the one protected by the other?
>
> **8.101.** The continuum of consciousness, like a queue, and the combination of constituents, like an army, are not real. The person who experiences suffering does not exist. To whom will that suffering belong?
>
> **8.102.** Without exception, no sufferings belong to anyone. They must be warded off simply because they are suffering. Why is any limitation put on this?

⚠️ **Two SEP entries by the same author render 8.102 differently.** The *Ethics in Indian Buddhism* entry (rev. 2024) gives it as *"They **are to be** warded off simply because they are suffering"* and attributes it explicitly to "Crosby and Skilton trans. 1995, 97"; the *Śāntideva* entry (2016) gives *"They **must be** warded off"*. Both are grep-verified in their own source. One is a slip against C&S; **I have not seen the Crosby & Skilton volume itself**, so I cannot say which. Any article quoting this verse must (a) pick one rendering, (b) attribute it to the SEP entry it came from rather than to C&S directly, or (c) obtain C&S 1995 p. 97 and quote from it. Do not present either as *the* C&S text on the strength of these notes.

An independent translation, from Priest, Garfield & Jenkins [raw — grep-verified], sharpens the point:

> **VIII: 101** A continuum and collection, / Just like such things as a series or an army, are unreal. / **The one for whom there is suffering does not exist.** / Therefore to whom will that suffering belong?
>
> **VIII: 102** Since all ownerless sufferings are / Without distinction, / They should be alleviated just because of being suffering, / What restriction can be made in that case?

"The one *for whom* there is suffering does not exist" is the *mattering-recipient* of the Map's premise 2, denied by name. This translation is the more useful of the two for the article: it makes contact with the Map's own vocabulary without any interpretive gloss on our part.

## Major Positions

### 1. The abhidharma reading — suffering-dharmas as ultimately real value-bearers

**Proponents**: Prajñākaramati (canonical commentary); Siderits; Goodman; presented (not endorsed) by Priest, Garfield & Jenkins.

**Core claim**, in the Cowherds' own words [raw — grep-verified]:

> On an abhidharma metaphysics, composite entities, such as armies, forests, and persons, are merely conventionally or nominally (saṃvṛti/prajñapti) real (sat). … **Momentary experiences of suffering and happiness are dharmas. They are hence ultimately real.** Suffering has the svalakṣaṇa of being unbearable, happiness the svalakṣaṇa of being desirable. Persons are composites; they are hence ultimately unreal, albeit conventionally real. They have no svalakṣaṇa at all. For this reason, **we should be concerned with suffering and happiness — they are ultimately real; but not with any distinctions between their bearers — they, and the distinctions between them, are only conventionally real.**

And the normative payoff [raw]: *"The claim that each person has a special relation to his or her own pain fails, because there are literally no people. If any pain should be alleviated, then all pain should."*

**Relation to the Map.** **Simultaneously the Map's best confirmation and its sharpest challenge**, which is why the eventual article must engage it most carefully.

- *Confirmation*: what survives the reduction is a **momentary felt event with an intrinsic evaluative character** — suffering's *svalakṣaṇa* is "being unbearable". That is very close to the Map's momentary experiential locus and to what `positions/value-in-selection` P-VS3 asserts in saying felt badness and badness are one fact. The Map's "momentariness multiplies the locus" line tracks the abhidharma inventory rather than rescuing the Map ad hoc.
- *Challenge*: the argument requires an **asymmetry** — suffering ultimately real, *bearers* only conventionally real. The Map's momentary locus is a bearer at the momentary grain. Grant it and the asymmetry disappears; without the asymmetry, 8.101–102 does not defeat the prudential objection of 8.97/8.99. **This is where the dispute stops being terminological.**

The Cowherds also record the internal instability, in a footnote that is directly on the Map's business [raw]: *"(101) says that persons are unreal. It does not say explicitly that dharmas are not. … But if they were unreal too, the contrast in the passage would appear to lose all force. Moreover, in that case, **if the people do not exist, neither does the pain!**"*

### 2. Goodman's universalist welfarist consequentialism

**Proponent**: Charles Goodman — *Consequences of Compassion: An Interpretation and Defense of Buddhist Ethics* (OUP 2009); the journal version at Goodman 2008; and his two SEP entries.

**Core claim**: the fundamental basis of Buddhist ethics is welfarist consequentialism — Theravāda as rule-consequentialism, Mahāyāna (Śāntideva) as act-consequentialism — with the Ownerless Suffering Argument doing the work of establishing agent-neutrality. From SEP *Ethics in Indian Buddhism* [raw — grep-verified]:

> As writers such as Mark Siderits (2003, ch. 9) have often noted, this strategy for justifying altruism, which some scholars now call the Ownerless Suffering Argument, closely resembles the arguments for consequentialism in Parfit 1984 (ch. 15). **In fact, it's hard to see how the Ownerless Suffering Argument could support any moral view that is not some form of universalist consequentialism.**

**⚠️ The finding that most helps the Map.** Goodman's own gloss of the argument's upshot **re-imports beneficiaries** [raw — grep-verified]:

> From the perspective of this argument, your suffering has no greater significance than that of anyone else, but it also has no less. **You are one of the many sentient beings whose welfare is to be promoted.** … This kind of prudential concern is compatible with the doctrine of no self, and is not the same as egoism.

A *welfare* is something a subject has. Goodman's considered position is therefore that experiencers are *not ranked* by whose they are, rather than that value floats free of experiencers — much closer to the Map's reply than the harvest note assumed, and the single most quotable confirmation available: the leading defender of Buddhist consequentialism, glossing the ownerless argument, speaks of sentient beings whose welfare is to be promoted. The Map can fairly read this as conceding the locus at the conventional level while denying it any *ranking* work.

**Structure of the monograph** [metadata — Crossref, 12 chapters]: 1 Introduction (3–8) · 2 Fundamental Buddhist Teachings (9–22) · 3 Main Features of Some Western Ethical Theories (23–46) · **4 Theravāda Ethics as Rule-Consequentialism (47–72)** · 5 Mahāyāna Ethics before Śāntideva (73–88) · **6 Śāntideva and After (89–108)** · 7 Transcending Ethics (109–130) · **8 Buddhist Ethics and the Demands of Consequentialism (131–144)** · 9 Buddhism on Moral Responsibility (145–164) · 10 Punishment (165–182) · **11 Objections and Replies (183–196)** · 12 A Buddhist Response to Kant (197–214). Chapters 6, 8 and 11 are the ones this subject needs.

### 3. Williams's objection — the experiencer requirement, argued from inside Buddhist studies

**Proponent**: Paul Williams, *Altruism and Reality: Studies in the Philosophy of the Bodhicaryāvatāra* (Routledge 1998); the ensuing exchange is Siderits 2000 (review, *PEW*) and Williams 2000 ("Response to Mark Siderits' Review", *PEW* 50(3): 424–53).

**Core claim**: Śāntideva's argument fails; suffering does not make sense without a self. If nothing owns the suffering there is nothing to which the reason-to-relieve attaches.

**This is the Map's own experiencer requirement, pressed by a Buddhologist against the tradition's own text** — the most important single discovery in this run, and what makes the subject more than a quibble: the objection the Map states in one paragraph has a twenty-eight-year adversarial literature behind it, with named opponents, published replies, and an edited volume (*Moonpaths*, chs. 4, 6, 8) organised partly around it.

⚠️ **Reading caution.** Finnigan groups Williams *with* the consequentialist interpreters — *"Siderits (2003, 2007) argues that it is unmistakably consequentialist, as do Williams (1998) and Goodman (2008)"* [raw]. Both readings can hold: Williams may think the argument yields consequentialism *if* sound while thinking it unsound. I have **not read Williams 1998 or Williams 2000**; the characterisation above is [summariser]/[snippet] for his actual position. **Do not attribute a specific formulation to Williams without obtaining one of the two texts.** Highest-value verification target before the article is written.

### 4. The meditation reading — the argument is not an argument

**Proponent**: Stephen Harris, "Does Anātman Rationally Entail Altruism? On *Bodhicaryāvatāra* 8:101-103" (*Journal of Buddhist Ethics* 18, 2011); developed in Harris 2018.

**Core claim**: reconstructions by Williams, Siderits and Pettit all fail; for a Mādhyamika, understanding anātman is *instrumental to* the bodhisattva path rather than an argument for it; the verses may be meditation instructions rather than a proof. Goodman's summary [raw — grep-verified]: *"he regards the Ownerless Suffering Argument as itself a mere upāya. It must be an upāya, Harris says, since it relies on the Abhidharma understanding of the doctrine of no self, whereas Śāntideva's actual view belongs to a very different form of Buddhist philosophy, the Madhyamaka."*

**Relation to the Map.** This reading **dissolves the challenge rather than answering it**, and the eventual article should say so plainly: if 8.101–102 is *upāya*, then Buddhist consequentialism never grounded the badness of suffering without a subject in the first place, and the conventionalist exit the Map's article describes has no textual owner. That is a live possibility, not a Map-friendly convenience, and it should be reported at the strength the literature gives it (contested; the Cowherds present three readings and decline to adjudicate).

### 5. Madhyamaka parity, and the failure of conventional grounding

**Proponents**: Priest, Garfield & Jenkins (the objection); Finnigan and Finnigan & Tanaka (the metaethical version).

Two distinct claims, both useful.

**(a) The Parity Reply.** [raw — grep-verified] *"For a Mādhyamika, from the fact that persons are not ultimately real, it does not follow that they are not real in any sense. And since action is conventional, and is undertaken by conventional agents, there is no reason to think that merely conventional distinctions are irrelevant to action and its goals. Indeed, **if it were the case that we could disregard things that exist merely conventionally, then we could disregard suffering, since this has no ultimate existence either!**"*

This is the Map's strongest defensive resource and it comes from inside the tradition: the Mādhyamika cannot deflate the experiencer without deflating the badness, because on Madhyamaka all things have the same ontological status. **Tenet 5 in the second direction** — parsimony about the self, pressed consistently, does not buy the reductionist the asymmetry the argument needs.

**(b) Conventional truth may not ground moral judgment at all.** Finnigan, "Madhyamaka Buddhist Metaethics: The justificatory grounds of moral judgments", *Philosophy East and West* 65(3): 765–785 (2015), abstract [raw, author's page]: Finnigan and Tanaka (2011) argue Madhyamaka's metaphysical commitments do not afford a satisfactory justificatory ground for moral judgments; against Tillemans's two-truths defence, the paper argues *"that Prāsaṅgika cannot satisfactorily justify their fundamental moral judgments by appeal to the notion of conventional truth."*

If that is right, the "conventionally real streams" route — which the live article and the 2026-07-17 note both credit to Siderits as a re-basing of value-talk — is **contested within Buddhist studies on its own terms**, not merely resisted by the Map. Neither records this. Second-most-valuable import from this run.

### 6. Chadha — eliminativism about persons too

Monima Chadha, "Eliminating Selves and Persons", *JAPA* 7(3): 273–294 (2021); *Selfless Minds* (OUP 2023); Chadha & Nichols (2023). Already partly in the corpus: `topics/eastern-philosophy-consciousness` L111 records that *"Chadha (2023) reads Vasubandhu's Abhidharma as eliminativist about selves and persons, and illusionist about the sense of self and every form of self-representation, which is more than the denial of a mere owner concedes."*

**Bearing.** Chadha closes the conventionalist exit from the other side. If *persons* are eliminated rather than merely reduced, "conventionally real streams" are not available as value-bearers either, and Buddhist ethics must ground value in momentary events alone — returning the whole weight of the question to the abhidharma reading and its asymmetry. The corpus has the premise; nothing has drawn this consequence.

## Key Debates

### Is the badness of suffering owner-independent, or does ownerlessness merely remove *ranking*?
- **Sides**: the abhidharma reading (owner-independent: suffering-dharmas are the ultimately real bad things) vs. Goodman's own gloss (ranking-independent: sentient beings whose welfare is to be promoted, none weighted above another).
- **Core disagreement**: whether "ownerless" is an ontological claim about what bears value or a normative claim about impartiality.
- **Current state**: unresolved and, so far as I found, **not posed in this form in the literature**. This is the article's opening. The Map is unusually well placed to pose it, because its own vocabulary (locus vs. career; base layer vs. diachronic layer) already separates the two questions.

### Does the argument survive the Madhyamaka turn?
- **Sides**: Williams and the Cowherds' majority say the abhidharma asymmetry is unavailable to a Mādhyamika; Siderits (in his *Moonpaths* chapter) dissents; Harris says the verses are *upāya* and the question does not arise.
- **Current state**: live; *Moonpaths* is organised around the disagreement and its introduction explicitly declines to settle it.

### Is Buddhist ethics consequentialist at all?
- **Sides**: Goodman, Siderits — consequentialist. Keown, Cooper & James — virtue ethics. Velez, B. Clayton — hybrid. Harvey — *sui generis*. Finnigan — the epistemology constrains the answer, and Keown's version conflicts with Dharmakīrti and Candrakīrti.
- **Current state**: unsettled, and complicated by what Goodman calls the **Fortunate Coincidence Thesis** — karma plus the claim that the highest well-being is compassionate means agent-good and general-good coincide almost everywhere, so the theories rarely come apart in practice [raw, SEP *Ethics in Indian Buddhism*].
- **Bearing on the Map**: the article must not write "Buddhist consequentialism" as the settled reading of the tradition. Write it as the reading on which the challenge to the experiencer requirement is sharpest, and note the classification is contested.
