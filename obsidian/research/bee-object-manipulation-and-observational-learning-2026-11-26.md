## Executive Summary

Three experiments from Lars Chittka's group at Queen Mary are the core literature. In Loukola et al. (2017), bumblebees learned to move a ball to a goal for sucrose. Bees that watched a demonstrator did better than bees shown none, and they usually moved the ball nearest the goal rather than the distant one they had seen moved. In Alem et al. (2016), bees learned to pull a string to retrieve an out-of-reach artificial flower, and the skill spread through colonies by social learning; the authors trace it to simple associative mechanisms and explicitly deny that it needs insight. In Galpayage Dona et al. (2022), bees rolled balls with no reward, and the authors find that this meets Burghardt's five behavioural criteria for play.

The primary authors describe this work as **object manipulation**, never as tool use. The "tool use" label comes from *Science*'s editor's summary of the 2017 paper ("Very clever bees use tools") and, as relayed by search results, from the publisher's summary of *The Mind of a Bee*, which says bees use simple tools. That label has reached the Map: [[universal-coupling-response]] L40 has "Chittka (2022) documented face recognition, tool use, and metacognition-like behaviour", and [[architectural-adequacy-at-the-built-edge]] L69 repeats the phrase.

The 2020s follow-ups take the deflationary side. Wen et al. (2024, *eLife*, with Chittka and Solvi as co-authors) found that bees choosing between connected and broken strings rely on image matching and associative learning rather than means-end understanding, and the eLife assessment grades that evidence "convincing". The lab's own 2025 review puts these lines of evidence at a modest grade: "None of these are, individually, formal demonstrations of consciousness".

For the Map, the findings are **compatible only**. They show flexible learning in a million-neuron brain, which meets the page's last functional marker, but a physicalist predicts that as readily as a dualist does. Nothing here separates universal coupling from threshold views, or dualism from physicalism.

## Key Sources

### Loukola, Solvi (published as Perry), Coscos & Chittka (2017) — ball transport by observation
- **Citation**: *Science* 355(6327): 833–836. DOI 10.1126/science.aag2360. PubMed 28232576.
- **Read**: full text, Science reprint PDF from Zenodo record 889455. The PDF shows the printed byline and the editor's summary.
- **Design**: In experiment 1, bees were pretrained to find sucrose at a ball in the centre of a platform. They were then trained to move the ball to the centre, with a plastic model bee on a stick demonstrating on failed early trials (n = 14 bees reached the test). In experiment 2 there were three demonstration conditions, with n = 10 bees each: a live trained conspecific moving the furthest of three balls; a "ghost" condition in which a hidden magnet moved the ball; and no demonstration. During training the two nearer balls were glued down, so the demonstrator could only move the furthest one.
- **Exact claims** (grepped in the raw text):
  - The task "required subjects to move an unattached object to a specified location in order to obtain a reward".
  - "Bees that observed demonstration of the technique from a live or model demonstrator learned the task more efficiently than did bees observing a “ghost” demonstration (ball moved via magnet) or without demonstration."
  - The success figures are live demonstration 99 ± 1%, ghost 78 ± 5.5% and none 34 ± 7.0%. Most bees solved the task on their very first test trial (25 of 30 bees). This is paraphrased because a page break splits the sentence in the PDF.
  - Bees used the closest ball in most successful trials, "indicating that bees were not attracted to the location where conspecifics were observed and that local enhancement (increased attention to the spatial location and movement of the demonstrator) did not play a role".
  - Conclusion: "shows an unprecedented degree of behavioral flexibility in an insect".
- **Authors' own grade**: behavioural or cognitive **flexibility** and **social learning**, with the behaviour described as "goal-directed". The body text has 0 hits for "tool" (the three hits are the editor's summary and the page furniture), 0 for "conscious" and 0 for "insight".
- **Authors' caveats**:
  - "In our experiment 1, bees could have learned through shaping."
  - The ghost demonstration also raised success, "suggesting that observation of a moving ball was enough for bees to solve the task". A conspecific is therefore not needed to get most of the effect.
- **The editor's summary** (*Science*, unsigned): it is headed "Very clever bees use tools" and says "Bumblebees were trained to see that a ball could be used to produce a reward." This is the source of the "tool use" framing. It is the journal's, not the authors'.
- **Name guard**: the printed byline gives Olli J. Loukola, Clint J. Perry, Louie Coscos and Lars Chittka, with the first two marked as equal contributors. Crossref and PubMed now list the second author as **Cwyn Solvi**, the same researcher under a changed name. Galpayage Dona et al. 2022 cite the paper as "Loukola, O. J., Perry, C. J., Coscos, L., & Chittka, L. (2017)". Either form is correct. The fold below uses the publisher's current record (Solvi). Do not treat Perry and Solvi as two people, and do not "correct" one to the other on other pages.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral or compatible. Flexible, goal-directed learning is what both a physicalist and a universal-coupling dualist expect of an insect brain.

### Alem, Perry, Zhu, Loukola, Ingraham, Søvik & Chittka (2016) — string pulling and its spread
- **Citation**: *PLoS Biology* 14(10): e1002564. DOI 10.1371/journal.pbio.1002564. PMC5049772. A correction was published as e1002589. Crossref still lists "Clint J. Perry" for this paper.
- **Exact claims** (grepped in the PMC text):
  - It is a "nonnatural object manipulation task": string pulling "to access a reward that was presented out of reach".
  - Innovators were rare. Of 50 bees, none solved the task on a first 5-minute exposure; on a second exposure, "two of 25 untrained bees succeeded". Stepwise training brought 23 of 40 to criterion.
  - Observational learning: 15 of 25 observers (60%) pulled the string on their first trial after watching a skilled demonstrator.
  - "Learning the behavior relied on a combination of simple associative mechanisms and trial-and-error learning". The authors name local enhancement (strong), stimulus enhancement (subordinate) and perceptual feedback.
  - Insight is denied. Observers failed a coiled-string test, which removes the visual feedback of the flower moving closer. By contrast, 3 of 8 highly experienced bees (more than 20 pulls each) solved the coiled string.
  - Diffusion: after 150 paired foraging bouts, 25/47, 17/29 and 12/28 foragers in the test colonies had learned to string pull, against none in the control colonies (n = 51, 58, 57). With more bouts, 34/47 and 18/28. Learners became demonstrators across "several sequential sets (“generations”) of learners".
  - Conclusion: "so long as animals have a basic toolkit of associative and motor learning processes, the key ingredients for the cultural spread of unusual skills are already in place and do not require sophisticated cognition."
- **Authors' own grade**: deflationary by design. The paper is an argument that culture-like spread needs only simple mechanisms.
- **Tool-use check**: "tool" appears only as "toolkit", "cognitive tools" and a citation about hominin tool-making. The paper never calls string pulling tool use.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The paper actively supports a mechanism-light reading of the behaviour.

### Galpayage Dona, Solvi, Kowalewska, Mäkelä, MaBouDi & Chittka (2022) — "Do bumble bees play?"
- **Citation**: *Animal Behaviour* 194: 239–251. DOI 10.1016/j.anbehav.2022.08.013. CC BY.
- **Surname guard**: the first author's surname is **Galpayage Dona**. Cite the paper as "Galpayage Dona et al. (2022)", never "Dona et al.", which is how the driver brief named it.
- **Coiner guard**: the five behavioural criteria for play are **Burghardt's (2005)**, from *The Genesis of Animal Play*. The paper applies them and does not originate them. The origin of the experiment is Loukola et al. 2017: "During the execution of this experiment, the team observed that bumble bees would often roll balls for no apparent benefit."
- **Exact claims** (grepped in the PDF after NFKC ligature normalisation):
  - Ball rolling "fulfils behavioural criteria for animal play and is akin to play in other animals".
  - "younger bees rolled more balls than older bees and male bees rolled individual balls for longer durations than females".
  - In a conditioned place preference test with 42 female bees, the bees preferred the chamber colour where they had rolled balls.
- **Authors' own grade** (the strongest affective claim in this literature):
  - "Our results contribute to the question of sentience in insects and lend further support for the existence of positive affective states in these animals."
  - "We suggest that the behaviour observed here has actual hedonic value for bumble bees".
- **Authors' caveat**: "We cannot exclude that object play in bumble bees occurs following a lack of stimulation that would otherwise be provided by the natural environment." That is, the play may be an artefact of captivity or enrichment.
- **Corpus status**: already covered at the right grade through Chittka et al. 2025 in [[animal-consciousness]] L96 and [[minds-without-words]] L83. **Do not duplicate it.** The primary paper is cited nowhere in the corpus ("Galpayage" 0/0/0), so the existing mentions rest on the 2025 review and on the *Mind of a Bee* attribution.

### Solvi, Gutierrez Al-Khudhairy & Chittka (2020) — cross-modal object recognition
- **Citation**: *Science* 367(6480): 910–912. DOI 10.1126/science.aay8064. PubMed 32079771.
- **Attribution guard**: the brief's "Solvi, Baciadonna & Chittka 2020" conflates two papers. Solvi, Baciadonna & Chittka is the **2016** *Science* paper on dopamine-dependent positive emotion-like states (353: 1529–1531, originally printed as Perry, Baciadonna & Chittka). ### Wen, Lu, Solvi, Dong, … Peng & Chittka (2024) — means-end comprehension tested and not found
- **Citation**: *eLife* 13: RP97018. DOI 10.7554/eLife.97018. PMC11410369 (version of record). Eleven authors; Chittka is last author and Solvi third.
- **Exact claims** (grepped in the PMC text):
  - "Our results suggest that bumblebees’ ability to distinguish between continuous strings and interrupted strings relies on a combination of image matching and associative learning, rather than means-end understanding."
  - "Bumblebees, whether with or without string-pulling experience, do not appear to understand the value of strings to target objects."
- **Authors' caveat**: "This negative result does not necessarily mean that bumblebees are entirely unable to comprehend the link between a means and an end, but our results suggest that for the paradigms tested here, such comprehension is not required."
- **Editorial grade**: the eLife assessment rates the work "Valuable" and the evidence "Convincing", and calls it "convincing evidence that bumblebees lack causal understanding in a string-pulling task".
- **Companion paper**: Zhou et al. (2025, *Insect Science* 32: 675–686, abstract only; Chittka co-author) found that bees learn features of both the string and the target, and that when the two conflict they prioritise the string's.

### Procenko, Bridges, Kowalewska, Juusola & Chittka (2026) — spread between colonies
- **Citation**: *Animal Behaviour* 232: 123439. DOI 10.1016/j.anbehav.2025.123439. CC BY. BioRxiv preprint 2025.03.03.641175.
- **Exact claims** (grepped in the PDF):
  - "string pulling can spread both within and between bumble bee colonies, and persist over time, under previously untested open diffusion conditions".
  - Some bees in control colonies learned it alone by trial and error.
  - The authors describe the 2016 spread as demonstrated "only in highly controlled paired dyad settings". This matches Alem's design of 150 paired bouts.
  - Conclusion: social learning lets animals establish local adaptations "in the absence of the computational power provided by large brains".
- **Grade**: social-learning and culture-adjacent. The PDF has 0 hits for "conscious".

- **Bridges et al. 2024**, *Nature* 627: 572–578. Bees learn from demonstrators a two-step puzzle box they fail to solve alone in up to 24 days. Already in [[cumulative-culture]] L136, [[interface-threshold]] L108 and [[theory-of-mind]].

## Major Positions

### Rich-cognition reading (Chittka's popular framing)
- **Proponents**: Chittka, in *The Mind of a Bee* (2022). As relayed by search results, the publisher's summary says bees use simple tools and learn by observing others; neither the book nor that page was read directly.
- **Core claim**: insect brains of about a million neurons support flexible, goal-directed cognition, and taken together the evidence raises the probability of insect experience.
- **Relation to site tenets**: it agrees in direction with the Map's case against a capacity threshold ([[universal-coupling-response]] L40, "Each finding pushes the threshold lower"). But the primary papers support "flexibility", not "tool use".

### Associative or deflationary reading (the primary papers' own mechanism claims)
- **Proponents**: Alem et al. 2016; Wen et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2025; Lu et al. 2024. Chittka co-authored all four.
- **Core claim**: the acquisition and spread of these skills is explained by local and stimulus enhancement, trial-and-error with perceptual feedback, image matching, and second-order conditioning. No insight or means-end understanding is needed.
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral. The deflationary case does not rest on parsimony, so Tenet 5 cannot discount it: coiled-string and broken-string tests directly probe for understanding and find none.

### Play and affect reading
- **Proponents**: Galpayage Dona et al. 2022; Chittka et al. 2025.
- **Core claim**: object play meeting Burghardt's criteria, together with conditioned place preference, supports positive affective states.
- **Relation to site tenets**: compatible with Bidirectional Interaction and Dualism, but the inference runs through functional markers that physicalist functionalism also predicts. See the deflationary-functionalist reading already in [[animal-consciousness]] L126–L128.

## Key Debates

### Is bee ball transport "tool use"?
- **Sides**: *Science*'s editor's summary and the *Mind of a Bee* publisher's summary say yes. The primary authors use "object manipulation", "an unattached object", "non-natural object". The Wen et al. *Insect Science* review (2024; online 2023; abstract only) uses "object use".
- **Core disagreement**: mostly terminological. I found no primary paper that argues bee ball transport meets a formal definition of tool use. I did not verify any formal definition (for example Shumaker, Walkup & Beck 2011) at source this run, so the fold does not adjudicate the definition. It moves the Map's wording to the authors' own term.
- **Current state**: unresolved in the literature, and nobody is arguing it. The calibrated wording is "object manipulation".

### Does observational learning in bees need more than association?
- **Sides**: Loukola et al. 2017 rule out local enhancement for ball transport and stress flexibility. Alem et al. 2016 find local and stimulus enhancement plus trial-and-error for string pulling. Lu et al. 2024 frame social learning as second-order conditioning.
- **Current state**: for string pulling, the 2024 eLife paper settles the means-end question in the negative for the paradigms tested, and its authors note the limit of a negative result. For ball transport, the ghost-condition result (78% against 34% with no demonstration) shows that watching the ball move carries most of the effect.

## Potential Article Angles

No standalone article is recommended; the sections are at cap. Two angles are worth folding in:

1. **Calibration of the erosion argument.** The threshold-erosion argument in [[universal-coupling-response]] L40 and [[architectural-adequacy-at-the-built-edge]] L69 lists bee "tool use". At the primary authors' grade this is goal-directed object manipulation, much of it learned by mechanisms the same group calls associative. That still erodes a capacity-based threshold, since a million-neuron brain does it. But it erodes a threshold defined by behavioural flexibility, not one defined by insight. A threshold theorist could therefore move the line above associative learning. The page's own adequacy criterion is architectural, so the page survives this, but the word "tool use" overstates the evidence the page leans on.
2. **The functional-marker list** (UCR L70) ends with "flexible behavioural responses that cannot be explained by fixed stimulus-response chains". Bee object manipulation meets this marker, and the follow-ups show it is met by associative learning. That says how low the marker sits, which is honest information the page currently lacks.
