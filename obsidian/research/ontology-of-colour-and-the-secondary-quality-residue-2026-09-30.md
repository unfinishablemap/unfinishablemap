## Executive Summary

Colour ontology sorts into five live families, and they differ chiefly on **where the intrinsic quality of colour ends up**, not on whether there is one. Reductive physicalism (Byrne & Hilbert 2003) puts colour in the object as reflectance and hands the quality question to a physicalist theory of experience. Relationalism (Cohen 2009) and dispositionalism (Johnston 1992) make colour a perceiver-involving relation or power, which is objectively investigable — but the relation's *relatum* on the perceiver side is a colour experience, so the quality is relocated rather than analysed. Eliminativism and projectivism (Hardin, Maund, Boghossian & Velleman, Pautz) say the quality colour experience presents is instantiated by nothing, and place it in experience explicitly. Chalmers's Edenic content formalises that placement: experience presents "perfect redness", a primitive quality that our world does not instantiate. **The single exception is realist primitivism** (Campbell 1993, Hacker 1987, Allen 2016, Gert 2017): it keeps the sui-generis quality *in the object* and denies the premise that physics inventories every property a body has. The upshot for the Map: the "secondary-quality residue" the [[galilean-exclusion]] relies on is, on four of the five ontologies, a residue in *experience* — the [[qualia]] debate — and the fifth does not remove it but moves it back into the world by rejecting the exclusion's own premise. No colour ontology dissolves the residue; the ones the outer reviewer cited (relationalism, physicalism) relocate it. The psychophysical concession the Map made today (relational structure of colour is objectively measurable) is correct and cuts against physicalism as much as against the Map: Pautz's structure argument and the unique-hue variability data (Kuehni 2004) show that the *structure* of colour appearance does not line up with reflectance structure either.

### Chalmers, "Perception and the Fall from Eden" (2006)
- **URL**: https://consc.net/papers/eden.pdf (preprint); publisher of record: Gendler & Hawthorne (eds.), *Perceptual Experience*, OUP 2006, pp. 49–125, DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199289769.003.0003 (Crossref: title, author "David J. - **Type**: Book chapter (raw PDF text extracted with pdftotext; line references are to the preprint, not the OUP pagination)
- **Key points**:
  - The phenomenologically adequate view of colour content is primitivism: "When I have a phenomenally red experience of an object, the object seems to be simply, primitively, red. The apparent redness does not seem to be a microphysical property, or a mental property, or a disposition, or an unspecified property that plays an appropriate causal role. Rather, it seems to be a simple qualitative property, with a distinctive sensuous nature. We might call this property perfect redness: the sort of property that might have been instantiated in Eden."
  - "There is good reason to believe that the relevant primitive properties are not instantiated in our world."
  - Footnote 4 records the split: "primitivists are divided on this issue.   - "In Edenic content, there need be no reference to normal causes, and no reference to experiences: instead, simple properties are attributed directly."
  - Conclusion: "the most fundamental content of perceptual experience is its Edenic content"; "It may be that perceptual experience is fundamentally equivalent to the presentation of an Edenic world."
  - Footnote 12: "The version of Russellian physicalism about phenomenal content advocated by Byrne and Hilbert (2003) may be particularly close to this view." Footnote 5 area: a primitivist can respond "by accepting that physicalism about ordinary objects is false."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral on the metaphysics of experience by design (the paper says it is "neutral on whether phenomenal character is" grounded in content). Compatible with Tenet 1 in that the presented quality is not a physical property of the object; not an argument for dualism.

## Major Positions and What Each Implies for the Residue

The question the task set: does any colour ontology leave an intrinsic phenomenal quality that no relational, dispositional or physical description captures — or does the residue relocate into experience?

### Reductive Colour Physicalism
- **Proponents**: Byrne & Hilbert (2003, 2007, 2020), Tye (2000), Jackson, McLaughlin (2003), Armstrong; Reid as ancestor (SEP §2.2).
- **Core claim**: colours are physical properties of objects — "types of reflectance" (Byrne & Hilbert 2003 abstract) — whose natures are hidden and empirically discovered.
- **Where the quality goes**: nowhere in colour. Colour is fully captured by physical description. The felt redness of the experience is handled by a separate theory of experience — Byrne & Hilbert's is representationalist ("Russellian physicalism about phenomenal content", Chalmers fn. 12) — which the Map already treats under [[first-order-representationalism]]. The residue is relocated to experience and then denied there by a further, independent physicalist commitment.
- **Pressure points**: (i) the structure argument (Pautz 2006): unique hues, binary hues and the opponent organisation of colour appearance have no reflectance counterpart; (ii) multiple realisation across surfaces, lights and volumes; (iii) variation (Kuehni 2004) — which reflectance class is *unique green* if observers disagree?
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral as a colour thesis; conflicts with Tenet 1 only through the representationalist supplement. The Map can grant reflectance physicalism about *colour* and still hold its line about *experience*.

### Colour Relationalism
- **Proponents**: Cohen (2004, 2009), Averill (1992); Chirimuuta's (2015) "adverbialist"/perceptual-pragmatist variant is a close cousin.
- **Core claim**: there is no *red simpliciter*; there are properties such as red-for-perceiver-A-in-circumstances-C (SEP §2.6). Cohen's Master Argument: perceptual variation gives no principled way to crown one variant veridical, so "an ecumenical reconciliation of the variants is preferable to an unmotivated stipulation in favor of just one of them" (Cohen 2009: 24, as quoted in SEP §2.6). Cohen's specific version is role-functionalism: colours are "functional roles involving perceptual effects on subjects" (abstract).
- **Where the quality goes**: into the perceiver-side relatum. The relation is objectively investigable — that is the psychophysics concession the Map made today, and it is correct — but the relation is *to a colour experience*, and relationalism is a theory of colour, not of that experience. SEP §2.6 names the residue exactly: whether relationalism "can account adequately for the phenomenology of visual experience" is the "crucial issue" between it and irrealism. Cohen's own reply, SEP notes, is "similar to that of Levin, in her defense of Color Dispositionalism" — i.e. it is a reply about experience, not about colour.
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral. The outer reviewer was right that the Map's exclusion page should name this option; wrong if the implication was that it dissolves the residue. It relocates the residue with unusual precision.

### Colour Dispositionalism
- **Proponents**: Locke/Boyle/Descartes as the "secondary quality" package (SEP §2.5; Bolton 2022 §4.2 on Boyle's "dispositive sense"); modern analytic form in Bennett, Dummett, McDowell, McGinn (1983), Peacocke (1984), Johnston (1992), Levin (2000).
- **Core claim**: colours are "powers to appear in distinctive ways to perceivers (of the right kind), in the right kind of circumstances" (SEP §2.5). Johnston's constituted-disposition version meets the causal-explanation constraint.
- **Where the quality goes**: into the "appear red" clause. The disposition is defined by its manifestation, and the manifestation is a colour experience. Johnston explicitly gives up Revelation — the claim that experience fully reveals the intrinsic nature of the colour — as "a small price to pay" (SEP §2.5). McGinn (1996) turned against his own earlier dispositionalism on phenomenological grounds — "Colors turn out not to look the way they are said dispositionally to be" (SEP §2.5 quoting McGinn) — and moved to primitivism.
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral as colour ontology; historically it is the very form the [[galilean-exclusion]] took, so it is not an *alternative* to the exclusion but its original vehicle.

### Realist Primitivism (the Simple View / Naïve Realism about colour)
- **Proponents**: Hacker (1987), Campbell (1993, 2005), McGinn (1996), Watkins (2005), Gert (2006, 2008, 2017), Allen (2011, 2015, 2016).
- **Core claim**: colours are "simple intrinsic, non-relational, non-reducible, qualitative properties" of physical bodies (SEP §2.1); "sui generis properties of objects, irreducible to physical or dispositional properties" (Gert 2017 abstract). Moderate Revelation (Campbell 2005, Gert 2008, Allen 2011) replaces Johnston's strong form.
- **Where the quality goes**: it stays in the object. This is the one ontology on which an intrinsic quality survives that no physical, relational or dispositional description captures — and it survives *in the world*, not in the mind. Campbell's abstract frames the whole position as a challenge to the "background argument" — "Physics tells us what is objectively there. It has no place for the colours of things. So colours are not objectively there" — i.e. to the Galilean premise itself.
- **Pressure points**: causal role (Hacker: no worse than solidity and liquidity, SEP §2.1); variation (Hardin, Cohen); metaphysical cost — Chalmers notes a primitivist may have to accept "that physicalism about ordinary objects is false".
- **Relation to site tenets**: the interesting case. Primitivism agrees with the Map that physical description leaves a quality out; it disagrees about where the quality lives. A primitivist world contains non-physical properties of *objects*; the Map's dualism locates the non-physical in *experience*. Both reject the completeness premise the exclusion rests on. The article should treat primitivism as a rival reading of the same residue, not as an ally.

### Eliminativism / Projectivism / Subjectivism
- **Proponents**: Hardin (1988/1993, 2004, 2008), Maund (1995, 2006, 2011), Boghossian & Velleman (1989), Averill (2005), Gatzia, Pautz (2006, 2017, 2020); historically Galileo, Descartes, Newton, Maxwell, Helmholtz (SEP §1.1).
- **Core claim**: the properties colour experience presents objects as having are instantiated by nothing. "The Eliminativist is a Color Primitivist, but of a different sort from Realist Primitivists" (SEP §2.3) — it accepts the primitivist analysis of the concept and denies instantiation. Pautz (2020: 380, via SEP): "the property being reddish is a bit like the property being a unicorn: it is an entirely uninstantiated property."
- **Where the quality goes**: explicitly into experience. Projectivism posits "subjective qualities which our visual system attributes to material objects" that "belong to (are intrinsic to) the visual experience" — the starred properties red\*, blue\* (SEP §2.4). SEP then splits subjectivism into a *dualist* form on which these qualities "are irreducible to physical properties" and a form that allows identity with brain properties. That split *is* the Map's residue question, now posed about experience rather than colour.
- **Relation to site tenets**: eliminativism about *colour* is compatible with Tenet 1 and is in fact the Galilean position; it is the second, experience-level question (dualist vs. physicalist subjectivism) that the tenets answer. Pautz's "partial virtual reality machine" is a representationalist irrealism that posits no mental items — a reminder that eliminativism about colour does not force sense-data or qualia.

### Chalmers's Edenic Content (the bridge)
- **Core claim**: experience presents "perfect redness", a primitive quality; nothing in our world instantiates it; Edenic (presentational) content is the most fundamental level of phenomenal content, and ordinary Fregean/Russellian content is derived from it by matching.
- **Where the quality goes**: Chalmers reproduces the eliminativist-primitivist result *for content* and is deliberately neutral on the metaphysics of phenomenal character. But his conclusion — "perceptual experience is fundamentally equivalent to the presentation of an Edenic world" — makes the perfect quality constitutive of what experience is. This is precisely the Map's residue stated in the vocabulary of perceptual content: the exclusion left perfect redness out of the object; Eden says it is still what experience presents; the open question is what a presenting of an uninstantiated quality *is*. That is the [[qualia]] / [[phenomenal-concepts-strategy]] debate, not the colour debate.
- **Caveat**: Chalmers's fn. 12 concedes that Byrne & Hilbert's Russellian physicalism "may be particularly close to this view" at the level of content. The bridge to a phenomenal-residue reading is therefore not something the Eden paper asserts; the Map would be making it.

## Key Debates

### Revelation
- **Sides**: Johnston (1992) states the strong form and drops it; Byrne & Hilbert (2007) use it to attack primitivism; Campbell (2005), Gert (2008), Allen (2011) defend a moderate form (necessary-condition reading, SEP §1.4/§2.1).
- **Core disagreement**: whether experience fully reveals the intrinsic nature of colour. If it does, dispositionalism and physicalism are false (colours do not *look* dispositional or like reflectances). If it does not, primitivism loses its main phenomenological argument.
- **Bearing on the residue**: Revelation is the sharpest statement that an intrinsic quality is *given* in experience. Notice that every party agrees the quality is given in experience; they disagree only about whether the giving is veridical. The residue is common ground; its location is the dispute.

### Variation and the Master Argument
- **Sides**: Cohen, Hardin, Averill vs. all objectivists (physicalist, primitivist, standard-observer dispositionalist).
- **Core disagreement**: whether perceptual variation (Kuehni 2004's ~600-observer unique-hue data) leaves any principled veridical variant.
- **Bearing on the residue**: this debate is entirely about colour's location and says nothing about experience; it is the strongest single reason to think the residue cannot be in the object as ordinarily conceived, which pushes it toward experience (relationalism, eliminativism) or toward a revisionary object property (Gert's indeterminate objective colours).

### Structure vs. quality
- **Sides**: Pautz (2006) and Hardin against reductive physicalism; Byrne & Hilbert (2003 replies) defending.
- **Core disagreement**: whether the similarity structure of colour experience can be read off reflectance structure.
- **Bearing on the residue**: the psychophysics concession the Map made today is about *structure* — the relational organisation of hue, saturation, lightness is objectively measurable (colorimetry, opponent models). Pautz's argument shows that measurable structure is a problem for physicalism, not a solution: the structure is the structure of *appearance*, and Gert draws the same lesson from the other side ("standard color spaces are appropriate for the description of color appearances"). Structure being tractable does not make the quality that carries the structure tractable — the same structure/quality split the Map's [[primary-secondary-quality-boundary]] page calls "structure described" versus "quality undergone".

## Potential Article Angles

1. **Recommended lead angle — "the sorting machine".** Colour ontology is a test of the secondary-quality residue in a precise sense: each ontology answers *where the intrinsic quality goes*, and every live option except realist primitivism sends it into experience. Physicalism hands it to a theory of experience; relationalism and dispositionalism define colour by a perceiver-side relatum that just is a colour experience; eliminativism and projectivism say so outright; Chalmers's Eden formalises it as presentational content. Primitivism alone keeps the quality in the object — and does so by rejecting the exclusion's completeness premise, which makes it a rival reading of the residue rather than a dissolution of it. Therefore the residue the [[galilean-exclusion]] points to is invariant across colour ontologies: it is a residue *in experience*, the [[qualia]] question, and the outer reviewer's intermediate views relocate it rather than remove it. The psychophysics concession stands, with the twist that measurable structure is a problem for physicalism too (Pautz, Kuehni, Gert). Tenet alignment: compatible with Tenet 1 (the quality is not a physical property of the object on any ontology except primitivism, which is non-physicalist about objects); engages Tenet 5 (Cohen's "theory of last resort" and "unmotivated stipulation" are parsimony judgements made under incomplete knowledge). The article should claim only *compatible*, and should say plainly that primitivism is the live alternative the Map's reading has to argue against, not ignore.
2. **Alternative angle — Revelation as the sharpest form.** Build the page around Johnston's five constraints, with Revelation as the hinge: every theory pays a price against one constraint, and the price each pays tells you where it puts the quality. Narrower and more scholastic; better as a section of angle 1 than as the lead.

**Target**: topics/, 2200–2800 body words, suggested slug `colour-ontology-and-the-secondary-quality-residue`. Keep the five-ontology survey to roughly 150 words each (the SEP does the survey; the Map's contribution is the sorting claim), spend the words on the sorting argument, Eden, primitivism as rival, and the structure/quality point. Follow `obsidian/project/writing-style.md`: front-load the sorting claim; named-anchor forward references to the primitivism section; "Relation to Site Perspective" claiming compatibility only.
