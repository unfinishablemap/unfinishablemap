## Executive Summary

The partial unity model (PUM) says a single subject's experiences can be co-conscious non-transitively: experience A is unified with C and B is unified with C, while A and B are not unified with each other. The model was first stated explicitly by Michael Lockwood in *Mind, Brain and the Quantum* (1989, ch. 6, pp. 87–94); Lockwood himself says so, and concedes that Trevarthen had "in effect" suggested it in the neurobiological literature. Nagel (1971) is not its author but an early source of the standing objection, that no one can project into such a perspective; Hurley (1998) supplied the vocabulary (weak vs strong unity) and the first reply (there is nothing it is like to be partially unified). Bayne (2010, ch. 2, pp. 37–46) builds the objection into an argument from Lockwood's own 1994 self-doubt, concludes that partial unity is weakly but not strongly inconceivable, and leaves "an air of suspicion" on the idea; in ch. 9 he prefers the switch model. Schechter (2014) states the strong form of the inconceivability argument in order to answer it (it would equally sink the two-streams model), yet rejects PUM for split-brain patients on empirical/individuation grounds and defends two subjects in one person (2018). The "2025 layered unity model" is real (Azenet Lopez, *Philosophical Studies* 182(8): 2159–2190), self-described as a partial rather than total unity. The verbatim wording of the Schechter sentence quoted at `voids/plurality-void` L39 could not be checked against the chapter; the only grep-verifiable published rendering is SEP's ("there is nothing..."), and no source for "There exists nothing..." was found.

## Fidelity questions

### (a) Who originated the model

**Lockwood 1989 is the coiner; Nagel 1971 and Hurley 1998 are not.**

- SEP note 49 (to §3.1.4): "Lockwood (1989) is the first to explicitly discuss this model. But see also Trevarthen (1974), Moor (1982) and Sperry (1984)." SEP note 34 (to §3.1.1): "Lockwood (1989) is the first explicit articulation of this model. But Trevarthen (1974) and Moor (1982) also implicitly suggest it."
- Lockwood's own words (1994, *Proceedings of the British Academy* 83, p. 92, grepped in the British Academy PDF): "The possibility that commissurotomy cases may involve a failure of the transitivity of co-consciousness, is one that I discussed and defended in my Mind, Brain and the Quantum (Lockwood, 1989, pp.87-94), unaware that the same suggestion had, in effect, already been advanced in the neurobiological literature" — he names Trevarthen. He also says what motivated it: "my efforts to imagine what it would be like to be conscious, while my corpus callosum was being very gradually severed, one fibre at a time."
- Schechter 2014 abstract (MIT Press / OpenAlex): the intermediate position is "proposed most explicitly by Lockwood", and "Philosophers have overwhelmingly rejected Lockwood's proposal."
- Bayne & Chalmers 2003 (consc.net PDF): "Others (e.g., Lockwood 1989) hold that the subject has a fragmented consciousness with nontransitive unity between the states: for example, the experiences of 'CAT' and of 'DOG' might each be unified with some background emotional state, but not with each other."
- **Nagel 1971** (*Synthese* 22: 396–413, Crossref-verified): considers a one-mind-but-poorly-integrated reading of the data among his five hypotheses and rejects it; SEP note 50 cites "Nagel (1971, 409–410)" as a source of the inconceivability objection, and Lockwood 1994 p. 95 cites "Nagel (1979, pp. 160,163)" (the *Mortal Questions* reprint) as someone who, like him, cannot project into a partially unified subject. So Nagel is a source of the **objection**, not of the model. I could not reach Nagel's text; the pp. 409–410 pointer is SEP's and the Speaks lecture-notes gloss ("one mind, involving both hemispheres, which is not as well integrated as normal minds") is secondary.
- **Hurley 1998** (*Consciousness in Action*) adds two things: the terminology, and the first reply to the objection. Lockwood 1994 p. 91: "The possibility of a failure of transitivity gives rise to a distinction for which Hurley uses the terms weak and strong unity." Bayne & Chalmers 2003 quote Hurley 1998 p. 165: "we cannot imagine what it is like for there to be partial unity. That doesn't show partial unity is unintelligible, because being partially unified isn't the sort of thing there could be anything it is like to be. We shouldn't expect to be able to imagine what it is like." Hurley's own commitment is mixed: SEP note 32 says "Among philosophers, Hurley (1998) can be read as advocating the view" (the switch model), and Bayne 2010 p. 50 n. says of Hurley (1998, 2003) that "She seems to have taken Lockwood's (1994) criticisms of it to heart." Do not list Hurley as a partial-unity proponent without that qualification.
- **Bayne & Chalmers 2003** add the transitivity framing: "It is plausible that subsumption is transitive, so that necessarily, if A subsumes B and B subsumes C, then A subsumes C. If so, the pairwise unity thesis will entail the phenomenal unity thesis for any finite set of phenomenal states", and they present partial unity as the denial of the total unity thesis. **Dainton 2000** is cited by SEP note 50 as a further source of skepticism about partial unity; I did not reach Dainton's text and have no page.

### (b) What Bayne 2010 argues against it; what Schechter 2014 and 2018 defend

**Bayne 2010** (*The Unity of Consciousness*, OUP; Google Books search-within on volume YpcSDAAAQBAJ, snippets with page numbers; a later control query returned zero for a phrase that had matched, so treat the zeros from that point as throttling, not absence):
- p. 37: the "partial unity model of consciousness might even capture the structure of consciousness in certain split-brain patients (see §9.4)". "Lockwood's proposal has met with a mixed response. Tye claims that there is no difficulty with partial unity..." (sentence cut by the snippet).
- p. 38: Bayne distinguishes the partial unity "which I, following Lockwood, am considering" from a weaker reading.
- p. 39: the objection "to partial unity is simply that it is difficult if not outright impossible to project oneself into the perspective of a partially unified subject (Lockwood 1994: 95). We can convert this thought into the following argument: (1) If partial unity were possible then there would be something distinctive it is like to be a partially unified subject..." (snippet ends).
- p. 40: Hurley's objection to that argument "centres on the claim that the contrast between partial unity and full unity is a matter of the relations between token experiences, whereas the subjective perspective has access only to..." (snippet ends).
- p. 44: "partial unity is inconceivable, but it is not, I think, strongly inconceivable. That leaves weak inconceivability. Is partial unity weakly inconceivable? I am inclined to think that it is. At any rate, I cannot 'see' that partial..." (snippet ends).
- p. 46: "...partial unity is impossible, but we cannot—I suggest—see that it is possible. The upshot of these reflections, I suggest, is that an air of suspicion ought to accompany any talk of partial unity."
- So the **a priori line** in Bayne is deliberately modest: weak inconceivability, suspicion rather than refutation. This matches SEP §3.1.4: "as opponents of the partial unity model themselves admit, this sort of inconceivability is hardly a guide to impossibility." The **empirical line** is the switch model (Bayne 2008, *J. Phil.* 105(6): 277–300; Bayne 2010 ch. 9, with p. 210 cited by Shoemaker's NDPR review): split-brain patients keep one fully unified stream whose contents alternate between hemispheres. Shoemaker's review: Bayne "argues against both the 'two streams' account of split brain cases and what he calls the partial unity model" and proposes the switch model.

**Schechter 2014** ("Partial Unity of Consciousness: A Preliminary Defense", in Bennett & Hill eds., *Sensory Integration and the Unity of Consciousness*, MIT Press, pp. 347–374, Crossref):
- Abstract: she "issues a preliminary defense of the partial unity model (PUM) of split-brain consciousness" and argues "that the major philosophical objections to that model apply no more to it than they do to the conscious disunity or duality model."
- SEP §3.1.4 (grepped): "A more promising version of the argument is that partial unity is inconceivable in that 'there is nothing that would even count as successfully imagining what it is like to have a partially unified consciousness' (Schechter 2014). But even this version of the argument can be resisted. As Schechter (2014) points out, it is not easy to see why this argument would not equally target the dual-stream account." And: "it is unacceptable, Schechter argues, to reject all contenders but the single stream view on purely a priori grounds."
- SEP then reports her **empirical** rejection: "Although Schechter thinks that partial unity cannot be rejected on a priori grounds, she has argued that there are empirical reason against partial unity." The argument: if independent evidence shows the two perspectives P1 and P2 belong to distinct subjects, a shared experience E3 "would belong to two numerically distinct subjects, which conflicts with the maxim that we should partially individuate experiences in terms of their subjects." SEP's own verdict: this "is not as straightforward as it might seem", because it rests on unresolved questions about how experiences and subjects are individuated.
- Net: Schechter is the person who says the inconceivability argument settles nothing, and who nonetheless does not hold PUM for split-brain patients. She is neither the objection's author nor a partial-unity believer. The plurality void's L39 "sharpened the point" misassigns her role.

**Schechter 2018** (*Self-Consciousness and "Split" Brains: The Minds' I*, OUP): defends a two-subjects (two minds) view combined with the claim that a split-brain patient is one person (SEP §3.1.5; Godfrey-Smith's NDPR review). Godfrey-Smith: partial unity is "somewhat neglected" in the book; Schechter (2014) had argued it is "coherent and a live option"; the book's Appendix argues the evidence for partial unity in affective states is not very convincing; clinicians "do tend to accept partial unity, in an informal way". SEP note 48 and the §3.1.1 text confirm she questions partial integration of affective experience. **Schechter 2025**, "Action unity of consciousness" (*Philosophy and the Mind Sciences*, doi 10.33735/phimisci.2025.11812, OpenAlex abstract): proposes "a new kind of unity relation that might persist after split-brain surgery" grounded in interhemispheric motoric integration, and asks whether it is a genuine kind of conscious unity. Relevant to the void's De Haan et al. (2020) line but not itself a PUM defence.

### (c) Is the "2025 layered unity model" real?

**Yes.** Azenet Lopez, "A layered unity model of split-brain consciousness", *Philosophical Studies* 182(8): 2159–2190, published online 12 June 2025, doi 10.1007/s11098-025-02339-3, open access (CC BY; Crossref, OpenAlex, LMU repository copy grepped). The 02-10 note's filing of it under "partial unity" is defensible on Lopez's own words: she concludes that split-brain consciousness "is a partial rather than total kind of unity", adds that "neurotypical consciousness itself might only be partially unified", and in n. 18 writes "See also Lockwood (1989, 1996), for earlier defenses of this view" (her "1996" is the Peacocke volume printing of Lockwood 1994). Her model is nevertheless a different mechanism from Lockwood's: local experiential layers break down while global layers stay unified, with attention doing the unifying; she distinguishes it from the switch model: "The switch model (Bayne, 2008, 2010) proposes that split-brain consciousness remains one single conscious stream, whose contents alternate from side to side. None of these models explains how simultaneous right- and left-side experiences could have a joint phenomenology." Her n. 20 adds that Prinz (2012) "also advocates partial unity with failures on transitivity". The `topics/split-brain-consciousness` L74 use of "layered unity" is consistent with this.

**Also current**: Quentin Chiang, "A New Argument for Partial Unity", listed on PhilArchive as forthcoming in *Ergo* (record added 2026-05), arguing for the partially unified stream view from congenitally acallosal humans. PhilArchive returned 403 to direct fetch; the venue and date come from a search summary only. Treat as unverified until the Ergo page is reached. Godfrey-Smith (2020, *Metazoa*, ch. 6) combines the two-streams model with partial unity (SEP §3.1.1 and note 35).

## Major Positions

### Partial unity model (PUM)
- **Proponents**: Lockwood 1989 (originator; wavering by 1994); Schechter 2014 (defence against a priori objections only); Prinz 2012 (per Lopez n. 20); Lopez 2025 (layered variant); Godfrey-Smith 2020 (hybrid with two streams); Chiang forthcoming (acallosal argument); clinicians informally (Godfrey-Smith's review of Schechter 2018).
- **Core claim**: co-consciousness, "the relation in which two experiences stand, when there is an experience of which they are both parts" (Lockwood's definition as reported in search results, 1989: 88; not grepped by me), is not transitive; a subject can have experiences A and B each unified with C but not with each other. Hurley's label: weak unity.
- **Explanatory payoff** (SEP §3.1.4): "a straightforward explanation of partial integration in split-brain subjects", and because partial unity is gradeable it can explain the difference between experimental and everyday behaviour "in terms of fluctuations in degrees of partial unity across different contexts."
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral on Dualism (Lockwood's own metaphysics is a quantum-flavoured Russellian view; Bayne and Schechter are not dualists; do not enlist any of them). For the Map the payload is structural: if PUM is even coherent, the question "how many consciousnesses?" can lack a whole-number answer, which is the plurality void's theme and consistent with the house line that singularity is a framework boundary (P-I5), not a datum. It must not be written as evidence for No Many Worlds.

### The inconceivability objection
- **Sources**: Nagel 1971 (409–410 per SEP), Lockwood 1994 p. 95 ("I must confess, however, that, in spite of having defended it in print, I am still by no means wholly persuaded that the concept of a merely weakly unified consciousness really does make sense"; "I am still unable to project myself into the position of a subject with a partially unified and partially disunified consciousness"), Bayne 2010 pp. 39–46, Dainton 2000 (per SEP; unverified).
- **Strong form**: not merely that we fail to imagine it, but that nothing would count as imagining it (the sentence Schechter 2014 states; SEP attaches it to her as the formulation, then reports her reply).
- **Replies**: Hurley 1998 p. 165 (nothing it is like to be partially unified, so imaginative failure is expected and harmless); Schechter 2014 (the argument would equally rule out two streams, since no single point of view can imagine two separate streams either; rejecting all rivals to the single stream a priori is unacceptable); SEP's second reply (a partially unified subject has no full perspective on all its experiences, which explains why we cannot project into one).
- **Current state**: opponents concede inconceivability is not impossibility (SEP; Bayne's "weak inconceivability"). The a priori case is widely regarded as inconclusive; the live disagreement is empirical and about individuation.

### Rivals in the split-brain debate
- **Two streams / duality**: Sperry, Puccetti, Marks 1980, Tye 2003, Schechter 2018 (two subjects, one person).
- **Switch model**: Levy 1977/1990 early; Hurley 1998 readable as such; Bayne 2008, 2010 the prominent advocate.
- **Unified agency / access-disunity**: Pinto et al. 2017 ("divided perception but undivided consciousness").
- **Layered unity**: Lopez 2025 (partial unity at local layers, preserved global unity via attention).

## Key Debates

### Does inconceivability settle anything?
- **Sides**: Nagel, Lockwood (1994), Bayne (2010) treat the projection failure as at least a mark against PUM; Hurley (1998) and Schechter (2014) deny it has probative force.
- **Core disagreement**: whether a failure to occupy a perspective is evidence about that perspective's possibility.
- **Current state**: ongoing, but the a priori opponents themselves downgrade the argument (Bayne's "air of suspicion"); SEP's own gloss is that the argument "is problematic".

### Individuating experiences by subjects
- **Sides**: Schechter 2014 (if P1 and P2 are perspectives of distinct subjects, a shared E3 would belong to two subjects, which the individuation maxim forbids) versus SEP's reply (if subjects are grounded in physical facts, two subjects might share correlates and so share an experience).
- **Current state**: open; SEP calls these issues "too close to the deep questions about unity that the debate revolves around."
