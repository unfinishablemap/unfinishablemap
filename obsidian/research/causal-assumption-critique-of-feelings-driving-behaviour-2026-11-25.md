## Executive Summary

Brian Key (neuroscientist) and Deborah J. Brown (philosopher), both at the University of Queensland, argue in "Making sense of feelings" (*Neuroscience of Consciousness* 2024(1), niae034) against what they name "the causal assumption", the commonsense idea that feelings such as pain cause behaviour. On their **sense making sense (SMS) hypothesis**, nonconscious neural processing is the common cause of both the behaviour and the feeling, and the feeling's function is to *explain* the behaviour to the brain that produces it. They reject epiphenomenalism on evolutionary grounds, so they claim a non-causal function. A 2025 sequel (*Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*) states the decisive premise outright: there is "no known mechanism by which pain could directly gate ion channels", so "the real cause of behaviour is neural activity". The programme has five publications to date and two independent published replies, both from pain scientists and both aimed at follow-ups; one was read in full (Edwards, Campbell & Haythornthwaite 2025, against the extension to thoughts). No philosophical critique of the 2024 paper turned up. **For the Map**, the critique splits cleanly. ### Key & Brown (2024), "Making sense of feelings"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/nc/niae034; full text PMC11412240 (fetched via `efetch db=pmc`). Article type "Rapid Communication" (brief report); epub 19 Sept 2024.
- **Type**: Theoretical paper (hypothesis plus conceptual model; no new data).
- **Key points** (all VERIFIED in the PMC XML text):
  - Names "the causal assumption" and holds it "is incompatible with the standard view of motor action in neuroscience".
  - SMS: "the primary function of feelings is not to cause behaviour but to 'explain' it". "The fact that the real cause of behaviour—the nonconscious neural processing—is a common cause of both the behaviour and the feeling is what explains why feelings seem not only to control behaviour". "Viewed in this way, feelings provide subjective awareness of the cause of behaviour rather than being causes themselves."
  - Huxley as source: "We extend Huxley's view by suggesting that valenced feelings are 'symbols' that refer to the neural causes of behaviour."
  - Anti-epiphenomenalism on evolutionary grounds: "Since epiphenomenalism is antagonistic to fundamental commitments of evolutionary biology, the view developed here challenges the standard view about the function of feelings without denying that feelings have a function"; "Why should the brain evolve mechanisms to produce conscious feelings that demand considerable energy expenditure (Mashour et al. 2020), if feelings were causally redundant?"
  - Explanation without causation: "While explanations may cite causes, they need not themselves be causes." A feeling "does not lead to an explanation but 'constitutes' the explanation" (drawing on Russell's knowledge by acquaintance and Giustina 2022: knowledge by acquaintance is "constituted by acquaintance and not caused by it").
  - Architecture (Fig. 2): a forward model predicts the motor command from a copy of the driving input, and an inverse model infers the "predicted driving input" (the inferred cause). "This predicted cause is the predicted driving input which is then fed back into the base layer to drive the behaviour. The predicted cause is also simultaneously processed by neural circuits to generate the conscious desire to act." The feeling is therefore a *collateral product* of a neural signal that does the causal work, which is Huxley's structure.
  - Guarding: "pain is not causing the guarding behaviour but rather it is the anticipation or prediction that inhibiting the neural cause will prevent the injury-inducing movements that is the driving cause."
  - The half-zombie: a creature "conscious of its behaviour but lacking explanatory feelings" that "finds itself writhing and grimacing but feels no pain" "could easily be consumed with understanding its behaviour at the expense of other life-saving actions"; "In the absence of feelings, our half-zombie would quickly become computationally overburdened." Also: "We propose that feelings act as heuristics to reduce computational cost". They hedge the conceivability: "Perhaps such a being is conceivable".
  - The authors set aside how feelings arise: "It is not our intention to address how brain circuits generate feelings."
  - Translational point: "The SMS hypothesis thus focuses a spotlight on the inappropriateness of many commonly used animal behavioural tests to distinguish between pain and nociception"; the failed analgesic "has simply influenced low-level circuitry".
  - Conclusion: "This locates the function of feelings with the way in which they inform decision-making rather than with their needing to be themselves direct causes of behaviour or actions."
- **Tenet alignment**: Conflicts with Bidirectional Interaction (directly, for pain). Neutral on Dualism: it is silent on what feelings are, and its non-causal feelings fit property dualism as readily as physicalism.

## The Argument at Full Strength

1. **The motor-control premise.** The standard neuroscience of action explains movement by neural commands. On their model, feelings are not among the inputs a motor circuit integrates, and no known mechanism lets a feeling "directly gate ion channels" or generate action potentials (2025, 2026).
2. **The common-cause model.** The same nonconscious process that drives behaviour also produces the feeling (via forward and inverse models of the motor command), which is why feelings *seem* to cause behaviour (Huxley's symbol relation, Wegner's apparent mental causation).
3. **Dissociations** (the evidence, all cited by K&B and **LEAD** at source unless noted):
   - Nociceptive withdrawal is reflexive and runs without awareness (Riddoch 1917, on the divided spinal cord), and first pain arrives after the withdrawal begins (Campbell & LaMotte 1983; Thorell et al. 2023).
   - Cortical stimulation separates feeling from movement. Premotor stimulation produces movement without intention, and parietal stimulation produces intention, and even awareness of movement, without movement (Desmurget et al. 2009, *Science* 324). Superior parietal stimulation halts movement with no felt intention to stop (Desmurget et al. 2018; Fornia et al. 2022).
   - Pain asymbolia: pain is reported without pain behaviour. K&B tie it to posterior parietal lesions (Schilder & Stengel 1931; Rubins & Friedman 1948; Griffith & Kind 2024); the Map's own page records insular lesions too.
   - The inverse case: four epilepsy patients showed full pain behaviour (grimacing, screaming) without felt pain (Hagiwara et al. 2020). This one is **VERIFIED** on the Map's `pain-asymbolia` page, which cites it at source.
4. **Confabulation** shows a drive to explain. Split-brain patient P.S. invented reasons for actions commanded to the right hemisphere (Gazzaniga & LeDoux 1978), and a patient made to laugh by cortical stimulation invented a cause (Fried et al. 1998). Illusions like the cutaneous rabbit (Geldard & Sherrick 1972) show that feeling presents *predicted* causes.
5. **The non-causal function.** Feelings constitute non-propositional, acquaintance-style "explanations" of behaviour that "inform decision-making" and cut computational cost (2024). By 2025 this is put as being "a ground but not a cause".
6. **The payoff.** Animal behavioural assays measure nociception, not pain, which explains why analgesics fail to translate. Which animals can feel becomes a matter of architecture, not behaviour.

**What is strong here.** Premise 3 and the payoff in 6 are well supported and mostly uncontroversial in pain science: behaviour neither entails nor is entailed by felt pain, and rodent reflex assays do not measure pain. The conjunction of premises 1 and 2 is a coherent, empirically adequate model.

**What is weak.** (a) The dissociations show only that feeling is *not necessary* for particular behaviours (reflexes, seizure automatisms), which no interactionist denies. That feeling is *never* a cause does not follow. (b) The guarding argument is a re-description. No data show guarding persisting with the feeling removed and its neural correlate intact. (c) Premise 1 is the mechanism argument, an argument from ignorance unless closure is added, and Brown's 2026 abstract shows closure is assumed. (d) The positive function is unstable (see the internal-tension argument below). (e) The authors decline to say how circuits generate feelings, yet they treat a mechanism-less *upward* dependence (brain to feeling) as unproblematic while rejecting a mechanism-less *downward* one. If feelings are distinct enough from their neural vehicles to lack those vehicles' causal powers, the upward "generation" relation is as unexplained as the downward one would be. This parity point is the Map's argument, not one found in any reply.

## Inference versus Causation: The Separation the Map Needs

**About inference (the evidential point), which the Map accepts.** Behaviour is a poor guide to feeling in both directions: pain without pain behaviour (asymbolia) and pain behaviour without pain (Hagiwara). Reflex-level and nociceptive assays cannot settle whether a creature feels. The Map already holds this:
- `concepts/pain-asymbolia` ("Complementary Dissociations"): "behaviour and reported pain dissociate in both directions", which "weakens any inference from one-directional dissociation to a separable phenomenal cause".
- `concepts/valence` ("Valence and Causal Work"): "the dissociation runs both ways"; the lesion's common-cause reading survives (P-MC1).
- `topics/fish-sentience-and-the-teleost-pain-debate` and `apex/competency-without-felt-experience` already refuse to read experience off behaviour or off anatomy.

**About causation (the metaphysical point), which the Map rejects.** The step from "behaviour is a poor guide to feeling" and "feeling is not necessary for this behaviour" to "feeling never causes behaviour" needs a further premise. In K&B that premise is closure plus the mechanism argument. The Map's Minimal Quantum Interaction tenet denies closure at quantum indeterminacies (`the-epiphenomenalist-threat`, "How the Map Escapes"), and the Map's Bidirectional Interaction tenet holds the causal claim.

The asymmetry matters for the article. The critique's *data* touch the Map only where the Map once read asymbolia as evidence *for* phenomenal causation. The pain pages have since retreated to "shared explanandum" and "compatible with, not evidence for", so the data now press on nothing the Map asserts. The critique's *conclusion* touches the Map's core tenet, but only through premises the Map has already declined.

**Overall grade.** The Map has no refutation of the causal-assumption critique and should not claim one. It has an exact account of where the disagreement lies (closure and mechanism), an evidential concession it has already made, and one argument from inside the opponent's position that shows SMS cannot keep both its anti-epiphenomenalism and its non-causal function.

## Relation to the Fish Programme

SMS continues Key's architectural-criterion programme: Key (2016), "Why fish do not feel pain"; Key, Zalucki & Brown (2022), "A First Principles Approach to Subjective Experience", *Front. Syst. Neurosci.* 16, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnsys.2022.756224 (cited by K&B; **LEAD** at source). The 2024 paper restricts feeling to "complex brains (i.e. brains that can re-represent neural information at different levels of abstraction; Key et al. 2022)" and closes: "any animal that possesses this minimal architecture or an equivalent architecture capable of executing the neural computations of these models will have, at least, the potential for subjective experience" (VERIFIED). It does **not** continue Diggles et al. (2024). That multi-author skeptics' statement argues from epistemics (definitions, replication, burden of proof), as the fish page already notes. SMS adds a *function-first* ground for Key's architectural test: if feelings do not cause behaviour, behavioural markers cannot reveal them, so only architecture can. The fish page's diagnosis already answers that inference ("structure determines function" is engineering; "structure determines feeling" is a metaphysical identity claim, per `apex/competency-without-felt-experience`). No edit is planned. A future pass on the fish page could add one clause noting that Key's later work grounds the architectural test in the denial of the causal assumption, but its open P3 at L76 has priority and must not be disturbed.

## Relation to Site Perspective

- **Bidirectional Interaction**: directly opposed for pain. The fold shows the opposition rests on closure and the mechanism argument, both premises the Map has declined. It also records that SMS's attempt to keep a function without causation fails by its own evolutionary standard.
- **Minimal Quantum Interaction**: this tenet is what denies the closure premise, but the critique's "no known mechanism" charge lands on the Map's acknowledged mechanism debt (P-Q10). The article must not imply that the Map has a known mechanism.
- **Dualism**: SMS is silent on it. Non-causal feelings distinct from their neural vehicles are compatible with property dualism, and the authors' refusal to explain generation leaves the hard problem where the Map says it is.
- **No Many Worlds**: not engaged.
- **Occam's Razor Has Limits**: the common-cause model looks simpler only once closure is assumed. Without closure, its extra posit (a non-causal "function" that selection somehow shaped) is no cheaper than a causal one.
