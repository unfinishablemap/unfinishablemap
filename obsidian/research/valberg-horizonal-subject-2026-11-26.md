## Executive Summary

J. J. Valberg's *Dream, Death, and the Self* (Princeton 2007) is the third inegalitarian first-person realism the Map should know, beside Hare's egocentric presentism and Merlo's subjectivism. Merlo (2021) files "Valberg's (2007) 'personal horizon' theory" with Fine and Hare as versions of Subjectivism, and names Valberg among those who "suggested" identifying reality with one's own point of view. The Introduction confirms half of that. Valberg's "personal horizon" is the subject matter within which the world appears; it is "nothing in itself"; and solipsism, "the thought that my horizon is (in a certain sense) the horizon", is in the context of death "something that we all know to be true". But he also grants "I know it is like that for everyone", and he does not resolve the conflict. He calls it an antinomy that philosophy can articulate but not solve: "The puzzle has no solution." So Valberg differs from both neighbours twice over. He puts the privilege in no property of experiences or objects, but in the horizon within which items are present. And he takes neither Hare's weak solipsism nor Merlo's two-level grounding: the inegalitarian truth and the egalitarian knowledge stand together as an "impossible fact". For the Map, the antinomy is the closest published neighbour of its own unresolved stance on the which-subject fact. It is not support. Valberg holds that the soul is "a fiction" (1992, via Tartaglia) and that consciousness is "neither material nor ethereal" (2017). He treats the dualism–physicalism debate as one confusion, and he makes the horizon "not a possible term of the causal relation". That leaves no room for Bidirectional Interaction if the Map's subject were the horizon. **Verdict: `fold`.** Both candidate sections are at cap. ## Terminology First (read before quoting)

- **"Horizonal subject" is not Valberg's phrase.** It appears 0 times in the Introduction and 0 times in Valberg 2017. In Valberg's usage the *subject* is "the one at the center of the horizon, the subject. And the subject is the human being" (p. 15). The *horizon* is "(in a certain sense) the self" (p. 11) and is "nothing in itself" (p. 12). Never write "for Valberg the subject is the horizon".
- **"Inegalitarian" and "egalitarian" are not Valberg's words** (0 and 0 in both texts read). The labels are Hellie's (2013) and Merlo's. Valberg's own vocabulary is "solipsism", the horizon's "claim to uniqueness" (p. 22), "the solipsistic puzzle of death" (p. 20), and "an impossible fact" (p. 19).
- **"Positional conception of the self"** is known only from chapter titles 14 and 15 (MUSE table of contents). Its content was not read.
- **"Pre-eminent"** and "a weak version of solipsism (arguably closely related to Hare's egocentric presentism)" are Wikipedia's glosses. Neither is attested in the text read.

## Key Sources

### Valberg, J. J., *Dream, Death, and the Self* (Princeton University Press, 2007), Introduction, pp. 1–24
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1515/9780691190181 (Crossref: Princeton UP, 2007, ISBN 9780691190181 for the e-book; print ISBNs 9780691128580 and 9780691128597). Sample chapter: http://web.archive.org/web/20150906235127/http://press.princeton.edu/chapters/i8416.pdf
- **Type**: Monograph, primary source.
- **The horizon** (Int.7, pp. 11–13). Valberg first discusses the "communal horizon", which is "our system of language-games". He then introduces "the 'personal' horizon … the subject matter that in the course of our reflections will emerge as the subject matter of the dream hypothesis, and of death: the subject matter, the horizon, that is (in a certain sense) the self" (p. 11).
  - "The world appears from within my horizon (my consciousness, my experience, my life), while that from within which it appears, the horizon that is 'mine,' passes us by" (p. 12).
  - "The horizon that is 'mine'—my consciousness, my experience, my life—is in a sense nothing: it is nothing in itself" (p. 12).
  - "By its very nature, the personal horizon tends to remain a hidden subject matter" (p. 12).
- **Subject as position** (p. 11): "it is only by being 'at the center' of the personal horizon, of the horizon that is 'mine,' that something (a human being) is a 'subject.'"
  - The activity is the subject's, not the horizon's (p. 15). Valberg corrects Sartre here: "that which is active (that which wills, which constitutes itself) is not the horizon, which is nothing in itself, but the one at the center of the horizon, the subject. And the subject is the human being. If anything is rationally active it is the human being, who is part of the world and therefore not nothing in itself."
- **Multiplicity is built in** (p. 11): "the fact that the communal horizon is 'ours,' that it is communal, entails a multiplicity of subjects". Also: "Without the horizon that is 'mine,' then, there can be no horizon that is 'ours.'"
- **Solipsism** (pp. 12–13). Part 2 asks in what sense death is "mine". The answer "points to solipsism: the thought that my horizon is (in a certain sense) the horizon. Solipsism, as it appears in the context of death, is not a quirky philosophical 'view' to be refuted or dismissed out of hand, but something that we all know to be true and thus believe".
- **The antinomy** (pp. 19–20).
  - The solipsistic puzzle of death: "the prospect of my death looms as the end of everything and in that sense as the death; yet I know it is like that for everyone. How could a multitude of deaths each be 'the' death?" (p. 20).
  - The general form: "The underlying puzzle takes the form of an antinomy or contradiction" (p. 20).
  - Death is "something that is impossible yet a fact: an impossible fact … And once everything is out in the open, there is nothing more (philosophically) that we can do. The puzzle has no solution." (p. 19).
  - The puzzles of death "depend solely on the status of the personal horizon: on its claim to uniqueness, on its nontemporality" (p. 22).
- **Time and causation** (pp. 20–21). The horizon "emerges (in our reflections on the dream hypothesis) as a subject matter that is outside time and therefore cannot cease to be". This is the "temporal puzzle of death". On causation: "The personal horizon, i.e., consciousness in the horizonal sense, cannot itself be included in time. Causation, however, presupposes temporality … Consciousness (in the horizonal sense), since it is not in time, is not a possible term of the causal relation—and yet, right now, because of what is happening in my brain, there is such a thing as my consciousness" (p. 21). Footnote 23 says this puzzle "has nothing to do with what philosophers call the 'qualitative character of experience' or with what has recently come to be known as 'the problem of the explanatory gap.'"
- **Lineage, credited by Valberg himself** (pp. 10 n. 8, 13–17).
  - The word "horizon" comes via the dictionary sense ("the limit of one's experience"), Husserl and Heidegger, with the sense "redirect[ed] … from a communal to a personal subject matter" (n. 8).
  - Kant's appearance/in-itself contrast depends on an unstated "'horizonal' conception of consciousness" (p. 14).
  - "Nothing in itself" fits Sartre's for-itself, but Valberg rejects Sartre's active for-itself (pp. 14–15).
  - Wittgenstein's Tractarian "'metaphysical subject,' the subject that is not part of the world but its 'limit,' is, I believe, the conception of the personal horizon" (p. 17, citing *TLP* 5.632–33 and 5.62).
- **Method** (pp. 1, 13): "in philosophy we discover only what we already know"; "nothing can rationally force us to accept that there is such a subject matter. Nothing can guarantee that it comes into view—except our being open to it."
- **Tenet alignment**: see the graded section below.

### Valberg, J. J., "Transcendence", *Journal of Philosophy of Life* 7(1), July 2017, 187–194
- **URL**: https://www.philosophyoflife.org/jpl201715si.pdf (open)
- **Type**: Paper, primary source; a critique of Tartaglia's *Philosophy in a Meaningless Life* (2016). It is the most compact statement of the horizonal conception in open text.
- **Key points**:
  - The debate both sides share: "The familiar debate in current philosophy about consciousness or experience assumes that it is something which occurs or goes on in us – that it is a phenomenon – the main issue being whether it occurs in our brains (the more popular view) or in our souls (the view to be avoided)" (pp. 191–192). Valberg agrees with Tartaglia "that it is therefore a mistake to conceive of consciousness as some kind of phenomenon (state or process or activity etc.) occurring in our heads or souls" (p. 191).
  - The alternative: "The alternative, I suggest, involves not that consciousness is transcendent, but that it is, as I might put it, horizonal" (p. 191). First-personally, "'consciousness (experience)' refers to that from within which my hand is present, not to a phenomenon but to a horizon" (p. 192).
  - "On the horizonal conception, consciousness (experience) is part neither of the brain or the soul; it is neither material nor ethereal. It is in a sense nothing, that is, nothing in itself: nothing apart from there being something given or present from within it." (p. 192).
  - The diagnosis of the problem of consciousness: running together the two conceptions "issues in the problematic conception of something which is both a phenomenon and yet somehow less than that – a shadowy or ethereal phenomenon" (pp. 192–193).
  - Causal direction: "Consciousness does not consist of, but is caused by, such phenomena" (p. 193), meaning brain events. "What has been omitted is not a phenomenon but a fact" (p. 193), the fact of presence. When the brain activity ceases, "that will be it: there will be just nothing" (p. 193).
  - On the Cartesian soul: "the idea of such a further reality seems foreign to any conception of reality which respects modern science" (p. 188).
- **Tenet alignment**: compatible with Tenet 1's bare irreducibility ("does not consist of"). It rejects substance dualism and diagnoses the dualism–physicalism debate as a confusion. See the grading below.

## Valberg's Position, Stated Exactly

1. **Two conceptions of consciousness.**
   - On the *phenomenal* conception, consciousness is a state or process "in our heads or souls". The physicalist and the dualist both assume it (2017: 191–192).
   - On the *horizonal* conception, consciousness is "that from within which" things are present (2017: 192). It is not a phenomenon of any kind.
2. **The personal horizon is nothing in itself.** It is nothing apart from the presence of something within it (2007: 12, 14; 2017: 192). It is "outside time" (2007: 20–21).
3. **Subjecthood is positional.** A human being is a subject by being "at the center" of a horizon (p. 11). The subject is the human being, part of the world, and it alone is active (p. 15). The horizon is "(in a certain sense) the self" (p. 11).
4. **Multiplicity is granted.** The communal horizon "entails a multiplicity of subjects", and the personal and communal horizons are interdependent (p. 11).
5. **The privilege.** My horizon is "(in a certain sense) the horizon" (p. 12), and the horizon has a "claim to uniqueness" (p. 22). In the context of death this solipsism is "something that we all know to be true" (p. 13). This is the inegalitarian half, and it is what Merlo picks up.
6. **The parity.** "I know it is like that for everyone" (p. 20). The publisher puts it: "since it is the same for everyone, this cannot be."
7. **No resolution.** The two together form "an antinomy or contradiction" (p. 20), an "impossible fact" (p. 19), and "The puzzle has no solution" (p. 19). Philosophy's task is to bring the puzzle to light, not to solve it.
8. **Causation.** The brain causes the horizon, although the horizon "is not a possible term of the causal relation" (p. 21). This is a second impossible fact, the "puzzle of the causation of consciousness" (p. 21). Nothing in the texts read has the horizon causing anything.

**What makes it "inegalitarian"**: item 5, the uniqueness claim. **What stops it being simply inegalitarian**: items 4, 6 and 7. Merlo's "suggested so" is accurate about item 5 and silent about items 6–7.

## How Valberg Differs from Hare and Merlo

| | Hare (2007, 2009) | Merlo (2016, 2021) | Valberg (2007, 2017) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where the privilege sits | Monadic *presence* on experiences and perceptual objects (per `concepts/egocentric-presentism`) | Subjective facts about *mental* states ("feltness") | In no item: the horizon "from within which" items are present, which is "nothing in itself" |
| Relational or monadic | Monadic, by insistence | A subjective fact, true simpliciter but not from all points of view | Presence is presence *from within* a horizon; the uniqueness belongs to the horizon |
| Other minds | Other creatures are conscious, but only one subject world exists ("a kind of solipsism") | Parity at the objective level, a spotlight at the subjective level, the objective grounded in the subjective | Multiplicity built into the communal horizon; parity known ("like that for everyone") |

| Motivation | The Grounding Problem for self-bias (ethics) | A phenomenological exercise plus the Appearance/Reality Principle | Dream hypothesis and death: discovering "what we already know" |
| Time | Parity with presentism / tense realism | Merlo 2013: specialness and egalitarianism framed for time | The horizon is outside time; 2013 paper: the uniqueness-generalisation for "now" is "impossible" |
| Metaphysics of mind | Lean physicalist ontology (per the Map's page, from secondary summaries) | Concerns the structure of facts; neutral on substrate | Neither material nor ethereal; soul "a fiction" (1992 via Tartaglia); horizon caused by the brain |

Two consequences the Map's pages do not yet record:

1. **Valberg escapes the objections the Map's egocentric-presentism page lists against Hare's placement.** Merlo's back-of-the-painting worry and McDaniel's point that unnoticed parts are present both target presence as a property of *objects*. On Valberg's view presence is a fact that holds "from within" a horizon, so neither applies. This is the note's reconstruction; neither Merlo nor McDaniel mentions Valberg.
2. **Valberg does not answer the communicability objection; he relocates it.** On his own account the uniqueness claim is something "nothing can rationally force us to accept" (p. 13), and it is stated only alongside its contradiction. The difficulty of stating it is, for him, part of the datum. That is close to Hare's reply on the egocentric-presentism page, but reached by a different route.

## Relation to List's Quadrilemma (the note's reconstruction)

List's four claims are first-person realism, non-solipsism, non-fragmentation and one world (`concepts/egocentric-presentism` §The Quadrilemma). Valberg asserts first-person realism (the horizon's uniqueness), non-solipsism (multiplicity; "like that for everyone") and one world (the horizon "contains the whole of the world, space, and time", per the publisher). He offers no theory of fragmentation; he records the clash as a contradiction he cannot remove. He therefore takes **no exit**. In the vocabulary of SF L110 he "sits in a triad not yet made consistent", but he treats that position as final rather than pending. Valberg never mentions List, Fine or fragmentalism in the text read. The mapping is the Map's.

This makes Valberg the published position nearest to the Map's present stance. SF L114 holds the fact "pending a choice", and `concepts/egocentric-presentism` L81 treats the privileged "I" as a genuine void. The difference matters. The Map's choice is open; Valberg says no choice is available. If the Map ever endorsed Valberg's verdict, the indexical objection under Tenet 4 would rest on one side of a contradiction its source calls insoluble.

## What Posit One Gains, and What It Must Answer

Posit One (`tenets/background-commitments` L32): "there is a fact of *which* subject this is, prior to and independent of any physical or functional criterion", plus diachronic persistence.

**Gains**
- **G1. A location for the which-subject fact that is no property of anything in the world.** Being "at the center" of the horizon is what makes a human being a subject (p. 11). This suits the Map's claim that no third-person description reaches the fact. It does so without Hare's monadic presence on objects, so the objections in point 1 above do not arise.
- **G2. A useful split.** Valberg separates two facts. (i) *That* a given human being has a horizon: on his view the brain causes this, so physical facts fix it causally (p. 21; 2017: 193). (ii) *That this horizon is "the" horizon*: the uniqueness claim (p. 22). Posit One's "primitive subject individuation" answers to (ii), not (i). Stating the posit with this split would make clear which part is independent of physical criteria.
- **G3. Primitive individuation without a non-physical bearer.** The bearer of Valberg's subjecthood is the human being; the horizon is no substance. So Valberg is a worked model on which Posit One's first component does not by itself require a non-physical subject. That bears on `concepts/where-the-substance-commitment-enters`, which says the substance lean is inherited from Posit One through agent causation. Valberg supports the "through agent causation" half: he puts agency in the human being, and the substance lean does not arise. This is the note's reconstruction.

**Must answer**
- **A1. The antinomy.** Valberg holds (ii) together with "it is like that for everyone" and calls the pair insoluble. A Map that borrows (ii) inherits the other half.
- **A2. Persistence.** The horizon is "outside time and therefore cannot cease to be" (pp. 20–21), while the human being at its centre is in time. Posit One's diachronic persistence (the same subject present at two times) does not map onto either cleanly. Valberg's own treatment is in chapters 18–23, which were not read; he says he will propose "solutions" to "certain familiar puzzles about first-person reference and personal identity" (p. 19). **Do not state his solution.**
- **A3. Bidirectional Interaction.** The horizon "is not a possible term of the causal relation" (p. 21). If the Map's subject were the horizon, it could not act. Valberg's active subject is the human being, "part of the world" (p. 15), which Posit One's subject is not.
- **A4. The diagnosis.** On Valberg's view the Map's dualism, like physicalism, rests on the phenomenal conception: consciousness as something "in our heads or souls". The corpus has no reply to this. "Horizonal" occurs once in content pages, in a Husserlian sense in `topics/microphenomenological-interview-method` L79, not Valberg's.

## Tenet Grading (compatible / suggestive / discriminating; no upgrades)

- **Tenet 1 (Dualism): compatible, with a challenge.**
  - Valberg's "Consciousness does not consist of, but is caused by, such phenomena" (2017: 193) is compatible with irreducibility.
  - It is not suggestive. Valberg rejects consciousness as any phenomenon, including a non-physical one: "neither material nor ethereal". He calls the soul "a fiction" (1992, via Tartaglia). And he counts dualism among the standard responses that "arise from a failure to recognise the distinction" (Tartaglia's summary, p. 196).
  - Footnote 23 (p. 21) disowns any link to qualia or the explanatory gap, so **Valberg must not be cited for explanatory-gap arguments**.
- **Tenet 2 (Minimal Quantum Interaction): silent.** Nothing in the texts read bears on it.
- **Tenet 3 (Bidirectional Interaction): conflicts** with any reading on which the Map's subject is the horizon (A3). In the texts read, causation runs one way only, from brain to horizon, and that is itself an "impossible fact". Valberg 2017 (p. 188) describes consciousness as seeming to "arise causally out of – and in turn may cause – events" in the body. That sentence states the Cartesian's problem; it is not Valberg's view.
- **Tenet 4 (No Many Worlds): compatible.** The view keeps one world, and the uniqueness claim is a non-deflationary first-person datum of the kind the indexical objection presupposes. Valberg holds that datum only inside an antinomy (A1), so it cannot carry the objection as a fact simpliciter. Not discriminating: he says nothing about quantum mechanics or branching.
- **Tenet 5 (Occam's Razor Has Limits): compatible.** Valberg's concession that "nothing can rationally force us to accept that there is such a subject matter" (p. 13) parallels the Map's concession that it cannot compel the deflationist. It does no more.

## Key Debates

### Accept the impossibility, or resolve it? (Valberg vs Tartaglia, 2017)
- **Valberg**: the puzzles of the personal horizon are "extraphilosophical" and have no solution. Philosophy articulates them.
- **Tartaglia**: "if something is impossible, then it cannot happen; so there must be something wrong with the reasoning" (p. 197–198). He proposes the Transcendent Hypothesis instead: the reality of consciousness transcends the horizon.
- **Bearing on the Map**: this is the Map's own choice between treating the which-subject fact as a void and owing a theory of it. The SF verdict (held pending) is on neither side yet.

### Is Valberg an inegalitarian?
- **Merlo 2021**: yes; Valberg is among those who "suggested" identifying reality with one's point of view.
- **The text**: in part. The solipsistic truth is asserted, but so is parity, and the conflict is left standing. Builes 2024 n. 11 cites the personal horizon only as a notion of "point of view". Either reading is defensible; the accurate statement is "inegalitarian on one horn of an antinomy he does not resolve".

## Guards for the Writer

- **Terminology**: see "Terminology First". Not "horizonal subject"; not "Valberg's inegalitarianism" in his own voice; not "the subject is the horizon".
- **Scope qualifiers travel with the quotes.** Solipsism is true "as it appears in the context of death" (p. 12) and "(in a certain sense)" (p. 12). Do not drop either.
- **Coiners.**
  - The subject as the *limit* of the world is Wittgenstein's idea (*TLP* 5.632), as Valberg himself says (p. 17).
  - The phrase "nothing in itself" echoes Sartre's for-itself, which Valberg partly rejects (pp. 14–15).
  - "Horizon" is borrowed from Husserl and Heidegger and redirected (p. 10 n. 8).
  - Valberg's own terms are "personal horizon", "communal horizon", "horizonal conception", "extraphilosophical puzzle", "solipsistic puzzle of death", "temporal puzzle of death" and "impossible fact".
  - "Egalitarianism" as a label is Hellie's (2013), via Merlo.
- **Valberg is not a dualist ally.** He gives Tenet 1 nothing beyond compatibility, and the horizon cannot do Tenet 3's work. Never enlist him for the explanatory gap (p. 21 n. 23).
- **Do not write that Valberg replies to Hare, or Hare to Valberg.** Both published in 2007. Hare's 2007 preprint has no "Valberg", and Hare 2009 was not read.
- **Wikipedia's Johnston comparison** ("arena of presence and action", *Surviving Death* 2010) is unverified. Do not cite it.
- **Valberg 1992 only "as quoted by Tartaglia 2017"**, and Valberg 2013 from the abstract only.
- **The quadrilemma mapping and G3 are the Map's reconstructions.** Valberg never mentions List, Fine, fragmentalism or Posit-One-style individuation.
- **No over-concession either way.** Valberg's antinomy does not refute Posit One. The Map's exit from the diagnosis (A4) is not yet stated anywhere in the corpus, so do not claim one.
