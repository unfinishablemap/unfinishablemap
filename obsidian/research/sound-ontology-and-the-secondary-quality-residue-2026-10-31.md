## Executive Summary

**Most sound ontologies are theories of a bearer, not of a quality, so colour ontology's question does not apply to them directly.** The SEP "Sounds" entry sorts the field by where the sound is. Proximal theories put it at the hearer: sensations (Maclachlan 1989) or proximal stimuli (O'Shaughnessy 2000). Medial theories put it in the medium; this is the wave theory of acoustics (Perkins 1983; Nudds 2010b, 2018; Kalderon 2017). Distal theories put it at the source, as a property (Pasnau 1999), a located event (Casati & Dokic 1994), a relational event (O'Callaghan 2007) or a disposition (Kulvicki 2008). Aspatial theories deny it a location (Strawson 1959; Scruton's "pure events"). Every view except the sensation view puts the sound in the physical world.

**The distal-event view puts the sound entirely in the world by making it an individual, and the residue moves to the audible qualities.** O'Callaghan's SEP entry says so directly: "According to theories in which sounds are individuals, sounds are not secondary or sensible qualities", while the pitch, loudness and timbre we hear "are analogous to colors" (§3.2.1). He then lists colour ontology's options for pitch: dispositions, their physical bases, "sensations or projected features of auditory experiences, or simple primitive properties of (actual or edenic) sounds". The Casati–Dokic–Di Bona entry states the same split in its own terms. It calls it "selective realism", which "would allow for subjective tonal qualities and for an external (non proximal) location of sounds" (§2.3.3). On property and dispositional views the sound *is* the quality ("sounds are not occurrent anythings: they are qualities", Kulvicki 2014: 218, via SEP §3.3), so the colour question applies to them without translation.

**The colour arguments transfer to pitch unevenly.** On O'Callaghan's assessment the periodicity theory of pitch "fares better on two counts" than physicalist theories of colour (§3.2.4). The unique/binary hue argument has no clear pitch counterpart. Pitch metamers are unified by one natural property, a shared fundamental frequency, "even those that lack a constituent at the fundamental" (§3.2.4). Two arguments do transpose: intersubjective variation, and the structural mismatch between pitch intervals and frequency intervals, which he calls "loosely analogous" to colour's structural argument (§3.2.5). His conclusion: "Color perhaps is a uniquely difficult case" (§3.2.1). The other SEP entry agrees from its side: the reduction of sounds to waves "is arguably more successful than the corresponding attempt to reduce colors to properties of electromagnetic waves" (§2.3.1).

**The finding that matters most for the Map is a correction.** `topics/auditory-consciousness-and-the-interface` L59 says pitch has "no such external home" as colour, because "there is no 'fundamental frequency' sitting in the world to identify the missing-fundamental pitch with". L81 says "the missing fundamental has no fundamental in the signal". Both are false as stated. A missing-fundamental tone has no *spectral component* at its fundamental, but its waveform repeats at the fundamental's period, and the page's own L59 says that "periodicity extraction yields the missing-fundamental pitch". The page's cited source (O'Callaghan, SEP §3.2.4) uses this very case to argue that pitch has a *better* physical candidate than colour. Three deep reviews praised the claim as "sharper than the colour case" (05-31, 06-16, 07-07). See [[#auditory-correction|the auditory correction]].

## The Research Questions, Answered

1. **Where does each sound ontology put the sound?** See the [[#positions|positions table]]. The SEP "Sounds" entry's own scheme: "Proximal theories would claim that sounds are where the hearer is. Medial theories—exemplified by mainstream acoustics—locate sounds in the medium between the resonating object and the hearer. Distal theories consider sounds to be located at the resonating object. Finally, aspatial theories deny spatial relevance to sounds." There are four distal variants: "the Property Theory, the Located Event Theory, the Relational Event Theory, and the Dispositional Theory" (§3). Only the sensation view (Maclachlan 1989, §1.1) puts the sound in the mind. Scruton's "secondary objects" (2009b, via §4.3) is the one non-physicalist ontology in the survey, and it is offered for sounds heard as music.
2. **Does colour ontology's argument go through for sound?** At the level of the sound, no, and the reason is categorial. On the event views sounds are particulars that bear qualities, so asking where "the sound's quality" goes is asking about pitch, timbre and loudness. At the level of audible qualities, yes in form: O'Callaghan sets out for pitch the same menu the colour page sorts. In force, partly: two colour arguments do not transpose and two do (answer 3 below). **The distal-event view does put sound entirely in the world, unlike any colour theory**, but only because colour has no bearer debate to correspond to it. The colour page never has to ask where *the colour* is, apart from the object that has it. The SEP "Sounds" entry notes the visual analogue would be "the activities of light sources" (§2.3.2). Pasnau's 2009 paper "The Event of Color" seems to run that transposition the other way (title and metadata only, see [[#gaps|Gaps]]).
3. **Is pitch/timbre the secondary quality, as distinct from the sound-event?** On individual views (located event, relational event, wave-as-individual), yes, per O'Callaghan §3.2.1. On property and dispositional views the sound is itself the quality (Pasnau 1999; Kulvicki 2008, 2014). Loudness is the odd case: Kulvicki's dispositional theory treats it "as a property not of the sound, but of the thwack" (SEP §3.3, paraphrasing; not Kulvicki's words). For pitch, the transfer runs as follows (O'Callaghan §§3.2.3–3.2.5):
   - **Physicalism.** "A straightforward account identifies pitch with periodicity (perhaps within some range). Having pitch is being periodic (see O'Callaghan 2007, ch. 6)." Chapter 6 of the 2007 book is "Audible Qualities", pp. 72–90 (Crossref). Whether O'Callaghan *endorses* the periodicity theory there was not verified. The SEP entry presents it sympathetically and then sets out the case against.
   - **What does not transpose.** The unique/binary hue distinction ("one response is to hold" that it is disanalogous to simple versus complex tones; "one might contend" that no pitch is essentially a mixture) and metamerism ("For each matching pitch, a single natural property does unify the class").
   - **What does transpose.** Intersubjective variation ("subjects differ in which frequency they identify as middle C"), with cochlear-implant pitch shifts as a near-analogue of spectrum inversion. Also structural mismatch: "equivalent pitch intervals do not correspond to equal frequency intervals"; the mel scale; Stevens et al. 1937 and Stevens & Volkmann 1940 argued that pitch is not frequency.
   - **The received psychoacoustic view, as he reports it.** Researchers "commonly reject the identification of pitch with frequency or periodicity", and "Pitch, on this understanding, belongs only to experiences", which "implies an error theory" (§3.2.5). That is the projectivist slot. For pitch it is the scientific default, while for colour it is a philosophers' position (Hardin, Maund, Pautz). Oxenham (2012, abstract) gives the psychoacoustic baseline: "the main physical correlate of pitch is acoustic periodicity, or repetition rate", with "many interactions that complicate the relationship".
   - **Timbre.** O'Callaghan defers it to Isaac 2017 (not read). The auditory page's "no single physical magnitude that plausibly identifies timbre" (L59) was not contradicted by anything read.
4. **Verification of the named works.** See [[#key-sources|Key Sources]] and [[#gaps|Gaps]]. In short: Pasnau 1999, O'Callaghan 2007 (with its chapter list), Pasnau 2000, Pasnau 2009, Casati, Di Bona & Dokic 2013, Nudds & O'Callaghan 2009, Scruton 2009b, Kulvicki 2014, Roberts 2017, Leddington 2019, Pautz 2014 and Oxenham 2012 were verified at Crossref. Casati & Dokic 1994 (a book with no DOI) and Kulvicki 2008 (Philosophers' Imprint, blocked) rest on the SEP bibliography. **Nudds is not a proximal or event theorist.** The SEP lists him with the physicalist wave theorists, and his 2018 "argument from the medium" targets both event theories. **Kulvicki's view is distal and dispositional, not proximal**, so the brief's "Kulvicki's proximal view" misclassifies it. The proximal theorists are Maclachlan (sensations) and O'Shaughnessy (2000, 2009: "the sound that we hear is nonetheless where we are", 2000: 447, via SEP §1.2).
5. **Who first proposed the distal-event view?** See [[#originators|Originators]]: Casati and Dokic, 1994.

## Originators {#originators}

- **Distal (located) event theory: Casati & Dokic, *La philosophie du son* (1994).** Two independent SEP entries agree. The "Sounds" entry: "An earlier one, The Located Event Theory, was defended by Casati and Dokic (1994)" (§3.2). Its authors are Casati, Dokic and Di Bona, so this is the originators describing themselves. The rival's entry ("Auditory Perception", by O'Callaghan) lists "Casati and Dokic 1994, 2005, O'Callaghan 2007, Matthen 2010" for the proposal that sounds are "public, distally-located, event-like individuals" (§2.1). No earlier contemporary event theorist was found.
- **Relational event theory ("disturbings" of a medium): O'Callaghan**, PhD thesis *Sounds* (Princeton, 2002), then *Sounds: A Philosophical Theory* (2007). It is "a hybrid theory (O'Callaghan 2007: 55)" (SEP "Sounds" §3.2.3, quoting), because, like the wave theory, it needs a medium for the sound to exist. Its ancient precedent is O'Callaghan's *reading* of Aristotle, *De Anima* II.8 420b15 ("everything that makes a sound does so by the impact of something against something else"). Aristotle also calls sound "a certain movement of air" (420b12). Do not credit Aristotle with the event view outright.
- **Contemporary distal property view: Pasnau 1999.** The SEP calls Kulvicki's view an extension of "Pasnau's original insight that sounds be considered as akin to colors in their being located features of objects" (§3.3). Pasnau later "rejoined the Event Theory". The SEP gives that year once as 2007 and once as 2009. Both point to the same paper, "The Event of Color" (*Philosophical Studies* 142(3), 353–369), which Crossref dates online 14 December 2007, in the 2009 volume.
- **The seventeenth-century wave and sensation views.** Galileo's §48 has sounds "fatti, e sentiti in noi" when a "frequente tremor dell'aria, in minutissime onde increspata" moves the eardrum. He ties high pitch to wave frequency: "dalla frequenza delle quali nasce l'acutezza del suono, e la gravità dalla rarità". The SEP reads this as Galileo holding "both a proximal theory—sounds as sensations—and a medial theory, thereby possibly originating a dualist account" (§2.1). **"Dualist" there means a two-sound account, not mind–body dualism. Never quote it as the latter.**
- **The bearer/quality split for sound, stated as a dialectic: Berkeley 1713, First Dialogue.** Hylas: "You must distinguish, Philonous, between sound as it is perceived by us, and as it is in itself". The qualities "LOUD, SWEET, ACUTE, or GRAVE" belong "only to sensible sound, or SOUND in the common acceptation of the word, but not to sound in the real and philosophic sense". Philonous concludes that "real sounds may possibly be SEEN OR FELT, but never HEARD". "Acute" and "grave" are high and low pitch. Berkeley's own conclusion is immaterialism. Hylas concedes that "sounds too have no real being without the mind", then resists for colour: "Pardon me: the case of colours is very different."

- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sounds/ (raw HTML read in full)

- **Key points**: the proximal/medial/distal/aspatial scheme; arguments against the wave theory (sounds are not heard to move or propagate; constant distal volume; the vacuum argument turned around with the analogy that "things can sensibly be taken to have colors in the dark"); the four distal variants and the objections to each; Kulvicki's dispositional view quoted at length. ## Major Positions {#positions}

| View | Proponents (as the SEP entries give them) | Where the sound is | What the sound is | Where the heard quality goes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sensation | Maclachlan 1989 | in the hearer (mind) | a sensation | experience; the sound *is* the quality |
| Proximal stimulus | O'Shaughnessy 2000, 2009 | at the hearer | an event at the ear | open; not addressed in the sources read |
| Wave (medial) | Perkins 1983; Nudds 2010b, 2018; Kalderon 2017; Meadows 2018; mainstream acoustics | in the medium | a compression wave | pitch and loudness correlate with frequency and amplitude; the quality question is not posed |
| Property (distal) | Galileo and Locke "in part"; Pasnau 1999; Roberts 2017; Leddington 2019 | at the source | a property of objects ("like colors") | the colour question applies unchanged |
| Located event (distal) | Casati & Dokic 1994; Pasnau 2009; Roden 2010 | at the source | a vibration event in the object | moves to pitch, timbre and loudness |
| Relational event (distal) | O'Callaghan 2002, 2007, 2010a | at or near the source | a disturbing of a medium | moves to pitch, timbre and loudness (O'Callaghan §3.2.1) |
| Dispositional (distal) | Kulvicki 2008, 2014, 2017 | at the source | a stable disposition to vibrate | the colour question applies; loudness belongs to the thwack |
| Aspatial | Strawson 1959; Scruton 2009b (pure events, "secondary objects") | nowhere intrinsically | Strawson: unlocated sound; Scruton: "things that happen but which don't happen to anything" | Scruton: bound up with how we perceive them |

**Relation to the tenets.** None of these views bears on dualism by itself. Every distal and medial view is physicalist about the sound. That is consistent with the Map, which is dualist about experience, not about sounds. The colour page's sorting concerns where the *quality* goes, and that question survives each view in the right-hand column.

## Key Debates

### Does the secondary-quality debate transfer from colour to sound?
- **Casati, Dokic & Di Bona**: as far as the debate goes, sound "does not seem to introduce new philosophically interesting elements that could challenge generalizations obtained ... from the example of colors".
- **O'Callaghan**: "Despite tradition, we should be wary"; two of the four colour arguments fail to transpose to pitch; "Color perhaps is a uniquely difficult case".
- **State**: the disagreement is one of emphasis. The *form* of the debate (the menu of theories) transfers; the *force* of the anti-physicalist arguments does not transfer evenly. Both entries agree that physicalist reduction fares better for sound or pitch than for colour.

### Event versus property
- Event theorists: sounds "start and cease. They are intrinsically temporal entities" (SEP §3.2.1). Property and dispositional theorists, and Cohen (2010), deny that this asymmetry with colour holds. Cohen 2010 is via the SEP only; he is the colour relationalist on the host page, and his crossing to sound is a lead.
- Nudds (2018), the argument from the medium: one can hear a sound when no environmental event occurs, so environmental events do not bear acoustic features (via SEP §3.2.4).

### Tinnitus
- On distal and event views, tinnitus is "an illusory or hallucinatory experience as of a sound" (O'Callaghan §2.1). There is a heard pitch and no sound at all. On the sensation view, tinnitus is a sound (SEP "Sounds" §1.1). This matches the auditory page's "a determinate heard quality with no signal at all" (L81). It is the auditory counterpart of the colour page's Eden question, "what a presenting of an uninstantiated quality *is*" (colour L72). **That link is the Map's inference.** The fold leaves it out for budget reasons; the auditory refine may use it.

> ## Sound: The Same Question at a Second Modality {#sound}
>
> Galileo doubted that sounds, any more than tastes or odours, outlast the removal of ears, tongues and noses (Galilei 1623, §48). Berkeley's Hylas distinguishes "sound as it is perceived by us, and as it is in itself", and Philonous infers that real sound so understood could be "*seen or felt*, but never *heard*" (Berkeley 1713, First Dialogue).
>
> Sound ontology asks a question colour ontology does not: where the sound itself is. The Stanford survey sorts the answers into proximal, medial, distal and aspatial theories (Casati, Dokic & Di Bona 2020). Property and dispositional views (Pasnau 1999; Kulvicki 2008) model sounds on colours, so the colour question applies to them unchanged. The event views, the located-event theory of Casati and Dokic (1994) and O'Callaghan's (2007) relational version, put the sound in the world by making it an individual: "According to theories in which sounds are individuals, sounds are not secondary or sensible qualities" (O'Callaghan 2020, §3.2.1). The question passes to pitch, timbre and loudness. O'Callaghan sets out the colour menu for pitch, ending with "sensations or projected features of auditory experiences, or simple primitive properties of (actual or edenic) sounds", the last being the [[#eden|Edenic]] option transposed.
>
> The transfer is uneven. The periodicity theory of pitch "fares better on two counts" than physicalist theories of colour (§3.2.4): tones that match in pitch share a fundamental frequency, even one with no component at that frequency, and pitch arguably has no counterpart of the unique/binary hue distinction. Two arguments do carry over: variation between listeners, and the mismatch between pitch intervals and frequency intervals, which O'Callaghan calls "loosely analogous" to the [[#structure|structural argument]] (§3.2.5). "Color perhaps is a uniquely difficult case" (§3.2.1).
>
> On the Map's reading, the sorting extends to sound at the level of qualities, not of bearers. A theory may put the sound in the world, and the question of where heard pitch goes still stands: periodicity physicalism hands the felt height of a tone to a theory of experience, as reflectance physicalism hands over felt redness. As with colour, the sorting shows where the question sits and leaves it open.

### Why Not an Article {#why-not-article}

The literature is substantial: two SEP entries, three monographs, an OUP collection. But its main axis is where the *bearer* is, and the Map takes no position on that. Every distal and medial theory is physicalist about sounds, which the Map can accept. The Map-specific yield comes to three points: the bearer/quality split; the uneven transfer of colour's arguments to pitch; and the correction to the auditory page. A ~360-word fold and a refine carry all three. A topics page would be a survey of sound location with one paragraph of tenet bearing. Topics stands at 368/370 with one slot claimed; spend no slot here. Revisit if the Map wants a page on **audible qualities** (pitch physicalism against the mel-scale and intersubjective-variation arguments, Pautz 2014 §3.5, timbre via Isaac 2017). That would be a genuine sibling to the colour page, and this note is its starting bibliography.

## Guards for the Fold Writer

- **Do not say any sound ontology supports dualism.** All the distal and medial theories are physicalist about sounds. Scruton's "secondary objects" is about sounds heard as music and is not a mind–body thesis. The SEP's "possibly originating a dualist account" (on Galileo) means *two kinds of sound*.
- **Do not overstate the transfer.** O'Callaghan says two colour arguments fail for pitch; the fold says so. Do not drop "The transfer is uneven", or "arguably" on the unique/binary point.
