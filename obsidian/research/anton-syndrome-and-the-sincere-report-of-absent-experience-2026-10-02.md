## Verdict (assess-first)

Anton syndrome is the clinical case in which a patient who is blind, usually from bilateral occipital damage, sincerely says they can see, describes scenes that are not there, and explains collisions by bad lighting. It is the false-positive member of the report-and-access wing: a confident report of the *presence* of a kind of experience the patient apparently lacks.

1. **It does not touch the Map's Layer 1 as written.** `phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence` L102 claims near-incorrigibility for the *global* claim that "something it is like" to be you is occurring. Anton patients are conscious; the global claim is true of them. What fails is a **modality-specific existence claim** ("I am having visual experience"). The Map has no named layer for that grain. Its gradient table (L172) reads "Phenomenal existence (that there is something it is like)" at "Very high", and a reader can take that row to cover modal existence. Anton is a documented counterexample to that broader reading, so the main deliverable for the article is a **scope clarification**: modal-existence claims carry Layer-2-grade (fallible) authority, not Layer-1 authority. Schwitzgebel's own concession lands on the same line. He grants "Maybe" infallibility for the judgement that one is conscious, and lists modality-specific claims among the fallible ones (Schwitzgebel 2008, p. 261; see [Major Positions](#major-positions)).
2. **Authority attaches to seemings, not to reports.** On the Map's constitutive-observation view (`constitutive-vs-referring-observation` L43), a visual *seeming* cannot occur without visual phenomenality. So the view predicts that each Anton case is one of two kinds: (A) a report or belief with no visual seeming behind it, or (B) a visual seeming (imagery, hallucination) that the patient misclassifies as perception. It rules out a third kind, a visual seeming with no visual phenomenality at all, which is how a higher-order theorist describes the case (Lane & Liang 2008). No evidence on record can tell (A), (B) and the higher-order description apart, because every measure is report or behaviour. The cost the Map must state openly: a sincere first-person report does not certify that a seeming stands behind it, and from outside nobody can tell which reports are seeming-backed.
3. **The clinical evidence is heterogeneous.** One case is traced to imagery confusion (Goldenberg et al. 1995). One shows the belief in sight arising only during periods of hallucination the patient himself recognised as unreal (Swartz & Brust 1984). Two show denial with *peripheral* blindness plus frontal damage, with no occipital lesion (McDaniel & McDaniel 1991; Rodríguez et al. 2024). Lesion-network studies find both modality-specific and supramodal components (Kletenik et al. 2023; Monai et al. 2023). The field's own verdict is "multifactorial" (Prigatano 2009). No single reading fits every patient.
4. **The philosophical uptake is real and has been underused.** Chalmers answers the Anton objection to fading qualia by name. Blindness-denial subjects "believe that they are having visual experiences when they likely have none", but "no rational system whose cognitive mechanisms are unimpaired can be so mistaken" (Chalmers 1995) [F]. Block classes anosognosia as "primarily a defect in A-consciousness, not P-consciousness" (Block 1995) [F]. Lane & Liang (2008) use Anton with hallucinations as "radical confabulation" against Rosenthal's two-component higher-order theory [F]. Macpherson (2010) has a whole paper pairing Anton with zombies under a disjunctive theory of introspection [M; unread, and the first thing to read before writing]. Allen-Hermanson (2015/2017) argues Anton does *not* support introspective scepticism [A]. Mogensen (2025, n. 18) presses Chalmers's Anton reply [F].
5. **Tier result.** Tenet 1: *compatible*, no support, non-discriminating. The datum is also not a threat, because Layer 1 is global. Tenet 3: *compatible*. Anton realises locally what the epiphenomenalist says is always possible, an experience-report not caused by the experience it reports. The Map's rationale (tenets L93) survives only in the form tenets L101 already gives it: report reliability *in unimpaired systems*. Nothing here is discriminating. Expect "compatible; the correlation reply already conceded", as the harvesting review predicted.

## Key Sources

### Clinical: primary and case literature

#### Anton (1899): the eponymous paper [M]
- **Citation**: Anton, G. (1899). Ueber die Selbstwahrnehmung der Herderkrankungen des Gehirns durch den Kranken bei Rindenblindheit und Rindentaubheit. *Archiv für Psychiatrie und Nervenkrankheiten*, 32(1), 86–127. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02126945
- **Access**: Crossref record only. Crossref's title string reads "…Rindenblindheit und **B**indentaubheit", an OCR error at the publisher record. "Rindentaubheit" (cortical deafness) is the reading in every secondary citation (e.g. Maddula et al. 2009 ref. 2).
- **Key points [S: Kumbier et al. 2005, 2007; Redlich 1945 P1; Kertesz 2023]**: Anton described patients unaware of cortical blindness *and* cortical deafness. His best-documented case, Juliane Hochriehser (1895/96 files, located by Kumbier, Haack & Herpertz 2007), was a 69-year-old dairymaid with cortical *deafness* from bilateral temporal lesions, so the eponymous paper is wider than visual denial. On Kertesz's summary, Anton's own explanation was disconnection: intact areas cut off from the damaged visual pathways, with speech areas confabulating in the absence of input.
- **Naming note**: Kondziella & Frahm-Falkenberg (2011) [A] and Shanmugam et al. (2021) [A] document Anton's eugenics advocacy. Shanmugam et al. propose replacing the eponym with "Bilateral Occipital Lobe Infarct Neglect Deficit (BLIND) Syndrome". The standard eponym remains universal in the literature. An article can use it and note the controversy in one sentence.

#### Babinski (1914) [S]
- **Citation** [S: Maddula et al. 2009 ref. 3]: Babinski, J. (1914). Contribution à l'étude des troubles mentaux dans l'hémiplégie organique cérébrale (anosognosie). *Revue Neurologique*, 27, 845–848.
- **Key point**: Babinski coined *anosognosie* for unawareness of **hemiplegia**, not blindness. "Anton-Babinski syndrome" is a later fusion of the two names. Not read.

#### Seneca, *Epistulae morales* 50.2 (c. 63 CE) [F]
- **URL**: https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/sen/seneca.ep5.shtml
- **Quote (Latin, verbatim)**: "Haec fatua subito desiit videre. Incredibilem rem tibi narro, sed veram: nescit esse se caecam; subinde paedagogum suum rogat ut migret, ait domum tenebricosam esse." (This note's gloss: she suddenly stopped seeing; she does not know she is blind; she keeps asking her attendant to move house, saying the house is dark.)
- **Use**: André (2018) [A] argues Harpaste "probably constitutes its first description", in the presence of "relatively well-preserved cognition". Seneca himself uses the case as a moral parable: no one recognises their own avarice. That a first-century philosopher already read the case as a lesson about self-knowledge is a usable opening, provided the article does not lean on the retrospective diagnosis.

#### Redlich & Dorsey (1945) [P1]
- **Citation**: Redlich, F. C., & Dorsey, J. F. (1945). Denial of blindness by patients with cerebral disease. *Archives of Neurology and Psychiatry*, 53(6), 407–417. https://doi.org/10.1001/archneurpsyc.1945.02300060010002. Crossref lists Redlich only and first page 407. The co-author and end page are the standard secondary citation.
- **Quote (first-page extract via OpenAlex)**: "denial of blindness in cases of pathologic conditions of the brain is frequent, although overlooked by most observers." Six cases.

#### Aldrich, Alessi, Beck & Gilman (1987): the best denominator [A]
- **Citation**: *Annals of Neurology*, 21(2), 149–158. https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.410210207
- **Quote**: "Only 3 patients denied their blindness, although 4 others were unaware of their visual loss." That is 3 of 25 cortically blind patients denying (12%) and 7 of 25 denying or unaware (28%). Kertesz (2023) [F] reports this as "about 25% of cortical blindness in one large series", which merges denial with unawareness.
- **Use**: this is the closest thing to a prevalence figure. No population study was found.

#### Goldenberg, Müllbacher & Nowak (1995): imagery without perception [A]
- **Citation**: Imagery without perception: A case study of anosognosia for cortical blindness. *Neuropsychologia*, 33(11), 1373–1382. https://doi.org/10.1016/0028-3932(95)00070-j (verified at Crossref; checks out).
- **Key points**: complete cortical blindness after bilateral PCA infarction; the patient denied blindness; MRI showed "almost complete destruction of primary visual cortex", with small spared remnants at the left occipital tip. Visual imagery was preserved (letter shapes, object shapes and colours).
- **Quotes**: "Her pretended visual experiences could frequently be traced back to synaesthetic translations of acoustic or tactile perceptions into mental visual images." "Possibly, the belief to see resulted from a confusion of mental visual images with real percepts."
- **Tenet relevance**: the anchor case for reading (B), experience present but misclassified. The authors' own "possibly" should be kept.

#### Zago et al. (2010) [A]
- **Citation**: A cortically blind patient with preserved visual imagery. *Cognitive and Behavioral Neurology*, 23(1), 44–48. https://doi.org/10.1097/WNN.0b013e3181bf2e6e
- **Key point**: Anton syndrome after bilateral occipital infarction with "what was likely complete bilateral damage to the primary visual cortex", yet "the patient's ability to visualize objects, color, and spatial imagery was preserved". A second imagery-preserved Anton case. The paper is aimed at the V1-and-imagery debate, not at the denial.

#### Swartz & Brust (1984): the most informative single case [A]
- **Citation**: Anton's syndrome accompanying withdrawal hallucinosis in a blind alcoholic. *Neurology*, 34(7), 969–973. https://doi.org/10.1212/wnl.34.7.969 (Crossref page field reads "969-969"; PubMed gives 969–73).
- **Quotes**: "a man totally blind following ocular trauma experienced formed visual hallucinations (\"alcoholic hallucinosis\"), the unreality of which he recognized. During these same periods, however, he believed that his vision had been restored, and confabulated descriptions of his surroundings (Anton's syndrome). He was otherwise mentally normal." Also: "dementia nearly always coexists"; "The basis of the syndrome is obscure".
- **Why it matters**: three things come apart in one patient. (i) Visual phenomenology is present: formed hallucinations. (ii) It is correctly classified *as hallucination*. (iii) A false belief in restored sight occurs anyway, and only in the same periods. The belief tracks the presence of visual phenomenology without being a misreading of the hallucinations themselves. The case fits neither pure (A) nor pure (B). It is also peripheral blindness, so it is Anton without cortical blindness.
- **Caution**: Lane & Liang (2008) describe this patient as suffering from delirium tremens. The primary abstract says "alcoholic hallucinosis" and "otherwise mentally normal". Alcoholic hallucinosis is the clear-sensorium syndrome, distinct from delirium tremens. An article should follow the primary abstract.

#### McDaniel & McDaniel (1991) [A]
- **Citation**: Anton's syndrome in a patient with posttraumatic optic neuropathy and bifrontal contusions. *Archives of Neurology*, 48(1), 101–105. https://doi.org/10.1001/archneur.1991.00530130113028
- **Quotes**: "Denial of monocular blindness, generalized anosognosia, and confabulation were prominent neurobehavioral features." "We suggest that the bifrontal dysfunction may have been a critical factor in the production of Anton's syndrome."
- **Caution**: the denied blindness was **monocular**. The case shows denial from a peripheral lesion with frontal damage, but it is not a case of total blindness denied.

#### Rodríguez et al. (2024; follow-up 2025) [A]
- **Citations**: Generalized anosognosia, anosodiaphoria, and visual hallucinations with bilateral enucleation after severe bifrontal brain injury… *Neurological Sciences*, 45(6), 2769–2774. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10072-024-07323-z. Follow-up: *Brain Injury*, 39(1), 35–38 (2025). https://doi.org/10.1080/02699052.2024.2396018
- **Quote**: "This case demonstrates that visual anosognosia and confabulations can occur without occipital lobe dysfunction or cortical blindness."
- **Caution**: the authors list "the conviction that vision will resume" among the features that mark "clear clinical differences with Anton syndrome". This patient expects sight to return; he does not claim to see now. It is a neighbour of Anton, and the article should not count it as a case of the sincere report of seeing. The title records visual hallucinations despite bilateral enucleation, so visual phenomenology was present here too.

#### Hartmann, Wolz, Roeltgen & Loverso (1991): "inverse Anton's syndrome" [A]
- **Citation**: Denial of visual perception. *Brain and Cognition*, 16(1), 29–40. https://doi.org/10.1016/0278-2626(91)90083-k (checks out).
- **Key points**: a patient diagnosed as cortically blind, who complained of complete visual loss, had a small preserved field and "remarkably preserved visual abilities". He named objects, colours and famous faces and read single words at over 50% accuracy, yet denied seeing.
- **Quotes**: "Upon confrontation regarding his apparent visual abilities, the patient continued to deny visual perceptual awareness, typically stating \"I feel it.\"" The authors propose "a disconnection of parietal lobe attentional systems from visual perception".
- **Use**: the modal false negative, which mirrors Anton. Unlike blindsight, this is rich identification (faces, words), not forced-choice guessing.

#### Carota, Bianchini, Pizzamiglio & Calabrese (2013): Anton and blindsight in one patient [A]
- **Citation**: The "Altitudinal Anton's syndrome": coexistence of anosognosia, blindsight and left inattention. *Behavioural Neurology*, 26(1–2), 157–163. https://doi.org/10.3233/BEN-2012-110224. The DOI did not resolve at Crossref; PMID 22713392 / PMC5214380 confirmed. The PMC copy is a scanned PDF and was not read.
- **Key points**: the patient "contentiously denied having any visual impairment" (superior altitudinal hemianopia). He made no intentional responses to blind-field stimuli, but blind-field primes shortened reaction times in the seeing field. The authors suggest "blindsight and right hemisphere damage might be both necessary conditions for denying bilateral blindness."
- **Use**: one patient shows the report error and the implicit processing at the same time. This supports the "false feedback from another visual system" mechanism named below. It is a single case with a stated hypothesis, not a finding.

#### Maddula, Lutton & Keegan (2009) [F]
- **Citation**: Anton's syndrome due to cerebrovascular disease: a case report. *Journal of Medical Case Reports*, 3, 9028. https://doi.org/10.4076/1752-1947-3-9028 (Crossref-verified; checks out). PMC2827161.
- **Quotes**: "Despite an objective diminution of her vision, our patient maintained she was able to 'see' things around her." "When asked to comment on the doctor's tie, she was quick with an answer, but one that was incorrect." "Although she would deny visual deficit, she would accept such assistance."
- **Mechanisms paragraph**: drawing on Heilman (1991) [S], the authors list disconnection ("In the absence of input, functioning speech areas often confabulate a response") and two more: "One suggests that the monitor of visual stimuli is defective and is incorrectly interpreting images. The other suggests the presence of false feedback from another visual system", via superior colliculus, pulvinar and temporo-parietal regions.
- **Use**: a clean illustrative case. The accepted-assistance detail matters philosophically. Denial in speech coexists with behaviour adapted to blindness, the dissociation between avowal and action that the anosognosia page already documents for hemiplegia.

#### Abutalebi et al. (2007) [A]
- *Behavioural Neurology*, 18(3), 183–186. https://doi.org/10.1155/2007/306075. Anton syndrome after a lesion confined to the left occipital lobe involving the corpus callosum: "Anton's syndrome may also follow from lesions disconnecting the occipital cortices." Evidence for the disconnection reading.

#### Atallah et al. (2024): systematic review [A]
- **Citation**: Insights into Anton Syndrome: When the brain denies blindness. *Journal of Clinical Neuroscience*, 120, 181–190. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jocn.2024.01.014
- **Key points**: PRISMA review, "64 studies encompassing 72 AS cases" (English full-text only). Median age 55 (range 6–96); "Stroke (40.3 %) topped causes"; improvement in 45.8%. The abstract also reports "visual anosognosia (90.3 %)" as a prevalent feature, which is odd for a syndrome defined by visual anosognosia. Report it as theirs if at all.
- **Counting caution**: Shanmugam et al. (2021) [A] say "only 28 case reports published from 1965-2016". PubMed returns 83 title/abstract hits for "Anton syndrome" alone. The discrepancy is definitional. Any count in the article should name its denominator.

#### Kertesz (2023/2024) [F]
- *Canadian Journal of Neurological Sciences*, 51(2), 300–304. https://doi.org/10.1017/cjn.2023.246 (OA). It defines the syndrome as "a denial of vision loss in the presence of relatively well-preserved cognition associated with confabulation". That clashes with Levine's (1990) claim that denial of *total* blindness requires severe intellectual impairment. Definitions differ on whether preserved cognition is part of the syndrome, and this matters for Chalmers's reply (below).

### Mechanism and theory

#### Levine (1990): the discovery theory [A]
- **Citation**: Unawareness of visual and sensorimotor defects: A hypothesis. *Brain and Cognition*, 13(2), 233–281. https://doi.org/10.1016/0278-2626(90)90052-p
- **Quotes**: interruption of a sensory pathway "is not associated with any immediate sensory experience that uniquely specifies the defect. Instead the sensory loss must be discovered by a process of self-observation and inference." "Hence unawareness of total blindness occurs only in association with severe intellectual impairment, precluding the required self-observation and inference." "…no information from such a field specifies the absence of a sensory stimulus."
- **Why it matters most for the Map**: Levine's premise is philosophical. *Absence of experience is not itself experienced*; knowing that one cannot see is an inference, not a deliverance of acquaintance. On that premise, the Anton patient's error is an error of **inference about** experience, a referring-level failure in the Map's vocabulary, and no failure of acquaintance. It is the strongest available reason to say Anton leaves the constitutive layer alone. It also shows the Map's self-stultification argument is asymmetric. Doubting that one has an experience is itself an experience, which protects against false *negatives* at the global level. Nothing comparable protects against false *positives* at the modal level, because no experience signals that one is missing.

#### Celesia, Brigell & Vaphiades (1997): hemianopic anosognosia [A]
- **Citation**: Hemianopic anosognosia. *Neurology*, 49(1), 88–97. https://doi.org/10.1212/wnl.49.1.88
- **Key points**: prospective, 32 consecutive patients with homonymous field defects; unawareness in 20 (62%). Dissociations from somatic anosognosia and neglect.
- **Quotes**: "Patients with phosphenes, photopsias, or visual hallucinations were usually aware of their visual field loss." "We suggest that HAN is most often related to failure of discovery of the deficits".
- **Discriminating value**: in hemianopia, positive visual phenomena went with *awareness* of loss, not with denial. That counts against any general version of reading (B) on which hallucinations cause denial. Hemianopia is not total blindness, so the transfer is analogical.

#### Kletenik, Gaudet, Prasad, Cohen & Fox (2023): lesion network mapping [F]
- **Citation**: Network localization of awareness in visual and motor anosognosia. *Annals of Neurology*, 94(3), 434–441. https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.26709. PMC10524951, author manuscript read in full.
- **Key points**: 24 literature-derived visual-anosognosia lesions, against 69 aware visual-field-deficit controls and 174 hemiplegia cases (95 anosognosic). Visual anosognosia connects to V2 and posterior cingulate; motor anosognosia to insula, SMA and anterior cingulate; "a shared, cross-modal network for awareness of deficits centered on memory-related brain structures" (hippocampus, precuneus).
- **Interpretation (theirs)**: "Memory-associated structures are necessary to recognize a deficit by comparing present inputs to priors stored in memory". This is a cognitive-awareness or comparator model, close to Levine's discovery theory.
- **Limits (theirs, verbatim)**: "we lacked a cohort of patients with complete cortical blindness who were aware of their deficit (the ideal control)". Also: "our visual anosognosia and visual controls are derived from the literature and lack formal testing for awareness".

#### Monai et al. (2023/2024): disconnection mapping [A]
- **Citation**: Convergence of visual and motor awareness in human parietal cortex. *Annals of Neurology*, 95(1), 90–103. https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.26791 (Crossref; online 2023).
- **Quotes**: "AS depends on bilateral striate and extrastriate occipital damage, and disconnection of ventral and dorsal frontoparietal regions involved in attention control." Conclusion: "the unique shared combination of content-specific and supramodal mechanisms in anosognosia."
- **Map relevance**: with Kletenik, this revises a sentence in the Map's anosognosia page. That page reads Vocat et al. (2010) as showing self-monitoring "is several capacities, one for each domain, and not a single faculty." Both 2023 studies find a domain-specific *and* a shared supramodal component. The anosognosia page has 1,074 words of headroom (2,925/4,000 by `analyze_length`) and can take the correction.

### Philosophy

#### Chalmers (1995): "Absent Qualia, Fading Qualia, Dancing Qualia" [F]
- **URL**: https://consc.net/papers/qualia.html (published in T. Metzinger (Ed.), *Conscious Experience*, Imprint Academic, 1995).
- **Quotes**: "Another objection notes that there are actual cases in which subjects are seriously mistaken about their experiences. For example, in cases of blindness denial, subjects believe that they are having visual experiences when they likely have none. In these cases, however, we are no longer dealing with fully rational systems. In systems whose belief-formation mechanisms are impaired, anything goes." "Joe is an eminently thoughtful, reasonable person, who exhibits none of the confabulatory symptoms of those with blindness denial." "The plausible claim is not that no system can be massively mistaken about its experiences, but that no rational system whose cognitive mechanisms are unimpaired can be so mistaken."
- **Tenet alignment**: Chalmers is a property dualist, and this is the most authoritative ally-side treatment of the case. He concedes the error (subjects "likely have none") and restricts first-person authority to **unimpaired rational systems**. Mogensen (2025, n. 18) points to Chalmers (1996, pp. 260–261) for the book version. That page range is unverified here.
- **The weak point**: the restriction is non-circular only if impairment can be identified independently of the error. In most cases it can: dementia (Swartz & Brust: "dementia nearly always coexists"), confabulation in other domains, frontal damage, generalised anosognosia. The pressure cases are those reported with preserved cognition (Harpaste on André's reading; Swartz & Brust's "otherwise mentally normal" patient; Kertesz's definition). Even there the belief formation is visibly impaired somewhere: confabulated scene descriptions resist correction.

#### Mogensen (2025) [F]
- **Citation**: How to resist the Fading Qualia Argument. *Synthese*, 206, art. 252. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-025-05338-3 (OA; read via a Wayback copy of the Springer PDF).
- **Quote (n. 18)**: "Note, however, that cases of Anton syndrome exhibit something like this profile. Thus, Chalmers needs to say why a commitment to the nomological possibility of Joe’s unnoticed blindness is significantly harder to accept than a commitment to the actuality of Anton syndrome. ## Major Positions

### Reading (A): a report without the experience (confabulation, monitoring, discovery failure)
- **Proponents**: Anton (disconnection, via Kertesz) [S]; Heilman (1991) "defective monitor" [S]; Levine (1990) discovery theory [A]; McDaniel & McDaniel (1991), frontal factor [A]; Kletenik et al. (2023), memory comparator [F]; Chalmers (1995), "likely have none" [F]; Block (1995), access defect [F].
- **Core claim**: there is no visual seeming. The report system lacks or misreads the "no vision" signal, and since no experience marks an absence, the gap is filled by confabulation.
- **Evidence for**: peripheral-blindness cases (Swartz & Brust; McDaniel); frontal involvement; dementia's frequent co-occurrence; the shared supramodal awareness network; confabulated scene descriptions that are wrong in detail (Maddula's tie).
- **Relation to the Map**: on this reading the error is in belief and report, at the referring level. The constitutive layer is untouched, because no seeming occurs and so none is misreported. The cost is the one the Verdict names: sincere phenomenal-existence *reports* can be produced without a seeming, so report alone never certifies Layer-1-grade standing.

### Reading (B): experience present but misclassified (imagery or hallucination taken for perception)
- **Proponents**: Goldenberg et al. (1995) [A]; Zago et al. (2010) imagery-preserved [A]; Heilman's "false feedback from another visual system" (superior colliculus, pulvinar) [S]; Carota et al. (2013), blindsight as a necessary condition [A]; Lane & Liang (2008), visual p-consciousness present with hallucinations [F].
- **Core claim**: there is a visual seeming, of imagery, hallucination or degraded residual input, and the error is about its *source* (perception versus imagery), a reality-monitoring failure.
- **Evidence for**: imagery traced to cross-modal input (Goldenberg); hallucinations co-occurring with the belief (Swartz & Brust; Kartsounis et al. 2009 [M, title only], "Anton syndrome, with vivid visual hallucinations", *JNNP* 80, 937–938, https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp.2008.151118); blind-field priming alongside denial (Carota).
- **Evidence against a general version**: in hemianopia, positive phenomena went with *awareness* of loss (Celesia 1997). Swartz & Brust's patient recognised his hallucinations as unreal and still believed he saw.
- **Relation to the Map**: the patient is right that visual phenomenality occurs and wrong about where it comes from. This fits the constitutive view, and weak Revelation's existence clause is satisfied. The `perceptual-reality-monitoring-void` is the natural home: Anton under (B) is the Perky effect at its clinical limit.

### Reading (C): a shared anosognosia mechanism
- **Proponents**: Monai et al. (2023) [A]; Kletenik et al. (2023) [F]; Prigatano (2009) [A]; Davies, Aimola Davies & Coltheart (2005) two-factor account (already cited on the Map's anosognosia page).
- **Core claim**: Anton is visual anosognosia, sharing supramodal mechanisms (frontoparietal attention, hippocampal–precuneus comparison) with hemiplegia denial, plus a modality-specific component.
- **Relation to the Map**: (C) is a claim about mechanism and can combine with (A) or (B) about phenomenology. Like the anosognosia reversals, it is fully intra-cerebral: in the anosognosia page's three-state vocabulary, "a serious live countermodel stands" to any interface reading.

### The higher-order overlay (philosophical, not clinical)
- **Proponents**: Lane & Liang (2008) as a problem for Rosenthal; Rosenthal's acceptance of targetless HOTs (`higher-order-theories` L69); the illusionist generalisation (`illusionism`).
- **Core claim**: Anton shows a seeming (a HOT) without a first-order visual state. Illusionists generalise this structure to all phenomenal seemings.
- **Relation to the Map**: this is the description the constitutive view rules out. It is not refuted by the data, because an imagery-generated first-order state (B) and a targetless HOT describe the same reports. The Anton case therefore *locates* the bedrock dispute that `constitutive-vs-referring-observation` L85 and `phenomenal-authority` L120 already name, at a clinical site. It does not move it.

## Key Debates

### Does Anton show first-person reports can be wholly wrong about whether experience is present?
- **Yes, at the modal grain**: Chalmers concedes it for blindness denial ("likely have none"), as do Block (access defect) and Metzinger (loss of higher-order insight).
- **Only about the source, not the presence**: Goldenberg (possibly), Lane & Liang (with hallucinations), the Swartz & Brust pattern.
- **Not at all, read properly**: Allen-Hermanson (2015/2017) [A] denies that Anton supports introspective scepticism. His argument is unread.
- **State**: open, and probably patient-specific. **No case** discriminates (A) from (B) by a measure independent of report. No imaging during "seeing" reports, and no metacognitive-confidence study of Anton patients, was found.

### Is first-person authority restricted to unimpaired systems, and is that restriction ad hoc?
- **Chalmers (1995)**: yes, restricted. Joe "exhibits none of the confabulatory symptoms of those with blindness denial".
- **Mogensen (2025, n. 18)**: the restriction needs defending, since the actuality of Anton makes Joe's unnoticed blindness less incredible.
- **State**: live. The restriction is non-circular to the extent that impairment has markers independent of the error. The literature supplies such markers in most cases (dementia, frontal damage, generalised anosognosia, confabulation elsewhere). Whether cognition is "relatively well-preserved" is itself disputed between clinical definitions (Kertesz 2023 against Levine 1990).

### Mechanism: one syndrome or several?
- Heilman's three mechanisms, Levine's discovery, Goldenberg's imagery, Monai's frontoparietal disconnection and Kletenik's memory comparator are each supported in some patients. Prigatano calls the field's consensus "multifactorial". The article should present this as a *family* of failures that share a report profile. It should not pick a winner, and it should avoid Cotard's "one channel, one failure" trap (`cotard-delusion`, Tenet 5 paragraph).

## The Report Typology (what Anton completes)

The harvesting review's typology (report of absence / absence of report / hedged report / report of presence) gains structure with a second axis, **grain**: global (that experience exists at all) against modal (that experience of a given kind exists).

| | False or contested **negative** report | False or contested **positive** report |
|---|---|---|
| **Global** (experience or self exists) | Cotard's "I do not exist", reported from somewhere (`cotard-delusion`) | The zombie's "I am conscious": hypothetical, and unobservable from outside by construction (Macpherson 2010's pairing, title only) |
| **Modal** (experience of this kind exists) | Blindsight's "I see nothing" (contested: Phillips 2021 on degraded conscious vision); inverse Anton, "I feel it" (Hartmann 1991) | **Anton**: "I can see" |

CMD (`covert-consciousness-and-cognitive-motor-dissociation`) sits outside the table as the *absence* of report, and depersonalisation (`depersonalisation`) as the *hedged* report. Three things follow:
1. The Map's self-stultification argument protects only the **global-negative** cell. Doubting one's own experience is itself an experience. Cotard is the clinical test of that cell, and the Map's verdict there is that the report still comes from somewhere.
2. The **global-positive** cell, where Layer 1 actually lives, has no clinical instance. A false positive there would need an unconscious subject asserting consciousness, which is the zombie case. Anton does not reach it.
3. The **modal** row is where reports fail in both directions under pathology. That row should carry Layer-2-grade authority, and it presses the Map's blindsight page. Blindsight L171 rests a dualist response on patients' reports of a "striking difference" between seeing and "sensing without seeing". Anton and inverse Anton show that brain-damaged patients misclassify seeing in both directions. The blindsight page's reliance on absence-reports needs the same caution it already extends to Phillips (L84).
