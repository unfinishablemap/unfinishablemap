## Executive Summary

I found **no study that tests Meskin's comparative claim**. The one experimental-philosophy study of aesthetic testimony — Andow (2019) — measures the *explanandum* (people's reluctance to defer aesthetically) rather than Meskin's *explanans* (whether aesthetic testifiers really are differentially insincere and incompetent). Neither Stanford Encyclopedia entry here presents his work as bearing on testifier reliability, and the aesthetic-testimony entry still calls that claim one "we face difficulty in verifying." The nearest evidence sits in adjacent literatures — wine-judging self-consistency, expertise effects on art preference, conformity effects on stated ratings — none carrying the mundane-testimony control a *comparative* claim requires. Separately, the flagship x-phi result about aesthetic judgement (folk anti-realism) has been substantially qualified by its own field, including by Andow himself.

## Part 1 — The Meskin Comparative Claim

### What the claim asserts

Robson and Wallbank's SEP entry states Meskin's position at §3.1.3: "He argues that aesthetic testifiers are more likely than non-aesthetic testifiers to be both insincere (for reasons of snobbery) and incompetent (for reasons of education)—and thus aesthetic testimony is on the whole unreliable." The objection is at §4.1.3: "The primary difficulty facing accounts which insist on (i)—the unreliability of aesthetic testifiers—is the difficulty we face in verifying this claim."

Two limbs, both *comparative*: each requires a measured difference against testimony on mundane matters, not merely a demonstration that aesthetic testifiers are sometimes insincere or ignorant.

### What Andow (2019) measured, and what it does not

Andow's "Aesthetic Testimony and Experimental Philosophy" (in Cova and Réhault's *Advances in Experimental Philosophy of Aesthetics*) is, as far as I found, the only experimental study directly on aesthetic testimony. The SEP's experimental-aesthetics entry describes it: Andow "asked his study participants whether they think that forming aesthetic beliefs based on testimony given by a friend or an expert is less *permissible* and *legitimate* compared to forming such beliefs based on first-hand experience," with non-aesthetic beliefs about size or price as the comparison.

The reported finding matters in two ways. First, the asymmetry is real at the level of folk attitude: people do treat aesthetic testimony as a worse basis for belief. Second — the part a downstream article should not lose — "this effect was not moderated by the participants' attitudes toward the status of aesthetic judgments," so taking aesthetic judgements to be objective or subjective did not predict how much a participant discounted aesthetic testimony. A follow-up (Andow 2020) found that aesthetic and moral testimony-based beliefs, unlike descriptive ones, are not seen as constituting *knowledge*.

**None of this tests Meskin.** Andow measured what people *think* about aesthetic testifiers; Meskin's claim is about what aesthetic testifiers *are*. Robson and Wallbank cite Andow for a different purpose — as evidence "that the folk are rather more sceptical about both testimony in general, and aesthetic judgement in general, than most philosophers are inclined to think appropriate" — while their §4.1.3 verdict on verifiability stands unrevised in the same entry. That is the strongest available confirmation that the specialist literature does not regard the comparative claim as tested.

One near-miss. Andow (2020) applied to the *moral* case exactly the design Meskin's claim would need: it tested whether the testimony asymmetry "stems from differing metaphysical beliefs across relevant domains, varying background levels of deception, or relative disagreement between individuals." "Background levels of deception" is the insincerity limb; "relative disagreement" approximates the competence limb. **The instrument exists and has been pointed at morality, not — as far as I found — at aesthetics.**

### Nearest evidence on the competence limb

Meskin's competence limb runs into a structural obstacle before any data arrive: scoring competence requires a criterion of correctness, and whether aesthetic judgements have one is the very question at issue. The literature works around this by measuring **self-consistency** instead of accuracy.

The cleanest instance is outside art. Hodgson (2008) embedded triplicate samples poured from the same bottle into flights of 30 wines at the California State Fair competition, 2005–2008, with 65–70 expert judges tested each year. His abstract: "About 10 percent of the judges were able to replicate their score within a single medal group. Another 10 percent, on occasion, scored the same wine Bronze to Gold. Judges tend to be more consistent in what they don't like than what they do. An analysis of variance covering every panel over the study period indicates only about half of the panels presented awards based solely on wine quality." His methodological argument transfers: "consistency is more fundamental than concordance in evaluating judge reliability because it is a better basis of measuring experimental error."

Right shape of evidence, wrong domain: credentialled aesthetic-adjacent experts fail to reproduce their own verdicts at a startling rate, but nothing here says whether they fail *more* than someone reporting yesterday's weather.

In the art case the expertise literature gives a partial and awkward answer. Van Paasschen, Bacci and Melcher (2015) had 60 participants (40 novices, 20 experts) rate 150 abstract artworks and portraits in lab and museum sessions, half the novices receiving brief art-historical training in between. "Art experts rated the artworks higher than novices on aesthetic facets (beauty and wanting), but no group differences were observed on affective evaluations (valence and arousal). The training session made a small effect on ratings of preference." Education shifts the cognitive layer and barely touches the affective one. For Meskin that cuts both ways: "want of education" is not empty, but what education changes is not obviously *competence at getting aesthetic value right*, and no correctness criterion is on offer.

An irony is worth recording. Meskin, Phelan, Moore and Kieran (2013) ran the mere-exposure effect against bad art and reported that repeated exposure to bad paintings makes people like them *less* — pushing back on James Cutting's finding that exposure to Impressionist paintings increased liking, which sceptics read as showing canon formation runs on contingency rather than merit. Meskin the experimentalist produced evidence that ordinary viewers *are* tracking quality, which sits awkwardly beside Meskin the philosopher's differential-incompetence claim.

### Nearest evidence on the insincerity limb

Here the gap is wider: I found no study measuring insincerity rates in aesthetic versus mundane testimony.

The closest experimental result is Hesslinger, Carbon and Hecht (2017), who showed 48 participants fake ratings from supposed previous raters (shifted ±2 on a 9-point scale) before they judged 20 artworks. Downward pressure moved judgements substantially (M = −1.54 against baseline); upward pressure did not move them at all (M = −0.12, not significantly different from zero). The authors are explicit that their design cannot separate "actual change of their individual and private opinions" from "merely public compliance" — precisely the distinction Meskin's insincerity limb turns on. There was also no mundane-testimony control condition.

The sociology of taste (Bourdieu's distinction thesis, Peterson's omnivore revision, recent psychology-of-distinction work) establishes a *motive* for aesthetic insincerity: expressed taste carries status information that weather reports do not. It supplies no measured differential rate, and treating motive as measurement is exactly the error L63 warns against.

## Part 2 — X-Phi on Aesthetic Judgement, and the Replication Picture

The field's flagship result concerns intersubjective validity rather than testimony. Cova, Olivola, Machery, Stich, Rose and colleagues (2019) surveyed over 2,000 respondents across 19 countries and reported that folk overwhelmingly reject normativism about aesthetic judgement — presented with aesthetic disagreement, most select "Neither is correct." Goodwin and Darley (2008) had earlier found aesthetic statements called opinions more often than moral, factual or conventional ones; Rabb et al. (2022) found the same on direct questioning.

**That result is now contested from inside the field — the part that matters most for anyone tempted to lean on it.** Andow (2021, *Philosophical Psychology*) ran further studies and concluded that "people may not dismiss normativism as decisively as earlier work suggested," participants apparently treating aesthetic judgements as having correctness conditions finely calibrated to the circumstances of judgement. Contesi, Terrone, Campdelacreu, García-Moya and Martí (2024, *Analysis*) argue the studies establish only the folk's *explicit* meta-aesthetic views, consistent with implicit realism in practice. Zangwill (2019) presses a conceptual objection: they conflate whether a given person is right or wrong with whether aesthetic judgements can be true or false at all, leaving realism untouched.

On the method's general reliability the SEP entry is measured: "research in experimental philosophy is relatively replicable (Cova, Strickland, et al. 2021), and relatively free of scientific misconduct such as p-hacking," while inheriting "a number of methodological challenges from experimental philosophy and related areas of psychology regarding instruments, samples, and stimuli" — questionnaires as the dominant instrument, WEIRD samples as standard.

The honest summary: x-phi of aesthetics is methodologically respectable and substantively unsettled, and the anti-realism finding is currently the shakiest thing in it. A shaky result cited confidently is worse than no result.

## Part 3 — Neuroaesthetics, Restricted to Judgement

Two results bear on judgement rather than on the neural correlates of pleasure.

**Kirk, Skov, Hulme, Christensen and Zeki (2009)** labelled the same artworks at random as gallery-sourced or computer-generated. Ratings were significantly higher under the "gallery" label, and the modulation correlated with medial orbitofrontal and prefrontal activity; the regions recruited by aesthetic judgement, they conclude, "are significantly biased by subjects' prior expectations about the likely hedonic value of stimuli."

Set that beside the Acquaintance Principle and a tension appears — one a downstream article must handle carefully rather than exploit. Philosophers report that we *resist* aesthetic deference; the psychology shows aesthetic valuation pushed around by minimal contextual testimony, down to the neural signal. The two are compatible, since the Acquaintance Principle is a normative claim about what should count as a legitimate aesthetic judgement rather than a descriptive claim about what moves ratings. Kirk et al. arguably *support* the pessimist: if a bare provenance label distorts valuation, first-hand experience is not the clean channel the optimist needs. The result constrains both camps and settles neither.

**Vessel, Maurer, Denker and Starr (2018)** measured shared taste across five visual domains: "preferences for images of faces and landscapes contain[ed] a high proportion of shared taste, while preferences for images of exterior architecture, interior architecture and artworks reflected strong individual differences." A within-subjects replication confirmed higher agreement for natural landscapes than for exterior architecture, "with no differences in reliability."

This supplies a **third pragmatic explanation of the testimony asymmetry that Meskin does not name, and that has data behind it**: for artworks, a perfectly sincere and perfectly competent testifier is still a weak predictor of *your* response, because taste for cultural artefacts is largely idiosyncratic. Neither insincerity nor incompetence is required. It also yields a falsifiable prediction the Map could record: the Acquaintance Principle should feel *weaker* for natural beauty and faces than for artworks, since deference costs less where taste is shared. I did not find that prediction tested.
