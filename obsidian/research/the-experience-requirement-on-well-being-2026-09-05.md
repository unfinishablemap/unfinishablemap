## Executive Summary

The experience requirement on well-being is the thesis that a subject's welfare cannot differ unless the phenomenology of the subject's experience differs. The Map asserts it — in five live files, mostly citing Rawlette 2016 — but no article argues it against the welfare-theory literature that has, since 2019, made it the object of a focused debate. That debate now has a definite shape. Eden Lin (2021) formalises the requirement as a supervenience thesis, rejects the two existing arguments for it (Kagan's, Bramble's), grants that only sentient beings are welfare subjects, and argues that the "sentience argument" for the requirement fails because a theory of the right shape explains the sentience datum without it. Willem van der Deijl (2021), independently and in the same journal volume, runs the sentience argument the other way and concludes that extra-experientialist theories can only honour welfare sentientism by ad hoc restriction or by abandoning invariabilism. Gwen Bradford (2023), Shelly Kagan (2019, pp. 32–34) and Andreas Mogensen (2025) push in the opposite direction — desire-satisfaction is a welfare good, desire does not obviously need consciousness, so unconscious welfare subjects follow — and Andrew Lee (2025) answers all of them by decoupling the theory of welfare *subjects* from the theory of welfare *goods*: a plant or a corporation may instantiate a welfare good without being anything that benefits from it.

**The finding that matters most for the Map is a distinction the literature draws and the Map's articles do not: a *global* experience requirement (Lin's supervenience thesis; Hawkins's narrow experientialism) versus a *local* one (every welfare good is identical to or partly constituted by conscious experience — Lee's "experientialism", and the position Lee attributes to Lin himself).** Lin denies the global requirement and holds the local one: his forthcoming *On the Plurality of Goods* claims that "all of the basic goods and bads are, in virtue of their natures, accessible only to sentient beings". That settles where the Map is exposed. [[positions/moral-status|P-MS1]]'s necessity direction needs only the local requirement plus the phenomenal theory of welfare subjects, both of which Lin grants; the global requirement is carried by [[positions/value-in-selection|P-VS3]] and [[topics/phenomenal-value-realism]], and it is *there* that Lin's paper bites. The 2026-09-05 sentientism refine (commit 6c344eda) reached this in outline — "Lin thus concedes the datum the necessity direction needs and threatens the Map's ground for it rather than the direction" — but described Lin's alternative as "a desire-satisfaction theory", which is his illustrative case (Heathwood's restricted desire theory) and not his own view (Lin 2017 argues *against* welfare subjectivism; his positive view is an objective-list pluralism of sentience-involving goods).

## Key Sources

### Lin 2021, "The Experience Requirement on Well-Being" [abstract; body via summariser]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-020-01463-6 — *Philosophical Studies* 178(3): 867–886 (print 2021; online 7 May 2020, which is why Lee and Mogensen cite it as "Lin 2020"). Crossref-verified. Not open access; the body was read through the page summariser only, so nothing below from the body is grep-verified.
- **Type**: Paper.
- **Abstract [abstract, verbatim]**: "According to the experience requirement on well-being, differences in subjects' levels of welfare or well-being require differences in the phenomenology of their experiences. I explain why the two existing arguments for this requirement are not successful. Then, I introduce a more promising argument for it: that unless we accept the requirement, we cannot plausibly explain why only sentient beings are welfare subjects. I argue, however, that because the right kind of theory of well-being can plausibly account for that apparent fact about welfare subjects even if the requirement is false, this argument does not succeed. I tentatively conclude that no compelling case can be made for the requirement."
- **Formulation [summariser]**: a supervenience thesis over subjects and worlds — for any S₁, S₂, W₁, W₂, if there is no phenomenological difference between S₁ at W₁ and S₂ at W₂, then their welfare levels are equal. Note the cross-subject, cross-world form: it is a claim about welfare *levels*, not about which entities have them.
- **The two existing arguments and his replies [summariser]**: (1) Kagan's argument from intrinsic properties (Kagan 1992, 1994): a change in someone's welfare must be a change in *them*, and if a person is their mental states, only mental changes count. Lin's reply: the premise that benefits must involve a subject's intrinsic properties is "no more plausible than" the requirement itself, so the argument does no dialectical work. (Van der Deijl 2021 fn 3 glosses Kagan the same way [raw].) (2) Bramble's argument from posthumous events (Bramble 2016a, *Ergo*): the requirement explains why the dead cannot be harmed. Lin's reply: a desire theorist can adopt a timing principle that rules out posthumous benefit without the requirement.
- **The sentience argument [summariser]**: if only sentient beings are welfare subjects and the requirement is false, then a non-sentient being could differ in welfare while every sentient being's phenomenology stayed fixed — implausible. Lin's reply: a desire-satisfaction theory of the right shape (he cites Heathwood 2005) restricts the relevant desires to those only sentient beings can have, so the sentience datum is explained without the requirement.
- **What kind of desire [via Mogensen, raw]**: Lin's own account of the relevant attitude is not functional. Mogensen quotes Lin 2020a (*PPR* 100(3), p. 520) on attitudinal desires: "it is plausible that [attitudinal desires] are partly constituted by a certain kind of phenomenology: to desire something in the [attitudinal] sense is, among other things, to feel a certain way about it." Lee 2025 fn 23 [raw] reports that "Lin [2020] argues that every welfare good at least partially involves consciousness." So the mechanism by which Lin's alternative excludes non-sentient beings is a *felt* attitude — a local experience requirement on the constitution of the good.
- **Lin's own theory of goods**: not a desire theory. Lin 2017, "Against Welfare Subjectivism", *Noûs* 51(2): 354–377 [via van der Deijl's reference list]; and the book project, *On the Plurality of Goods*, whose stated claim is that "all of the basic goods and bads are, in virtue of their natures, accessible only to sentient beings", which "will allow it to explain why, although more is relevant to one's welfare than the phenomenology of one's experiences, only sentient beings are welfare subjects" [snippet from edenlin.com; the 2026-09-05 Claude outer review quoted the same sentence].
- **Related Lin papers**: "Welfare Invariabilism", *Ethics* 128(2): 320–345 (2018) — "Invariabilism is the view that the same theory of welfare is true of every welfare subject" [snippet]; van der Deijl quotes p. 324 [raw]: the "more important reason to favor invariabilism is that it is difficult, on reflection, to see what could explain why a theory of welfare might be true of some subjects but false of others". "How to Use the Experience Machine", *Utilitas* 28(3): 314–332 (2016) — defends some force for the machine against the status-quo-bias rebuttals [via van der Deijl 2019, raw]. "Two Kinds of Desire Theory of Well-Being", *Midwest Studies* 46: 55–86 (2022), DOI 10.5840/msp202212231 [Crossref]. "Welfare, Connection, and Recognition", *PPR* (2026), DOI 10.1111/phpr.70082 [abstract, OpenAlex]: uses the experience machine to argue for a social basic good, then proposes as candidates "connection, the condition that obtains whenever two beings are conscious of each other" and "recognition, the condition that a being is in whenever another being is veridically conscious of it as a conscious being".
- **Tenet alignment**: Conflicts with P-VS3's global requirement; aligns with P-MS1's necessity direction on both the datum and the ground-shape (goods that need a feeling subject). The most useful hostile-then-friendly source in the set.

### van der Deijl 2021, "The Sentience Argument for Experientialism about Welfare" [raw]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-020-01427-w — *Philosophical Studies* 178(1): 187–208 (print 2021; online 12 Feb 2020). Open access; full text read.
- **Type**: Paper.
- **Definitions [raw]**: "Theories that accept that only what affects a person's experience can affect the degree of someone's wellbeing endorse the experience requirement"; theories that deny it are "extra-experientialist". Of Parfit's tripartite taxonomy "only hedonism … unambivalently accepts it". Fn 2 distinguishes his broad definition from Hawkins's narrow one — experientialism "is sometimes defined as the view that the sole bearers of intrinsic value are experiences (e.g. Hawkins 2015, 155)" — and notes that "the broad definition allows for welfare goods that are themselves non-experiential, but gain welfare value once they are experienced".
- **Welfare sentientism [raw]**: all and only sentient beings are welfare subjects — "a highly basic belief, and … therefore difficult to argue for". The demarcation question "has, interestingly, not attracted much attention in the literature (cf. Lin 2018 …; Sumner 1996; and Bernstein 1998 who defend a sentience-account; and Kraut 2009, sect. 2, who rejects welfare sentientism)". He quotes Sumner 1988, p. 164 [via]: welfarists and rightists agree that "(1) a creature has moral standing just in case it has interests, (2) having interests requires sentience, or the capacity for experience, and (3) many animals have interests". And: "The claim that sentience is central to wellbeing is thus weaker than the claim that it is central to moral status. It indeed seems plausible that the reason why we think sentience matters for moral consideration is because we think wellbeing matters for moral consideration."
- **The argument [raw, verbatim]**: "(1) On extra-experientialist accounts of wellbeing, welfare goods may affect our welfare without entering into our experience. (2) For an extra-experientialist theory that contains such a good, X, these theories either allow that X can be had by non-sentient beings or that X can be had by sentient beings after death (when sentience is gone). (3) In order to be coherent with welfare sentientism, theories that are overextensive must either adopt an ad hoc constraint, or require backward determination. (4) Neither of these moves is a satisfactory response to the dilemma." Desire-satisfactionism faces "incompleteness" (it must say what a non-desiring sentient being's welfare is), and the only fix — a different theory for those subjects — "denies welfare invariabilism". Achievement (Bradford 2013, p. 205: an achievement is "a process which culminates in a product") and knowledge face overextensiveness. Sumner's authentic-happiness hybrid (1996, ch. 6) is extra-experiential and inherits the problem; fn: Sumner is "skeptical about the possibility of posthumous welfare" (1996, p. 127).
- **Fn 7 [raw]** quotes Sumner 1996, p. 74: "If well-being is given the sort of objective interpretation Aristotle suggested, then the class of welfare subjects will embrace all creatures to whom notions such as functioning or flourishing can be meaningfully applied" — the sentence that links objective-list welfare to biocentrism.
- **Objections he anticipates [raw]**: §5.1 denying welfare sentientism ("it would seem highly counterintuitive to either ascribe wellbeing to non-sentient entities, or to deny that some sentient entities are welfare subjects"), and §5.2 denying that coherence (invariabilism, non-ad-hocness) is a desideratum.
- **Tenet alignment**: The published argument *for* the Map's global requirement, and the one Lin answers. Its premise is exactly the Map's welfare sentientism. Note it needs invariabilism — a premise Lin supplied and the Map has never stated.

### van der Deijl 2019, "Is Pleasure All That Is Good about Experience?" [raw]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-018-1090-y — *Philosophical Studies* 176(7): 1769–1787. Open access; full text read.
- **Key points [raw]**: Experientialism has "gone from the status quo to a nearly extinct view in the past century" and is "undergoing a revival (e.g. Crisp 2006a; Tännsjö 2007; Bradley 2009; Moen 2016; Bramble 2016a)". The stylised fact that experientialism = hedonism is defended by Bramble 2016a, p. 88 [via]: "If the experience requirement is true, then hedonism seems likely to be true as well" — and by Heathwood 2013, p. 6 [via]: the requirement "arguably implies that only certain experiences or mental states can be intrinsically good for a person". Van der Deijl denies the entailment: "broader experientialism is the view that only what affects our experience can alter our wellbeing, but that the degree of someone's wellbeing is not merely determined by the pleasure and pain they experience", with "novelty of experience, the experience of human compassion, and a sense of self-understanding" as extra-hedonic contributors "as long as a person enjoys these experiences". Motivation: the philosophy-of-swine objection is specific to hedonism; the experience-machine objection applies to any experientialism but its force "appears much less strong" after De Brigard 2010 and Weijers 2014, though "Lin (2016) recently argued that some of the force … withstands these recent rebuttals".
- **Tenet alignment**: This is the Map's "Beyond Hedonism" move in the welfare literature, with a different list. [[topics/phenomenal-value-realism]] should cite it.

### Lee 2025, "Consciousness Makes Things Matter" [raw]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.3998/phimp.1956 — *Philosophers' Imprint* 25. Author PDF read in full (andrewyuanlee.com).
- **Abstract [raw, verbatim]**: "This paper argues that phenomenal consciousness is what makes an entity a welfare subject, or the kind of thing that can be better or worse off and that can have a life worth living. I develop a variety of motivations for this view, and then defend it from objections concerning death, non-conscious entities that have interests (such as plants), and conscious subjects that necessarily have welfare level zero. I also explain how my theory of welfare subjects relates to experientialist and anti-experientialist theories of welfare goods."
- **Capacity vs state versions [raw]**: "According to the capacity version of the phenomenal theory, an entity is a welfare subject just in case it has the capacity for consciousness. According to the state version … just in case it's in a conscious mental state. If you're in a dreamless sleep, you aren't in a conscious mental state, but you still have the capacity for consciousness." He works with the capacity version — the Map's reading exactly.
- **The atoms argument [raw]**: consciousness's ethical significance cannot be explained by its being *necessary for* welfare goods: "for all (or at least most) welfare goods, being made of atoms is a necessary condition … Yet nobody thinks that being made of atoms is an ethically significant property." Nor by its being *sufficient for* some good, since that requires consciousness itself to be a welfare good, which "few think". Hence the phenomenal theory of *subjects*.
- **The full-blooded-sense test [raw]**: "Does your houseplant have a life worth living? Is your life better or worse than Microsoft's? How desirable is it to be in the position of ChatGPT?" — "when we talk about the 'welfare' of non-conscious entities such as plants, corporations, or AIs, we aren't invoking the same full-blooded sense of 'welfare'".
- **§2 independence result [raw]**: "the phenomenal theory is logically independent of both experientialism and anti-experientialism". Van der Deijl, Lin, Bradford and Kriegel "all assume that the phenomenal theory of welfare subjects stands or falls with experientialist theories of welfare goods"; Lee denies it. Against Bradford (fn 25): one can hold "(a) desire-satisfactionism …, (b) a permissive, functionalist theory of desire (where even non-conscious entities, such as thermostats and bacteria, can have desires), and (c) the phenomenal theory of welfare subjects" — "only conscious entities benefit from the satisfaction of those desires". The analogy: "only entities bound by a gravitational force have weight, weight is determined by mass, yet even entities that aren't bound by a gravitational force have mass". Fn 24: "Sumner [1996: 43, 127–128] seems to endorse both anti-experientialism and the phenomenal theory."
- **Zero and welfare neutrals [raw]**: a conscious subject whose only experiences are neutral grey is a welfare subject on the phenomenal theory with necessarily zero welfare; van der Deijl handles the case with "welfare neutrals". Fn 20 quotes Kahane & Savulescu 2009, p. 13 [via]: "it is doubtful that a mental life consisting only of a bare stream of consciousness — a sequence of random and hedonically neutral sensations — could be said to involve interests of any kind" — a rejection of *sufficiency* that matches the Map's Vulcan verdict.
- **Fn 23 [raw]** — the "recent works" that tie candidate goods to consciousness: Brogaard & Chudnoff 2020 (empirical knowledge), Smithies 2019 p. 17 (all knowledge), Stampe 1987 / Strawson 1994 / Oddie 2005 / Smithies & Weiss 2019 (desire), Roberts 2009 (friendship), Kriegel 2019, Lin 2020.
- **Tenet alignment**: The nearest published statement of the Map's necessity direction, reached without dualism; supplies the answer to Bradford/Kagan/Mogensen the Map currently lacks. His speculative principle — a good must "affect whatever property makes x a welfare subject" — reverses the explanatory order so that "the phenomenal theory is used to explain experientialism", which is the Map's own order of derivation (P-VS3 → P-MS1 is the reverse; see angle 2 below).

### Bradford 2023, "Consciousness and Welfare Subjectivity" [abstract; argument via Mogensen and Lee, raw]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1111/nous.12434 — *Noûs* 57(4): 905–921 (online 16 Sept 2022; the Map's reference list and Mogensen cite "2022").
- **Abstract [abstract, verbatim]**: "Many philosophers tacitly accept *the View*: consciousness is necessary for being a welfare subject. That is, in order to be an eligible bearer of welfare goods and bads, an entity must be capable of phenomenal consciousness. However, this paper argues that, in the absence of a compelling rationale, we are not licensed to accept the View, because doing so amounts to fallacious reasoning in theorizing about welfare: insisting on the View when consciousness is not in fact important for welfare value in a systematic and significant way is objectionably 'consciousist.' As a result, the View does not advance our understanding of the value of consciousness. The paper further diagnoses why we may be attracted to the View, and what we should accept instead."
- **The argument [via Mogensen, raw]**: "Kagan (2019: 33) and Bradford (2022) both set out this form of argument and recommend that we follow where it leads, acknowledging the possibility of unconscious welfare subjects" — the form being: if desire-fulfilment is a welfare good (even one among many) and desire does not require consciousness, non-conscious desirers are welfare subjects. Bradford's own goods are perfectionist — achievement as "a process which culminates in a product" (2013, p. 205 [via van der Deijl]) — which is why the 2026-09-05 outer review called it "Bradford's perfectionism".
- **Tenet alignment**: The direct denial of P-MS1's necessity direction from inside welfare theory — the "worked case of non-conscious welfare" that consciousness-value-connection's shift condition 3 names, argued rather than merely conceivable. Lee's reply is the published answer; the Map has not yet given one beyond a sentence.

### Kagan 2019, *How to Count Animals, more or less* — the welfare and agency passages [via Mogensen, raw, page-numbered; book abstract]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198829676.001.0001.
- **Key points**: pp. 32–34 — the tension between welfare sentientism and desire theories (Mogensen: "as a number of philosophers have noted recently (Kagan 2019: 32–34; van der Deijl 2020; Bradford 2022)"); p. 33 the desire argument for unconscious welfare subjects. p. 20 [via, verbatim]: agency = behavioural desires, "dispositions to act in ways that tend – according to one's beliefs – to bring about specified states of affairs." p. 28 [via, verbatim], the robot planet: you are about to dissect a small robot "when another robot, its mother, comes racing up to you, desperately pleading with you to leave it alone … It simply doesn't matter to me that the child and its mother are 'mere' robots, lacking in sentience." Kagan concludes agency suffices for standing without sentience.
- **Tenet alignment**: Kagan is a rival to necessity on *two* fronts the Map has not separated: (i) unconscious welfare via desire (the Bradford front), and (ii) agency as an independent ground of standing (the Mogensen 2025 *PPR* front). ### Mogensen 2025, "Desire-Fulfilment and Consciousness" [abstract; GPI WP 24-2024 raw]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-025-02455-0 — *Philosophical Studies* 183(2): 511–529; working paper at globalprioritiesinstitute.org.
- **Abstract [abstract, verbatim]**: "I show that there are good reasons to think that some individuals without any capacity for consciousness should be counted as welfare subjects, assuming that desire-fulfilment is a welfare good and that any individuals who can accrue welfare goods are welfare subjects. While other philosophers have argued for similar conclusions, I show that they have done so by relying on a simplistic understanding of the desire-fulfilment theory."
- **Key points [raw]**: The desire that matters is *attitudinal* (Sumner 1996, p. 121; Heathwood 2019), not behavioural — "it isn't plausible that the satisfaction of merely behavioural desires is a welfare good". Attitudinal desire involves "a (disposition to exhibit some) positive affective state, such as an emotion". The whole result then turns on whether emotion can be unconscious: "I think it is reasonable to give at least modest credence to the view that emotional states are not inherently bound to consciousness". His reading of Kagan's robots: "What does the work in this thought experiment is surely the attribution to that robot of a kind of mental state that corporations saliently lack …: namely, emotion." The corporation reductio: "We do not feel sympathy for Samsung." He explicitly notes Lee's escape (fn: "we could question the assumption that any individuals capable of possessing the welfare goods identified by a given theory of welfare should be counted as welfare subjects according to that theory (Lee forthcoming)") and sets it aside by assumption.
- **Companion**: "Once More, Without Feeling", *PPR* 111(1): 343–365 (2025), DOI 10.1111/phpr.70018 [abstract]: "a pluralist theory of moral standing, on which both welfare subjectivity and autonomy can confer moral status … autonomy does not entail welfare subjectivity, but can ground moral standing in its absence", while highlighting "plausible views on which autonomy entails phenomenal consciousness".
- **Tenet alignment**: The strongest current version of the unconscious-welfare-subject case, and it is honest that it rests on unconscious *affect* — which relocates the dispute to the phenomenal/functional reading of valence the Map already fights at [[concepts/valence]] (Carruthers 2018). The Map's Vulcan and illusionism material is the reply; the Samsung line is a gift.

### Basl 2019, *The Death of the Ethic of Life*, and Basl & Sandler 2013 [abstract; NDPR via summariser; 2013 via snippet]
- **URLs**: https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780190923877.001.0001; NDPR review by Kent Dunnington, 10 April 2020, https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/the-death-of-the-ethic-of-life/; Basl & Sandler, "The Good of Non-Sentient Entities: Organisms, Artifacts, and Synthetic Biology", *Studies in History and Philosophy of Biological and Biomedical Sciences* 44(4): 697–705 (2013), DOI 10.1016/j.shpsc.2013.05.017 [Crossref].
- **Key points**: OUP abstract [abstract]: the book "articulates why the ethic is immune to most of the standard criticisms raised against it, but also why such an ethic is untenable … it argues for an old conclusion in an entirely new way." Structure [summariser]: Part I builds the strongest biocentrism — non-sentient organisms have welfare, grounded in etiological teleology ("something can have a telos in virtue of being selected for"; ch. 3 "The Etiological Account of Teleological Welfare"); Part II shows the same ground confers welfare on artefacts and collectives, and "having a welfare does not entail having direct moral status". Basl & Sandler 2013 [snippet]: "given the etiological account of teleology, non-sentient living things, synthetic organisms, and artifacts all have a good of their own." Dunnington's caveat [summariser]: the argument bites less on non-naturalist biocentrists.
- **Tenet alignment**: Basl's is the *welfare-without-status* position — welfare in Lee's non-full-blooded sense, status withheld — and the reason he withholds it is the artefact dilemma, not an experience requirement. He therefore supports P-MS1's necessity *conclusion* (non-sentient welfare bearers lack direct status) while being neutral on its *ground*, exactly as the sentientism refine says. What the article can add: Basl's own restriction ("the well-being of non-sentient beings … is morally significant only to the extent that it matters to sentient beings" [via the 2026-09-05 Claude review; not re-verified]) is the local experience requirement applied to *significance*, which is where the Map's derivation needs it.

### Sumner 1996, *Welfare, Happiness, and Ethics*; Sumner 1988 [via van der Deijl and Lee, raw]
- **Key points**: The classic sentience-grounded welfare theory that is nevertheless anti-experientialist — welfare is authentic life-satisfaction, and inauthenticity from deception or heteronomy lowers welfare without entering experience (ch. 6, via van der Deijl). p. 74 on the Aristotelian option extending welfare subjects to whatever can flourish [via]; p. 121 on attitudinal wanting [via Mogensen]; pp. 43, 127–128 endorsing both anti-experientialism and the phenomenal theory of subjects [via Lee fn 24]; 1988, p. 164, interests require "sentience, or the capacity for experience" [via van der Deijl]. Sumner therefore *instantiates* Lee's independence result before it was stated — sentience-grounded subjects, extra-experiential goods.
- **Tenet alignment**: The proof that welfare sentientism does not entail the global requirement; the Map should stop treating "sentience-grounded welfare theory" and "experientialism" as one position.

### Griffin 1986, *Well-Being*; Kagan 1992, "The Limits of Well-Being" [via; unverified pages]
- Griffin names "the experience requirement" in Part One and rejects it in favour of the informed-desire account (the standard citation is pp. 13ff; the Internet Archive copy was not available for search-inside this pass, so the page is unverified). The SEP "Well-Being" entry (Crisp, rev. 13 Jan 2026) [summariser] attaches the phrase to non-hedonists who "suggest that what matters is valuable consciousness, and consciousness can be valuable for non-hedonic reasons", citing Kraut 2018 and Kriegel 2019 — i.e., it now treats broad experientialism as the live form. Kagan 1992, *Social Philosophy and Policy* 9(2): 169–189: the deceived-businessman case and the intrinsic-properties argument [via Lin's summariser and van der Deijl fn 3].

### Hawkins 2010, 2014, 2015, 2016 [snippet; 2015 via van der Deijl]
- "The Subjective Intuition", *Philosophical Studies* 148(1): 61–68 (2010), DOI 10.1007/s11098-010-9505-4 [Crossref]: the contemporary reading of subjectivism as honouring the individual's evaluative perspective "dooms subjective theories to failure"; we should "reinstate certain features of traditional hedonism, particularly the idea that felt experience plays a role that no theory of welfare can afford to ignore" [snippet]. "Well-Being, Time, and Dementia", *Ethics* 124(3): 507–542 (2014) [snippet]. "The Experience Machine and the Experience Requirement", in Fletcher (ed.), *The Routledge Handbook of Philosophy of Well-Being* (2015) — pages given as 371–381 by van der Deijl's reference list and OpenAlex, 355–365 by the Routledge listing; unresolved — separates hedonism from experientialism and argues that Nozick's set-up cannot distinguish self-interested from non-self-interested reasons for declining "without begging the question against the hedonist" [snippet]; also the source of the narrow/broad distinction (van der Deijl fn 2). "What's Good for Them? Best Interests and Severe Disorders of Consciousness", in Sinnott-Armstrong (ed.), *Finding Consciousness* (OUP 2016), pp. 180–206 [snippet].
- **Tenet alignment**: Hawkins is the welfare theorist closest to the Map's temperament — felt experience as ineliminable — without the identity thesis. Zero live citations.

### Kriegel 2019; Kriegel forthcoming; Lee (Geoffrey); Siewert 1998 [abstract / via / snippet]
- Kriegel, "The Value of Consciousness", *Analysis* 79(3): 503–520 (2019), DOI 10.1093/analys/anz045 [abstract, OpenAlex]: a survey placing "theories of well-being, animal and medical ethics" and epistemology under one question, with epistemic, ethical and aesthetic value "fundamentally distinct and incommensurable". Kriegel, "The Value of Consciousness to the One Who Has It", in Lee & Pautz (eds.), *The Importance of Being Conscious* (OUP, listed 2025): "it's very natural to derive one's view on who the wellbeing subjects are from one's view on what wellbeing consists in" [via Lee 2025, raw]; considers five explanations of why a zombie's life is never worth living, and holds that one "would have to be seriously confused" to think Kagan's robots are harmed [snippet]. Geoffrey Lee, "Consciousness, Pseudo-Consciousness, and the Moral Significance of Consciousness", same volume [snippet]: no deep natural divide between conscious beings and the rest; many kinds of "inner light", none "uniquely significant morally". Siewert, *The Significance of Consciousness* (Princeton 1998), ch. 9 [snippet]: we would not choose to become zombies even if it changed nothing observable, so consciousness has intrinsic value. Andrew Lee, "Is Consciousness Intrinsically Valuable?", *Philosophical Studies* 176(3): 655–671 (2019), DOI 10.1007/s11098-018-1032-8 [Crossref; content via Shepherd 2024]: a world containing only a maximally simple, valence-free experience has no non-derivative value over a non-conscious one.
- **Tenet alignment**: Kriegel is an ally on the zombie datum and against Kagan; Geoffrey Lee is the deflationary rival the Map's illusionism material already fights; the 2019 Lee result is why the Map grounds value in *valence* rather than consciousness as such.

### The experience machine: Nozick 1974 and the empirical turn [IEP via summariser; Goldman abstract via snippet]
- IEP, "The Experience Machine" (Buscicchi) [summariser]: the target is "mental statism"; De Brigard 2010's reverse machine (only 13% chose reality when told they were already plugged in, against 70%+ in the original); Weijers 2014's "Stranger No Status Quo" scenario (55% for reality). Bramble, "The Experience Machine", *Philosophy Compass* 11(3): 136–145 (2016). Goldman, "Well-Being and Experience", *The Journal of Ethics* 26(2): 175–192 (online 2020) [snippet]: both plugging in and refusing can be rational "depending on the subject's central desires", so the near-even empirical split supports "a rational desire satisfaction account … rather than experientialism or its denial".
- Rawlette's own reply [summariser, précis at utilitarianism.net]: the machine "isolates us from information about our future welfare" and leaves us "incapable of any action" promoting well-being — an instrumental reply; and her experience requirement is treated as an observation about the concept of intrinsic value ("If we never experienced pleasure or pain or any other positive or negative experiential quality, we wouldn't have any idea what it meant for something to have intrinsic value") rather than argued for.
- **Tenet alignment**: The classic argument *against* the Map's global requirement, unanswered anywhere on the Map. Lin 2026 turns it into an argument for a consciousness-involving social good, which is the Map's best lead (angle 3).

### The AI-welfare front: Goldstein & Kirk-Giannini 2025 vs Veit 2026 [abstract]
- Goldstein & Kirk-Giannini, "AI Wellbeing", *Asian Journal of Philosophy* (2025), DOI 10.1007/s44204-025-00246-2; arXiv 2509.11913 [abstract]: "a wide range of theories of mental states, when combined with leading theories of wellbeing, predict that certain existing artificial systems have wellbeing". Veit, "Is Consciousness Required for AI Welfare?", *Asian Journal of Philosophy* 5(1) (2026), DOI 10.1007/s44204-026-00382-3 [abstract, verbatim]: "their arguments fail to establish that consciousness is dispensable for well-being … consciousness (or rather sentience) should still be treated as a requirement for AI welfare." Veit's mechanism [summariser]: "consciousness is required to be a welfare subject even if it is not required for the possession of particular welfare goods" — Lee's decoupling again — plus the charge that G&K-G's view counts never-conscious capacity-bearers as zero-welfare subjects and treats "prudentially insignificant unconscious desires as genuinely welfare-enhancing". Cecchinato, "No Consciousness, No Welfare", in progress (scheduled talk, March 2026) [snippet].
- **Tenet alignment**: Shows the experience requirement is now the hinge of the AI-welfare debate; [[topics/ethics-of-possible-ai-consciousness]] should link to whatever article results.

## Major Positions

### Global experientialism (the experience requirement proper)
- **Proponents**: Rawlette 2016 (hedonic, identity thesis); Bramble 2016a (hedonic); van der Deijl 2019/2021 (pluralistic); Kraut 2018; Hawkins (narrow form, 2010/2015 sympathies); the Map (P-VS3; [[topics/phenomenal-value-realism]]).
- **Core claim**: no difference in welfare without a difference in phenomenology (Lin's formulation); or, narrowly, experiences are the sole bearers of intrinsic prudential value.
- **Key arguments**: Kagan's intrinsic-properties argument; Bramble's posthumous-harm argument; van der Deijl's sentience argument (welfare sentientism + invariabilism + non-ad-hocness); Rawlette's conceptual observation.
- **Relation to site tenets**: This *is* P-VS3 read as a welfare thesis. Lin's rebuttals of the first two arguments transfer directly to the Map's "unread proposition" test, which consciousness-value-connection already concedes may be "a restatement".

### Local experientialism without the global requirement (Lin's position)
- **Proponents**: Lin 2017/2020a/2021/forthcoming; Sumner 1996 (structurally); Lee 2025 counts it as "impure experientialism".
- **Core claim**: every basic good is, by its nature, accessible only to sentient beings (a felt attitude or a consciousness-involving relation is a constituent), yet welfare can differ without phenomenology differing — e.g., a felt desire satisfied versus unsatisfied by an unexperienced worldly fact.
- **Key arguments**: invariabilism plus welfare sentientism are honoured by making sentience-involvement a feature of each good; the experience machine and deception cases show extra-experiential differences matter.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Grants everything P-MS1's necessity derivation uses. The disagreement with the Map is confined to P-VS3's "value resides in experience", and the Map should say so rather than fight Lin on sentientism.

### Phenomenal theory of welfare subjects, independent of goods (Lee)
- **Proponents**: Lee 2025; Veit 2026; Kriegel forthcoming (on subjects); Kahane & Savulescu 2009 on the sufficiency side.
- **Core claim**: consciousness (capacity version) is what makes an entity a welfare subject; this is logically independent of experientialism about goods.
- **Relation to site tenets**: The necessity direction of P-MS1 stated as a first-order welfare thesis with no dualist premise; its cost is that it treats "welfare subject" as basic rather than derived from the goods, which the Map's derivation from P-VS3 does not.

### Anti-experientialist welfare subjects (Bradford, Kagan, Mogensen)
- **Proponents**: Bradford 2023 (perfectionist goods; "consciousism"); Kagan 2019, p. 33 (desire); Mogensen 2025 (attitudinal desire backed by possibly-unconscious affect); Goldstein & Kirk-Giannini 2025 (AI).
- **Core claim**: if a welfare good can be instantiated without consciousness, its instantiator is a welfare subject; the View is an unearned prejudice.
- **Relation to site tenets**: The live denial of necessity from inside welfare theory. Two replies are available and the Map has used neither in print: Lee's decoupling (goods without subjects), and the observation, from Mogensen's own analysis, that once behavioural desire is excluded the case rests on *unconscious affect* — the functional reading of valence the Map already contests.

### Etiological welfare without status (Basl)
- **Proponents**: Basl 2019; Basl & Sandler 2013.
- **Core claim**: non-sentient organisms have a selection-grounded welfare; so do artefacts; therefore welfare of that kind cannot ground direct moral status.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Ally on the necessity conclusion, neutral on the ground; his welfare is Lee's non-full-blooded sense.

### Agency as an independent ground of standing (Kagan; Mogensen 2025 PPR)
- **Proponents**: Kagan 2019 (agency suffices, robots); Mogensen 2025 (autonomy confers status without welfare subjectivity).
- **Core claim**: the necessity of sentience for *status* fails even if it holds for *welfare*.
- **Relation to site tenets**: A rival to P-MS1's necessity direction that is not biocentrism and that the register does not name; Mogensen's own hedge — views on which autonomy entails consciousness are "plausible" — is the Map's opening, and Kriegel's "seriously confused" is the blunt ally.

## Key Debates

### Does welfare sentientism entail the experience requirement?
- **Sides**: van der Deijl (yes, on pain of ad hocness or variabilism) vs Lin (no: the right theory of goods explains the datum) vs Lee (the question is malformed: subjects and goods are independent).
- **Core disagreement**: whether the theory of welfare subjects is derived from the theory of goods (Kriegel: "very natural"), or the reverse (Lee's speculative principle), or neither.
- **Current state**: open; Lin and van der Deijl were simultaneous (both online 2020) and neither has replied to the other in print as far as this pass found; Lee 2025 is the synthesis. **The Map's position**: it derives subjects from value (P-VS3 → P-MS1), so it sits with Kriegel's "natural" order and is exposed to Lin's objection to *that* order; Lee's reversal would immunise P-MS1 at the price of making it non-derived — which the register's "numbered last, logically first" ordering would have to record.

### Can there be an unconscious welfare subject?
- **Sides**: Bradford, Kagan, Mogensen, Goldstein & Kirk-Giannini (yes, via desire or perfectionist goods) vs Lee, Veit, Kriegel, van der Deijl, Lin, the Map (no).
- **Core disagreement**: whether instantiating a good makes one a subject (the auxiliary premise Lee isolates), and whether attitudinal desire/emotion can be unconscious.
- **Current state**: Mogensen concedes "modest credence" and calls Kagan's robots "the best we can do"; the corporation case is the shared reductio. The Map's [[concepts/valence]] draw with Carruthers is the same dispute in different dress.

### Does the experience machine refute experientialism?
- **Sides**: Nozick, Lin 2016, Lin 2026 (yes, or at least some force survives) vs De Brigard, Weijers, Hawkins 2015, Rawlette (no, or not as set up) vs Goldman (it supports neither side).
- **Current state**: the empirical results are split and the interpretation contested; the philosophically live move is Lin 2026's, which extracts a *consciousness-involving* social good from the machine — the machine world lacks another mind veridically conscious of you. **The Map's opening**: on phenomenal value realism the deficit Lin identifies is itself a deficit of experience — someone else's — so the case can be read as a difference in the world's total felt value rather than a counter-example to value's residing in experience, at the cost of moving from *welfare of the subject* to *value in the world*. Whether that is a reply or a change of subject is exactly what the article must adjudicate.

### Is the Map's requirement an argument or a restatement?
- **Sides**: consciousness-value-connection concedes the question; Lin presses it.
- **Current state**: what the Map has that Lin's targets lack is the *identity thesis* — if intrinsic goodness is a phenomenal quality (Rawlette; [[topics/phenomenal-value-realism]]'s coherence and transparency tests), then a welfare difference with no phenomenal difference is a difference in intrinsic value with no difference in any bearer of it, which is incoherent on the thesis. That is an argument from the nature of value rather than from welfare subjects, and it is only as strong as the identity thesis; Lin can reply that it begs the question, and the honest answer is that the disagreement lies at the identity thesis, which has its own tests, not at sentientism.
