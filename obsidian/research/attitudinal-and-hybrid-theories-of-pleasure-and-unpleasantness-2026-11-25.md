## Executive Summary

The pleasure-and-welfare literature divides theories of what makes an experience pleasant into *felt-quality* theories (an experience is pleasant because of how it feels) and *attitudinal* theories (it is pleasant because its subject takes a pro-attitude to it). Daniel Pallies (2021, read in full) maps the dispute with six "HONEST" challenges: Heterogeneity, Oppositeness and Normativity against felt-quality theories; Euthyphro, Separateness and Togetherness against attitudinal ones. He argues that *dispositional hybrid* theories avoid most of them, and that one variant, the necessitation theory, avoids all six. Three findings matter for the Map.

1. **Oppositeness has a reply the Map can use directly.** Klocksiem (2010, read in full) argues that pleasure and pain are opposites "because pleasure is good and pain is bad", and that the desire theory's own solution has the wrong logical structure. Under P-VS3's identity (felt badness *is* badness), the Map can take that reply without a single bipolar hedonic scale, which `topics/valence-as-a-natural-kind` already finds unsupported. The cost is that oppositeness bottoms out in the opposition of good and bad. Pallies himself ranks Oppositeness the *least* forceful of the six.
2. **The attitudinal family puts pressure on P-VS3, not on Dualism.** On a pure attitudinal theory whose attitude need not be felt (Heathwood's definition places no phenomenal condition on the desire; SEP reports that Feldman's attitudinal pleasure involves no essential feeling), nothing felt is the badness, so the identity claim loses its relatum. Felt-attitude hybrids (Kahane 2009, read in full; Lin 2018, abstract) and Pallies's necessitation theory keep it. Neither question touches Dualism, which agrees with `concepts/valence`'s orthogonality claim.
3. **Heterogeneity is the strongest objection and the Map has already met it in one form.** Pallies ranks it first. The host page answers it with Labukt's pluralist hedonic tones. Pluralism multiplies the oppositeness question (what makes *each* positive tone opposed to *each* negative one?), and the value reply is the one answer that works tone by tone (this last point is the note's own inference).

### Pallies (2021), "An honest look at hybrid theories of pleasure" (read in full)
- **Citation**: Pallies, D. (2021). An honest look at hybrid theories of pleasure. *Philosophical Studies*, 178(3), 887–907. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-020-01464-5 (online first 2020; affiliation at OpenAlex: University of Southern California).
- **Read from**: the Wayback raw snapshot of the PhilArchive deposit (see routes above). Preprint pagination.
- **What it does (VERIFIED)**:

  - Dispositional hybrid theories hold that "pleasurable experiences are all and only those experiences which dispose us to desire them in virtue of feeling the way that they do" (p. 1).
  - He does not adjudicate the challenges: "I will not assess whether those challenges succeed or fail" (p. 4).
  - **His own ranking** (fn 11, p. 16): "I rank the forcefulness of the six challenges as follows, from most forceful to least forceful: (1) Heterogeneity, (2) Togetherness, (3) Normativity, (4) Euthyphro, (5) Separateness, (6) Oppositeness."   - Felt-quality replies he lists (p. 8): heterogeneity, by arguing that pleasures "really do feel alike, despite our intuitions to the contrary (Crisp 2006, p. 109-110; Smuts 2011, p. 256-257; Bramble 2013, p. 209-211)"; oppositeness, by "developing views according to which pleasure and unpleasure are clear opposites (Klocksiem 2010; Moen 2013)"; normativity, "by arguing we have reasons to pursue pleasure even if we are indifferent to it (Goldstein 1980; Rachels 2000, p. 201; and Bramble 2013, p. 213-214)".
  - Why the HON challenges miss attitudinal theories (p. 8): "attitudes—unlike felt-qualities—have clear opposites".
  - Weak dispositional theory (p. 21): "(Weak Dispositional Theory) An experience e of a subject s is pleasurable for s iff e has a felt-quality such that s is robustly disposed to non-instrumentally desire experiences with that felt-quality upon becoming aware that she is having an experience with that felt-quality." It avoids H, O, N and S, but not Euthyphro or Togetherness.
  - Strong dispositional theory (p. 23): a non-empty set of felt-qualities [Q*] necessarily disposes its subject to non-instrumental desire; "An experience is a pleasure iff it has a felt-quality which is a member of [Q*]." It "is actually a version of the felt-quality theory, in addition to being a version of the attitudinal theory" (p. 24). With Distinctness it licenses E2Q, "e(Qc) is pleasurable because it has felt-quality Qc", alongside E1 (we desire it because it is pleasurable).
  - The price (pp. 24–25): "If we accept Distinctness and the strong dispositional theory, we must reject Hume’s Dictum: the thesis that there are no necessary connections between distinct existences." The alternative is role functionalism, which denies Distinctness; for the functionalist "E1 and E2Q are prima facie circular" (p. 26).
  - Score (p. 26): "The weak dispositional theory avoids four of the HONEST challenges; the functionalist theory avoids five of them. The necessitation theory avoids all six challenges." "The necessitation theory has a spiritual predecessor in the theory of pleasure described by Thomas Sprigge." (fn 21)
  - Taxonomy (fn 2, p. 4): "These representationalist theories are not generally classified as versions of the felt-quality theory, but it’s not obvious why they are not so classified." He counts Bain, Martínez and Cutter & Tye as felt-quality theories in his minimal sense, and reports that Bain (2017, p. 2) agrees about his own view (via Pallies).
  - Composite hybrids (p. 18): "Versions of this general idea have been developed by Guy Kahane (2009, 2016), Murat Aydede (2018), and Eden Lin (2018)." "Lin develops this idea into a hybrid theory of pleasure, though he does not ultimately endorse it."

That is right in outline, but Pallies claims "most, if not all", and only the necessitation variant avoids all six. He also does not assess the challenges and ranks Oppositeness last. A reader who takes the review's framing ("the manuscript provides no answer to Oppositeness") as Pallies's emphasis inverts his ranking.

The necessitation theory's rejection of Hume's Dictum for experiential properties fits the Map's phenomenal-powers discussion (`topics/consciousness-and-the-metaphysics-of-laws-and-dispositions`). ### Klocksiem (2010), "Pleasure, desire and oppositeness" (read in full)
- **Citation**: Klocksiem, J. (2010). *Journal of Ethics and Social Philosophy*, 4(2), 1–7. https://doi.org/10.26556/jesp.v4i2.134. Open access; PDF at https://www.jesp.org/pdf/36346f73-90e0-8165-959c-ef2121a1990e
- **VERIFIED (abstract, journal page)**: "I propose that pleasure and pain are opposites because pleasure is good and pain is bad, and good and bad are clear opposites."
- **VERIFIED (body)**:
  - Against the desire solution (p. 3): "To be averse to p is to desire that not-p, but being pained that p has no such relationship to being pleased that not-p." His example: he is pained by having a cold, yet "generally take[s] no pleasure in not having a cold". The structure of good and bad matches pleasure and pain instead: if x is good, not-x may be bad *or neutral*.
  - Cost he accepts (p. 5): "This account of the oppositeness of pleasure and pain has the consequence that if value nihilism were true, then pleasure and pain would not be opposites." His reply: "One, since nihilism is necessarily false, there are no nihilistic worlds".
  - Order of explanation (p. 5): "If pleasure were not good, it would not be attractive – its goodness explains why we desire it." This is the E1 side of Pallies's Euthyphro.
- **Guard: Klocksiem is not a felt-quality theorist.** Fn 10 (VERIFIED): "I do not mean to presume any particular theory of the nature of pleasure and pain here, though I suspect that views according to which they are purely sensory are inconsistent with the data, and I am attempting to show that desire-based views are false, as well." Pallies lists him among the felt-quality theorists' replies. The reply is *available to* the hedonic account, but do not call Klocksiem a hedonic or felt-quality theorist.
- **Limit of transfer (the note's own judgement)**: Klocksiem's counterexamples are propositional ("pained by p"). Heathwood's RSUD is a de re desire about a sensation, and an attitudinal theorist can reply that the absence of a sensation is not itself a sensation the subject could be pleased by. The value reply to oppositeness does not depend on the counterexamples, so this weakens only the anti-desire half.
- **Tenet alignment**: aligns with P-VS3 (phenomenal value realism) if the goodness and badness are taken as constitutive. Klocksiem treats it as intrinsic, essential value, citing Moore, Carson and Feldman.

## Major Positions

### Felt-quality (hedonic) theories
- **Proponents**: Broad, Duncker and Sprigge (historical, via Heathwood fn 7); Crisp 2006, Smuts 2011, Bramble 2013 (via Pallies); Rachels 2000; Labukt 2012 (pluralist hedonic tone, already on the host); Moen 2013 (dimensionalism). On Pallies's minimal definition, representationalists too (Bain, Martínez, Cutter & Tye).
- **Core claim**: an experience is pleasant or unpleasant in virtue of how it feels.
- **Relation to the Map**: this is the Map's hedonic account. It needs answers to H, O and N. The Map already argues N (the painkiller and reasons standoff on `concepts/valence`), and Labukt answers H on the host. O is open, and this note supplies the value reply.

### Attitudinal theories
- **Proponents**: Alston 1967, Brandt 1979, Parfit 1984 and Carson 2000 (via Heathwood or Pallies); Heathwood 2007; Feldman 1988 and 2004 (attitudinal pleasure); Parfit 2011 ("hedonic liking", via Pallies).
- **Core claim**: an experience is pleasant or unpleasant because its subject bears a pro- or con-attitude to it.
- **Relation to the Map**: orthogonal to Dualism. The attitude's nature is left open, and Pallies notes guise-of-the-good, attention-based and functionalist theories of desire. This is a pressure on P-VS3 when the attitude is unfelt. EST favours the Map: Togetherness (Pallies ranks it second of six and the strongest against attitudinal theories) and Euthyphro (Heathwood concedes the "less intuitive horn").

### Hybrid theories
- **Composite or felt-attitude**: Kahane 2009 and 2016, Aydede 2018 (physicalist), Lin 2018. Pleasure is partly constituted by an attitude that has its own phenomenology.
- **Dispositional (Pallies 2021)**: weak (4 of 6), functionalist (5 of 6) and necessitation (6 of 6).
- **Adverbial (Aydede 2014)**: hedonic tone as an adverbial modification of sensory quality, reductive and psychofunctionalist.
- **Relation to the Map**: the Map cannot take the *functionalist* variant, which makes the felt quality partly its dispositional role. That is the pandispositionalist collapse that `topics/consciousness-and-the-metaphysics-of-laws-and-dispositions` rejects. The necessitation variant is open to the Map at the price of rejecting Hume's Dictum for experiential properties. Kahane's variant keeps badness phenomenal. Both keep P-VS3's relatum.

## Key Debates

### Oppositeness, at full strength
- **The objection (Heathwood 2007, p. 27; Aydede 2014 v2.4 restates it as an attitudinal advantage)**: felt qualities as such have no opposites, since middle C and F# are merely different. If pleasantness and unpleasantness are felt qualities, their opposition is either a brute stipulation or unexplained. Desire and aversion are opposites by construction (desiring that p and desiring that not-p). The objection is sharpest against the Map's own redness analogy, because red and green are incompatible but are not opposite in value. It also grows under Labukt's pluralism, which the host relies on: with several positive and several negative tones, every cross-pair needs an opposition that no shared feel supplies.
- **Replies**: (a) dimensionalism (Moen 2013), with poles of one bipolar hedonic dimension, which leaves bipolarity primitive and presupposes the single scale the host doubts; (b) value (Klocksiem 2010), where pleasure and pain are opposite because good and bad are, so the opposition follows each tone's value distributively and needs no shared dimension; (c) adverbial (Aydede), a continuum of modification and a physicalist version of (a).
- **Weight**: Pallies ranks Oppositeness the least forceful of the six. Report that, but do not use it to discount the demand. The Map's best answer is (b), whose honest cost is that the opposition of good and bad is taken as basic. Every axiology needs that opposition anyway, so it is a shared primitive, not a new one. Klocksiem also accepts that under value nihilism pleasure and pain would not be opposites. For the Map, nihilism about phenomenal value is the illusionist defeater already booked against P-VS3.

### Heterogeneity, at full strength
- **The objection (Sidgwick, via Pallies; Heathwood 2007, p. 26)**: introspection finds no single feel common to the pleasures of chocolate, a sunset and a solved proof, nor to burns, nausea and grief. Pallies ranks it the most forceful of the six. Even friendly models concede the introspective datum. Aydede says that when we try to isolate a pleasure-quality, "we always seem to end up with the sensory qualities themselves".
- **Replies**: pluralist tones (Labukt, on the host); higher-order tone or dimension (Kagan, Crisp, Smuts, Moen); distinctive-feeling-involvement (Bramble); "not compelling" (Lin 2018, abstract). The attitudinal answer moves unity outside the experience: in Heathwood's words, the desire solution "appeals to an extrinsic feature of the sensation".
- **Map status**: the host's pluralism keeps P-VS3 distributively but leaves the scalar currency (P-VS1) exposed, as the host already concedes. Dimensionalism would rescue the currency philosophically, but it runs into the Kron and Lindquist findings the host reports.

### What the Map's hedonic reading says to each family
- **Felt-quality rivals**: none; it is one.
- **Pure attitudinal with an unfelt attitude (Feldman per SEP; Heathwood's definitions as written)**: the identity claim fails for that view, because there is no felt badness to be badness. The Map answers with Togetherness (the tack) and the Euthyphro order (we dislike pain because it is bad). It does not yet have a decisive argument against the view.
- **Felt-attitude hybrids (Kahane; Lin)**: compatible with P-VS3. The felt dislike carries the value.
- **Dispositional hybrids (Pallies)**: the necessitation variant is the hedonic account plus a necessary motivational link. The Map can hold it if it accepts non-Humean necessary connections for experiential properties. The functionalist variant is closed to the Map.
- **Dualism**: no source surveyed claims that the choice among these families bears on dualism. Aydede's hybrid is explicitly physicalist. Do not write that attitudinal theories threaten dualism.

## The Attitudinal Alternative {#attitudinal}

The fragmentation above splits a felt hedonic quality. The pleasure literature's other answer to heterogeneity removes it. On attitudinal theories, an experience is pleasant or unpleasant because of its subject's attitude to it: for Chris Heathwood, a sensation is unpleasant when its subject "desires, intrinsically and de re, at t, of S, that it not be occurring at t". Daniel Pallies sorts the dispute into six challenges, three against felt-quality theories (heterogeneity, oppositeness, normativity) and three against attitudinal ones (Euthyphro, separateness, togetherness), and judges the two sets "about as forceful" as each other.

Oppositeness is Heathwood's: two felt qualities, such as "middle C on a piano and a sensation of F# on a banjo", are "in no way opposites", so a felt-quality theory leaves unexplained why pleasure and pain are, while desire and aversion are opposites by construction. Pallies ranks it the weakest of the six. Justin Klocksiem's reply is open to the Map: pleasure and pain are opposites "because pleasure is good and pain is bad". Under P-VS3's identity, felt goodness and felt badness are value and disvalue, so the Map can take that reply without assuming one bipolar scale, which the results above do not supply. The reply grounds the opposition in that of good and bad, and explains it no further.

Togetherness is the Map's strongest counter. Heathwood grants that it "can seem incredible" that the feeling of stepping barefoot on a tack is painful "only due to the attitude that we happen to take up towards it."

The identity claim pays only if the attitude need not be felt, since then nothing felt is the badness. On Guy Kahane's experiential dislike theory, dislike "is necessarily felt" and the state of disliking pain is bad "because of how it intrinsically feels"; on Pallies's necessitation theory, felt qualities necessarily dispose their subject to desire them, at the price of denying Hume's Dictum. Neither choice touches Dualism.
