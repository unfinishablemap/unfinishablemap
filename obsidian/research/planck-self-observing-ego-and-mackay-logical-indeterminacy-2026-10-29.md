# Research: Planck's Self-Observing Ego and MacKay's Logical Indeterminacy

**Date**: 2026-10-29
**Search queries used**: `MacKay "On the Logical Indeterminacy of a Free Choice" Mind 1960`; `Rummens Cuypers "Determinism and the paradox of predictability" Erkenntnis 2010 MacKay`; `Coghill 2023 Glasgow thesis MacKay logical indeterminacy`; `Popper 1950 "Indeterminism in Quantum Physics and in Classical Physics" self-prediction "Oedipus effect"`; `Landsberg Evans 1970 "Free will in a mechanistic universe"`; `MacKay "Freedom of Action in a Mechanistic Universe" 1967`; Internet Archive metadata and full-text search for the primary texts.

## Executive Summary

Planck (1932) and MacKay (1960) explain why a choice feels open while it is being made without any appeal to indeterminism. Planck's version limits the causal method: a subject cannot study its own present act causally because the observer would be the object ("no eye can see itself", p. 163), a limit he calls a priori and extends to Laplace's intelligence contemplating its own ego (p. 164). MacKay's version is logical: no prediction of an agent's choice can be one the agent would be correct to believe before choosing, because believing it alters the brain state predicted, so for the agent the choice is "logically indeterminate, until we make it" (p. 37), with physical determinism neither affirmed nor denied (p. 32). Neither treats felt freedom as an illusion; both treat it as a standpoint truth. For [[trilemma-of-selection]] the bearing is partly adverse: felt *openness* is predicted under Horn 1 as firmly as under Horn 3 and cannot discriminate between them. The account does not reach *effort*: nothing in the self-observation limit predicts that felt cost should track difficulty across domains, deplete, or recruit distinct circuits. Verdict `fold`: one paragraph in §Compatibilist Objections that installs Planck and MacKay accurately, concedes openness, restricts the "systematic pattern" reply to effort, and names the Map's one discriminator (the conditional signature) as statistical rather than phenomenological. The Map does not answer Planck; it narrows the claim he refutes.

## Key Sources

### Planck, *Where Is Science Going?* (1932), ch. V, pp. 157–168
- **URL**: Internet Archive `whereissciencego00plan_0` (W. W. Norton, New York, 1932, tr. James Murphy; raw OCR re-read in full for pp. 157–169, page numbers from running heads). The task brief named Allen & Unwin; the scanned edition is the Norton printing the first-quantum page already cites.
- **Type**: Book
- **Key points** (every quotation raw-verified; the OCR double-spaces words and misreads "principle" as "frincifle" and "a priori" as "a friori", so matching used alphanumerics-only normalisation and the two misreads are corrected silently):
  - Causality is universal: "Under all circumstances the law of causation is valid, because of its transcendental character" (p. 157); "the highest types of human intelligence are subject to the causal law in the processes that result in even their greatest achievements" (p. 158).
  - The exception is a standpoint: "there is a point, one single point in the immeasurable world of mind and matter, where science and therefore every causal method of research is inapplicable, not only on practical grounds but also on logical grounds, and will always remain inapplicable. This point is the individual ego" (p. 161).
  - Self-knowledge of the *past* is possible to a "relative degree" once the facts are "distanced in the past, so that our present complex of living emotions and inclinations would not enter as factors into the observation" (p. 162).
  - The key sentences: "in principle there is no reason why we should not discover the causal connections in our own personal conduct, but that in practice we never can do so because this would mean that the observing subject would also be the object of research. And that is impossible; for no eye can see itself" (p. 163).
  - Planck rejects the illusion reading: to say freedom "is only apparent and results solely from the defects of our own understanding" is "entirely mistaken", like "suggesting that the inability of a runner to outrun his own shadow is due to his lack of speed" (p. 163). The limit "is a truth that is based on a perfectly sound logical foundation of an a priori kind" and "would hold good even in the case of the super-intelligence postulated by Laplace", which "would have to renounce the idea of studying the activities of its own ego" (p. 164).
  - Two standpoints: a Supreme Wisdom "sees the succession of cause and effect in everything we do. But this does not in the least invalidate our own sense of responsibility for our own actions" (p. 164); "Here is the place where the freedom of the will comes in and establishes itself, without usurping the right of any rival" (p. 164); "the freedom of the ego here and now, and its independence of the causal chain, is a truth that comes from the immediate dictate of the human consciousness" (p. 165); "our own lives are in the last analysis subject to causation, though the ego as regards its immediate destiny cannot be subject to that law" (p. 167).
- **Wobble to record**: p. 163 says the limit holds "in practice" while "in principle" the causes could be found; p. 164 upgrades it to a priori and logical. The first-quantum page's "epistemic rather than modal" fits p. 163; p. 164 is what MacKay later formalises. Cite the pair.
- **Tenet alignment**: Conflicts with Tenets 2 and 3 as a positive view (two-standpoint determinism about the will); silent on Tenet 1.

### MacKay, "On the Logical Indeterminacy of a Free Choice", *Mind* 69(273): 31–40 (1960)
- **URL**: https://archive.org/details/sim_mind_1960-01_69_273 (full OCR downloaded and read; pages from running heads). Upgrades the prior note's search-result entry.
- **Type**: Journal article (revised from a 1958 Venice congress paper)
- **Key points** (raw-verified):
  - Target: the "stock argument" that a fully informed observer "could know the outcome of my choices with certainty before I made them, and my impression of freedom in making these choices would therefore be an illusion, due to mere ignorance of the true state of affairs" (p. 31).
  - Determinism left open: the analysis "will involve us in no necessity to deny (or affirm) the physically-determinate character of brain processes" (p. 32). Against the quantum route: "we would be misguided and immoral to regard such indeterminacy as essential to the 'freedom' about which we have this 'firm subjective conviction'" (p. 31).
  - The logical core: a prediction "to be successful, must allow for any relevant effect its formulation and communication will have on my brain; but these effects could not all in general be calculated unless the prediction itself were already known, so that in general the exact calculation can never be completed" (p. 32). Popper 1950 is credited as "a similar logical situation", though "the present argument does not depend on the validity of Popper's thesis" (p. 32).
  - Certainty is standpoint-relative: "my brain could be physically determinate in operation, and yet unpredictable in principle to members of a community whose predictions are accessible to me" (p. 33); "It is people, not propositions, who are certain" (p. 33).
  - The agent who *knows* a correct secret prediction exists still validly believes his choice open: he "is not merely entitled to believe that his choice is undetermined, but that this is the only view of it that he may validly hold until he has made it" (p. 35).
  - "There are essentially just two parties in the situation, one looking on and one making a choice. The onlookers' view can logically be recommended as valid only to one of the two" (p. 36).
  - The thesis about felt freedom: "our 'firm subjective conviction of freedom' is not primarily a belief about the unpredictability of our brain-processes but is the entirely justifiable corollary of these peculiar logical facts" (p. 37); "To us, our choice is logically indeterminate, until we make it. For us, choosing is not something to be observed or predicted, but to be done" (p. 37).
  - Agent and spectator: "two complementary stories, one validly believable only from the standpoint of detached onlookers, which the agent would be wrong to try to believe until after his action, the other validly believable from the standpoint of the agent" (pp. 38–39); "Past and future have an asymmetric logic for an agent" (p. 38, note).
  - Calibration in his own voice: "To discover that nothing physically odd need accompany a free action, though we have no way as yet of knowing whether it does, undoubtedly disposes of a potential 'mystery'" (p. 40).
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral on Tenets 1–4 by design; a rival to any use of felt openness as evidence for indeterminacy.

### Popper, "Indeterminism in Quantum Physics and in Classical Physics", *BJPS* 1(2): 117–133; 1(3): 173–195 (1950)
- **URL**: https://archive.org/details/british-journal-for-the-philosophy-of-science_1950-11_1_3 (Part II OCR read, pp. 173–190) and `..._1950-08_1_2` (Part I).
- **Key points** (raw-verified; the OCR renders "(II)" as "(IL)"): three arguments that a predictor cannot predict itself, "(I) as a variant of the so-called 'paradox of Tristram Shandy'; (II) as a physical application of so-called 'Goedelian sentences'; and (III) as a utilisation of what I usually call the 'Oedipus effect'" (p. 173); the Oedipus effect is "The effect of a prediction (or a similar piece of information) upon the events or objects to which the prediction refers" (pp. 187–188); "we may be faced with an insurmountable difficulty in the way of all self-prediction. I believe that this is indeed the case" (p. 189). Part I p. 129 introduces the "one-way membrane" a predictor would need. Rummens & Cuypers judge the Gödel argument answerable and read Popper as conceding his case is "not fully convincing" at p. 193 (not verified here).

### Rummens & Cuypers, "Determinism and the Paradox of Predictability", *Erkenntnis* 72(2): 233–249 (2010)
- **URL**: https://rd.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10670-009-9199-1.pdf (open access; read in full).
- **Key points** (verified in the PDF text): "even in a deterministic universe, there are fundamental, non-epistemic limitations on the ability of one subsystem embedded in the universe to predict the future behaviour of other subsystems" (abstract); the structure "does not ... depend on some supposedly unique ontological characteristics of cognitive events, or on the presence of some special kind of free will" and "could, for instance, easily be programmed into a computer" (p. 237). MacKay "tried to use the paradox as the starting point for a compatibilist theory"; Landsberg & Evans (1970), Evans & Landsberg (1972) and Grünbaum (1971) "have convincingly shown that MacKay ultimately failed in developing a plausible compatibilist position" (pp. 245–246). Footnote 12: his notion of truth demands predictions be "revelation-robust as well as belief-robust", which "begs all the relevant questions regarding compatibilism" (p. 246). The general verdict: "(embedded) unpredictability is neither a sufficient nor a necessary condition for human freedom" (p. 246).
- **Tenet alignment**: Compatibilist authors. Their "neither a sufficient nor a necessary condition" is the line the fold should borrow, since the Map agrees with it from the other side.

## Answers to the Questions Set

**(a) Planck.** The will is causally determined (pp. 157–158, 167) and the subject cannot apply the causal method to its own present act (pp. 161–164). He does not conclude that felt freedom is an artefact of defective understanding; he calls that reading "entirely mistaken" (p. 163) and relocates freedom to the agent's standpoint (p. 165). The accurate label is two-standpoint determinism, kept consistent by the a priori impossibility of self-observation (p. 164). "Epistemic rather than modal" is fair to p. 163; "illusion" is unfair to pp. 163–165 and the fold must not say it.

**(b) MacKay.** For any agent A and prediction P of A's choice, P cannot be both correct and validly believed by A beforehand, because A's believing P is a brain event P did not include (p. 37). Determinism is granted for argument and left undecided (p. 32). The conclusion is two "complementary stories", onlooker and agent, checkable against each other only in retrospect (pp. 38–39). Felt freedom is "the entirely justifiable corollary" of these facts, "not primarily a belief about the unpredictability of our brain-processes" (p. 37).

**(c) Uptake.** Popper 1950 is the acknowledged ancestor (p. 32). The standing verdict (Rummens & Cuypers 2010, p. 246; Ismael 2016 and Gijsbers 2021 per the prior note) is that the self-prediction limit is real, determinism-compatible, machine-instantiable, and "neither a sufficient nor a necessary condition for human freedom", and that MacKay's compatibilist theory built on it failed. So the point is compatibilist-friendly only in the weak sense that determinism does not entail self-predictability. For Horn 3 MacKay is neither rival nor ally on the metaphysics (neutral on determinism, hostile to the quantum route, p. 31); he is a rival to one evidential move, the use of felt openness as a datum favouring indeterminacy.

**(d) Map-bearing.** Planck and MacKay explain felt openness without indeterminacy, and MacKay shows it survives the agent's knowledge that a correct prediction exists (p. 35). Three consequences:

1. **L113's reply survives in its effort half only.** The opponent's paragraph is framed as "felt openness" and the Map's reply as the systematic pattern of *effort* (difficulty-tracking, depletion, distinct circuits). The self-observation limit concerns what the agent can know or validly believe about the outcome, not what it costs to reach it, so it predicts none of the three. The reply is sound about effort and empty about openness; the fold should say so. L54 (Horn 1) already pivots from openness to effort and needs no change. [[phenomenology-of-choice-and-volition]] L60 and L165 still read felt openness as matching collapse; that reading is now known to be non-discriminating (flag for a later pass; out of scope).
2. **What would discriminate.** Nothing phenomenological: under Horn 3 the agent is also an embedded predictor and the limit binds it too. The only discriminator the Map can name is the conditional signature in [[apex/born-preserving-causal-efficacy]] §Where a Selection Could Show, which is statistical and unrun. The honest sentence is that Planck is unanswered on openness and the Map's exposure lies elsewhere.
3. **Tenet 5** constrains the Map's own reasoning: it bars preferring Horn 3 for its richness and bars dismissing Planck as deflationary; it does not license the opposite move, since it says simplicity is unreliable under incomplete knowledge, not that complexity is favoured. The Map keeps Horn 3 on its other anchors (effort, the argument from reason, willed-versus-instructed neural data, the mixed horn's failure) while openness leaves the list. Nothing here concedes epiphenomenalism: openness may be veridical under Horn 3; its presence is simply predicted either way.

The indexical reply on [[first-quantum-free-will-wave-1927-1940]] L83 answers Schrödinger's 1936 dilemma, that a selection leaving the statistics untouched must say what distinguishes it from chance (which outcome a subject actualises, per trial). It does not touch Planck, whose argument concerns what the agent can know of its own causes; the fold must not present it as if it did.
