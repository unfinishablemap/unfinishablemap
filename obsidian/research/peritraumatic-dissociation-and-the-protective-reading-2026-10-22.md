## Executive Summary

Peritraumatic dissociation means depersonalisation, derealisation, altered time sense, numbing or amnesia during or just after a traumatic event. It is the best-studied test of whether trauma-linked dissociation protects the person who has it. The answer depends on the timescale, and the literature itself states the timescale distinction: "Many authors have argued that the immediate effects of peritraumatic dissociation are adaptive, i.e., protect the individual from intense emotional states such as helplessness, horror, and fear, but that in the long-run, peritraumatic dissociation increases the risk of general psychopathology and of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in particular" (Lensvelt-Mulders et al. 2008).

1. **Long run.** Dissociation at the time of trauma marks a *worse* course, not a better one. Pooled correlations with later PTSD symptoms are r = .35 across 68 studies (Ozer et al. 2003), r = .36 across 35 (Breh & Seidler 2007) and .401 across 59 studies and 16,547 participants (Lensvelt-Mulders et al. 2008). In youth the figure is r = .17 (k = 5; Memarzia et al. 2021).
2. **Independence.** Under multivariate control much of that predictive power goes. It passes to *persistent* dissociation (Briere et al. 2005; Murray et al. 2002; Panasetis & Bryant 2003) and to early symptoms (Marshall & Schell 2002; van der Velden & Wittmann 2008). It does not go everywhere: Werner & Griffin (2012) kept a unique 4%.
   - *[Cross-review correction, 2026-10-22: the grouping above overstates two of its citations. **Murray et al. 2002** does not show predictive power passing to persistent dissociation under control. Its abstract says "All measures of dissociation, particularly persistent dissociation 4 weeks after the accident, predicted chronic PTSD severity", and "Dissociative symptoms predicted subsequent PTSD over and above the other PTSD symptom clusters". It does not say which dissociation measure carried the over-and-above result. Murray therefore supports "persistent dissociation predicts better" (see "The Timescale Analysis") and offers some evidence of independent prediction. It is not evidence that peritraumatic dissociation loses its value under control. **Panasetis & Bryant 2003** is likewise a comparison of association strength ("Persistent dissociation was more strongly associated with ASD severity and intrusive symptoms than peritraumatic dissociation"), not a multivariate pass-off. Of the three, only Briere et al. 2005 reports the pass-off. On the "everywhere" point, van der Velden & Wittmann found 6 of their 17 studies positive, 3 strongly ("Only 3 of the 6 studies with positive results reported a strong independent predictive value of PD"), and Werner & Griffin's 4% is from the 62 of 92 participants who were followed up. The article cites neither Murray nor Panasetis & Bryant, and does not rest on this grouping.]*
3. **The moment.** The only direct test of the momentary version found none. *[Cross-review 2026-10-22: the claim to be the only one is false as worded. A forward-citation search of Fikretoglu 2006 (27 citing works in Europe PMC) found two laboratory tests that point the same way. In Danböck et al. 2023 (*Behav Res Ther* 164:104289; 69 healthy women, aversive film and pain), "Dissociation was linked to higher self-reported negative-affect". In Danböck et al. 2024 (*J Psychopathol Clin Sci* 133(1):76–89; 71 PTSD patients, preregistered trauma-script imagery), dissociation was "linked to higher self-reported negative affect responses to trauma scripts", and the results "did not confirm posttraumatic dissociation-induced emotional numbing". Fikretoglu remains the one study of real trauma found that tests the hypothesis directly.]* On felt distress, Fikretoglu et al. (2006; N = 1,026) found that high dissociators almost never report low distress, and they read dissociation as a by-product of high distress. Panic during the event accounts for 47% of the variance in peritraumatic dissociation (Bryant & Panasetis 2005). Some autonomic dampening may occur (Griffin et al. 1997; Holmes et al. 2004), but the Map already records that no reliable autonomic signature exists (Beutler et al. 2022, cited on [[dissociative-subtype-of-ptsd]]).
4. **Measurement.** Almost all of this rests on retrospective self-report. That report drifts with current symptoms (Marshall & Schell 2002; Thompson-Hollands et al. 2021: >40% of patients showed reliable change in recalled dissociation for the *same* event). The state itself "may not be accessible in the moment" (van der Hart et al. 2008).

**Consequence for the Map.** The failure of the momentary-protection reading is symmetric. The interface reading's "the mind protecting itself" and the rival defence cascade's "opioid analgesia functions on a subjective level to mitigate the intensity of subjective fear" (Kozlowska et al. 2015) are both claims about the moment, and the available evidence supports neither. The subtype page's verdict, that the trauma-selectivity point goes to the rival, is untouched, and the interface reading regains nothing. What changes is the datum both readings were explaining: "protective" shrinks to an untested claim about the moment, attached to a state the person can only report afterwards.

## Key Sources

### A. The predictive association

**Ozer, Best, Lipsey & Weiss (2003)**, *Psychological Bulletin*. Meta-analysis of 68 studies and seven predictors of PTSD or its symptoms.
- Key points:
  - All seven predictors were significant. The pre-trauma predictors were smallest and "peritraumatic dissociation the largest (weighted r = .35)".
  - Conclusion: "peritraumatic psychological processes, not prior characteristics, are the strongest predictors of PTSD".
- The number of studies and participants behind the dissociation estimate, and its moderators, were **not seen**.
- van der Hart et al. (2008) report that Ozer's study found the dissociation studies highly heterogeneous. This is a secondary report, and the source word is hyphenated across a line break, so paraphrase it.
- Tenet alignment: neutral.

**Breh & Seidler (2007)**, *J Trauma Dissociation*. Meta-analysis of 35 studies.
- Mean r = 0.36. "The average effect size in the quasi-prospective studies was r=0.34".
- They call it "a moderate risk factor" and ask whether peritraumatic dissociation is "a predictor, a symptom, or something else entirely".

**Lensvelt-Mulders et al. (2008)**, *Clinical Psychology Review*. Full text read. Multilevel meta-regression of 59 studies (83 conditions) and "the results of 16,547 participants".
- **Effect size.** "The standardized correlation between peritraumatic dissociation and PTS across studies was .401" (95% CI .364–.438).
- **Heterogeneity.** It was explained by method rather than theory: time elapsed (β = .633), design, sample type and study type. The same paper also says "Our combined studies were too heterogeneous in their outcomes to compute an overall effect size or grand mean". **Do not present .401 as a settled grand mean.**
- **Time elapsed.** "The more time elapsed between these events and the first measurement, the higher the correlation between peritraumatic dissociation and PTS". The authors suggest that in clinical samples "the past is defined in the light of the present situation".
- **Against the pure-artefact reading.** "longitudinal studies reported a stronger positive relation between peritraumatic dissociation and PTS than the retrospective studies". They conclude the relation "is not a mere artefact of biased self-reports".
- **Causation.** "no conclusions could be drawn as to causal relations between peritraumatic dissociation and PTS".
- **Coding caveat.** In their code sheet, "longitudinal" includes designs whose first measurement was itself retrospective (study type 3). A "prospective" study here rarely means measurement during the event.
- Tenet alignment: neutral. This is the source for the timescale framing quoted in the Executive Summary.

**Marmar et al. (1994)**, *AJP*. 251 male Vietnam theatre veterans from the National Vietnam Veterans Readjustment Study, using the PDEQ rater version.
- Dissociation was assessed from "retrospective reports of dissociation during the most disturbing combat trauma events".
- It had incremental value over war-zone exposure and general dissociative tendencies: "the greater the dissociation during traumatic stress exposure, the greater the likelihood of meeting criteria for current PTSD".
- This is the founding validation of the field's main instrument.

**Memarzia, Walker & Meiser-Stedman (2021)**, *J Affect Disord*. Youth meta-analysis of 32 studies.
- "dissociation (k = 5; r = 0.17, 95% CI=0.03-0.29)" against subjective threat at r = 0.37 (k = 28).
- Limitation stated: peritraumatic processes were commonly assessed with single items.
- This is the only post-2015 meta-analysis found in this run.

**Tortella-Feliu et al. (2019)**, umbrella review. Abstract only.
- The peritraumatic factors with "convincing or highly suggestive evidence" are cumulative exposure, trauma severity and being trapped in an earthquake. "Data from prospective studies were less conclusive."
- **Peritraumatic dissociation's own grade was not seen.** Do not cite this review for a grade on dissociation.

### B. What survives multivariate control

**Briere, Scott & Weathers (2005)**, *AJP*. Abstract only. Two samples: 52 community participants and 386 from the general population, all trauma-exposed.
- Univariately, peritraumatic dissociation, persistent dissociation, peritraumatic distress and generalised dissociation all related to PTSD.
- Multivariately, "PTSD status was no longer related to peritraumatic dissociation once other variables (especially persistent and generalized dissociation) were taken into account". By contrast, "persistent dissociation was a strong predictor at univariate and multivariate levels".
- Their reading: "it is less what happens at the time of a trauma (e.g., disrupted encoding) that predicts PTSD than what occurs thereafter (i.e., persistent avoidance)".
- "Persistent dissociation" is their term for trauma-specific dissociation that begins during or after the event and continues to the time of assessment.
- **Design caveat.** The abstract describes a single assessment, so peritraumatic dissociation was recalled. The multivariate null is itself a cross-sectional, retrospective result.

**Murray, Ehlers & Mayou (2002)**, *BJPsych*. Two prospective road-traffic samples (27 and 176).
- All dissociation measures predicted PTSD severity at 6 months, persistent dissociation at 4 weeks most strongly.
- "Persistent dissociation and rumination 4 weeks after trauma are more useful in identifying those patients who are likely to develop chronic PTSD than initial reactions".

**Panasetis & Bryant (2003)**, *J Trauma Stress*. 53 civilian survivors. Persistent dissociation was more strongly associated with acute stress disorder severity and intrusions than peritraumatic dissociation. Paraphrase only.

**van der Velden & Wittmann (2008)**, *CPR*. Systematic review of 17 prospective type-I trauma studies, all assessing peritraumatic dissociation within one month and controlling for mental health at that time.
- "The majority of the 17 identified studies showed no or only weak indications of an independent predictive value of PD for PTSD symptomatology following type I traumas."
- "Only 3 of the 6 studies with positive results reported a strong independent predictive value of PD."

**Wittmann, Moergeli & Schnyder (2006)**, *J Trauma Stress*. 214 accident victims, PDEQ at 5 days. "Peritraumatic dissociation explained less than 3% of variance." Initial re-experiencing and hyperarousal predicted better.

**Marshall & Schell (2002)**, *J Abnorm Psychol*. Cross-lagged panel study of community-violence survivors.
- "Recollections of peritraumatic dissociation assessed within days of exposure differed from recollections measured at 3- and 12-month follow-up interviews".
- "Baseline recollections of peritraumatic dissociation were not predictive of follow-up PTSD symptom severity after controlling for baseline PTSD symptom severity."
- The authors call this inconsistent with the view that peritraumatic dissociation *leads to* worse PTSD.

**Werner & Griffin (2012)**, *J Trauma Stress*. Full text read. 92 female assault survivors (62 at follow-up).
- Peritraumatic and follow-up persistent dissociation each added unique variance: "contributing 4% (p = .05) and 8% (p = .008) of the variance, respectively".
- **This is a positive independent result for peritraumatic dissociation. Include it; do not overstate the null.**

**Lebois et al. (2022)**, *AJP*. AURORA cohort. Already cited on the subtype page, for the vmPFC finding only.
- 1,464 self-reports; 145 scanned at 2 weeks. Self-reported and brain-based persistent derealisation at 2 weeks predicted worse 3-month posttraumatic stress, distinct from childhood maltreatment and current symptoms.
- Conclusion: "persistent derealization is both an early psychological and biological marker of worse later psychiatric outcomes".
- **Use for the article.** The very vmPFC signature the subtype page reads as passive-defence overmodulation is, two weeks out, a risk marker. The rival, too, has to locate any protection in the moment.

**Post-2020 prospective work, measured close to the event**:
- **Dokkedahl & Lahav (2024)** (full text): 200 Israeli civilians completed the PDEQ during the 12-day rocket campaign of 10–21 May 2021 (T1) and again 1–2 months later (T2).
  - Perceived threat moderated the dissociation–PTSD link for every cluster except avoidance: "detriments of PD are evident when the trauma is appraised as being highly threatening".
  - This is one of the few designs in which "peritraumatic" means during an ongoing threat.
- **Dokkedahl & Lahav (2023)**: 226 civilians from the same campaign. Tonic immobility and peritraumatic dissociation mediated perceived threat → PTSD symptoms. Paraphrase only.
- **Figueroa et al. (2024)**: 387 emergency-department adults within 72 hours of trauma. Peritraumatic dissociation partially mediated lifetime trauma → 1-month PTSD: "the mediation was partial, accounting for 17.9% of the lifetime trauma exposure count's total effect".
- **Birmes et al. (2003)**: 35 assault victims, PDEQ within 24 hours. Peritraumatic dissociation plus acute stress symptoms explained 33% of variance at 3 months. Paraphrase only.

### C. Direct tests of the momentary claim

**Fikretoglu et al. (2006)**, *J Nerv Ment Dis*. 709 police officers and 317 peer-nominated civilians. **This is the only study found that names and tests the momentary-protection hypothesis.** *[Cross-review 2026-10-22: scope it to studies of real trauma. Danböck et al. 2024 tested "posttraumatic dissociation-induced emotional numbing" in 71 PTSD patients with trauma scripts and did not confirm it, and Danböck et al. 2023 induced dissociation in the laboratory. Both found more negative affect with more dissociation. See Executive Summary item 3.]* It asked "whether peritraumatic dissociation serves to protect trauma-exposed individuals from experiencing high levels of peritraumatic distress or is an epiphenomenon" of it.
- Result: an overall moderate-to-strong linear relation between distress and dissociation. "Among those with high levels of dissociation, very few reported low levels of distress."
- Conclusion: "Our results do not provide support for the idea that dissociation protects individuals from experiencing high levels of distress at the time of the trauma but rather suggest that dissociation is an epiphenomenon of high levels of distress observed in a subset of individuals".
- **Caveats.** Cross-sectional; both distress and dissociation are retrospective self-reports, so they share method variance.
- **Term trap**: see Guards.

**Fikretoglu et al. (2007)**, same sample. Physical and cognitive panic symptoms completely mediated the relation between fear, helplessness and horror and dissociation in civilians, and partially mediated it in police. Paraphrase only.

**Bryant & Panasetis (2005)**, *Br J Clin Psychol*. 51 civilian survivors: "panic reactions during the trauma accounted for nearly half of the variance (47%) of peritraumatic dissociation".

**Griffin, Resick & Mechanic (1997)**, *AJP*. Full text read. 85 rape survivors, assessed in the laboratory within 2 weeks with an 8-item Peritraumatic Dissociation Index modified from the PDEQ rater version. This was an extreme-groups design: high n = 16, low n = 31, moderate n = 38 excluded.
- High dissociators met PTSD symptom criteria at 94% (15/16), against 55% (17/31) of low dissociators.
- "there was a suppression of autonomic physiological responses in the high dissociation group". Among PTSD-positive subjects, heart rate was lower during the trauma narrative (72.7 vs 80.8 bpm) and skin-conductance responses were fewer.
- Their subjective distress went the other way: Subjective Units of Distress were *higher* in the high group, and "these subjects' self-report ratings of distress were significantly higher than their physiological measures of arousal".
- The authors' own verdict: "peritraumatic dissociation may be a risk factor for the development of PTSD rather than an adaptive coping mechanism as previously suggested".
- **Scope.** This is a lab recounting two weeks later, not the peritraumatic moment. The dampening is autonomic, not felt. The sample is small. Beutler et al. 2022 (already on the Map) found no reliable cross-study autonomic trend.

**Holmes, Brewin & Hennessy (2004)**, *J Exp Psychol Gen*. Trauma-film analogue in non-clinical participants: "Increases in dissociation and decreases in heart rate during the film were also associated with later intrusions." This is in-the-moment measurement under analogue conditions. Dissociation and heart-rate slowing co-occur, and both predict more intrusions, not fewer.

**Koopman, Classen & Spiegel (1996)**, *J Trauma Stress*. 187 survivors of the 1991 Oakland/Berkeley firestorm. Dissociative symptoms in the immediate aftermath were related to "trying to get closer to the fire and going into blocked-off areas and crossing police barricades".
- This is a single cross-sectional self-report datum against reading the momentary state as protective *in the survival sense*.
- It cuts against a withdrawing subject's self-protection as much as against a defence cascade. Treat it as suggestive only.

### D. The defence-cascade bridge (the rival's own momentary claim)

**Kozlowska et al. (2015)**, *Harv Rev Psychiatry*. Full text read. The source the subtype page cites for the cascade.
- Tonic immobility is "a response to inescapable threat, or a strategy of last resort, when active defense responses have failed".
- On the subjective level, "it is probable that, during the passive defense responses, opioid analgesia functions on a subjective level to mitigate the intensity of subjective fear". **This is the rival's own momentary-protection claim, and it is the claim Fikretoglu's null bears on.**
- "tonic immobility, peritraumatic dissociation, and dissociative PTSD may be mediated by a shared neural network".
- Kozlowska et al. note that tonic immobility includes derealisation and depersonalisation and is "sometimes—but not always" counted among peritraumatic reactions.

**Möller, Söndergaard & Helström (2017)**, *Acta Obstet Gynecol Scand*. 298 women seen within a month of sexual assault, 189 followed to 6 months.
- "70% reported significant tonic immobility and 48% reported extreme tonic immobility".
- Tonic immobility predicted PTSD "(OR 2.75; 95% CI 1.50-5.03, p = 0.001)" and severe depression (OR 3.42).
- So the passive end of the cascade, like peritraumatic dissociation, is a long-run risk marker.
- **Do not equate tonic immobility with depersonalisation.** It is a motor state that often includes dissociative experience.

### E. Measurement: retrospective self-report

**Candel & Merckelbach (2004)**, *Compr Psychiatry*. Critical review.
- The literature relies "heavily … on retrospective reports" (paraphrase; the verbatim string is "studies in this area heavily rely on retrospective reports of dissociative reactions during the trauma").
- "people in general and PTSD patients in particular find it difficult to give accurate descriptions of past emotional states". They name forgetting, attribution and malingering.

**Thompson-Hollands, Marx, Lee & Sloan (2021)**, *Psychol Trauma*. Full text read. 126 treatment-seeking adults with PTSD rated the PDEQ for the *same index event* six times over 60 weeks.
- "> 40% of participants experiencing reliable change from baseline at least once during the study". Scores fell overall and tracked current CAPS-5, BDI-II and PCL-5 scores.
- "Our findings suggest the PDEQ does not provide temporally stable assessment of peritraumatic dissociation".
- In plain terms: as the disorder improves, people remember having dissociated less.

**van der Hart et al. (2008)**, *J Trauma Dissociation*. Full text read. On why measurement during the event is hard: "The subjective experience of PD may not be accessible in the moment and may only be understood or recognized after the immediate danger has passed". They attribute the point to Ozer et al. 2003; cite it as van der Hart et al.'s statement.
- The same review found no statistical difference between cross-sectional and longitudinal results. Paraphrase: the sentence is broken by a page header.

**Bedard-Gilligan & Zoellner (2012)**, *Memory*. 16 studies. The dissociation–fragmentation link holds for self-rated fragmentation but not for rater-coded or computer-coded fragmentation: "initial evidence points more towards a strong self-reported association between constructs that is not supported on more objective fragmentation coding". This is a self-report/objective split of the kind [[metacognitive-access-versus-retention]] tracks.

**Construct heterogeneity**:
- Thompson-Hollands, Jun & Sloan (2017) describe peritraumatic dissociation as "a complex array of reactions to trauma, including depersonalization, derealization, and emotional numbness".
- van der Hart et al. (2008, Table 5) show that instruments disagree on whether amnesia, numbing and reduced awareness count.
- Bryant (2007): "The evidence challenges the notion that a linear relationship exists between dissociation and psychiatric morbidity", and "Future research should abandon the global construct of dissociation".
- Bryant et al. (2011), on acute stress disorder in DSM-5: "the current emphasis on dissociation may be overly restrictive".

## The Timescale Analysis

"Protective" can mean four things. The table sets out what each would need and what was found.

| Sense of "protective" | What would show it | Evidence found | Status | Who claimed it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Against later disorder (long run) | Dissociators do better later | Positive correlation with later PTSD (r ≈ .35–.40 adults; .17 youth); tonic immobility OR 2.75 | **Fails** on the pooled evidence. *[Cross-review 2026-10-22: "nobody defends it" was false. Greene 2018 (J Anxiety Disord 57:1–6) took twice-daily experience-sampling reports from 96 civilians during the 2014 Israel–Gaza conflict. Dissociative reactivity predicted PTSS along an inverted U and posttraumatic growth linearly, and the author concludes that peritraumatic dissociation "may be both adaptive and maladaptive".]* | Neither reading |
| 2. Relief of felt distress in the moment | High dissociators report less peritraumatic distress | Fikretoglu 2006 null (N = 1,026); panic explains 47% of the variance (Bryant & Panasetis 2005); higher SUDS in Griffin's high group | **No support on retrospective report** | Interface reading ("withdrawing felt ownership of threatening experience"); rival (Kozlowska: opioid analgesia mitigating "subjective fear"); the trauma literature's "many authors" |
| 3. Autonomic dampening in the moment | Lower arousal during the event or its recall | Griffin 1997 (lab, 2 weeks, n = 16); Holmes 2004 (film analogue); against both, Beutler 2022: no reliable trend | **Mixed and small**; where present, it predicts more intrusions | Rival (hypoarousal of passive defence) |
| 4. Survival function (evolutionary) | Better physical outcomes under inescapable threat | Not testable with these designs; Koopman 1996 is weakly against | **Untested in humans** | Rival (defence cascade); the interface reading does not claim it |

**Reading for the article.** Sense 1 is settled against, and neither Map reading needs it, because both readings frame protection as momentary. Sense 2 is the sense the flagship's wording ("unbearable", "threatening experience") and Kozlowska's "subjective fear" both use. It is the sense with a direct test, and the test is null. Sense 3 is physiology, not experience, and is weak. Sense 4 belongs to the rival alone and is not touched. So for both readings, "protective" retreats to an untested evolutionary claim (sense 4) or to a momentary felt state (sense 2) that the evidence does not show and that the person can only report afterwards.

Persistent dissociation weeks later is the better predictor (Briere; Murray; Panasetis & Bryant; Lebois). That matters because the DSM-5 subtype the Map discusses is *persistent or recurrent* depersonalisation, the timescale at which dissociation is most clearly pathological. Bryant's chapter (2009/2010, unread; reported by Werner & Griffin 2012) argues "most peritraumatic dissociation is adaptive while persisting dissociation is pathological". This is the cleanest statement of the two-timescale view. Cite it only through Werner & Griffin.

## Major Positions

### Momentarily adaptive, costly later (the two-timescale view)
- **Proponents**: Lensvelt-Mulders et al. (2008) attribute it to "many authors": Bennet & Hacker 2003; Bremner 1997; Briere et al. 2005; van der Kolk, van der Hart & Marmar 1996; Marmar, Weiss & Metzler 1998. Griffin et al. (1997) attribute it to Koopman et al.: "Koopman et al. argued that dissociation may be adaptive during and immediately following a traumatic event". Bryant 2009 holds it too (via Werner & Griffin). Dokkedahl & Lahav (2024) restate it, citing Bryant 2009: the immediate effects may be adaptive because they let the person tolerate extreme stress (paraphrase only).
- **Core claim**: dissociation dulls overwhelming affect now, but impairs processing and so raises later risk.
- **Relation to tenets**: neutral. Both Map readings can adopt it, so it adjudicates nothing between them.

### By-product of overwhelming arousal (no protective function)
- **Proponents**: Fikretoglu et al. 2006 and 2007; Bryant & Panasetis 2005 (dissociation as a panic correlate). Griffin et al. 1997 lean this way.
- **Core claim**: dissociation accompanies extreme distress and panic and reflects it. It does not relieve it.
- **Relation to tenets**: it removes the "protective" half of the datum for *both* readings. A brain-only arousal account and an interface account can each say that overwhelming input disrupts coupling. The finding favours neither.

### Integrative failure (Janet's line)
- **Proponents**: as reported by van der Hart et al. (2008), Janet held that peritraumatic dissociative reactions are "a manifestation of acute integrative failure (rather than a survival coping strategy)". Werner & Griffin (2012) set Breuer & Freud's "psychological protective mechanism" against Janet's "psychological deficit".
- **Relation to tenets**: an integrative-failure reading fits a "channel disrupted" description better than a "subject withdrawing" one. It still does not discriminate between production and interface readings.

### Artefact of retrospective report
- **Proponents**: Candel & Merckelbach 2004; Marshall & Schell 2002; Thompson-Hollands et al. 2021 (instability).
- **Opponents**: Lensvelt-Mulders et al. 2008 ("not a mere artefact of biased self-reports", because longitudinal studies show *stronger* relations).
- **Current state**: the bivariate association is robust. Its independence and its causal reading are not.

## Key Debates

### Risk factor or correlate?
- **Sides**: Ozer 2003 and Breh & Seidler 2007 (risk factor) against Briere 2005, van der Velden & Wittmann 2008 and Marshall & Schell 2002 (non-independent correlate). Werner & Griffin 2012 find a small unique contribution.
- **State**: unresolved. The weight of prospective work says peritraumatic dissociation adds little once early symptoms or persistent dissociation are known.

### Protective in the moment?
- **Sides**: the two-timescale view, against Fikretoglu's by-product reading.
- **State**: one direct test, cross-sectional and retrospective, and negative. No study found that measured felt distress *during* dissociation and compared it with non-dissociators under the same threat. *[Cross-review 2026-10-22: two laboratory studies come close, with a shared aversive stimulus and affect rated per condition or per script: Danböck et al. 2023 and 2024. Both are negative for relief. Neither is a real trauma.]*

### Can the state be measured at all?
- **Sides**: Candel & Merckelbach and Thompson-Hollands (unstable), against Lensvelt-Mulders (robust enough).
- **State**: van der Hart et al. concede that the experience may be inaccessible in the moment. Dokkedahl & Lahav (2024) are the closest to real-time measurement. *[Cross-review 2026-10-22: Greene 2018, twice-daily phone reports through 30 days of the 2014 conflict, is closer.]*

## Relation to Site Perspective (for the article)

**Calibration rule (binding, from the task):**
- The page must **not** claw back the subtype page's verdict that the trauma-selectivity point goes to the rival.
- If the momentary-protection reading fails, it fails for **both** the interface reading and the rival's defence cascade, and **neither gains**.
- Dualism stays at **compatible with, not evidence for**.
- Never concede epiphenomenalism.

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]**: compatible with, not evidence for. The literature constrains a datum both readings explain and decides nothing about irreducibility. Two clauses are honest:
- The subtype page's comparative verdict stands. The rival explains why dissociation arises under inescapable threat and through which circuitry. The peritraumatic data make dissociation look triggered by panic and extreme distress, which fits a triggered mechanism on either reading.
- The protective half of the datum is weaker than both pages assumed. That removes an explanandum; it does not return a point to the interface reading.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]**: "the mind protecting itself" is an agentive gloss, and the literature finds no sign of purposive control in the moment. Dissociation tracks panic and distress. This counts against the *purposive-withdrawal* gloss, not against mental causation in general. The rival's "purposiveness inherited from evolution" equally lacks momentary evidence of relief.

Fikretoglu's "epiphenomenon" is a psychometric term meaning a by-product of distress. It says nothing about whether conscious states are causally idle, and the article must not let it drift into epiphenomenalism. The strongest correlate in these data is felt distress itself, and that fact is silent on mental causation in both directions. Do not build a pro-dualist argument on it either.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]]**, **[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]]**: not engaged.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]**: "adaptive now, costly later" is a tidy story, and both readings borrowed it. The data complicate it: the predictive power is not independent, recall is unstable, and the one momentary test is null. The complication transfers no credit to either reading.

**The introspection connection** (the Map's own angle). Every reading of "protective in the moment" needs a fact about what the trauma felt like *during* the trauma. The field's own reviewers say that fact may be inaccessible at the time (van der Hart et al. 2008), and its report is rebuilt later under the influence of the present (Lensvelt-Mulders; Marshall & Schell; Thompson-Hollands). This is a concrete clinical instance of [[inaccessible-past]] (memory reconstructs, and the present overwrites) and of the retrospective-only detection in [[confabulation-void]].

The point cuts against the Map as much as for it: first-person evidence is weakest exactly where the Map's interface gloss placed a phenomenal claim. The Map should state it as a constraint on its own evidence, in line with [[evidential-status-discipline]].

## Guards for the Article Writer

**Author stances (do not invert):**
- **Lensvelt-Mulders et al. 2008** *defend* the association against the artefact critique ("not a mere artefact of biased self-reports"). They are not critics of peritraumatic dissociation. Two of their authors (van der Hart, Steele) are structural-dissociation theorists.
- **Briere et al. 2005** found peritraumatic dissociation non-independent. They are not against dissociation as a predictor: persistent dissociation was their strong predictor. Lensvelt-Mulders list them among holders of the momentarily-adaptive view. That attribution has not been checked in Briere's text, so do not quote Briere for "adaptive".
- **Griffin et al. 1997** treat peritraumatic dissociation as a *risk factor* and explicitly *not* "an adaptive coping mechanism". They do suggest it is "a coping strategy for severe trauma" with maladaptive consequences. Keep both halves.
- **Werner & Griffin 2012** found a positive unique contribution. They support the predictive value of both peritraumatic and persistent dissociation.
- **Fikretoglu et al. 2006** are from Marmar's group (the PDEQ's authors). Their null on protection is an insider result.
- **Marshall & Schell 2002** replicate the correlation. They dispute only the causal direction.
- **Kozlowska et al. 2015** are clinicians presenting the cascade as a management framework. The opioid-relief sentence is their hypothesis ("it is probable"), not a finding.
- **van der Velden & Wittmann 2008** do not deny the bivariate association.

## Gaps in Research

- **Ozer 2003 full text**: not read. The k and N behind r = .35 and the moderators (time since trauma, assessment method) are unverified. Secondary hints exist (a flashcard site; van der Hart's "quite heterogeneous") but are not citable.
- **Briere 2005 full text**: not read. Sample details beyond the abstract, the measures, and whether the paper itself endorses the momentarily-adaptive view are unseen.
- **No adult meta-analysis after 2008 was found.** Tortella-Feliu 2019's grade for dissociation is unseen. An extended search for 2015–2026 adult meta-analyses would be worthwhile.
- **No study was found that measures felt distress during dissociation in real time** against non-dissociators under the same threat. Fikretoglu is retrospective. *[Cross-review 2026-10-22: the laboratory analogue exists, in Danböck et al. 2023 and 2024 (see Executive Summary item 3). The real-trauma version is still missing.]* This is the experiment the momentary reading needs.
- **Peritraumatic analgesia** (the PDEQ analgesia item; Pitman-style naloxone-reversible analgesia in veterans) was not pursued. It is the one form of momentary relief with a plausible physiological test.
- **Defence-cascade survival claims in humans**: none were found or searched systematically.
- **Channel selectivity at the moment of trauma**: the peritraumatic literature does not measure whether dissociation spares "channels needed for ongoing function" (the flagship's wording). It neither supports nor refutes the *selective* half of the datum.
- **DSM-5 acute stress disorder**: whether dissociation was dropped as a *required* cluster was not checked at the DSM source. Only Bryant et al.'s 2011 proposal was read. Do not state the DSM-5 outcome without checking.
- **Bryant 2009/2010 chapter**: the year is unsettled (2009 in citations, 2010 in the British Library record), and the chapter was not read.
