## Executive Summary

Robert J. Howell's *subjective physicalism* holds that "the world is completely physical, but no objective theory—including physics—can completely describe the world": some physical states "must be undergone in order to be fully grasped" (Howell 2009, p. 315). He reached it by recasting Jackson's knowledge argument as an argument against *objectivism*, the thesis that an objective theory can be complete. On that reading the argument refutes "objective physicalism and objective dualism alike" (2007, p. 149). Howell raises the "form of dualism" charge against his own view (2009, p. 321). He answers it with an extensionalist metaphysics of properties and possible worlds. On that metaphysics the subjective "aspects" of physical properties are "not themselves properties" (p. 335), because they are intensionally individuated and inseparable from the physical properties they belong to. His reason for preferring this to dualism is mental causation and closure (2007, p. 171; 2009, p. 333). On the Map's analysis ([[#map-application|below]]), subjective physicalism meets all three conditions of the disguised-property-dualism diagnostic as written. It nonetheless sits at the boundary. It issues no promissory note, it states its costs, and its monist disavowal rests on an argued metaphysics rather than a promise of reduction. The verdict is `fold`: concepts is at cap, and the material belongs mainly in `concepts/objectivity-and-consciousness`. That page currently infers an ontological gap from the in-principle failure of objectivity, the step Howell's view exists to block. A shorter fold goes into the quiddity page, which also has an attribution defect to correct.

## Key Sources

### Howell, R. J. (2009). The ontology of subjective physicalism. *Noûs* 43(2), 315–345 — READ IN FULL
- **URL**: https://rjhjr.com//wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Ontology-Subjective-Physicalism.pdf (author-posted, published pagination)
- **Type**: Paper. The fullest open statement of the view; the book's chapter of the same title (pp. 101–122) develops it.
- **Key points**:
  - The thesis: "According to subjective physicalism, the world is completely physical, but no objective theory—including physics—can completely describe the world." Some physical states are subjective "in that those states must be undergone in order to be fully grasped" (p. 315).
  - **Two versions** (p. 316). *Inclusive* subjective physicalism: a complete physics refers to every property, but some properties cannot be fully grasped except subjectively. *Exclusive*: some physical properties "can be grasped only subjectively" and are "not identical with any property mentioned in a completed physics". The paper defends only the inclusive version; the exclusive one is developed in Howell (2008b), unread here.
  - **Objectivity condition** (p. 318): "An objective theory cannot require that one enter or be able to enter any token state of determinate type T in order to fully understand states of type T." Note 10 (p. 340): "subjectivity and objectivity are features of theories, points of view or perspectives; they are not features of things or properties."
  - **Physicalism defined** without epistemic elements: "physicalism is true iff everything metaphysically supervenes upon the physical" (p. 319). The narrow physical is defined negatively: "Something is physical iff it is fundamental, contingent, and is not phenomenal or intentional" (p. 320).
  - **Against the phenomenal concept strategy**: the subjective nature of experience "seems to predate phenomenal concepts". "That there is something it is like to experience conscious states is more fundamental than the fact that there is something peculiar about our knowledge of those states" (p. 316). On phenomenal concepts, "either phenomenal concepts are not explaining what needs explaining, or they are not themselves explicable" (p. 338). Note 65 says subjective physicalism "should agree with the dilemma that Chalmers (2006) poses for the phenomenal concept strategy".
  - **His own statement of the dualism charge**: "It is very tempting to view subjective physicalism as a form of dualism" (p. 321). He sets out "The Dualist's Dilemma": "Either Mary comes to grasp new properties when she leaves her room, in which case property dualism is true, or she doesn't, in which case she grasps everything there is to grasp using only physical descriptions" (p. 321).
  - **His reply**: the dilemma rests on optional metaphysics. "The subjective physicalist should adopt a fully extensionalist metaphysics that repudiates methods of individuating properties and possible worlds in terms of concepts or cognitive capacities" (p. 321). The presentation argument (Black, via Smart) needs *property intensionalism*. The conceivability argument needs *world intensionalism*, which he argues is self-refuting (pp. 330–331; Howell 2008a).
  - **Aspects**: what Mary grasps are aspects, which "are not themselves properties, but are instead part of the nature of properties that are not expressible by physical description" (p. 335). "Aspects are to properties as conceptual parts are to atoms"; "the subjective aspect cannot exist without the physical aspect and vice versa"; "aspects are intensionally individuated while properties are not" (all p. 335).
  - **Mental causation**: because aspects are inseparable, "it is not the case that such aspects could be removed without causal difference" (p. 336). Against the dualist, conceivability is "defeasible", and we "should suspect that our conceptions have let us down when they commit us to properties that do not integrate into a coherent worldview and that seem fated to causal impotence" (p. 333). One premise of the case against intensional properties is that "the complete causal story about the world should be able to be told in terms of properties that have objective individuation conditions" (p. 325).
  - **Type-B**: "by this definition subjective physicalism is a form of Type-B materialism" (p. 337), "distinct and more satisfying" than its phenomenal-concept rivals.
  - **The nearness to dualism, in his words**: "Ironically, in the end the subjective physicalist's closest friend is perhaps the property dualist." They share three claims. There is something it is like to instantiate certain states; this can be fully grasped only by instantiating them; objective sciences leave it out. They differ on whether physicalism can acknowledge all three, a difference with "its source in the darker wilds of ontology". "Subjective physicalism simply shows that the dualist's ontological commitments upstream of his dualism are optional" (p. 338).
  - **Declared costs**: "subjective physicalism does not put the mind body problem to rest, nor does it explain the subjective." A complete explanation would require eliminating the gap, and "Subjective physicalism denies that this is possible. We should not expect, because we cannot get, an objective explanation that captures all of the aspects of conscious experience." "Thus there is still an explanatory gap, but it is perhaps less threatening since it simply falls out of the nature of objective theorizing" (pp. 339–340). He adds a tu quoque: "Dualism itself cannot explain why only certain states are lawfully connected to the presence of qualia" (p. 339).
  - **Inclusive and exclusive converge**: for readers unconvinced that aspects are not properties, the exclusive view, on which "a complete physics would leave out 'subjective properties,' but such properties are physical nonetheless", will appeal; and "Once the notion of property is expanded in this way, it must be admitted that there is little to distinguish between inclusive and exclusive subjective physicalism" (p. 339).
- **Tenet alignment**: Conflicts with Dualism at the ontological step only. It agrees with the Map on the gap's permanence, the failure of objectivism and the failure of the phenomenal concept strategy. It conflicts with Bidirectional Interaction through its closure-based case against dualism.

### Howell, R. J. (2007). The knowledge argument and objectivity. *Philosophical Studies* 135, 145–177 — READ (abstract, §1, §11, notes)
- **URL**: https://rjhjr.com//wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Howell2007_Article_TheKnowledgeArgumentAndObjecti.pdf (Springer PDF, published pagination); DOI 10.1007/s11098-007-9077-0
- **Key points**:
  - All physicalist responses to Jackson that grant Mary an epistemic gain reduce to an "acquaintance response". That response violates the objectivity condition, so the argument shows that "objective theories cannot provide a complete understanding of the world" (abstract).
  - "insofar as dualism is itself an objectivist theory, the knowledge argument shows dualism to be false as well" (p. 146). "the knowledge argument does not force us to choose between subjective physicalism and subjective dualism. It simply succeeds in ruling out objective physicalism and objective dualism alike" (p. 149).
  - The tie-breaker toward the physicalist option is closure: "in light of the plausible causal closure of the physical, the position deserves our interest" (p. 171).
  - n. 47 (p. 174): "Such a position might provide a more comfortable home to the view of Searle (1992), though that is not the only form subjective physicalism can take."
  - n. 45 (p. 174): the Copenhagen privileging of an observer would make physics "a non-objective science to some degree", but "The type of subjectivity here seems of a completely different kind, however, than that posed by the problem of qualia."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral to supportive on Dualism's premise. The Map's dualism, which holds that phenomenal properties "exist, but not objectively" (`objectivity-and-consciousness` L136), is a *subjective* dualism in Howell's taxonomy. That is the Map's reading; Howell does not discuss the Map.

### Howell, R. J. (2015). The physicalist's tight squeeze: A posteriori physicalism vs. a priori physicalism. *Philosophy Compass* 10(12), 905–913 — READ IN FULL
- **URL**: https://rjhjr.com//wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Howell-2015-Philosophy_Compass.pdf; DOI 10.1111/phc3.12291
- **Key points**:
  - It presses two arguments against a posteriori (type-B) physicalism, the camp his 2009 paper places subjective physicalism in. The *transparency* argument: phenomenal concepts are transparent, so physical descriptions that capture pain's essence should not leave it open whether the thing described is painful. The *scope* argument: the premise truth is exhaustive, so it includes every referential feature that could ground an a posteriori necessity.
  - **Brute necessitation is dualism by another name**: "if 'a posteriori physicalism' is true only because of brute necessitation, it should more accurately be called something like necessary emergentist dualism" (p. 909). **Coiner guard**: note 22 on that sentence cites Wilson (2005), so do not credit Howell with the label. His positive argument is that a base property which necessarily gives rise to phenomenal states must have a disposition to do so. That disposition is "basic" and "proto-phenomenal", and "if phenomenality or protophenomenality is basic, physicalism is false" (p. 910).
  - Conclusion: "the committed physicalist might need to take another look at a priori physicalism". "Between the incoherence facing a posteriori physicalism and the intuitive implausibility facing a priori physicalism, the physicalist remains in a rather tight squeeze" (p. 910).
  - **Do not over-read**: the paper never says where subjective physicalism stands. It cites the 2013 book only in notes, and note 23 says the brute-necessitation section "draws heavily from Alter and Howell (2015), which in turn draws from Howell (2013) and Howell (2009)". It does **not** retract subjective physicalism. Whether the book escapes these arguments is the subject of its unread ch. 6 (see Leads).
- **Tenet alignment**: Supportive of the Map's case that type-B carries a dualist-shaped cost. It is Howell's own version of a disguised-dualism charge, aimed at the brute-necessitation variant.

## Major Positions

### Subjective physicalism (inclusive)
- **Proponent**: Howell (2007, 2009, 2013).
- **Core claim**: everything metaphysically supervenes on non-mental fundamentals. Physics may refer to every property, but some properties have subjective aspects graspable only by undergoing them. Aspects are intensionally individuated, inseparable "conceptual parts" of physical properties, so no second property is added.
- **Key arguments**: the knowledge argument against objectivism; extensionalism about properties and worlds; the claim that intensional properties would be epiphenomenal.
- **Relation to site tenets**: it shares the Map's anti-objectivism, its permanent gap and its rejection of the phenomenal concept strategy. It parts at property individuation (Dualism) and at causal closure (Bidirectional Interaction, Minimal Quantum Interaction).

### Subjective physicalism (exclusive)
- **Proponent**: Howell (2008b; unread here).
- **Core claim**: some physical properties are not mentioned in a completed physics and can be grasped only subjectively, yet are physical because they supervene.
- **Relation to site tenets**: this version stands nearest the diagnostic's target. A property that physics never mentions and that only a subject can grasp counts as physical on supervenience alone, which is where labelling does the most work. Howell himself says the two versions are hard to distinguish once "property" is read broadly (2009, p. 339).

### "Subjective dualism"
- **Source**: Howell's label (2007, p. 149), not developed by him.
- **Relation to site tenets**: on Howell's taxonomy the Map's dualism is subjective. It holds that phenomenal properties are not objectively capturable, and it takes the knowledge argument's lesson the same way. On Howell's own terms (2007), the knowledge argument does not choose between his view and the Map's; closure does (the Map's reading).

## Key Debates

### Is subjective physicalism dualism in disguise?
- **Sides**: Howell raises the charge himself and denies it (2009, p. 321; extensionalism, aspects). Brown (2014) says the aspects "seem close to non-physical qualia". Howell (2015 Compass) presses a related charge against brute-necessitation a posteriori physicalism, without saying whether it touches his own view.
- **Core disagreement**: whether an intensionally individuated, inseparable aspect that physics cannot express is a property. Howell's text locates this in "the darker wilds of ontology" (2009, p. 338).
- **Current state**: open. The book's ch. 6 (pp. 123–151) addresses necessitation without deducibility (per McClelland); it is unread here.

### Does the case against dualism need closure?
- **Sides**: Howell: the exclusion argument and "the plausible causal closure of the physical" (2007, p. 171; 2009, pp. 325, 333). McClelland: Howell assumes exclusion is sound.
- **Current state**: this is the precise point of divergence from the Map (see [[#map-application|below]]).

## The Diagnostic Applied (the Map's analysis) {#map-application}

Everything in this section is the Map's analysis. Howell never discusses the diagnostic, and none of these verdicts is his. The conditions are those of `concepts/disguised-property-dualism` §The Diagnostic (L37–L49).

**Condition (1), two irreducible structures: met as written.** The condition lists "descriptions, geometries, aspects, or property-types". Subjective physicalism has a physical description and a subjective aspect "not expressible by physical description" (p. 335), which no objective explanation will capture (p. 339). Howell would answer that aspects are not a second structure *in the world*. They are intensionally individuated "conceptual parts" of one property. The DPD page already grants a parallel move to Friston: "a dual-*aspect* claim about one property, not a smuggled second property-type" (L57). So condition (1) holds on its wording. Whether it holds in substance is exactly what Howell disputes.

**Condition (2), monist disavowal: met.** "Metaphysically, we are of a piece with the rest of the world" (p. 339).

**Condition (3), no reduction shown: met as written, under all three standards.**
- *Logical derivation*: denied in principle. Subjective physicalism is type-B. A complete explanation "would require the elimination of any explanatory gap between the subjective and the objective. Subjective physicalism denies that this is possible" (p. 339).
- *Empirical identity with an explanatory bridge*: none offered. The aspect–property tie is unexplained; McClelland notes that acquaintance "is left unexplained (p. 147)".
- *Progressive programme*: none claimed. Howell expects the special sciences to help "to some degree" only (p. 339).

**Why it is nonetheless a boundary case.** Three features separate it from the pattern the DPD page describes. That pattern is a "two-structure architecture with monist disavowal carried by a promise about reduction" (L49), whose "promissory note … is rarely cashed" (L104).
1. **No promissory note.** Howell says the gap is permanent and why. The Map's own routing page already records that "Type-B locates the gap in the permanent distinctness of phenomenal and physical concepts, so a promissory-note reply aimed at Type-B misses" (`type-a-type-b-and-type-c-physicalism` L112). Subjective physicalism is a clean instance: the diagnostic's letter catches it, and its stated target does not fit it.
2. **The disavowal rests on an argument, not a promise.** Howell argues that the inference from two aspects to two properties needs property intensionalism, which he rejects on independent grounds (profligacy, the "whiff of anti-realism", epiphenomenal intensional properties; pp. 324–326, 330). Condition (3)'s three standards measure *explanatory* reduction, and they do not evaluate this kind of defence. A diagnostic that counts "aspects" as "structures" therefore leans on the intensionalism Howell rejects. The Map should state that dependence rather than score the case as a clean hit.
3. **The costs are declared.** The DPD page's defence of the Map's own two-structure view is "That the Map states its debts rather than promising to retire them" (L96). Its complaint against the disguised variants is that "they refuse to acknowledge the cost" (L83). Howell states his: the view "does not put the mind body problem to rest, nor does it explain the subjective" (p. 339); the acquaintance relation is unexplained; physics cannot give a complete understanding. On this criterion subjective physicalism stands nearer the Map than the disguised variants do.

**Where the diagnostic still has purchase.**
- *Exclusive subjective physicalism*: properties not mentioned in completed physics, graspable only subjectively, counted physical by supervenience alone. Howell concedes the inclusive and exclusive versions are hard to tell apart once "property" is read broadly (p. 339). Brown says the aspects "seem close to non-physical qualia".
- *The ground of the necessitation*: Howell's own later arguments supply the strongest form of the charge. If the physical base necessitates the subjective aspect, the 2015 Compass paper's reasoning offers three options. The necessitation is deducible, which subjective physicalism denies. Or it is brute, "something like necessary emergentist dualism" (Howell, citing Wilson). Or the base carries a basic proto-phenomenal disposition, and then "physicalism is false" (p. 910). Inclusive subjective physicalism answers that the aspect is part of the nature of the physical property, so the necessitation is identity, not a law. Whether that answer survives his own transparency and scope arguments is not settled in any text read here. Brown presses the same point ("one wonders if they threaten the physicality of the base properties").
- *The Chalmers cost argument*: at bottom, the residue the diagnostic detects is Chalmers's charge against type-B, already on the Map at `type-a-type-b-and-type-c-physicalism` L99. That charge reads: "a principle that has the epistemic status of a fundamental law, but gives it the ontological status of an identity". Howell's tu quoque ("Dualism itself cannot explain why only certain states are lawfully connected to the presence of qualia", p. 339) concedes parity of explanatory debt. It does not answer the cost.

**Verdict (Map's analysis).** Subjective physicalism passes the diagnostic's spirit and fails its letter. The case shows that, against principled type-B views, condition (3) is satisfied by construction. The diagnostic then reduces to the older cost argument plus a dispute about how properties are individuated, and it cannot settle that dispute itself. The DPD page's own self-application concedes that the asymmetry it defends is "a real but permanently contestable difference" (L96). Subjective physicalism sits on that contestable line, in the middle: it asserts metaphysical unification, disclaims explanatory unification in principle, and defends the combination by argument.

**What subjective physicalism concedes that the Map asserts.**
1. No objective theory, physics included, gives a complete understanding of the world (2007 abstract; 2009, p. 315). The Map's `objectivity-and-consciousness` holds the same premise.
2. The explanatory gap is permanent and in principle (2009, p. 339).
3. Mary learns something genuine, and the standard physicalist replies do not dissolve it (2007, all replies reduced to acquaintance; 2009, pp. 336–338 on the indexical and phenomenal-concept replies).
4. The phenomenal concept strategy fails as an explanation; Chalmers's dilemma is accepted (2009, p. 338 and n. 65).
5. The felt character is prior to and independent of phenomenal concepts (2009, p. 316).
6. Objective dualism falls with objective physicalism (2007, p. 149). The Map's dualism survives this only as a "subjective dualism", which its objectivity page already implies (L136). That is the Map's reading.
7. The view lies close to property dualism ("closest friend", 2009, p. 338; "surprisingly close", book p. 162, per McClelland).

**Where the Map parts from it.** There are two points, and the first is a framework boundary.
- **Causal closure.** Howell's tie-breaker against subjective dualism is closure and exclusion (2007, p. 171; 2009, pp. 325, 333; book p. 164 per McClelland). The dualist's properties "seem fated to causal impotence" only on the premise that "the complete causal story about the world should be able to be told in terms of properties that have objective individuation conditions" (p. 325). Tenets 2 and 3 deny closure at quantum indeterminacy, so the tie-breaker does not reach the Map. This removes a defeater and earns no evidence ([[type-a-type-b-and-type-c-physicalism]] L105 makes the same point against type-B's closure reply). McClelland's remark that Howell assumes exclusion is sound is an independent statement of the same weakness.
- **Property individuation.** The Map counts what Howell calls an aspect as a property. The Map has no argument here that Howell lacks; it is the bedrock disagreement Howell names.
