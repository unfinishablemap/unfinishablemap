## Executive Summary

1. **The classification debate is real, documented in primary text, and two of its three primary documents are now in hand.** ICD-10 (1992) files *Depersonalization-derealization syndrome* as F48.1 under *F48 Other neurotic disorders* and, in its differential-diagnosis paragraph, separates it from "dissociative disorders (where awareness of change is lacking)" (Blue Book p. 172). ICD-11 (CDDR, March 2024) moves it to *6B66 Depersonalization-derealization disorder* inside the *Dissociative disorders* grouping (CDDR pp. 391–394), where DSM-IV and DSM-5 had already placed it. Medford, Sierra, Baker & David (2005) call both placements problematic and report that the Hunter et al. 2003 model holds "that depersonalisation is best conceptualised as related to anxiety disorders rather than to dissociative conditions" (full text verified).
2. **The Hunter 2003 model is reachable at abstract level only, and the reviewer's quotation is not in the abstract.** The abstract's own claims are: "compelling evidence to link DPD with the anxiety disorders, particularly panic"; "it is the catastrophic appraisal of the normally transient symptoms of DP/DR that results in the development of a chronic disorder"; symptoms "misinterpreted as indicative of severe mental illness or brain dysfunction"; a "vicious cycle of increasing anxiety and consequently increased DP/DR symptoms"; maintenance by "specific avoidances, 'safety behaviours' and cognitive biases". The phrase the outer review quoted — "catastrophically misinterpreted as signs of deteriorating or damaged mental health" — appears verbatim on the Psychology Tools summary page and nowhere in the primary text I could reach. The article must use the abstract's wording.
3. **The same research unit later qualified the anxiety thesis.** Sierra, Medford, Wyatt & David (2012; 291 DPD cases) found a low anxiety–depersonalisation association "only apparent in those patients with low intensity depersonalization, but not in those with severe depersonalization", a "'statistical dissociation'" that "adds further evidence in favour of depersonalization disorder being an independent condition and suggests that its association with anxiety has been overemphasized." Michal et al. 2009 (German population survey, n = 1,287) conclude depersonalisation "can not be reduced to a negligible variant of depression or anxiety". The debate is therefore not anxiety-camp versus dissociation-camp but a three-way question — anxiety-spectrum, dissociative, or independent — and the authors of the 2003 model sit on more than one side of it over time.
4. **The substance and medical exclusions are primary-text verifiable in ICD-11 and ICD-10.** CDDR: symptoms "are not due to the effects of a substance or medication on the central nervous system (including withdrawal effects) and are not due to a disease of the nervous system (e.g. temporal lobe epilepsy), head trauma or another medical condition" (p. 392), and "Psychoactive substance use, especially of marijuana or hallucinogens, is a common precipitant ... the diagnosis can only be assigned if the symptoms persist beyond the period of intoxication or withdrawal" (p. 393). The drug-induced literature's central finding is Medford et al. 2003 (40 of 164 clinic cases): "Drug-induced DP does not appear to represent a distinct clinical syndrome."
5. **The prevalence update is an update, not a correction.** Yang, Millman, David & Hunter 2023 give "0% to 1.9% amongst the general population, 5-20% in outpatients and 17.5-41.9% in inpatients" and "around 1%"; their own discussion reads the general-population range as "1% to 2% ... remaining consistent with previous findings (Hunter et al., 2004)" (AOP p. 25). And the outer review's claim that Hunter 2004's "in the region of 1-2%" is "not locatable" in the abstract is false: the PubMed abstract's Discussion paragraph contains it verbatim (retrieved by `efetch` today).
6. **Bearing on the hub's affective reading.** ICD-10 states that "The complaint of loss of emotions is the most frequent among these varied phenomena" (p. 172), which is primary-text support for the affective reading's *descriptive* adequacy; the Hunter model supplies an anxiety-driven route into the fronto-limbic suppression the hub already cites (Sierra & Berrios 1998; Sierra & David 2011); the 2012 "statistical dissociation" result cuts the other way. None of this moves the Map's two-tier verdict: whichever chapter the manuals file the syndrome under, every option on the table is a physicalist clinical frame, so the significance tier stays null and the house position stays *compatible with, not evidenced by*, Tenet 1. Do not enlist Hunter, Medford, Sierra or David.

## What the Manuals Say (Primary Text Where Reached)

### ICD-10 Blue Book (WHO 1992), F48.1 — primary, pp. 171–172

Listed under **F48 Other neurotic disorders** (categories list p. 31 and p. 133; F44 *Dissociative [conversion] disorders* is a separate block). Description: "A disorder in which the sufferer complains that his or her mental activity, body, and/or surroundings are changed in their quality, so as to be unreal, remote, or automatized." ... "The complaint of loss of emotions is the most frequent among these varied phenomena." ... "Elements of the syndrome may also occur in mentally healthy individuals in states of fatigue, sensory deprivation, hallucinogen intoxication, or as a hypnogogic/hypnopompic phenomenon."

Diagnostic guidelines, verbatim: "For a definite diagnosis, there must be either or both of (a) and (b), plus (c) and (d): (a) depersonalization symptoms, i.e. the individual feels that his or her own feelings and/or experiences are detached, distant, not his or her own, lost, etc; (b) derealization symptoms, i.e. objects, people, and/or surroundings seem unreal, distant, artificial, colourless, lifeless, etc; (c) an acceptance that this is a subjective and spontaneous change, not imposed by outside forces or other people (i.e. insight); (d) a clear sensorium and absence of toxic confusional state or epilepsy."

Differential diagnosis, verbatim: "The disorder must be differentiated from other disorders in which 'change of personality' is experienced or presented, such as schizophrenia (delusions of transformation or passivity and control experiences), dissociative disorders (where awareness of change is lacking), and some instances of early dementia. The preictal aura of temporal lobe epilepsy and some postictal states may include depersonalization and derealization syndromes as secondary phenomena."

Two things to carry: ICD-10 has no distress/impairment criterion and no free-standing substance exclusion — (d) excludes "toxic confusional state" and epilepsy only; and the 1992 text's "awareness of change is lacking" is the sentence Medford et al. 2005 later turned into the argument against the dissociative placement.

### ICD-11 CDDR (WHO, 8 March 2024), 6B66 — primary, pp. 391–394

Filed under the **Dissociative disorders** grouping (contents p. vii: 6B60–6B6Z, with 6B66 at p. 391). *Essential (required) features*, verbatim:

- "Persistent or recurrent experiences of either depersonalization or derealization, or of both symptoms, are required for diagnosis." Depersonalization "is characterized by experiencing the self as strange or unreal, or by feeling detached from – or as though one were an outside observer of – one's thoughts, feelings, sensations, body or actions. Depersonalization may take the form of emotional and/or physical numbing, a sense of watching oneself from a distance or 'being in a play', or perceptual alterations (e.g. a distorted sense of time)." Derealization "is characterized by experiencing other people, objects or the world as strange or unreal (e.g. dreamlike, distant, foggy, lifeless, colourless or visually distorted), or by feeling detached from one's surroundings."
- "During experiences of depersonalization or derealization, reality testing remains intact. The experiences are not associated with delusions or beliefs that the individual is being controlled by external people or forces."
- "The symptoms are not better accounted for by another mental disorder (e.g. post-traumatic stress disorder, an anxiety or fear-related disorder, another dissociative disorder, personality disorder)."
- "The symptoms are not due to the effects of a substance or medication on the central nervous system (including withdrawal effects) and are not due to a disease of the nervous system (e.g. temporal lobe epilepsy), head trauma or another medical condition." (p. 392)
- "The symptoms result in significant distress or significant impairment in personal, family, social, educational, occupational or other important areas of functioning. If functioning is maintained, it is only through significant additional effort." (p. 392)

*Additional clinical features* (p. 392): "Catastrophic cognitions (e.g. frequent fears of 'going crazy') may occur, along with a lack of vividness in autobiographical memories. Loss of the sense of 'ownership' of some memories or physiological hyporeactivity to emotional stimuli may also be present." *Boundary with normality* (p. 392): transient experiences "under the influence of substances ... typically remit when these emotional or physical states change." *Course features* (p. 393): "emotional stress, anxiety or negative affect, sensory overstimulation, sleep deprivation or substance use can exacerbate symptom intensity"; co-occurrence with "mood disorders, anxiety and fear-related disorders or personality disorder ... does not appear to alter the severity"; childhood interpersonal trauma is associated but "the association is not as strong as for other dissociative disorders (e.g. dissociative amnesia, dissociative identity disorder)"; "Psychoactive substance use, especially of marijuana or hallucinogens, is a common precipitant of depersonalization and derealization symptoms. However, depersonalization-derealization disorder diagnosis can only be assigned if the symptoms persist beyond the period of intoxication or withdrawal." *Culture-related features* (p. 393): intentionally induced states in "spiritual or meditative practices ... should not be assigned a diagnosis". *Boundaries* (p. 394): other dissociative disorders, schizophrenia, depressive disorders, panic attacks ("may persist for a time after the panic episode subsides ... a separate diagnosis ... is not warranted" if exclusively during or briefly after), other anxiety and fear-related disorders.

So ICD-11 carries the dissociative placement while writing the anxiety-camp's observations into the entry itself (catastrophic cognitions, panic boundary, weaker trauma link than its chapter-mates). The ICD-11 MMS browser text (the short "Description" with its synonym list including "depersonalisation neurosis") was reached only through a code-database mirror — **[second-hand]**; the CDDR is the primary clinical text and is what the article should cite.

### DSM-5 (APA 2013) Criteria A–E — reprint, second-hand; DSM-5-TR (2022) not reached

Criteria A–E as transcribed on a clinical reference page **[second-hand]**: A, "The presence of persistent or recurrent experiences of depersonalization, derealization, or both" with the two sub-definitions ("unreality, detachment, or being an outside observer with respect to one's thoughts, feelings, sensations, body, or actions" / "unreality or detachment with respect to surroundings"); B, "During the depersonalization or derealization experiences, reality testing remains intact."; C, "The symptoms cause clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning."; D, "The disturbance is not attributable to the physiological effects of a substance (e.g., a drug of abuse, medication) or another medical condition (e.g. - seizures)."; E, "The disturbance is not better explained by another mental disorder, such as schizophrenia, panic disorder, major depressive disorder, acute stress disorder, posttraumatic stress disorder, or another dissociative disorder." Note the brief's label "Criterion C substance exclusion" is off by one — the substance/medical exclusion is **Criterion D**; C is distress/impairment.

DSM-5-TR: the text itself was not reached. What is known from reachable sources: First et al. 2023 (*Psychiatric Services* 74(8): 869–875, abstract verified) describe the TR as containing "modifications, mostly for clarity, of the diagnostic criteria for more than 70 disorders" without naming them; the APA "Updates to DSM-5-TR Criteria and Text" page lists no update for DPDR or the dissociative chapter; the MSD Manual entry (Spiegel, reviewed 2025) presents the same A–E list "in the DSM-5-TR" **[second-hand]**; an open 2025 review cites the DSM-5-TR entry at "pp. 344–347" **[second-hand]**. The article should say "DSM-5 (2013; carried into DSM-5-TR 2022 per secondary sources)" and must not claim to have read the TR. DSM-IV's wording, "alteration in the perception or experience of the self so that one feels detached from and as if one is an outside observer of one's mental processes or body", is quoted with page range (APA 1994, pp. 488–490) in Medford et al. 2005 (verified in that full text).

## The Classification Debate

### Positions

**Dissociative placement (DSM-III-R → DSM-5; ICD-11).** Spiegel et al. 2011 (the DSM-5 dissociative-disorders work group; abstract verified) recommended that "Depersonalization Disorder (DPD) should [include] derealization symptoms as well" (the abstract's own text has the garble "should derealization symptoms") and concluded "There is a growing body of evidence linking the dissociative disorders to a trauma history, and to specific neural mechanisms." Berrios & Sierra 1997 (abstract verified) record the shift and its cost: "Depersonalization has of late become subsumed under the dissociative disorders. The definitional instability of the latter, however, has caused further complications to the study of depersonalization."

**Neurotic / anxiety-spectrum placement (ICD-10; Hunter et al. 2003; Medford et al. 2005).** Medford et al. 2005, *Nosology* paragraph, verbatim: "Both ICD–10 and DSM–IV betray uncertainty as to the nosological status of depersonalisation: in the former, it is included under the vague heading of 'other neurotic disorders', whereas in the latter it is listed under dissociative disorders, an equally problematic classification, as the hallmark of true dissociation is a lack of subjective awareness of change. By contrast, sufferers from depersonalisation are all too aware of a disturbing change in their experience of themselves and/or their surroundings – indeed, in the primary disorder, this awareness of change is the very essence of the presenting complaint. Other ways in which depersonalisation differs from dissociative disorders are explored at length in Hunter et al (2003)." And in *Psychological approaches*: the 2003 model "is based on the idea ... that anxiety and depersonalisation are intimately related, and that depersonalisation is best conceptualised as related to anxiety disorders rather than to dissociative conditions." Supporting data from the same unit: Baker et al. 2003 (204 cases) — "Depersonalisation symptom scores correlated with both anxiety and depression and a past history of these disorders was commonly reported. 'Dissociative amnesia' was not prominent."; Medford 2005 adds that 43% of that series "describe a previous history of anxiety or panic attacks". Hunter, Sierra & David 2004 close their epidemiology review with "The relationship between DP/DR and certain other psychiatric disorders (e. g. panic) suggests possible common pathophysiological or aetiological factors." Lambert et al. 2002 (organic review) also list anxiety as a suggested factor.

**Independent-condition position (Sierra et al. 2012; Michal et al. 2009, 2016).** Sierra, Medford, Wyatt & David 2012, quoted above: association with anxiety "only apparent in those patients with low intensity depersonalization"; "'statistical dissociation'"; "its association with anxiety has been overemphasized." Michal et al. 2016 (223 cases, ICD-10 F48.1): "Despite the high comorbidity of DDS patients with depressive disorders and comparable burden with symptoms of depression and anxiety, the clinical picture and course of both patient groups differed strongly." Simeon 2004 (review): comorbid mood, anxiety and personality disorders "but none predict symptom severity"; "The most common immediate precipitants of the disorder are severe stress, depression and panic, and marijuana and hallucinogen ingestion."

### Reading the debate correctly

- The anxiety thesis and its qualification come from the **same Maudsley/IoP unit** (Sierra, Medford, David are authors on both the 2003/2005 and the 2012 papers). The article must not present "Hunter et al." and "Sierra et al." as opposing schools; it is one group's view maturing, with Hunter's CBT line (2003, 2005, 2017) continuing in parallel.
- The nosological argument that *does* persist across all of them is the insight criterion: ICD-10's "(c) ... insight" and "(where awareness of change is lacking)", DSM Criterion B, CDDR "reality testing remains intact", Medford's "all too aware". That is the hub's first structural fact doing nosological work, and it is the cleanest bridge from this page to `concepts/depersonalisation`.
- Coiner/populariser guard: the cognitive-behavioural model is Hunter, Phillips, Chalder, Sierra & David 2003; the "anxiety disorders rather than dissociative conditions" sentence is Medford, Sierra, Baker & David 2005's *gloss* on it, not a Hunter 2003 quotation. Cite each to its own paper.

## The Hunter 2003 Maintenance Model

Hunter, E. C. M., Phillips, M. L., Chalder, T., Sierra, M. & David, A. S. (2003). Depersonalisation disorder: a cognitive-behavioural conceptualisation. *Behaviour Research and Therapy*, 41(12), 1451–1467. doi:10.1016/s0005-7967(03)00066-4. PMID 14583413. **Abstract verified; full text not reached.**

Abstract, usable verbatim: "We suggest that there is compelling evidence to link DPD with the anxiety disorders, particularly panic. This paper proposes that it is the catastrophic appraisal of the normally transient symptoms of DP/DR that results in the development of a chronic disorder. We suggest that if DP/DR symptoms are misinterpreted as indicative of severe mental illness or brain dysfunction, a vicious cycle of increasing anxiety and consequently increased DP/DR symptoms will result. Moreover, cognitive and behavioural responses to symptoms such as specific avoidances, 'safety behaviours' and cognitive biases serve to maintain the disorder by increasing awareness of the symptoms, heightening the perceived threat and preventing disconfirmation of the catastrophic misinterpretations."

Medford et al. 2005's restatement (verified): the model "suggests that there are various ways in which depersonalisation may initially arise, related to some external psychological stressor and/or as a consequence of a change in mental state (e.g. low mood, anxiety, drug use). Crucially, in those in whom depersonalisation becomes chronic and pathological, the appearance of depersonalisation features is interpreted as highly threatening ('catastrophic attribution'), leading to a range of cognitions and behaviours that can serve to perpetuate and intensify the symptoms." Treatment targets named there: "psychoeducation, the reduction of avoidant 'safety behaviours' (such as avoiding social situations) and excessive self-observation (e.g. looking in the mirror to see if one has changed), and challenging the 'catastrophic' attributional style (e.g. ideas such as 'My brain is not working')."

Outcome evidence: Hunter, Baker, Phillips, Sierra & David 2005 (open study, 21 patients; abstract verified): "Significant improvements in patient-defined measures of DP/DR severity as well as standardised measures of dissociation, depression, anxiety and general functioning ... and 29% of participants no longer met criteria for DPD at the end of therapy" — "further trials with larger sample sizes and more rigorous research methodology are needed". Hunter, Charlton & David 2017 (*BMJ* 356: j745) is a clinical review with no abstract on PubMed — **lead only**.

**Quote-status warning for the writer.** The outer review's "catastrophically misinterpreted as signs of deteriorating or damaged mental health" is the Psychology Tools page's sentence, not the abstract's. Until the 2003 full text is read it is **unverified in the primary**; use the abstract's "misinterpreted as indicative of severe mental illness or brain dysfunction" instead. Note also that the model is a maintenance model: it says nothing about *what* is subtracted, only why a transient subtraction becomes chronic. Its bearing on the hub is therefore on the affective reading's aetiology (anxiety → suppression), not on the architecture tier.

## The Substance and Medical Exclusions

- **Criterion text**: DSM-5 D (above, second-hand); ICD-11 CDDR (primary, p. 392) including "withdrawal effects"; ICD-10 (d) "absence of toxic confusional state or epilepsy" (primary, p. 172). The CDDR threshold rule (p. 393): diagnosis "can only be assigned if the symptoms persist beyond the period of intoxication or withdrawal". So the exclusion is of *intoxication-bound* depersonalisation, not of drug-*precipitated* chronic depersonalisation, which both ICD-11 and the Maudsley series admit into the disorder.
- **Medford, Baker, Hunter, Sierra, Lawrence, Phillips & David 2003** (*Addiction* 98(12): 1731–1736; abstract verified): 40 of 164 chronic cases "related the onset of symptoms to an episode of illicit drug use"; the drug group was younger and more male, but "when the drug-induced group was compared with an age and sex-matched subset of the non-drug group, differences between groups largely disappeared"; "Drug-induced DP does not appear to represent a distinct clinical syndrome. The neurocognitive mechanisms of the genesis and maintenance of DP are likely to be similar across clinical groups, regardless of precipitants." Medford et al. 2005 (verified): "particularly cannabis, but the subsequent course and nature of their condition does not appear to differ markedly from cases not involving drug use".
- **Simeon, Knutelska, Nelson & Guralnik 2003** (117 cases; abstract verified): precipitants "severe stress, depression, panic, marijuana ingestion, and hallucinogen ingestion, and none of these predicted symptom severity." **Hürlimann, Kupferschmid & Simon 2012** (abstract verified): six adolescent cases of "persistent depersonalization disorder in adolescence after consuming cannabis", noting the association "is widely neglected". Yang et al. 2023 (AOP p. 23, verified): "drug use could trigger experiences of depersonalization and derealization (Madden & Einhorn, 2018) and drug-induced depersonalization is similar to non-drug-induced depersonalization (Medford et al., 2003)." Madden & Einhorn 2018 (cannabis-induced DPDR) — **lead, not fetched**.
- **Ketamine**: the manuals' lists name marijuana and hallucinogens; the MSD Manual adds ketamine **[second-hand]**. The Map's existing ketamine material is mechanistic (`obsidian/research/ketamine-dissociation-retrosplenial-rhythm-2026-10-16.md`; 37 live files, none nosological). The new page should cross-reference that note's NMDA/retrosplenial material for the *mechanism* and keep the *classification* point here: a pharmacological precipitant yields a syndrome the specialist unit could not distinguish from the non-drug form.
- **Medical exclusion**: Lambert, Sierra, Phillips & David 2002 (abstract verified): 4 new and 47 literature cases of "organic depersonalization"; "Epilepsy and migraine appear to be the disorders most commonly associated with depersonalization"; their "secondary status precludes a DSM-IV diagnosis"; they propose "an organic subtype". ICD-10's own text already flags the "preictal aura of temporal lobe epilepsy".
