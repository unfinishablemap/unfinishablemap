## Executive Summary

The "property-to-substance" literature is a loose family of five arguments, not one argument, and the SEP section the outer review pointed at (§4.6, "From Property Dualism to Substance Dualism") does not summarise any of them: it borrows Zimmerman's title but its content is Lowe's bundle-theory argument for a unitary subject, and it hands the independence-sense argument to Weir in one sentence. The five routes are: (i) Francescotti 2001 — no plausible analysis of "physical particular" lets one accept non-physical properties while denying non-physical particulars; (ii) Searle 2002 — the property dualist's own metaphors ("over and above", "gives rise to", "emergent") "make it impossible to state the theory without implying a version of substance dualism"; (iii) Zimmerman 2010 — garden-variety material objects (brains, bodies) have vague, semantically-decided boundaries, so a precise psychophysical law cannot select one of them as bearer of qualia; the property dualist is driven either to "speculative materialism" (an undiscovered sharply-bounded physical bearer) or to emergent substance dualism, and the latter is at least as plausible (Mackie 2011 contests both steps); (iv) Schneider 2012 — on either the bundle or the substratum theory of substance, a bearer of sui generis qualia cannot be classed as a *physical* substance; (v) Weir 2023/2024 — the conceivability argument's own premises (transparent phenomenal concepts, necessary truths knowable a priori) run in reverse: a "ghost world" is as conceivable as a zombie world, so the phenomenal facts do not necessitate anything physical, so there is at least one non-physical substance in Descartes' independence sense; the only exit is panpsychism. Lycan 2013 is an assessment rather than a route: nine standard objections to substance dualism scored against property dualism, verdict "PD is perhaps a little better off than SD. But not so much better off that property dualists should go on boasting."

For the Map the net is mixed and specific. Zimmerman's vagueness route and Lycan's scorecard support the Map's declared lean (`concepts/substance-property-dualism` L159) and sharpen one of its booked debts: the pairing law owed under P-SC2 must be vagueness-tolerant, because an emergent subject faces its own "too many souls" problem that Zimmerman answers only with a redundancy clause in the emergence law. Weir's parity argument, the strongest of the five, delivers *more* than the Map wants (a substance in the independence sense, capable of disembodiment) by a premise the Map explicitly declines to rely on (modal rationalism; the Map holds only weak Revelation, `concepts/dualism` L106), and *less* than Posit One needs (Weir concedes "at least one substance per individual", and treats subjecthood as plausibly a second-order property of experiences — the opposite of primitive subject individuation). Robinson's route costs physical realism about individuals, which the Map's structure aspect keeps. The honest statement is therefore: the literature shows property dualism is a less stable resting place than the "received view" supposes, which is a reason for the Map's lean, but every route that reaches a *Cartesian* substance does so by a premise the Map does not hold, so the lean stays a lean.

## Key Sources

### SEP, *Dualism* (Robinson & Weir, substantive revision 17 Oct 2025)
- **URL**: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/dualism/
- **Type**: Encyclopedia
- **Key points**:
  - §2.4 states the "received view": "a widely accepted opinion in the first decades of the present century has held that although substance dualism is no longer a respectable option, property dualism is an important position that must be taken seriously (see Weir 2023, 31–33). The idea that property dualism is preferable to substance dualism is itself regularly challenged, however (Francescotti 2001; Searle 2002; Zimmerman 2010; Schneider 2012; Lycan 2013; Robinson 2016; Weir 2023)."
  - §4.6 "From Property Dualism to Substance Dualism" opens: "The argument discussed below presses the case that the mind is a substance in the sense that it is a unitary entity, not a Buddhist or Humean bundle. The body is Hume's bundle, Foster's and Berkeley's replies, and Lowe 1996's argument that "psychological modes are essentially modes of persons". **Zimmerman is not discussed in §4.6 at all.**
  - §3.1 separates the two senses of "substance" (independence: "something that can exist by itself", Weir 2023, 38–44; and unitary persisting entity). The Map's lean is in the second sense; Weir's argument delivers the first.
  - Disclosure: both SEP authors are proponents in the list they cite (Robinson 2016; Weir 2023). The entry is a reliable map of the literature but not a neutral referee of it.

### Zimmerman, D. (2010). "From Property Dualism to Substance Dualism." *Aristotelian Society Supplementary Volume* 84(1): 119–150.
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8349.2010.00189.x (Crossref-verified: title "I—Dean Zimmerman: From Property Dualism to Substance Dualism", OUP, v84 i1 p119–150, issued 2010-06). Full text 403 at OUP; abstract via OpenAlex inverted index.
- **Type**: Paper (Joint Session symposium, paired with Mackie)
- **Key points** (abstract, verbatim): "Property dualism is enjoying a slight resurgence in popularity, these days; substance dualism, not so much. But it is not as easy as one might think to be a property dualist and a substance materialist. The reasons for being a property dualist support the idea that some phenomenal properties (or qualia) are as fundamental as the most basic physical properties; but what material objects could be the bearers of the qualia? If even some qualia require an adverbial construal (if they are modifications of the thing that is conscious because of them, not properties of something else to which the subject of consciousness is related), then the property dualist can be driven to speculative forms of materialism none of which, at this point, looks more likely to be true than the more modest versions of emergent dualism defended by contemporary substance dualists."
  - The argument's engine, per three independent secondary sources (Mackie 2011 abstract; Weir 2024 p. 33; Babaei 2026), is **bearer vagueness**: "garden variety" material objects such as brains or bodies have vague boundaries, which Zimmerman "views ... as the result of semantic indecision. It follows that positing that a garden variety physical object is the bearer of phenomenal properties makes nature implausibly deferential to our linguistic practices. The only alternative for property dualists is that the physical bearers of phenomenal properties are undiscovered strictly bounded physical objects" (Weir 2024, p. 33). The Problem of the Many supplies the multiplicity: millions of overlapping aggregates with equal claim to be "the brain".
  - Zimmerman's preferred landing is *emergent* substance dualism (Hasker-style), which faces its own "too many souls" version of the problem; his answer is a redundancy clause: overlapping physical triggers produce one soul (Babaei 2026's reconstruction; Lycan 2013 fn 2 and fn 14 confirm Zimmerman is an "emergentist" rather than Cartesian substance dualist).
  - Lycan's one-sentence summary (2013, fn 4): "Zimmerman (2010) argues that if one holds PD but not SD, one probably must also be an adverbialist regarding sensory qualities, and that given adverbialism, a weak form of substance dualism is more plausible than any strict materialism."
- **Tenet alignment**: Aligns with the Map's substance lean; its emergentist landing matches the Map's non-Cartesian framing (Lowe/Zimmerman as "naturalistic substance dualism", `concepts/substance-property-dualism` L61).
- **Quote status**: abstract verbatim (OpenAlex); body-text claims are secondary (Mackie, Weir, Babaei), marked as such throughout.

### Mackie, P. (2011). "Property Dualism and Substance Dualism." *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 111(1pt1): 181–199.

- **Type**: Paper (symposium reply)
- **Key points** (abstract, verbatim): "I attempt to rebut Dean Zimmerman's novel argument (2010) ... I take issue with Zimmerman's contention that the vagueness of 'garden variety' material objects such as brains or bodies makes them unsuitable candidates for the possession of phenomenal properties. ### Lycan, W. G. (2013). "Is Property Dualism Better Off than Substance Dualism?" *Philosophical Studies* 164(2): 533–542.
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-012-9867-x (Crossref-verified; online 2012-02-12). Full text fetched: https://andrewmbailey.com/papers/Lycan.pdf (author-hosted copy; all quotes grep-verified in it).
- **Type**: Paper
- **Key points**:
  - Thesis: "I think that most of the standard objections to substance dualism (SD) count as effectively against PD, and that PD is hardly more plausible, or less implausible, than SD." Concedes SD is "logically stronger than PD; so one would need a reason for accepting SD over and above PD".
  - Nine objections scored. Four have "a little differential force" against SD (excrescencehood, neural dependence, causal pairing, evolution); five have none (interaction, nonspatiality, laws of physics, epistemology, unity/individuation). On interaction: "How could a nonphysical property causally affect physical ones, any more than could a nonphysical substance?"
  - Two objections PD faces that SD does not: **strong emergence** of properties ("If a brain state or event itself has immaterial properties, i.e., that are not properties of an immaterial thing, the properties are emergent in an objectionably strong sense"; credited to Chalmers in conversation, fn 19) and **introspective acquaintance** ("a brute physical brain seems the wrong sort of thing to be acquainted with immaterial properties").
  - Recommends spatialising egos: "I see no good reason not to suppose that immaterial selves are located roughly where central nervous systems are", which "would entirely obviate the causal pairing problem" — a move the Map's `concepts/pairing-problem` L67 already attributes to Hasker and Zimmerman without Lycan.
  - Verdict (§4.1): "PD is perhaps a little better off than SD. But not so much better off that property dualists should go on boasting that they are not so crazy as to be Cartesians."
  - Fn 4 quotes Francescotti p. 114 verbatim: "on any plausible definition of a physical particular, it is unclear how one can consistently endorse property dualism…while rejecting substance dualism".
- **Tenet alignment**: Aligns with Tenet 5's role in `concepts/where-the-substance-commitment-enters` (Relation section): the parsimony-first dispreference for SD is shown to rest on objections that apply to PD too. Lycan's 2018 chapter "Redressing Substance Dualism" (*Blackwell Companion to Substance Dualism*, Crossref 10.1002/9781119468004.ch2) restates the scorecard; its abstract adds that "materialism is not significantly better supported than dualism" — a stronger claim than the 2013 paper makes, keep the two separate.

### Schneider, S. (2012). "Why Property Dualists Must Reject Substance Physicalism." *Philosophical Studies* 157(1): 61–76.
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-010-9618-9 (Crossref-verified; online 2010-09-17). Full text fetched from the author's site (schneiderwebsite.com, "SchneiderPropertyDualism.pdf"); quotes grep-verified.
- **Type**: Paper
- **Key points**:
  - Target: the conjunction "(SP) Substance Physicalism: all substances are physical" and "(PD) Property Dualism: mental and physical properties are distinct", which she says "has been characterized as the default position in philosophy of mind" (citing Kim 2006).
  - Claim: "If either of the currently leading conceptions of substance are in force, (PD) cannot uphold substance physicalism; rather, (PD) leads to substance dualism." On the bundle theory: "Given that non-physical properties are constituents of the bundle, why would the bundle be physical?" and "why are there not two bundles instead: a physical one" and a mental one. Mutatis mutandis for the substratum view.
  - Conclusion: the position "that takes physical substances to be the bearer of mental properties, is not well-founded"; the demand is that property dualists "explain how your substances turn out to be genuinely physical".
  - Fn 1 reports Chalmers "reasonably sympathetic but agnostic" about substance dualism (email, Aug. 2009) — consistent with SEP's "preference for substance dualism over property dualism (Chalmers 2016, 24)" already at `concepts/substance-property-dualism` L71.
  - Weir's critique (2024, pp. 33–34): "Schneider's argument seems to presuppose that the mind is a substance", which on the independence definition "requires defense". A reply by Eric Yang, "The compatibility of property dualism and substance materialism" (PhilArchive YANTCO-6), surfaced in search results only — **lead, not verified**.
- **Tenet alignment**: Aligns; but it reaches substance dualism by an ontology-of-substance route (bundle/substratum) the Map has not adopted (`concepts/four-category-ontology` is Lowe's grid, a third option Schneider's fn mentions and sets aside).

### Searle, J. R. (2002). "Why I Am Not a Property Dualist." *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 9(12): 57–64.
- **URL**: full text fetched from https://www.neurohumanitiestudies.eu/archivio/searle-final.pdf (header "Journal of Consciousness Studies, 9, No. 12, 2002, pp. 57–64"); reprint Crossref 10.1017/cbo9780511812859.010 (*Philosophy in a New Century*, 2008, pp. 152–160, the edition `concepts/biological-naturalism` cites).
- **Type**: Paper
- **Key points**:
  - The dilemma the outer review wanted attributed here (its item 11): proposition (4) — if the physical universe is causally closed "consciousness ... must be epiphenomenal"; if not, "my bodily movements have too many causes. We have causal overdetermination."
  - The **Addendum** is the property-to-substance step: "How, in short, can the property dualist avoid lapsing into substance dualism? The difficulty comes out in the metaphors that the property dualist uses to express the thesis. Typical metaphors are that consciousness is something 'over and above' brain processes, that brains 'give rise to' consciousness and, of course, that consciousness is an 'emergent' property of the brain." ... "The dualism in property dualism forces them to postulate a separate entity. Ironically, the very dualism of the property dualist picture makes it impossible to state the theory without implying a version of substance dualism."
  - Searle's own exit is biological naturalism, not substance dualism: "Consciousness does not exist in a separate realm."
- **Tenet alignment**: The dilemma conflicts with Tenet 3 as posed; the Map's standing reply is that quantum indeterminacy denies the premise that the neural story is "entirely sufficient" (no overdetermination where physics leaves the outcome open). The Addendum is grist for the lean but is a rhetorical, not a metaphysical, argument.

### Francescotti, R. (2001). "Property Dualism without Substance Dualism?" *Philosophical Papers* 30(2): 93–116.
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1080/05568640109485079 (Crossref-verified). Abstract via OpenAlex; body unverified (p. 114 quote via Lycan 2013 fn 4).
- **Type**: Paper
- **Key points** (abstract, verbatim): "The assumption here is that one can consistently believe that (1) mental properties are not physical properties, while denying that (2) mental particulars are not physical particulars. But is this assumption true? This paper considers several analyses of what makes something a physical particular (as opposed to a non-physical particular), and it is argued that on any plausible analysis, accepting (1) requires accepting (2) as well."
- **Coiner note**: the **earliest** entry in SEP's challenger list and the first explicit "PD entails SD" paper; Searle 2002 is independent and a year later; Zimmerman 2010 supplied the phrase that became the SEP section title; Weir 2023/2024 is the book-length populariser. Do not credit Zimmerman as originator of the inference.

### Robinson, H. (2016). *From the Knowledge Argument to Mental Substance: Resurrecting the Mind*. Cambridge University Press.
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9781316092873 (Crossref-verified monograph, 2016-02-05). Blurb via OpenAlex; content via Ball 2016 (NDPR) and the CUP chapter list ("Some current arguments for substance dualism" is ch. 14).
- **Type**: Book
- **Key points**:
  - Blurb: "Bringing together the discussion of reductionism and semantic vagueness in an original and illuminating way, Howard Robinson argues that non-fundamental levels of ontology are best treated by a conceptualist account, rather than a realist one."
  - Ball: Robinson "defends a view on which the mind is an immaterial substance, there are no physical individuals, and everything from ordinary objects to the categories of the special sciences is mind-dependent." "Robinson's main argument for the broader idea that all non-basic entities are mind-dependent depends on his account of vagueness." "On Robinson's view it is a conceptual truth that physical objects depend on minds for their existence; but minds depend on bodies and brains only causally and not conceptually."
  - So the route is: vagueness → conceptualism about all non-basic (composite) individuals → no physical individual can be the bearer → mental substance. It shares Zimmerman's engine (vagueness of candidate bearers) but drives it to an idealist-leaning conclusion.
- **Tenet alignment**: Conflicts in part. The Map's `concepts/bi-aspectual-ontology` keeps physical structure as a real aspect; Robinson's conceptualism about physical individuals is dearer than the Map wants. Lycan 2013 fn 13 also notes Robinson 2003 defends epiphenomenalism, against Tenet 3.

- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003378600 (Crossref: monograph published 2023-05-23; the hardback copyright page and NDPR say 2024 — hence "Weir 2023/2024" in the review. SEP cites it as Weir 2023.) Chapters (Crossref): 2 "Why Does Everyone Hate the Soul?" ### Weir, R. (2024). "Property Dualism Implies Substance Dualism." *Journal of Philosophical Theological Research* 26(1): 21–46.
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.22091/jptr.2024.10209.2989 (DOAJ-verified; Crossref returns 404 for this DOI — the journal registers through DOAJ; CC BY 4.0). Full text fetched (ensani.ir mirror; PDF self-citation line: "Weir, R. (2024). Property Dualism Implies Substance Dualism. Journal of Philosophical Theological Research (Mind, Body, and Consciousness special issue), 26(1), 21-46"). Published online 2024-02-04. All quotes grep-verified.
- **Type**: Paper (compact statement of the book's parity argument)
- **Key points**:
  - "This paper argues that property dualism, as it is usually understood, is incoherent. The commitments that are meant to lead to property dualism actually lead to substance dualism. The argument presented here adds weight to David Chalmers' suggestion that the serious nonphysicalist options are in fact various kinds of panpsychism and substance dualism."
  - Diagnosis of the received view: the usual justification for PD-not-SD is interaction, which "is not a good justification. For as these theorists acknowledge, problems about interaction apply equally to property dualism (Chalmers, 1996, p. 150; Kim, 2005, p. 156)." Also: "theorists rarely specify what is meant by the terms 'property' and 'substance'".
  - The argument (p. 35): "The first premiss ... says that the phenomenal facts about the world do not a priori entail the existence of anything physical. The second premiss says that if the phenomenal facts do not a priori entail the existence of anything physical, then they do not necessitate the existence of anything physical. The conclusion is that some nonphysical things satisfy the independence definition of substance." Support for premiss one: "a ghost world is a minimal phenomenal duplicate of our world without anything physical. The ghost world seems just as conceivable as the zombie world." Premiss two is the property dualist's own modal rationalism: "the argument for property dualism depends on the thesis that necessary truths expressed by transparent concepts are a priori and that both physical and phenomenal concepts are transparent."
  - Answers the Chalmers/Goff indexical objection to Descartes: "that argument does not use the indexical 'I'. ... An effective solution is simply to redescribe the facts about consciousness without using indexicals."
  - On Zimmerman and Schneider: "Both arguments put property dualists under some pressure to posit nonphysical substances, but neither is decisive." (p. 34)
  - Exit routes: opacity of physical concepts "avoids substance dualism only by positing panpsychism"; and a new position, "transcendent panpsychism", on which physical things have hidden essences constituting "transcendent features of mentality" such as subjecthood or agency. Weir himself holds "It is plausible, for example, that the property of being a subject is just the second-order property of having some phenomenal property" — i.e. he does **not** treat subjecthood as primitive.
  - Concedes the gap to selves: "whilst I do not rule out subjectless substance dualism by definition, such a position is unlikely to find many supporters."
- **Tenet alignment**: Mixed; analysed below.

## Major Positions

### The received view: PD respectable, SD not
- **Proponents**: Kim 2005/2006 (quoted by Schneider: "Dualism is no longer a dualism of two sorts of substances; it is now a dualism of two sorts of properties"), Chalmers 1996 (early), Jackson 1982.
- **Core claim**: non-physical properties, physical bearers; substance physicalism "a starting point for discussion rather than a conclusion in need of defense" (Kim 2006, p. 274, via Schneider).
- **Relation to site tenets**: This is the position Tenet 1's neutrality treats as one of two stable options. If the challengers are right, the neutrality is between SD and something that collapses into SD or panpsychism — which the Map should say plainly.

### Bearer-vagueness emergentism (Zimmerman; Babaei)
- **Core claim**: no garden-variety object can be the law-selected bearer; an emergent simple subject can.
- **Key arguments**: semantic indecision about boundaries; Problem of the Many; adverbial qualia need a modified subject, not a related object.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Supports the lean and the Map's "primitive subject unity" reason (L159 first consideration). Its cost is a redundancy clause in the emergence law, which the Map should book under P-SC2 as the shape the owed pairing law must take.

### Parity substance dualism (Weir; Lycan as fellow-traveller)
- **Core claim**: the conceivability argument's premises entail non-physical substances in the independence sense; interaction objections do not discriminate.
- **Relation to site tenets**: see "Tenet Evaluation".

### Conceptualist substance dualism (Robinson)
- **Core claim**: vagueness → no physical individuals → mental substance; physical objects mind-dependent.
- **Relation to site tenets**: too expensive — removes the physical-side realism the structure aspect keeps.

### Substance-metaphysics route (Schneider)
- **Core claim**: on bundle or substratum theories, a mind with sui generis qualia is not a physical substance.
- **Relation to site tenets**: supportive, but presupposes the mind is a substance (Weir) — which is Posit One's posit, so the Map cannot use it as independent support without double-counting.

### Biological-naturalist dismissal (Searle)
- **Core claim**: PD's metaphors imply SD; both are wrong; consciousness is a state the brain is in.
- **Relation to site tenets**: the Addendum is quotable on the instability of PD; the dilemma is answered by the quantum interface.

## Key Debates

### Does vagueness of bearers really favour an emergent subject?
- **Sides**: Zimmerman, Babaei vs Mackie 2011 (derivative thinkers; speculative materialism "significantly more attractive than Zimmerman allows"), Roelofs 2024 (accept many minds — `concepts/manyism`), Weir 2024 (only "to the degree that undiscovered strictly bounded physical substances are considered less plausible than nonphysical substances").
- **Current state**: open; Zimmerman's own answer to "too many souls" is a redundancy clause critics call ad hoc.

### Does the conceivability argument deliver substances?
- **Sides**: Weir (yes, by parity) vs Chalmers/Goff (indexical opacity blocks Descartes' version — Weir replies his version has no indexical) vs anyone who declines modal rationalism (the Map, per `concepts/dualism` L106).
- **Current state**: new (2023–24); Cucu's review accepts it; no published rebuttal located.

### Is PD better off than SD at all?
- **Sides**: Lycan (marginally; two objections run the other way) vs the received view.
- **Current state**: Lycan's scorecard is widely cited and not, to my knowledge, answered point by point.

## Tenet Evaluation: What the Arguments Add to, or Cost, the Map

The Map's declared position (`concepts/substance-property-dualism` L159, rewritten today) is a *lean* toward substance dualism on two general grounds — primitive subject unity sidestepping constitutive combination, and Zimmerman's adverbial consideration — while the operational commitments (agency; the indexical objection to many-worlds) need only a persisting subject drawn from Posit One, and Tenet 1 stays neutral (`concepts/where-the-substance-commitment-enters`).

**What supports the lean.**
1. Lycan's scorecard removes the parsimony asymmetry: the interaction, nonspatiality, conservation and epistemology objections "have no differential force", so a parsimony-first dispreference for the heavier ontology is thinner than it looks. This is exactly the work `where-the-substance-commitment-enters` already asks Tenet 5 to do; Lycan supplies an external, non-dualist-motivated ally (Lycan is not himself a dualist).
2. Zimmerman's vagueness route gives the Map's "primitive unity" consideration a sharper form: not merely that combination is hard, but that no vague aggregate can be the *law-selected* bearer. This fits the Map's psychophysical-indexing-law framing, where a law must pick a subject.
3. Francescotti, Searle's Addendum and Schneider converge on the point that "property dualism with physical bearers" is under-described rather than cheap; this licenses the Map to say the received view's economy is partly unpaid rent (`concepts/disguised-property-dualism` makes the mirror-image point).

**What the arguments cost, or fail to give.**
1. **Weir delivers the wrong substance.** His conclusion is a substance in the *independence* sense — something whose existence "does not necessitate the existence of anything other than" itself, which "entails the possibility of disembodiment" (Cucu). The Map's lean is in the *unity/persistence* sense (Lowe's self, "not necessarily separable" from the body, L61). Accepting Weir's parity argument would commit the Map to disembodiability, which none of its operational homes needs and which its emergentist sympathies (Hasker, Zimmerman) resist.
2. **Weir's premise is one the Map declines.** The parity argument runs on modal rationalism plus transparent phenomenal concepts. `concepts/dualism` L106 says the Map "holds only weak Revelation, on which experience reveals part of a quality's character, not its full essence, so whether that grasp licenses the step is a debt the Map owes, neither won nor lost." A property dualist who accepts the zombie argument on modal-rationalist grounds is Weir's target; the Map is not, so it can neither be forced by the argument nor borrow it.
3. **Weir undercuts Posit One's individuation half.** He treats subjecthood as "plausibly ... just the second-order property of having some phenomenal property" and concedes his argument yields only "at least one substance per individual". Posit One posits *primitive subject individuation*. So the strongest property-to-substance argument on the market delivers substances but not the determinate subject the Map's second home (indexical identity against many-worlds) requires. The lean cannot be upgraded to a derivation by citing Weir.
4. **Zimmerman's landing has a pairing cost.** Emergent substance dualism faces "too many souls" and answers with a redundancy clause in the emergence law. The Map already books a pairing law as owed (P-SC2). This literature says what shape that law must have: tolerant of boundary vagueness in the physical trigger, producing one subject per overlapping cluster. That is a sharpening of the debt, not a payment.
5. **Robinson's route is dearer.** Conceptualism about all physical individuals removes the physical-side realism the bi-aspectual ontology keeps. Not for the Map.
6. **Schneider double-counts.** Her argument presupposes the mind is a substance; the Map posits exactly that in Posit One, so citing Schneider as support would count the posit twice (the discipline `where-the-substance-commitment-enters` already applies to its two homes).

**Net.** The literature supports the Map's *lean* and its *Tenet 5 framing*, and it exposes that the received view's "property dualism is cheaper" is largely unargued (Weir: "They do not offer much explanation for this inference"). It does not let the Map convert the lean into a commitment, because each route to a full substance uses a premise the Map does not hold (modal rationalism; bundle/substratum ontology; conceptualism about objects). The right installed wording is therefore: "the received preference for property dualism is contested on five independent grounds, which is one more reason the Map leans; but the arguments that reach a Cartesian substance do so by premises the Map declines, so the lean stays a lean."

> ## Is Property Dualism a Stable Resting Place?
>
> The neutrality above treats both ontologies as places a dualist can stand. A body of work contests that for property dualism specifically: Francescotti (2001) argues that no plausible account of a "physical particular" lets one grant non-physical properties while denying non-physical particulars; Searle (2002) adds that the property dualist's own metaphors — "over and above", "gives rise to", "emergent" — make it "impossible to state the theory without implying a version of substance dualism"; Schneider (2012) argues that on either a bundle or a substratum theory of substance a bearer of sui generis qualia is not a physical substance; and Zimmerman (2010), beside the adverbial point recorded in [[substance-property-dualism]], presses bearer vagueness: brains and bodies have semantically-decided boundaries, so a precise psychophysical law cannot select one of them, and the property dualist is driven either to an undiscovered sharply-bounded physical bearer or to an emergent subject (Mackie 2011 defends the former). Lycan (2013) scores nine standard objections to substance dualism and finds five apply equally to property dualism and two — strong emergence of properties, and a brain's acquaintance with its own immaterial properties — apply only to property dualism; his verdict is that property dualism is "perhaps a little better off", not enough to boast about. Weir (2023; 2024) runs the conceivability argument in reverse: a "ghost world" is as conceivable as a zombie world, so by the property dualist's own modal rationalism the phenomenal facts necessitate nothing physical, and there is a non-physical substance in Descartes' independence sense.
>
> This literature is a reason the Map leans, and it supports the use made of Tenet 5 below: the parsimony case for the lighter ontology rests on objections that do not discriminate. It does not let the Map convert the lean into a commitment. Weir's conclusion is a substance that could exist disembodied, which no home on this page needs, and it rests on modal rationalism, which the Map holds only as an open debt ([[dualism]]); he also concedes the argument yields "at least one substance per individual" rather than the determinate subject [[tenets/background-commitments|Posit One]] posits. Zimmerman's emergent subject faces its own "too many souls" problem and answers it with a redundancy clause in the emergence law — the shape the pairing law owed under [[positions/subject-census|P-SC2]] would have to take. The tenet's neutrality is therefore narrower than it looks, and the lean stays a lean.
