## Executive Summary

Prolonged grief disorder (PGD) is the diagnosis for grief that persists and disables beyond a set interval: at least six months after the death in ICD-11 and at least twelve months (for adults) in DSM-5-TR, which added it in 2022. Both centre on yearning for or preoccupation with the dead person and both require that the grief exceed expected social, cultural or religious norms. Pooled prevalence after non-violent bereavement is about one in ten (9.8%, Lundorff et al. 2017), rising to about half after unnatural deaths (49%, Djelantik et al. 2020), and estimates move substantially with the criteria and algorithm used. The question the Map asks has a clinical answer already: Shear and Shair (2005) state that acute grief resolves when the internal representation of the deceased is revised "to incorporate the reality of the death", and that "Failure to accomplish this integration results in the syndrome of complicated grief". So PGD is, by its own leading models, the bereavement void's lag failing to close, and the Map must credit that framing to them. What the Map can add concerns self-knowledge. Boelen (2010) holds that the failed integration "is mainly an implicit process" with only a partial "introspectively accessible counterpart", a sense of unrealness; and the felt grief carries no mark of the disorder's boundary, since longing is the commonest experience of all bereavement and taxometric work suggests disordered grief differs "only in severity" (Eisma 2023). The line is drawn from outside, by duration, impairment and norms. ### Prigerson, Boelen, Xu, Smith & Maciejewski (2021), "Validation of the new DSM-5-TR criteria for prolonged grief disorder and the PG-13-Revised (PG-13-R) scale"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.20823 (PMID 33432758; PMC7801836)
- **Type**: Paper, *World Psychiatry* 20(1): 96–106. **Read**: version-of-record abstract (PMC `efetch`); full text as the accepted manuscript in Oxford's repository (ORA, uuid c38b86ec), titled "Validation of the newly proposed DSM criteria for prolonged grief disorder and the PG-13-Revised (PG-13-R) scale". Its Table 1 is the *proposed* criteria set, which the APA page and Eisma (2023) confirm as the adopted one.
- **Key points**:
  - PGD is "an attachment disturbance featuring yearning for the deceased, loss of meaning and identity disruption" [raw-abs].
  - The 12-month floor was set in answer to public comment: "In order to be sensitive to the concern expressed in the public commentary about pathologizing normal grieving and diagnosing a grief-related disorder “too soon” after the death, the newly proposed DSM PGD criteria specify that 12 months must elapse since the death. This time frame contrasts with the ICD-11 diagnostic guidelines for PGD, requiring a period of 6 months" [AAM].
  - DSM-5 (2013) had "persistent complex bereavement disorder" (PCBD) in Section III, "among “conditions for further study”" [AAM]; a 2019 workshop led the DSM Steering Committee to move the disorder to Section II.
  - Three samples assessed at 12–24 months (Yale N=270, Utrecht N=163, Oxford N=239). DSM-criteria prevalence at first assessment "was 4.4%, 15.3% and 10.9% for the Yale, Utrecht and Oxford samples, respectively" [AAM]. The PGD diagnosis was distinct from PTSD (phi=0.12), MDD (phi=0.25) and GAD (phi=0.26) [raw-abs].
  - Symptom rates at syndromal level in Yale (community sample, mean 16.7 months post-loss): yearning 35.2%, identity disruption 22.6%, disbelief 6.3%, preoccupation 2.6% (Table 3) [AAM]. "the preoccupation item was infrequently reported at syndromal levels" [AAM].
  - Opposition noted by the authors: "a 2015 international online survey of public attitudes revealed that approximately 25% of respondents did not endorse the position that grief could be a mental disorder" [AAM].
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The construct is psychiatric and attachment-theoretic.
- **Caution**: Prigerson and Maciejewski are co-authors of Maciejewski et al. (2007), the bereavement void's main limiting source, and lead proponents of PGD. Their papers are advocacy as well as evidence on the question of recognition.

### American Psychiatric Association, "Prolonged Grief Disorder" (patient page)
- **URL**: https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/prolonged-grief-disorder (physician review August 2025; accessed 2026-11-19). **Read**: full page.
- **Key points**: "For a diagnosis of prolonged grief disorder, the loss of a close other person must have occurred at least a year ago for adults and at least 6 months ago for children and adolescents" [raw]. The eight listed symptoms include "Identity disruption (such as feeling as though part of oneself has died)", "Marked sense of disbelief about the death" and "Avoidance of reminders that the person is dead" [raw]. "An estimated 4%-15% of bereaved adults will experience the persistent symptoms of prolonged grief disorder" [raw]. "For most people, grief-related symptoms following the death of a close other decrease over time and do not impact their everyday functioning" [raw].
- **Use**: this is the APA's own public statement of the criteria; cite it, not the DSM-5-TR text.

### Eisma (2023), "Prolonged grief disorder in ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR: Challenges and controversies"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1177/00048674231154206 (PMID 36748103; PMC10291380)
- **Type**: Review/critique, *Australian & New Zealand Journal of Psychiatry* 57(7): 944–951 (online 2023-02-06). CC BY. **Read**: full text.
- **Key points**:
  - Table 1 sets the two criteria sets side by side. ICD-11 E: "The pervasive grief response has persisted for an atypically long period of time following the loss, markedly exceeding expected social, cultural or religious norms for the individual’s culture and context. Grief responses lasting for less than 6 months, and for longer periods in some cultural contexts, should not be regarded as meeting this requirement" [raw]. DSM-5-TR E: "The duration and severity of the bereavement reaction clearly exceeds expected social, cultural or religious norms for the individual’s culture and context" [raw].
  - The central validity gap: "How can we distinguish PGD from normal grief?" [raw]. "The evidence that PGD is characterized by qualitatively different experiences than normal grief is yet unconvincing" [raw]. Two taxometric studies "supported a dimensional rather than a categorical conceptualization of grief" (Holland et al. 2008; Kliem et al. 2018), and "This suggests that pathological grief is not characterized by the presence or absence of specific symptoms, but instead differs from normal grief only in severity" [raw].
  - On cut-offs: "considering that both grief severity and duration are continuous variables, any criteria derived in this manner will be arbitrary" [raw]; "Being on the far end of a continuum of grief severity or duration does not automatically imply that the grief you experience is pathological" [raw]. Impairment "is partially subject to the same arbitrariness" [raw].
  - Medicalisation: "for some groups of bereaved adults, severe and persistent grief appears a normal (i.e. frequently occurring) response to an abnormal life-event" [raw], citing Djelantik et al. (2020, 49% after unnatural deaths).
  - Pharmacotherapy: a large RCT "demonstrated no efficacy of citalopram as a treatment for prolonged grief symptoms" [raw]; naltrexone is under trial "based on the idea that PGD resembles addiction wherein the bereaved person continues to seek a connection with the deceased" [raw].
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. It is the best single open source for the contested side and for the side-by-side criteria.
- **Note**: Eisma's reference list spells the second author of the *Lancet Psychiatry* letter "Francis"; Crossref gives "Frances" (see Leads).

- **Key points**: their Table 1 gives the then-current ICD-11 wording, including "Difficulty accepting the death" and "Feeling one has lost a part of one’s self" among examples of intense emotional pain, and "more than 6 months at a minimum" [raw]. ### Lundorff, Holmgren, Zachariae, Farver-Vestergaard & O'Connor (2017), "Prevalence of prolonged grief disorder in adult bereavement: A systematic review and meta-analysis"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2017.01.030 (PMID 28167398)
- **Type**: Meta-analysis, *Journal of Affective Disorders* 212: 138–149. **Read**: abstract only.
- **Key points**: fourteen studies of "non-psychiatric, adult populations exposed to non-violent bereavement" [raw-abs]; "a pooled prevalence of PGD of 9.8% (95% CI 6.8-14.0)" [raw-abs]; higher mean age associated with higher prevalence; "high risk of external validity bias" [raw-abs]; "the findings should be interpreted cautiously" [raw-abs]. The studies predate both final criteria sets.
- **Note**: the "O'Connor M" here is Maja O'Connor (Aarhus), not Mary-Frances O'Connor of the learning account. Do not merge them.

### Djelantik, Smid, Mroz, Kleber & Boelen (2020), "The prevalence of prolonged grief disorder in bereaved individuals following unnatural losses: Systematic review and meta regression analysis"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2020.01.034 (PMID 32090736)
- **Type**: Meta-analysis, *Journal of Affective Disorders* 265: 146–156. **Read**: abstract only.
- **Key points**: 25 articles; "The random-effects pooled prevalence was 49%, 95% CI [33.6, 65.4]" [raw-abs]; death of an only child, violent killings and non-western location associated with higher prevalence; longer time since loss with lower.

### Treml, Linde, Brähler & Kersting (2024), "Prolonged grief disorder in ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR: differences in prevalence and diagnostic criteria"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2024.1266132 (PMID 38389981; PMC10881750)
- **Type**: Paper, *Frontiers in Psychiatry* 15: 1266132. **Read**: abstract (from the PMC full-text record).
- **Key points**: representative German sample (N=2,509; 1,071 bereaved). "The point prevalence of PGD among the bereaved varied between 4.7%-6.8%, depending on the criteria and diagnostic algorithm" [raw]; DSM-5-TR prevalence "significantly lower" than ICD-11; "The most common symptoms were intrusive thoughts/images related to the deceased person, longing for the deceased person, and difficulty accepting the loss" [raw]; "applying the minimum ICD-11 criteria could lead overestimating the prevalence" [raw].

### Shear & Shair (2005), "Attachment, loss, and complicated grief"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1002/dev.20091 (PMID 16252293)
- **Type**: Theory paper, *Developmental Psychobiology* 47(3): 253–267. **Read**: abstract only.
- **Key points**: a provisional model guided by Hofer's question "What exactly is lost when a loved one dies?" [raw-abs]. "death of an attachment figure produces a state of traumatic loss and symptoms of acute grief. These symptoms usually resolve following revision of the internalized representation of the deceased to incorporate the reality of the death. Failure to accomplish this integration results in the syndrome of complicated grief" [raw-abs].
- **Why it matters**: this is the "lag that does not close" stated by the clinicians themselves, fifteen years before DSM-5-TR. **Coiner warning**: the Map must present the non-closure framing as theirs (and Boelen's), not as the Map's discovery.
- **Terminology**: "complicated grief" here is the generic older name. Shear et al.'s later (2011) complicated-grief *criteria* define a broader entity than PGD (see Maciejewski et al. 2016).

### Boelen, van den Hout & van den Bout (2006), "A cognitive-behavioral conceptualization of complicated grief"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2850.2006.00013.x
- **Type**: Theory paper, *Clinical Psychology: Science and Practice* 13(2): 109–128. **Read**: abstract only (OpenAlex inverted index).
- **Key points**: "Three processes are seen as crucial in the development and maintenance of CG: (a) insufficient integration of the loss into the autobiographical knowledge base, (b) negative global beliefs and misinterpretations of grief reactions, and (c) anxious and depressive avoidance strategies" [raw-abs].

### Boelen (2010), "A sense of 'unrealness' about the death of a loved-one: An exploratory study of its role in emotional complications among bereaved individuals"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1557
- **Type**: Paper, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 24(2): 238–251 (online 2009-02-18; print 2010). **Read**: abstract only (OpenAlex inverted index).
- **Key points**: "it was proposed that the ‘lack of integration of the loss’ is mainly an implicit process, but has an explicit, introspectively accessible counterpart in the form of ‘a sense of unrealness’ that can be defined as a subjective sense of uncertainty or ambivalence about the irreversibility of the separation" [raw-abs]. Self-report data from 397 mourners; unrealness items "were distinct from those of CG and depression", and unrealness "was significantly associated with CG, when controlling for negative cognitions, avoidance and concomitant depression" [raw-abs].
- **Why it matters**: the only source found that speaks directly to what is and is not introspectively available in non-closing grief, and it does so as a proposal.

### Keser & Boelen (2025), "Testing the cognitive-behavioral model of prolonged grief disorder (PGD): distinct and shared pathways to PTSD and depression"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-025-03078-0 (PMID 40624708; PMC12235822)
- **Type**: Paper, *BMC Psychology* 13: 746. CC BY. **Read**: full text.
- **Key points**: 728 Turkish adults bereaved of a first-degree relative, self-report. The model's first component is "the inability to integrate new information about the deceased’s physical absence, the permanence of separation, and the irreversibility of death into autobiographical memory" [raw]. Its second includes "negative interpretations of grief symptoms (e.g., “ If I fully experience my feelings or accept the death , I will lose control or go crazy ”)" [raw] (spacing as in the PMC text). The Experienced Unrealness Scale has five items, e.g. "It feels unreal that he/she is gone forever" [raw]. Finding: "a sense of unrealness appeared to be specific to PGD only" [raw]. One cross-sectional sample.
- **Caution**: the methods sentence describing the EUS contains an evident typo ("irreversibility of anxiety"); do not quote it.

### Doering, Boelen, Eisma & Barke (2021), "Validation of a German Version of the Grief Cognitions Questionnaire and Establishment of a Short Form"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.620987 (PMID 33536985; PMC7848142)
- **Type**: Paper, *Frontiers in Psychology* 11: 620987 (published 2021-01-18). CC BY. **Read**: full text.
- **Key points**: the GCQ's nine subscales include "‘Appropriateness of Grief’ describes negative evaluations of one’s own grief reactions (4 items). ‘Cherish Grief’ reflects beliefs about the importance of cherishing the pain of the loss (3 items). ‘Threatening Interpretations of Grief’ contains catastrophic misinterpretations of in themselves harmless symptoms of grief (4 items)" [raw]. Illustrative cognitions in the introduction: "‘I don’t mourn the way I should do’" and "‘As long as I mourn, I do not really have to let him/her go’" [raw]; these are the authors' examples, not necessarily GCQ items.
- **Why it matters**: the mourner's beliefs about her own grief are measured, and the cognitive model treats some of them as maintaining factors. Associations, not demonstrated causes.

### O'Connor & Seeley (2022), "Grieving as a form of learning"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2021.08.019 (PMID 34520954; PMC8858332)
- **Type**: Theory and review, *Current Opinion in Psychology* 43: 317–322. Already cited by [[bereavement-void]]. **Read this run**: author manuscript in PMC, grepped for PGD.
- **Key points**: "semantic knowledge of the everlasting nature of the attachment figure, and episodic, autobiographical memories of the death are in conflict, perhaps explaining the duration of grieving and generating predictions about complications in prolonged grief disorder (PGD)" [raw]. "Difficulties pre-loss, such as spousal dependency or pre-existing hippocampal volume, can prolong learning and predict PGD" [raw]. They cite a review that "has conceptualized PGD as a disorder of reward" [raw], and warn that "not distinguishing acute and/or typical grief and prolonged grief within the sample muddies the waters further" [raw].
- **Link**: Bonanno et al. (2002), also cited by the bereavement void, report that "Chronic grief was associated with preloss dependency" [raw-abs].

### Maciejewski, Maercker, Boelen & Prigerson (2016), "'Prolonged grief disorder' and 'persistent complex bereavement disorder', but not 'complicated grief', are one and the same diagnostic entity"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.20348 (PMID 27717273; PMC5032512)
- **Type**: Paper, *World Psychiatry* 15(3): 266–275. **Read**: abstract only.
- **Key points**: in Yale Bereavement Study data, PGD, PCBD and the proposed ICD-11 test agreed closely, with rates "similarly low (∼10%)" [raw-abs]; the complicated-grief test's "estimate of rate of disorder was three-fold higher (∼30%)" and "it had no predictive validity" [raw-abs].
- **Why it matters**: "complicated grief" is not a safe synonym for PGD when a specific criteria set is meant.

### Maciejewski, Zhang, Block & Prigerson (2007), "An Empirical Examination of the Stage Theory of Grief"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.297.7.716. Already cited by [[bereavement-void]]. **Read this run**: abstract only; JAMA full text blocked.
- **Key point for this note**: the abstract's conclusion is the empirical rationale for the six-month threshold: "Given that the negative grief indicators all peak within approximately 6 months postloss, those who score high on these indicators beyond 6 months postloss might benefit from further evaluation" [raw-abs]. The bereavement void's statement that people meeting complicated-grief criteria were excluded was not re-checked here (the page records a full-text check at its review).

### Shear, Frank, Houck & Reynolds (2005) and Shear et al. (2016): treatment trials
- **URLs**: https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.293.21.2601 (PMID 15928281); https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2016.0892 (PMID 27276373). **Read**: abstracts only.
- **Key points**: 2005, 95 participants: response "51%" for complicated grief treatment against "28%" for interpersonal psychotherapy [raw-abs]. 2016, 395 participants: complicated grief treatment with placebo against placebo alone, "82.5% vs 54.8%" [raw-abs]; citalopram alone was "not significantly different from PLA" at weeks 12 and 20 [raw-abs].
- **Why it matters**: the non-closing lag can often be closed by a targeted psychotherapy. Even in its disordered form the bereavement void's first face is a weak, time-bound limit and can be treated.

### Johnson, First, Block, Vanderwerker, Zivin, Zhang & Prigerson (2009), "Stigmatization and receptivity to mental health services among recently bereaved adults"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1080/07481180903070392 (PMID 19697482; PMC2834798)
- **Type**: Paper, *Death Studies* 33(8): 691–711. **Read**: full text.
- **Key points**: 135 widowed Yale Bereavement Study participants, of whom "The low number of cases (n=16) with CG limited the statistical power" [raw]. "Most (93.8%) of the respondents with CG reported that if a clinician told them that they had CG that they would be relieved to know that they had a recognizable problem" [raw]; 87.5% "would be relieved to know that they were not going crazy" [raw]. Hypothetical attitudes, small n, authors from the PGD-proposing group.
- **Use**: weak evidence that a diagnosis can tell the griever something about her state she did not take herself to know. Do not build on it.

### Lichtenthal et al. (2011), "Underutilization of mental health services among bereaved caregivers with prolonged grief disorder"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1176/ps.62.10.pss6210_1225 (PMID 21969652; PMC3694810)
- **Type**: Brief report, *Psychiatric Services* 62(10): 1225–1229. **Read**: full text (author manuscript).
- **Key points**: 86 bereaved caregivers; 16% met PGD criteria; "only 43% of caregivers who met criteria for prolonged grief disorder accessed mental health services" [raw]. "Not having mental health concerns was the most common reason provided for not using mental health services, reported by 25 of the 49 (51%) bereaved caregivers whose responses were available" [raw]. That reason is reported for the whole sample, not broken down by PGD status.
- **Use**: do **not** cite it as evidence that people with PGD fail to recognise their state; the data do not separate them.

### Ratcliffe, Richardson & Millar (2023), "On the Appropriateness of Grief to Its Object"
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1017/apa.2021.55
- **Type**: Paper, *Journal of the American Philosophical Association* 9(2): 318–334 (online 2022-04-05). CC BY. **Read**: full text (White Rose 181657). The PDF's text layer drops numerals, so cite no page numbers from it.
- **Key points**: "The principal object of grief, we suggest, is a loss of life possibilities, which is experienced, understood, and engaged with over a prolonged period" [raw]. Against Moller and Marušić: "it is not surprising or puzzling that grief changes over time and generally subsides. Indeed, any profound upheaval will require some form of prolonged adjustment process for much the same reasons. It would therefore be puzzling if grief did not" [raw]. They allow inappropriate grief: "a grief reaction would not be appropriate to its object where it involved steadfastly preserving aspects of an experiential world that are rendered unintelligible by a death. Grief can also be excessive, as when intense or prolonged grief reactions arise without either losses of close relationships or substantial disturbances of life structure" [raw].
- **Why it matters**: a philosophical criterion for non-closing grief that is neither duration nor cultural norm. They do **not** discuss PGD; do not say they do.
- **Gap on the Map**: [[bereavement-void]] presents Marušić's puzzle without this published reply (which thanks Marušić). Reported under Defects.

### Supporting, abstract level

(2009)**, *PLoS Medicine* 6(8): e1000121, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000121 (PMC2711304) [read, full text]. 291 bereaved respondents; the 2009 criteria require yearning and "at least five of the following nine symptoms" including "difficulty accepting the loss" and "avoidance of the reality of the loss", present "at least six mo from the death" [raw].

A model integrating "attachment, self-identity, and autobiographical memory" that "places emphasis on how one's sense of identity influences yearning, memories of the deceased, appraisals, and coping strategies, to maintain a focus on the loss" [raw-abs].
- **Breen, Penman, Prigerson & Hewitt (2015)**, *Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease* 203(8): 569–573, https://doi.org/10.1097/NMD.0000000000000331 [abstract]. 348 participants; "the majority (74.7%) agreed that grief could be considered a mental disorder" [raw-abs]; reasons against included that grief "is normal, temporary, in response to an event" [raw-abs].
- **Stelzer et al. (2020)**, *Psychopathology* 53(1): 8–22, https://doi.org/10.1159/000505074 [abstract]. Interviews with 24 health professionals (10 Chinese, 14 German-speaking): most supported PGD's inclusion; "Main concerns included misdiagnosis, pathologization, and a lack of specificity of criteria" [raw-abs].

## Major Positions

### PGD as a distinct, valid disorder
- **Proponents**: Prigerson, Maciejewski, Boelen, Maercker, Shear, Killikelly.
- **Core claim**: a minority of bereaved people have a persistent, disabling grief, distinct from depression and PTSD, that predicts later harm and responds to targeted treatment.
- **Key arguments**: psychometric unidimensionality and temporal stability (Prigerson et al. 2021); low phi with PTSD, MDD and GAD; predictive validity; treatment response (Shear et al. 2005, 2016).
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral. The construct is defined by symptoms, time, impairment and norms.

### Medicalisation critique
- **Proponents**: Wakefield (2012, lead), Bandini (2015, lead), Cacciatore and Frances (2022, lead), and Eisma (2023, who reviews them while accepting that some bereaved people need treatment).
- **Core claim**: no consensus definition of normal grief exists, the evidence for a qualitative difference is "yet unconvincing", cut-offs on continuous variables are arbitrary, and in some groups (e.g. after unnatural deaths) severe persistent grief is the common response to an abnormal event.
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral, but it bears on the Map's self-knowledge question (below).

### Integration-failure models of PGD
- **Proponents**: Shear and Shair (attachment working models); Boelen et al. (autobiographical integration, negative cognitions, avoidance); Maccallum and Bryant (identity and the self-memory system); O'Connor and Seeley (learning and prediction).
- **Core claim**: acute grief resolves when the representation of the deceased and the self is revised to include the death; PGD is where that revision fails or is blocked.
- **Relation to site tenets**: all are physicalist-compatible psychological or neural models. None argues for or against dualism.

## Key Debates

### Is PGD qualitatively different from ordinary grief?
- **Sides**: proponents (distinct syndrome, Prigerson et al. 2021) against dimensional readings (taxometric studies via Eisma 2023).
- **Core disagreement**: kind or degree.
- **Current state**: open. Eisma's reading of the taxometric evidence is "only in severity".

### What should the timing threshold be?
- **Sides**: six months (ICD-11; Maciejewski et al. 2007's peak data) against twelve (DSM-5-TR, chosen in response to public concern about diagnosing "too soon").
- **Current state**: unresolved; Killikelly and Maercker: "As yet, the best time-point ... has not been scientifically validated". Prevalence differs by criteria set (Treml et al. 2024).

### Whose norms?
- **Core disagreement**: both manuals make exceeding "expected social, cultural or religious norms" a criterion. Cultural evidence read here is thin (Stelzer et al. 2020, qualitative, n=24; Killikelly and Maercker's examples are second-hand).

### Overlap with depression and PTSD
- **Current state**: proponents report distinctness (phi 0.12–0.26 in Prigerson et al. 2021) together with substantial comorbidity and shared predictors (Keser & Boelen 2025 find the cognitive variables predict PTSD and depression too, with unrealness specific to PGD).

## The Map-Specific Question

### Is PGD the case where the lag fails to close?

Yes, by the leading clinical models' own description, and that framing belongs to them. Shear and Shair (2005) say acute grief resolves when the internalised representation of the deceased is revised "to incorporate the reality of the death" and that failure of "this integration results in the syndrome of complicated grief". Boelen et al. (2006) put "insufficient integration of the loss into the autobiographical knowledge base" first among PGD's maintaining processes, and O'Connor and Seeley (2022), the bereavement void's source for the lag, say the conflict between semantic and episodic knowledge generates "predictions about complications in prolonged grief disorder". In the void's terms, PGD is the lag persisting past the interval at which it usually closes, with impairment.

Three qualifications the article writer must keep:

1. **Disbelief is optional.** DSM-5-TR lists "Marked sense of disbelief about the death" as one of eight symptoms of which three are needed; ICD-11 lists "difficulty accepting the death" among examples of required emotional pain. In the Yale sample at about 17 months only 6.3% reported disbelief at syndromal level, against 35.2% for yearning (Prigerson et al. 2021, AAM), though in a representative German sample "difficulty accepting the loss" was among the commonest symptoms (Treml et al. 2024). What persists most reliably is yearning; syndromal preoccupation was rare in the Yale sample (2.6%). The bereavement void's claim that the lag is "a lag in expectation, with belief largely intact" survives for PGD only as a tendency, and the measured states are felt ones: a "sense" of disbelief and a "sense of unrealness" ("It feels unreal that he/she is gone forever"). No source read shows people with PGD failing to believe that the death occurred. Do not write that they "cannot believe" it.
2. **Degree, not kind (contested).** If Eisma's reading of the taxometric studies is right, PGD is the far end of the same continuum, and saying the lag does not close means it closes much more slowly or not within the window the manuals set.
3. **It can be treated.** Targeted psychotherapy roughly doubled response over interpersonal therapy (51% vs 28%) and beat placebo in a larger trial (82.5% vs 54.8%). The limit is time-bound, as the void already says of its first face.

### What PGD shows about what the bereaved can know of their own state

This is the Map's analysis, built on the sources above; no source states it in these terms.

- **The unintegrated part is mostly out of view.** Boelen (2010) proposes that the lack of integration "is mainly an implicit process" whose "introspectively accessible counterpart" is a sense of unrealness. If that is right, the griever has access to a felt symptom of the non-closure and not to the non-closure itself. This extends the bereavement void's first face: in ordinary grief the lag is temporary and the survivor can watch it shrink; in PGD the same lag persists with only its symptom in view.
- **Grief carries no felt mark of its own disorder.** Longing, the core criterion, is "the most frequently reported experience in bereavement" (Eisma et al. 2020), and disordered grief may differ "only in severity" (Eisma 2023). Nothing in the felt state says *this is now a disorder*. The criteria supply the line from outside: time since the death, impairment, and comparison with what the griever's culture expects, and Eisma argues that each cut-off on a continuous variable is arbitrary.
- **The mourner's beliefs about her grief are part of the clinical picture.** The GCQ measures "negative evaluations of one’s own grief reactions" and "beliefs about the importance of cherishing the pain of the loss" (Doering et al. 2021), and the cognitive model treats catastrophic readings of one's own grief ("If I fully experience my feelings or accept the death, I will lose control or go crazy") as maintaining it (Keser & Boelen 2025). Here self-interpretation does more than observe the state; on the model it helps keep it going. This parallels the Map's [[sour-grapes-void]] theme of a self-reading that shapes its object, though the sources are cognitive-behavioural and claim association, not demonstrated causation.
- **The criteria answer a different question from Marušić's.** Marušić (2018, via [[bereavement-void]]) holds that the usual explanations of grief's fading cite "the needs of the griever", which explain the fading without justifying it. The PGD criteria draw their line partly by impairment, which is the griever's needs, and partly by social norms. So the diagnosis says when grief disables, not whether its persistence fits its reason. The "Cherish Grief" cognition ('As long as I mourn, I do not really have to let him/her go') looks from inside rather like the stance Marušić describes, in which ending grief looks like failing one's reasons. The cognitive model classes it as maladaptive. The philosophical question whether it is mistaken remains where Marušić left it, unless Ratcliffe, Richardson and Millar's (2023) reply holds, which offers a criterion of inappropriate grief ("steadfastly preserving aspects of an experiential world that are rendered unintelligible by a death") that does not depend on duration or norms.
- **Moller's worry does not reverse into a vindication.** If resilience is "a form of benign—or at least adaptive—blindness" (Moller 2007, p. 312, as quoted in [[bereavement-void]]), one might think the person who does not recover sees the loss more fully. The PGD symptom list includes "Emotional numbness" and "Feeling that life is meaningless", and the condition predicts suicidality and functional impairment. Nothing read supports treating PGD as fuller registration of the loss. The Map should not suggest it.

### Relation to the tenets

- **Dualism**: not engaged. The models are attachment-theoretic, cognitive-behavioural and neural (reward, hippocampal learning). Nothing here discriminates between dualism and physicalism, and the clinical literature must not be enlisted for dualism. The Map's own claim in [[emotion-and-dualism]] that grief's depth resists functional analysis is not supported by this material.
- **Occam's Razor Has Limits**: illustrated only. The single word "grief" covers yearning, felt unreality, beliefs about one's own grief and impairment, which come apart in the measures; a physicalist psychology draws the same distinctions.
- **Bidirectional Interaction, Minimal Quantum Interaction, No Many Worlds**: not engaged.

For most bereaved people intense grief eases within months. Prolonged grief disorder is the diagnosis for the minority in whom it persists and disables. ICD-11 and, from 2022, DSM-5-TR both require yearning for or preoccupation with the dead person, further symptoms, impairment (or, in DSM-5-TR, distress), and a grief that exceeds expected social, cultural or religious norms. Failure to accomplish this integration results in the syndrome of complicated grief" (Shear & Shair 2005, using an older name).
