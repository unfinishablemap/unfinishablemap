## Executive Summary

Acedia (Greek *akēdia*, roughly "not-caring") is the sixth of Evagrius Ponticus's eight tempting thoughts (*Praktikos* 6) and "the most burdensome of all the demons" (*Praktikos* 12). John Cassian carried it into Latin monasticism (*Institutes* X), and Aquinas defined it as "sorrow for spiritual good" (*ST* II-II q. 35). The texts agree on a striking structure. Acedia does more than make the cell feel dull. It **presents reasons to leave**: the place is unedifying, the brethren uncharitable, distant monasteries "better suited for salvation", the sick and one's kinsfolk in need of visits, and even, in Evagrius, the doctrine that "God is to be worshipped everywhere". The tradition holds that these reasons are untrustworthy and trains the monk to stay rather than weigh them. It discounted them by how they behaved, not by what they said: they arrive at fixed hours "like some fever", they all point one way, and they are said to dissolve, followed by "unspeakable joy", if the monk outlasts them. Modern scholarship agrees that acedia is neither laziness (Aquinas himself: "sloth is opposed to joy") nor straightforwardly depression (Daly 2007) and is contested as boredom (Bahrisch 2016; Dahm 2021). **The Map's analysis** (labelled below) is that the tradition ran checks on an affect's *process* rather than its *content*. Those checks escape the emotional-epistemology void's verification circularity only in part: they can show that a verdict depends on the state, which falls short of showing it false, and the tradition's positive verdict on the cell came from the vocation and a later affect. **Verdict: `fold`.** Acedia adds a case and a practice but no new limit. Its parts map onto the [[boredom-void|boredom void]] (projection, avoidance that keeps itself in place, slowed time), the [[mood-void|mood void]] (whole-soul envelopment) and the [[confabulation-void|confabulation void]] (presented reasons that are not the operative ones). ## Key Sources

### John Cassian, *Institutes* Book X ("Of the spirit of accidie")
- **URL**: https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/350710.htm
- **Type**: Primary text, public-domain translation: Edgar C. S. Gibson, *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, 2nd series, vol. 11 (1894), "Revised and edited for New Advent by Kevin Knight". **Read in full.**
- **Key points** (all quotations grep-verified in the raw page):
  - X.1: acedia is what "the Greeks call ἀκηδία, which we may term weariness or distress of heart". It is "especially trying to solitaries" and "especially disturbing to a monk about the sixth hour, like some fever which seizes him at stated times". Some elders call it "the midday demon spoken of in the ninetieth Psalm" (Vulgate numbering, Ps 91 in Hebrew numbering).
  - X.2, the reasons the state presents: it "produces dislike of the place, disgust with the cell, and disdain and contempt of the brethren". The monk "cries up distant monasteries" and "describes such places as more profitable and better suited for salvation". He "fancies that he will never be well while he stays in that place". The reasons turn pious: "the disease suggests that he ought to show courteous and friendly hospitalities to the brethren, and pay visits to the sick", and "that it would be a real work of piety" to visit a neglected religious woman. Bodily and temporal signs come with it: at "the fifth or sixth hour" comes "such bodily weariness and longing for food", and he "frequently gazes up at the sun, as if it was too slow in setting".
  - X.3: flight backfires. The soul seeks consolation "in visiting some brother, only to be afterwards weakened the more by this remedy which it seeks for the present".
  - X.5: two outlets, slumber or being "driven out from the monastic cloister, even though under some pious excuse or pretext".
  - X.24: Abba Paul, who did not need the income, made palm-leaf baskets all year and burned them, "simply for the sake of purifying his heart, and strengthening his thoughts, and persisting in his cell, and gaining a victory over accidie".
  - X.25: Abba Moses to the young Cassian, who had fled to Abba Paul: "You have not freed yourself from it, but rather have given yourself up to it as its slave and subject". The chapter closes: "a fit of accidie should not be evaded by running away from it, but overcome by resisting it."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The demonology is the tradition's, not the Map's.

### Evagrius Ponticus, *Praktikos* (chs. 6, 10, 12, 27–29, 36)
- **URL**: http://www.ldysinger.com/Evagrius/01_Prak/00a_start.htm (frame; text in `01_Praktikos.htm`). A second copy is at https://earlychurchtexts.com/public/evagrius_of_pontus_eight_logismoi.htm.
- **Type**: Primary text, Greek (*Sources Chrétiennes* 171; PG 40) with English by Luke Dysinger OSB, marked "translation in public domain" on both sites. **Read (chs. 1–40 scanned; the chapters below in full).** The two sites carry slightly different wordings of Dysinger's ch. 12 (ldysinger: "First it makes the sun appear to slow down or stop, so the day seems to be fifty hours long"; earlychurchtexts: "...seem to slow down or stop moving, so that the day appears to be fifty hours long"). **Quote the ldysinger.com wording only**, as below.
- **Key points**:
  - Ch. 6: eight "generic" thoughts, the sixth "that of acedia". "We cannot [control] whether these [tempting-thoughts] can agitate the soul or not; but whether they remain [in us] or not, and whether they move the passions or not – that we can [control]." (The Stoic first-movements structure the Map already discusses for *logismoi*.)
  - Ch. 10: "Gloominess" (*lypē*, sadness) is a separate thought that "sometimes arises from frustrated desires; but sometimes it is the result of anger." **Evagrius keeps sadness and acedia apart**, which bears on the depression question.
  - Ch. 12: "The demon of acedia, which is also called the noonday demon (Ps 90.6), is the most burdensome of all the demons", besetting the monk "at about the fourth hour (10 am)... until about the eighth hour (2 pm)". "First it makes the sun appear to slow down or stop, so the day seems to be fifty hours long." It "assails him with hatred of his place, his way of life and the work of his hands; that love has departed from the brethren". It "makes him desire other places" and adds a doctrinal premise: "After all, pleasing the Lord is not dependent on geography, the demon adds; God is to be worshipped everywhere." It "employs, as they say, every [possible] means to move the monk to leave his cell and flee the racecourse." Afterwards: "No other demon comes immediately after this one; rather, after the struggle the soul receives in turn a peaceful state and unspeakable joy".
  - Ch. 27: "When we are oppressed by the demon of listlessness, then with tears let us divide our soul in two, one part encouraging the other, sowing good hopes within us, soothing with David's chant" (Ps 41:6 LXX).
  - Ch. 28: "We must not abandon our cell in time of temptation, making eloquent excuses: we should stay seated within and persevere". The Greek *prophaseis eulogous dēthen plattomenon* is closer to "fabricating ostensibly reasonable pretexts". Also: "Fleeing from such conflicts and trying to shun this teaches the nous to be incompetent, fearful and fugitive."
  - Ch. 29: readiness "as though he were to die tomorrow" "cuts off the thoughts of acedia".
  - Ch. 36: other demons touch "only one part of the soul; but the noonday [demon] (i.e. acedia) generally envelops the whole soul and suffocates the nous."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral.
- **Not read**: *On the Eight Thoughts* (its acedia chapter is ch. 6, per Dysinger's cross-references) and *Antirrhetikos* Book 6 (scriptural answers to acedia's thoughts). Sinkewicz (2003, OUP) and Brakke (2009, *Talking Back*, Cistercian) translations are copyrighted. **LEADS.**

### Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 35 ("Of sloth")
- **URL**: https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3035.htm
- **Type**: Primary text, Fathers of the English Dominican Province translation (2nd rev. ed., 1920). The translation renders *acedia* as "sloth". **Read arts. 1, 2 and 4 in full; art. 3 skimmed.**
- **Key points**:
  - a. 1 corpus: "sorrow about spiritual good is evil in itself"; acedia "denotes sorrow for spiritual good".
  - a. 1 obj. 3 (quoting Cassian): acedia's complaint that other monasteries are better "seems to point to humility". The reply treats it as ingratitude, since one "contemns the good things he has received from God". **The reasons look like a virtue (self-criticism) and are reclassified.**
  - a. 1 ad 2: bodily timing. "those who fast are harassed by sloth towards mid-day, when they begin to feel the want of food, and to be parched by the sun's heat." This rests on a principle: "on account of some bodily transmutation a man becomes apt to commit some particular sin."
  - a. 1 ad 4: which temptations to flee and which to resist. Acedia is resisted, "when perseverance in the thought diminishes the incentive to sin, which incentive arises from some trivial consideration. This is the case with sloth, because the more we think about spiritual goods, the more pleasing they become to us, and forthwith sloth dies away." Aquinas thus predicts that **engaged attention defeats the state**. Compare the boredom void's face 4, where absorbed engagement ends attention-deficit boredom by definition, and its face 3, where avoidance keeps a low forecast uncorrected.
  - a. 2: acedia is a special vice because its object is "the Divine good" in which charity rejoices. Obj. 3 and the answer separate it from laziness: "idleness is opposed to carefulness, whereas sloth is opposed to joy."
  - a. 4 reports Gregory (*Moralia* XXXI.45) assigning acedia six "daughters" ("malice, spite, faint-heartedness, despair, sluggishness in regard to the commandments, wandering of the mind after unlawful things"), and says "Gregory more fittingly... calls sloth a kind of sorrow". Isidore is reported as separating sloth from sorrow. **Gregory not read directly; cite as Aquinas's report.**
  - Do **not** quote a. 4's "eternal objects of pleasure"; it appears to be a transcription error for "external".
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. Aquinas's object ("the Divine good") is theological, and the Map does not adopt it.

### Andrew Crislip (2005), "The Sin of Sloth or the Illness of the Demons? The Demon of Acedia in Early Christian Monasticism"
- **Type**: Paper, *Harvard Theological Review* 98(2): 143–169, doi:10.1017/S0017816005000908 (Crossref). **Opening page only** (OpenAlex).
- **Key points (opening page)**: Evagrius (ca. 345–399) calls acedia "the most troublesome of all" of the eight thoughts. Cassian is his "Latin-speaking protégé". In the Latin tradition acedia "has generally been understood as the sin of sloth". Interpreters have read it "primarily" as "laziness, ennui, or boredom", and "More recently, acedia has been considered analogous to the modern clinical condition of depression". Andrew Solomon took the title of *The Noonday Demon: An Atlas of Depression* (2001) from it. Bahrisch (2016) quotes Crislip (p. 145): "the very persistence of the term acedia betrays the fact that none of the modern or medieval glosses adequately conveys the semantic range of the monastic term."
- **Not read**: Crislip's thesis (the title points to an illness model). **LEAD.** Do not state his conclusion.

### Rebecca Konyndyk DeYoung (2004; 2014)
- **Type**: Paper, "Resistance to the Demands of Love: Aquinas on the Vice of Acedia", *The Thomist* 68(2): 173–204, doi:10.1353/tho.2004.0026 (first two pages read via OpenAlex). Chapter, "Sloth: Some Historical Reflections on Laziness, Effort, and Resistance to the Demands of Love", in K. Timpe & C. A. Boyd (eds.), *Virtues and Their Vices* (OUP, 2014), pp. 176–198, doi:10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199645541.003.0009 (abstract read).
- **Key points**: 2004 opening: popular use "typically associate[s] sloth with, or even define[s] it as, laziness". Her puzzle: "Is physical weariness the cause of acedia's sorrow, as some passages seem to suggest? Or does acedia have deeper, spiritual roots?" 2014 abstract: Evagrius and Cassian defined acedia "as a lack of enduring commitment to one's religious vocation". Sloth is "aversion to the demands of love, not to effort per se". The slothful resist "by escapism (restlessness) or by apathy (false rest)", so "the busy activity and diligence esteemed by contemporary culture can count among sloth's classic symptoms". - **Relevance**: DeYoung's weariness-or-spiritual-roots puzzle is the epistemic question asked from inside the tradition: is the state's cause bodily or does it concern the vocation? Her book *Glittering Vices* (Brazos, 2009; 2nd ed. 2020) was **not read. LEAD.**

### Robert W. Daly (2007), "Before Depression: The Medieval Vice of Acedia"
- **Type**: Paper, *Psychiatry: Interpersonal and Biological Processes* 70(1): 30–51, doi:10.1521/psyc.2007.70.1.30, PMID 17492910. **Abstract read** (PubMed).
- **Key points**: "In medieval Europe, acedia was a vice, an undesirable trait of character acquired in the course of living a life." ### Eunju Bahrisch (2016), "Why Did the Demon Come at Noontide? Understanding Acedia in Medieval Monastic Life"
- **URL**: https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10199336/1/Why_Did_the_Demon_Come_at_Noontide%20%281%29.pdf
- **Type**: Paper, *Kodikas/Code* 39(1–2): 39–47. **Read in full** (open repository PDF).
- **Key points**: acedia "was often misunderstood as equivalent to modern-day boredom". She argues it "was neither sloth nor boredom, but it was an active craving for physical comfort", and she "carefully suggest[s] that acedia might be connected to anxiety caused by hunger": the monastic meal came after None (about 3 pm), so the noontide attack fell in the fasting hours. She reads it as "a stuffed and saturated emotional state with dissatisfaction, anxiety, anger and desire for physical pleasure", not "an empty state of mind". She quotes Toohey (2004, p. 139) that Cassian's acedia was characterised by "tiredness, hunger, and the slowing of time, and by a desire to escape oneself through sleep or company". She reports, citing Svendsen 2005 p. 51, that by the Renaissance acedia was "substituted with melancholy".
- **Caution**: the hunger hypothesis is hers and offered tentatively. Her statement that Gregory "put despair (tristitia) and sloth together into acedia" conflicts with Aquinas's report that Gregory "calls sloth a kind of sorrow" (absorbing acedia into *tristitia*). Do not repeat her Gregory sentence.

## Major Positions

### Acedia as a demonic temptation to be resisted (Evagrius, Cassian)
- **Proponents**: Evagrius (*Praktikos*); Cassian (*Institutes* X); the Abba Moses saying in X.25.
- **Core claim**: acedia is an incoming thought (*logismos*) that the monk cannot prevent but can refuse to keep (Prak. 6). Its reasons are pretexts to be outlasted, not weighed.
- **Practice**: stay in the cell (Prak. 28; Cassian X.25); manual work, even pointless work (X.24); divide the soul so one part encourages the other (Prak. 27); answer with psalms; keep death in mind (Prak. 29).
- **Relation to site tenets**: none directly. The demon is the tradition's attribution. What the Map can use is the epistemic structure, a self-report the subject is trained to disbelieve, not the metaphysics.

### Acedia as a vice of the will opposed to charity's joy (Aquinas; DeYoung)
- **Core claim**: acedia is "sorrow for spiritual good", specifically the divine good charity rejoices in. It is distinct from laziness ("idleness is opposed to carefulness, whereas sloth is opposed to joy"). Bodily states (fasting, noon heat) make it "more insistent", but its object is spiritual. DeYoung: "aversion to the demands of love, not to effort per se", whose symptoms include busyness.
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral. The Map should not adopt "spiritual good is a good in very truth" as its own premise. That premise is what lets Aquinas classify the sorrow as unfitting ("evil in itself").

### Acedia as proto-depression or a relative of melancholia (medicalising readings)
- **Proponents**: Altschule (1965, title); Solomon (2001, title, per Crislip); Toohey (1990), **reported** by secondary sources to treat acedia as close to depression (**unverified, LEAD**).
- **Counter**: Daly (2007): "akin to clinical depression" but "should not be identified with depression". Norris (interview): "Not exactly". Evagrius already separates *lypē* (sadness) from *akēdia* (Prak. 10 vs 12).

### Acedia as boredom or pre-boredom
- **Proponents**: O'Brien (IEP): "a form of pre-boredom or boredom with sloth". Dahm (2021): "restless boredom is a primary construal". Svendsen (*A Philosophy of Boredom*, 2005; his chapter on acedia **not read, LEAD**).
- **Counter**: Bahrisch (2016): "neither sloth nor boredom". Wenzel (1966): its nature "changed" over the centuries, so no single modern gloss fits. Crislip (as quoted by Bahrisch): no gloss "adequately conveys the semantic range".

## Key Debates

### Is acedia boredom, depression, sloth, or its own state?
- **Sides**: boredom (IEP; Dahm, as a construal); depression-adjacent (Altschule; Solomon; reported Toohey); distinct vice (Daly; DeYoung; Bahrisch); laziness (the popular sense, which every scholar consulted rejects).
- **Core disagreement**: whether the defining feature is phenomenal (restlessness, slowed time, aversion), motivational (flight from a commitment), or evaluative (sorrow at a good).
- **Current state**: unresolved. The texts share a profile no single modern category captures: it recurs at fixed hours, offers reasons, aims at flight or sleep, and lifts if outlasted. **For the Map: never write "acedia is boredom" or "acedia was depression".** "A boredom-like state" or "monastic listlessness" is safe.

### When is a felt push to disengage trustworthy?
- **Sides**: the functional account (Elpidorou 2014; Bench & Lench 2013, already cited in the boredom void) treats boredom's push as a regulatory signal that keeps one from staying "trapped in unfulfilling situations". The monastic account treats the same push, aimed at a chosen vocation, as presumptively untrustworthy.
- **Core disagreement (the Map's framing)**: the two need not contradict each other. The functional account describes a signal tuned to short-horizon goals. The monk's vocation is a long-horizon commitment whose payoff, on the tradition's own account, comes only after persisting ("after the struggle... unspeakable joy", Prak. 12; "the more we think about spiritual goods, the more pleasing they become", Aquinas). A short-horizon signal applied to such a commitment lacks the evidence that would vindicate or defeat it. Benn's summary states the dilemma, and his resolution is unread.
- **Current state**: open. This is the strongest material for the Map and belongs in the fold.

### Is the cause bodily or spiritual?
- **Sides**: Aquinas a. 1 ad 2 (bodily transmutation, fasting, noon heat, making the vice "more insistent") and Bahrisch (hunger) against Aquinas a. 2 and DeYoung 2004 (the object is spiritual).
- **Relevance**: if a bodily variable such as hunger predicts when the reasons arrive, then the reasons are timed by something they do not mention. That is the tradition's best evidence that they are not the operative grounds.

## The Map's Analysis: What the Taxonomy Claims to Know {#map-analysis}

*Everything in this section is the Map's own analysis. No cited author makes these claims in this form.*

**1. Acedia supplies grounds where boredom withholds them.** The boredom void's face 1 is a want that names no particular target, and its face 2 is a verdict that "steers behaviour without displaying its grounds". Acedia, as Cassian and Evagrius describe it, is fluent with grounds. It names a target (distant monasteries, the sick, one's kin), gives reasons, and can argue from the monk's own doctrine ("God is to be worshipped everywhere"). So its limit is not missing grounds but **surplus grounds**: reasons presented as the grounds of the verdict which, on the tradition's account, are its products. That is the [[confabulation-void|confabulation void]]'s structure, generated explanations mistaken for self-knowledge, applied to an affect's verdict instead of to past behaviour. Tolstoy's Vronsky, quoted in the boredom void, "taking it for a desire and an object", is the objectless form. Acedia is the form that argues.

**2. The tradition's checks were process checks, not content checks.** The monks did not answer acedia's reasons on their merits. They discounted them by:
- (a) **timing**: the state arrives "at stated times", in the fasting hours (Cassian X.1–2; Aquinas a. 1 ad 2);
- (b) **direction**: every reason serves one conclusion ("every [possible] means to move the monk to leave his cell", Prak. 12), and new reasons replace refuted ones (Cassian's list moves from the brethren's faults to food, health, hospitality and piety);
- (c) **disguise**: the reasons take the form of virtues (piety in X.2; humility in Aquinas a. 1 obj. 3);
- (d) **sequel**: if outlasted, the state lifts (Prak. 12; Aquinas a. 1 ad 4).
Checks (a)–(c) are non-affective. They use the dynamics of the verdict, not a second affect, and so they partly escape the [[emotional-epistemology-void|emotional-epistemology void]]'s verification circularity. But they are genetic arguments. They show that the reasons depend on the state and are timed by variables they do not mention. **That falls short of showing them false.** The cell might really be unedifying, and noon might simply be when one notices. Check (d) is affective, a later joy outranking an earlier aversion, so it re-enters the circle. The tradition's confident verdict that the cell was worth staying in came from outside the experience, from the vocation and its theology (Aquinas: "spiritual good is a good in very truth"). This is the fold's central point.

**3. Whole-soul envelopment explains why a constructed observer was needed.** Prak. 36 says acedia "envelops the whole soul and suffocates the nous", unlike thoughts that touch one part. If so, no uninvolved faculty is left to judge the reasons, which is the [[mood-void|mood void]]'s constitutive opacity. Prak. 27's remedy, "divide our soul in two, one part encouraging the other", deliberately builds a second party where none occurs naturally. The mood void's "Counterfactual triangulation" and "Testimony and mirroring" approaches are modern versions of the same move. (Map reading; Evagrius frames it as consolation, not epistemics.)

**4. The decisive test needs what the state removes.** The tradition's claim is that the good of the cell shows itself only to one who stays (Prak. 12 sequel; Aquinas a. 1 ad 4). Acedia's whole function is to stop the staying. The verdict therefore lacks, by its own operation, the experience that would correct it. That is the boredom void's face 3 mechanism ("avoidance driven by the forecast removes the experience that would correct it") on a vocational scale. It also shares a shape with the [[sour-grapes-void|sour-grapes void]] ("the one decisive test needs the option the adapted subject lacks") and the [[transformative-experience-void|transformative-experience void]]. Cassian X.3 and Moses in X.25 make the behavioural half of the claim: flight strengthens the state. Prak. 28 adds that flight "teaches the nous to be incompetent, fearful and fugitive".

**5. The functional account and the monastic account conflict only over the horizon.** See the second debate above. The Map can say that a short-horizon disengagement signal applied to a long-horizon commitment cannot, from inside, tell a bad commitment from a commitment not yet paid out. That is Benn's dilemma (summary-level). It is **framework-independent**: a physicalist and a dualist meet the same limit.

**6. Norris's criterion relies on the access the mood void denies.** Distinguishing acedia from depression by whether one "know[s] the reasons why" depends on introspective access to the causes of mood. That access is what the mood void's origin-opacity layer questions. The criterion may still be useful in practice, but it is a self-report about causes, not a validated marker.

**What this adds that the boredom void does not already cover**: (1) the surplus-grounds form, in which presented reasons are not the operative grounds; (2) a historical practice that treated an affect's self-report as presumptively untrustworthy, with explicit process checks; (3) the analysis that such checks debunk without refuting. Points 3–5 above are re-applications of existing voids (mood, boredom face 3, sour grapes).

**Tenet bearing**: none of this supports dualism, and the fold must not enlist it. Acedia is no harder for physicalism than boredom. The Occam's Razor Has Limits tenet gets nothing either: the simple story (the monk is simply bored) fails, but the failure lends no support to a non-physical story. Nothing goes into any Relation to Site Perspective section.

## Verdict: `fold`, not a new void {#verdict}

Voids holds 124 of 125 (measured 21:3xZ with `tools.evolution.state.count_section_files('voids')`; topics 370/370 and concepts 370/370 are full). The last voids slot needs a state whose presented grounds the subject systematically cannot evaluate from inside, and which is not a sub-case of an existing void.

**For a standalone `voids/acedia` (slug free per `check_slug`)**: the surplus-grounds form is distinctive, the tradition is a rare natural experiment in self-distrust, and the process-check analysis is new to the Map.

**Against, which decides it**:
1. Each limit acedia shows already has a home: projection onto the place (boredom face 2), avoidance that keeps itself in place (boredom face 3; [[self-maintained-cognitive-limits|self-maintained limits]]), slowed time (boredom's duration border; Prak. 12's "fifty hours"), whole-soul envelopment (mood void), presented-but-not-operative reasons (confabulation void), and the decisive test needing the option the state removes (sour-grapes void). What remains new is a **case and a practice**, which suits a fold.
2. The construct is contested at its core. Scholars disagree whether it is boredom, depression, a craving for comfort or a vice of the will, and its meaning "changed in its very nature" across the centuries (Wenzel 1966). A void page keyed to "acedia" would rest on a moving target, or would have to define the void as the epistemic structure, which belongs to emotional epistemology.
3. Its defining object, in Aquinas, is theological ("the Divine good"). A voids page would have to strip that out, and what is left is the boredom-and-vocation structure the existing voids carry.
4. The slot is scarce, and the bar is "genuinely distinct". Acedia is a clear sub-case of a conjunction of existing voids. Spending the 125th slot on it would be poor value.

**Would merit a page if headroom opened?** Possibly in `topics` (e.g. `topics/acedia-and-the-epistemics-of-self-distrust`), not voids, as a history-of-ideas treatment. That is not recommended now.
