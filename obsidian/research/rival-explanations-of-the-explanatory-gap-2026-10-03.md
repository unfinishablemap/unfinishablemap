## Executive Summary

The explanatory gap has at least six rival explanations in the literature: dualism (the gap is ontological); the phenomenal-concepts strategy in a fallacy form and a conceptual-isolation form; Type-B accounts that do without phenomenal concepts; lack-of-understanding or ignorance accounts; introspective-illusion accounts; and psychological-bias accounts. No source read runs the dualism–PCS comparison as an inference to the best explanation with loveliness criteria. The literature does supply criteria the parties share. Chalmers (2007) frames PCS as "a physical explanation of the explanatory gap" and makes the one explicitly comparative cost claim; Chalmers (2018) adds a contrast test and a "meta-problem challenge"; Papineau, Sundström and Chalmers all use an over-generation test; and Kammerer (2019) adds a reflective-stability test. Scored on these, the comparison splits by explanandum. Dualism does better on the first-person data (the justificatory asymmetry, the restriction of the felt gap to phenomenal states, the stability of the intuition under reflection), but each of those advantages rests on the contested master argument or is shared with ignorance and illusion accounts. Type-B does better on the third-person data, the problem reports themselves, which it explains at no cost while dualism explains them only through a causal role the Map holds as owed. Against Type-B the tier stays *compatible*.

## The Explananda

"The gap" names several data, and the rivals divide over which they must explain and which they may explain away. Chalmers (2007) states the target: if PCS succeeds, "we may not have a direct physical explanation of consciousness, but we will have the next best thing: a physical explanation of the explanatory gap", and "One might plausibly hold that if we have a physical explanation of all the epistemic data that generate arguments for dualism, then the force of these arguments will be undercut" (he concedes the point for the purposes of his paper). The table is the Map's reconstruction of those epistemic data.

| # | Explanandum | Kind | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | Phenomenal truths are not a priori derivable from physical truths | a priori | the epistemic gap of Chalmers 2007; granted by Type-B and dualism alike |
| E2 | The knowledge and conceivability intuitions, held to be correct | a priori | Type-B "accepts a robust understanding of our epistemic situation on which many of our problem intuitions (e.g. knowledge and conceivability intuitions) are correct" (Chalmers 2018, p. 21) |
| E3 | The felt gap: a mind–brain identity seems to leave something unexplained | psychological | Papineau 2011, p. 5 |
| E4 | Problem reports and judgments | psychological, third-person | "we need only explain the fact that we have the problem intuitions; we do not also need to explain their correctness" (Chalmers 2018, p. 21) |
| E5 | The contrast profile: the felt gap attaches to phenomenal states, not to mental states generally or to other recognitional concepts | psychological | Papineau 2011, p. 15; Chalmers 2018, pp. 20, 22 |
| E6 | The justificatory asymmetry between Mary and Zombie Mary | a priori, first-person | Chalmers 2007 |
| E7 | Reflective stability: careful reflection sustains the intuition | psychological | Kammerer ms. pp. 15, 20 |

E3, E4, E5 and E7 are facts about human psychology, open in principle to experiment; Papineau writes that "It would be useful to have further empirical investigation of this issue" (2011, p. 17). E1, E2 and E6 are a priori or first-person, and under the routing page's rule (L122) they bear on plausibility rather than tier.

## The Lot

| Rival | Where the gap comes from | Chalmers's type | Exemplars (verification level in §Key Sources) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dualism | an ontological gap, with acquaintance taken as primitive | D or E | the dualist of Chalmers 2007 |
| Fallacy accounts | deploying a phenomenal concept involves the experience it refers to, deploying a physical concept does not, and we project that difference onto the referents | B | Loar 1990, Papineau 1993/2002/2011, Tye 1999 (Kammerer's family; Chalmers 2018's "use-mention fallacy") |
| Conceptual-isolation PCS | recognitional, quotational or constitutional concepts make the identity a posteriori and non-derivable, and the gap's uniqueness follows from theirs | B | Loar (via Chalmers 1999), Balog 2012, Carruthers & Veillet 2007 |
| Type-B without phenomenal concepts | special features of experiences or sensory representations, not of concepts | B | Tye 2008; Fazekas 2011 |
| Lack of understanding | we lack the concepts or truths that would make the identity intelligible | C (Stoljar 2006) or F (McGinn, per Chalmers) | Stoljar 2005, 2006; McGinn 1989; Nagel (per Kammerer) |
| Introspective illusion | introspection misrepresents our states as having qualities nothing has | A (strong) or B (lower-order weak) | Pereboom 2011, Frankish 2016, Graziano 2013, Humphrey 2011 (per Kammerer) |
| Psychological bias | dual-process conflict, separate cognitive modules, identifiable biases | neutral | Fiala, Arico & Nichols 2011; Bloom 2004 (via Papineau 2011); Berent 2024 |

Russellian monism (type-F) also explains the gap, through physics' silence on intrinsic natures. [[russellian-monism]] and [[ignorance-hypothesis]] cover it, and the page should name it in one clause as a member of the lot. The bias accounts explain the intuition and are silent on its correctness, so they combine with any metaphysics and compete with dualism only where dualism uses problem intuitions as data.

## Key Sources

### Chalmers, "Phenomenal Concepts and the Explanatory Gap" (2007)
- **URL**: https://consc.net/papers/pceg.html (author's version); https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195171655.003.0009
- **Type**: Book chapter, in T. Alter & S. Walter (eds.), *Phenomenal Concepts and Phenomenal Knowledge*, OUP, pp. 167–194. The author's version gives 2006; the corpus cites the 2007 imprint.
- **Access**: full text (author's version)
- **Key points**:
  - Frames PCS as an explanation of the gap in competition with dualism's: "we may not have a direct physical explanation of consciousness, but we will have the next best thing: a physical explanation of the explanatory gap."
  - The dilemma's two premises: "(1) If P&~C is conceivable, then C is not physically explicable" and "(2) If P&~C is not conceivable, then C cannot explain our epistemic situation."
  - **The comparative cost passage**, the nearest thing to a second-order comparison in the texts read: "If one rejects physicalism, there is no obvious problem in accommodating these epistemic features of consciousness." A dualist can take acquaintance as primitive because "the dualist is already committed to positing primitive mental features", whereas "The physicalist must either explain the features, or accept a further explanatory gap."
  - Conclusion: "For this reason, the phenomenal concept strategy cannot reconcile ontological physicalism with the explanatory gap."
  - The justificatory asymmetry (E6): Mary's belief "is plausibly justified with something approaching Cartesian certainty", while "there is a strong intuition that Zombie Mary's corresponding belief is not justified to the same extent".
- **Tenet alignment**: Supports Tenet 1 against Type-B, conditionally on the master argument. The cost passage favours any view that rejects physicalism (D, E or F), so it does not single out interactionism.
- **Map use**: The master argument is already the centrepiece of [[phenomenal-concepts-strategy]] (L67–85). The "next best thing" framing and the cost passage are new to the corpus, and they make the comparison explicit.

### Chalmers, "The Meta-Problem of Consciousness" (2018)
- **URL**: https://consc.net/papers/metaproblem.pdf
- **Type**: Journal article, *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 25(9–10), 6–61
- **Access**: full text (published version, author's upload)
- **Key points**:
  - Separates two explananda that PCS and dualism weigh differently. Solving the meta-problem needs only the having of problem intuitions, not "their correctness" (p. 21), whereas type-B PCS "accepts a robust understanding of our epistemic situation" on which knowledge and conceivability intuitions are correct (p. 21).
  - **The contrast criterion**: "Contrasting these intuitions with our related intuitions about phenomena such as colour and belief can help us to determine whether a proposed mechanism explains what is distinctive about the phenomenal case" (p. 20). Against recognitional concepts: "When we recognize a cactus, we do not have problem intuitions anything like those we have in the phenomenal case" (p. 22). The feature doing the work, he adds, must be one that both explains the intuitions and is physically explicable: "if so it is this feature that will be doing the explanatory work" (p. 22).
  - On the use-mention fallacy, crediting Sundström: "the strategy also over-generates to falsely suggest that we should not accept all sorts of identities that we in fact accept"; "So I am inclined to set this strategy aside as one of the least promising explanations" (p. 32). Footnote 25 records that "Papineau (2011) responds to Sundstrom, and Kammerer (2018b) responds in turn to Papineau".
  - **The meta-problem challenge**: "If a theory says that mechanism M is the basis of consciousness, then it needs to explain how mechanism M plays a central role in bringing about judgments about consciousness" (p. 36). Footnote 28 ties it to his 1990 "coherence test".
  - On interactionist realism: "It is by no means straightforward to see how consciousness could play precisely the role required, either on a panpsychist or an interactionist picture (or even on a biological or quantum picture)" (p. 42). In the conclusion, of non-epiphenomenalist realism: "once we can explain our conviction that consciousness exists without assuming that consciousness exists, the fact that the conviction is true seems somewhat miraculous" (p. 56).
  - Debunking target: "one could make a debunking argument against non-reductionist views of consciousness" (p. 44).
  - Footnote 12 (p. 19) reports Bogardus (2013) as arguing that proposals "due to Fiala, Nagel, Loar, and Papineau, and others concerning the source of our dualist intuitions do not succeed as defeaters for arguments for dualism".
  - Machine reports, n. 26 (p. 35): "The Muehlhauser/Shlegeris system mirrors at least some aspects of our phenomenal reports, while being so simple that most people would deny that it is conscious."
  - Verdict on the field: "neither illusionism nor realism has a truly satisfactory response to the charge of absurdity" (p. 56).
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral overall. The contrast criterion favours dualism over fallacy accounts; the meta-problem challenge favours Type-B over dualism.

### Papineau, "What Exactly is the Explanatory Gap?" (2011)
- **URL**: https://www.davidpapineau.co.uk/uploads/1/8/5/5/18551740/explanatorygappdf.pdf ; https://doi.org/10.1007/s11406-010-9273-6
- **Type**: Journal article, *Philosophia* 39(1), 5–19, in a special issue, "Consciousness, Reductionism and the Explanatory Gap" (ed. L. de Bruin & A. Newen), that also carries Fazekas 2011
- **Access**: full text (published version, author's upload)
- **Key points**:
  - Reverses the direction of explanation: "The feeling of an ‘explanatory gap’ arises only because we cannot stop ourselves thinking about the mind-brain relation in a dualist way" (p. 5); the felt gap "stems from an implicit commitment to dualism" (p. 8); "if only we were able fully to persuade ourselves of mind-brain identities, we would see that they leave nothing unexplained. The problem is that we find it very hard to believe such identities" (p. 11).
  - The Type-B explanation of E3 is therefore two-stage: the felt gap is explained by a dualist intuition, and that intuition by something else. Papineau weighs four candidates for the something else: Bloom's separate mindreading and folk-physics modules, the antipathetic fallacy, "aspirations of transparency", and Melnyk's failure to "merge the files".
  - He applies the over-generation test himself, against Bloom: "The problem is that Bloom’s story seems to overgeneralize"; "It looks as if the intuition of dualism hinges on the way we think specifically about phenomenological states, rather than about mental states in general" (p. 15).
  - On his own fallacy, "it has always seemed to me a very seductive fallacy" (p. 16). Sundström "argues that it predicts dualist intuitions in cases where there are none" (p. 16), with the identity "The experience of seeing something as red = John’s most salient current experience". Papineau: "Sundström’s point is well-taken, but I am not sure it is conclusive" (p. 17). He offers two escape routes (the experience is surreptitiously activated on the right-hand side, or not activated on the left) and calls for experiment (p. 17).
  - On transparency: "Are people in general intuitively attracted to dualism because they assume that phenomenal introspection must reveal the essential nature of phenomenal states? I am not sure." (p. 17). This links the comparison to [[revelation-thesis]].
- **Tenet alignment**: Conflicts with Tenet 1. Its over-generation test is nonetheless the strongest dualist resource in the comparison.

### Kammerer, "Does the Explanatory Gap Rest on a Fallacy?" (2019)
- **URL**: http://hdl.handle.net/2078.1/229392 (accepted manuscript); https://doi.org/10.1007/s13164-018-0424-1
- **Type**: Journal article, *Review of Philosophy and Psychology* 10(4), 649–667 (online 2018). Chalmers 2018 cites it as "Kammerer (ms)".
- **Access**: full text (accepted manuscript; page numbers are the manuscript's)
- **Key points**:
  - Abstract: "One of the most common strategies to do so consists in interpreting the alleged “explanatory gap” between phenomenal states and physical states as resulting from a fallacy, or a cognitive illusion." And: "In this paper, I argue that the explanatory gap does not rest on a fallacy or a cognitive illusion. This does not imply the falsity of physicalism, but it has consequences on the kind of physicalism we should embrace."
  - **The taxonomy**: Fallacy Accounts, of which Papineau's is "the most typical example", and two physicalist alternatives, "Introspective Illusion Accounts" and "Lack of Understanding Accounts" (ms. p. 8). The latter "have been defended in different forms by various authors in the last decades (McGinn, 1989; Nagel, 1974, 2000, Stoljar, 2005, 2006)" (ms. p. 9). He takes the second label from Sundström (2008, 2011).
  - **The criterion, reflective stability**: fallacies are not sustained by careful reflection and the intuition of distinctness is, so "the psychological process that causes it is more similar to a case of valid reasoning than to a fallacy" (ms. p. 15); "Careful reflection sustains intuitions about experiences – not simply about how it feels to use certain concepts" (ms. p. 20). He grants that his stronger claim, that only careful reflection produces the intuition, "may be less easy to support" (ms. p. 16).
  - **The bad-lot consequence**: "by arguing against Fallacy Accounts, we may give some indirect weight to the disjunction of these two views" (ms. p. 9).
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral to Tenet 1. It removes one physicalist explanation, and the beneficiaries are its physicalist siblings as much as dualism. Kammerer defends illusionism elsewhere ([[illusionism]] L111, L123).

### Stoljar, "Physicalism and Phenomenal Concepts" (2005)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0268-1064.2005.00296.x
- **Type**: Journal article, *Mind & Language* 20(5), 469–494
- **Access**: publisher abstract
- **Key points**: The paper names the strategy (Chalmers 2007: "Following Stoljar (2005), we can call this the phenomenal concept strategy") and "argues that no version of the strategy is successful". Its abstract ends: "The paper ends by contrasting the phenomenal concept strategy with a rival strategy—I call it the missing concept strategy. I suggest that the missing concept strategy presents a more plausible response to the issues about physicalism and experience." It is the one verified source that sets two physicalist explanations of the gap against each other and ranks them.
- **Tenet alignment**: Conflicts with Tenet 1; its ranking cuts against PCS on physicalist grounds.

### Tye (1999) and Tye (2008)
- **URLs**: https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/108.432.705 ; https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/9780262012737.001.0001
- **Type**: Journal article, *Mind* 108(432), 705–725; monograph, *Consciousness Revisited: Materialism without Phenomenal Concepts*, MIT Press (Crossref dates it 2008-12-12; elsewhere the corpus cites it as 2009)
- **Access**: publisher abstracts
- **Key points**: The 1999 abstract: "In supposing otherwise, philosophers have fallen prey to a cognitive illusion, induced largely by a failure to recognize the special character of phenomenal concepts." Ten years later the same author "argues that the strategy is mistaken" (publisher's book abstract), and the chapter "Physicalism and the Appeal to Phenomenal Concepts" aims "to show that the view assuming a conjugal relationship between physicalism and phenomenal concepts is gravely mistaken" (chapter abstract). For the article this shows that Type-B is not identical with PCS: a Type-B physicalist can abandon the phenomenal-concepts explanation and look for another.

### Fazekas, "Cognitive Architecture and the Epistemic Gap: Defending Physicalism without Phenomenal Concepts" (2011)
- **URL**: http://real.mtak.hu/8550/1/CogArch%26EpistGap%20%40%20Philosophia.pdf (penultimate draft); https://doi.org/10.1007/s11406-010-9274-5
- **Type**: Journal article, *Philosophia* 39(1), 21–29
- **Access**: full text (penultimate draft)
- **Key points**: "the account provided is an alternative to the Phenomenal Concept Strategy"; "It is argued that certain sensory representations, as accessed by higher cognition, lack constituent structure." The conceptual features PCS invokes are "only symptoms which can themselves be explained in terms of the features of sensory representational states". Of the Type-B explanations read, this one makes the most direct cognitive-architecture claim, and so it is the most exposed to empirical test.

### Berent, "Consciousness isn't 'hard'—it's human psychology that makes it so!" (2024)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/nc/niae016
- **Type**: Journal article, *Neuroscience of Consciousness* 2024(1), niae016
- **Access**: abstract
- **Key points**: Sets the ontological explanation of the gap against a psychological one: "problem intuitions" are "linked to psychological biases, so they cannot be trusted to reflect what consciousness is", and "I conclude that the roots of the "hard problem" are partly psychological." It is the only experimental source in the comparison, and it is already discussed at [[meta-problem-of-consciousness]] L133.

## Who Compares Them, and How (Q1)

No source read runs dualism and PCS as rival explanations ranked by loveliness. Across all fetched texts, "abduct" and "lovel" do not occur, and "best explanation" occurs once, in Chalmers 2003, about how protophenomenal properties might be known. Positive control: "explanatory power" returns two hits in Papineau 2011. The comparison exists under other names.

1. **Chalmers 2007** sets the target ("a physical explanation of the explanatory gap") and makes the one explicitly comparative cost claim: a dualist primitive against a physicalist choice between explaining the features and a further gap.
2. **Chalmers 2018** sets desiderata for any explanation of problem intuitions (topic-neutrality, contrast with colour, belief and cactus recognition, and the meta-problem challenge) and ranks candidates, placing the use-mention fallacy among "the least promising explanations".
3. **Papineau 2011** gives the Type-B explanation in two stages and weighs four candidates for the second by the over-generation test.
4. **Kammerer 2019** sorts physicalist explanations into three families and tests one of them by its psychological profile.
5. **Stoljar 2005** ranks two physicalist strategies, PCS and the missing-concept strategy, and prefers the latter.
6. **Bogardus 2013**, as Chalmers reports it, asks whether debunking explanations defeat the arguments for dualism, which is the dialectical form the comparison takes.
7. **Berent 2024** sets ontology against psychology using experiments.

The IBE framing is the Map's. What these texts call an explanation "of the gap", an explanation of "problem intuitions" or a "debunking" explanation is a rival explanation of a datum in Lipton's sense, and the criteria below are drawn from them.

## Criteria and Scores (Q2)

Each row names who states the criterion and whose scoring is reported.

**C1, scope.** Each rival fails a different explanandum. Dualism explains the first-person data at a stroke, since a real ontological gap would be felt, would be restricted to phenomenal states, and would sustain reflection. It reaches the reports (E4) only if consciousness helps cause them. Type-B explains the reports at once and owes an account of the justificatory asymmetry (E6) that the master argument says it cannot give.

**C2, contrast.** Every party uses this criterion: Papineau against Bloom ("seems to overgeneralize"), Sundström against Papineau, and Chalmers against indexical, recognitional and use-mention accounts. Dualism passes trivially. The fallacy form fails Sundström's case unless one of Papineau's two escape routes works, and he calls the point "well-taken". The conceptual-isolation form passes by construction, since it holds phenomenal concepts to be sui generis; Loar "accepts explicitly that there are no other counterexamples" ([[primitive-identities-and-strong-necessities]] L79, quoting Chalmers 1999). That moves the dispute to C4. Ignorance and illusion accounts pass whenever their mechanism is specific to phenomenal states.

**C3, the meta-problem challenge.** Type-B identifies the basis of consciousness with the mechanism that produces judgments about it, so the challenge is met without further posits. Dualism meets it only in its realizationist form. On [[meta-problem-of-consciousness]] the interactionist reply "removes a defeater" (L75), and the modal and explanatory forms of the debunking argument "remain open" (L87). Chalmers aims the debunking argument "against non-reductionist views of consciousness" (p. 44), so the coincidence charge is dualism's to answer and not Type-B's. This is the only criterion scored on uncontroversially empirical data (E4), and it favours Type-B.

**C4, independent motivation.** The ad hoc charge ([[phenomenal-concepts-strategy]] L121–125) has a mirror image. Dualism's acquaintance relation and psychophysical law are also posited to explain these data, and nothing outside the consciousness debate motivates either. Chalmers (2018, p. 22) puts the burden precisely: some feature of phenomenal concepts may both explain the intuitions and be physically explicable, "but if so it is this feature that will be doing the explanatory work". Fazekas's structureless sensory representations are one attempt to name such a feature with independent cognitive-architecture content. Map's scoring: symmetric.

**C5, cost in primitives.** Chalmers's comparative passage is the strongest dualist point in the comparison and the only explicitly comparative one: the dualist "is already committed to positing primitive mental features", while "The physicalist must either explain the features, or accept a further explanatory gap." Three discounts apply. The passage holds only if the master argument holds, since a physically explicable feature would let the physicalist explain it. By the parity result, Type-B's bridge is already epistemically primitive, so the count comes to one primitive each ([[primitive-identities-and-strong-necessities]] L59). And the Map's interactionism adds selection laws (same page, L107), so the Map's own dualism scores worse here than dualism in general. Tenet 5 discounts the count of kinds on both sides.

**C6, reflective stability.** Kammerer argues the intuition of distinctness behaves like the output of valid reasoning, which tells against the fallacy accounts. Dualism, the ignorance accounts and the illusion accounts all predict the same stability, which is why Kammerer ends with a physicalist disjunction. The point is introspective and armchair; Kammerer grants that his stronger claim "may be less easy to support". The [[phenomenal-concepts-strategy]] page's persistence paragraph (L139) is an informal version of this criterion.

**C7, the dilemma.** Chalmers concludes that PCS "cannot reconcile ontological physicalism with the explanatory gap". Díaz-León (2010) concludes that he "has not posed any serious problem", and Carruthers and Veillet (2007) charge equivocation; [[phenomenal-concepts-strategy]] L79 records the exchange as open. The dilemma rests on zombie conceivability, the premise [[positions/arguments-for-dualism#^p-d1|P-D1]] treats as shared across the conceivability cluster.

**Result (Map's scoring).** Dualism leads on C2 and C6, against the fallacy form only and shared with the ignorance and illusion accounts, and on C5, conditional on C7. Type-B leads on C3, the one criterion scored on third-person empirical data. C1 and C4 are ties and C7 is open. The second-order comparison therefore does not favour dualism by stated criteria. Its one dualist result that is specific to dualism (C5) depends on the master argument, which the routing page already counts as a separate reply (L91), so it adds no independent evidence.

## Discriminating Predictions (Q3)

1. **The over-generation contrast.** Fallacy accounts predict felt gaps wherever a phenomenal concept faces a co-referring term that does not activate the experience, as in Sundström's "John's most salient current experience" case; dualism predicts none outside phenomenal–physical identities. The data are armchair intuitions, Papineau has two replies, and he asks for experiments (2011, p. 17). This discriminates dualism from the fallacy family only. The conceptual-isolation form, the ignorance accounts and the illusion accounts make dualism's prediction too.
2. **Reflective stability.** Fallacy accounts predict that careful reflection weakens the intuition; dualism, the ignorance accounts and the illusion accounts predict that it does not. This discriminates against the fallacy family only (Kammerer).
3. **AI and zombie systems.** PCS-style and illusion accounts predict that any system with the right architecture produces problem reports. Chalmers takes the design stance toward a robot, reports Molyneux's (2012) argument that a robot "would inevitably have problem intuitions", and notes that a simple system already mirrors "some aspects of our phenomenal reports" (pp. 20, 33, 35). On the Map's own pages, interactionist dualism licenses no prediction of a divergence: [[meta-problem-of-consciousness]] L111 holds that a mindless system's reports "would be statistically ordinary anyway" under the Born-preserving mechanism. By the Map's own concession, then, this does not discriminate. A dualist who predicted that architecture-only systems lack problem intuitions would have a discriminating claim, but the Map has disowned it.
4. **Uniqueness among a posteriori identities.** Both rivals predict it: Type-B through Loar's sui generis concepts ([[primitive-identities-and-strong-necessities]] L79). It does not discriminate unless the master argument succeeds.
5. **Psychological variation.** Bias accounts predict that problem intuitions vary with identifiable biases, and Berent (2024) reports experiments finding that they partly do. Dualism need not deny some bias, but the result weakens problem intuitions as evidence for any face-value reading, so it costs the dualist's use of E3, E5 and E7 more than it costs Type-B. It favours the physicalist rivals mildly and does not separate dualism from Type-B on the gap itself.
6. **The too-strong/too-weak dilemma** (Chalmers 2007) constrains any concept-based explanation but makes no prediction. The dilemma that [[meta-problem-of-consciousness]] L69 aims at illusionism, a solution either too simple to explain the intuitions or too powerful to stay non-phenomenal, has the same form.
7. **Tenet 3's outcome deviation.** The only discriminator between dualism and every physicalist rival is an intention-conditioned departure from Born statistics, and no such test has been run ([[inference-to-the-best-explanation-against-dualism]] L82).

## Tier Under P-M1 (Q4)

- **Against Type-B: *compatible*.** The second-order comparison has been run here by stated criteria. It favours Type-B on the one empirical criterion, favours dualism only conditionally on the master argument, and finds the remaining dualist advantages shared with physicalist rivals. Under [[positions/methodology-and-calibration#^p-m1|P-M1]], defeater-removal (the master argument against PCS, Tenet 3 against the coincidence charge) raises no tier.
- **Against the fallacy family within Type-B: *compatible* now.** If experiments confirmed the over-generation contrast and reflective stability, the result would be *suggestive* against that family without separating dualism from the ignorance and illusion accounts, the same verdict the routing page gives Type-C (L123).
- **The IBE page's provisional *suggestive* against Type-B (L74, L80) should read *compatible*.** The comparison it waited on has been run and does not lift the tier.
- **What would move it.**
  - Experimental contrast and stability data: *suggestive* against fallacy accounts, non-separating.
  - A topic-neutral mechanism that reproduces the full profile (E4, E5, E7) in an artificial system: strengthens Type-B and illusion accounts, and removes dualism's lead on E5 and E7.
  - General acceptance that the replies to the master argument fail: plausibility only, since the argument is a priori.
  - Tenet 3's outcome deviation: *discriminating*.

## Major Positions

On the second-order question itself there are four positions.

### Dualism explains the gap better
- **Proponents**: Chalmers 2007; Bogardus 2013 (per Chalmers 2018)
- **Core claim**: no physically explicable feature of phenomenal concepts explains the epistemic situation, while dualism accommodates it with a primitive it already has.
- **Relation to site tenets**: aligns with Tenet 1; reaches Type-B only through the master argument.

### Type-B explains the gap, and the felt gap is a symptom
- **Proponents**: Papineau 1993, 2002, 2011; Loar 1990; Tye 1999; Balog 2012; Díaz-León 2010; Carruthers & Veillet 2007
- **Core claim**: the gap is predicted by identity plus the special nature of phenomenal concepts; the felt gap comes from a dualist intuition, which has a psychological explanation.
- **Relation to site tenets**: conflicts with Tenet 1; it is the live opponent the routing page names.

### Neither: a different physicalist explanation
- **Proponents**: Stoljar 2005, 2006 (missing concepts); Tye 2008 and Fazekas 2011 (no phenomenal concepts); Kammerer 2019 (illusion or lack of understanding)
- **Core claim**: PCS mislocates the source of the gap.
- **Relation to site tenets**: conflicts with Tenet 1, and it is why defeating PCS does not deliver dualism.

### Standoff
- **Proponents**: Chalmers 2018, who concludes that "neither illusionism nor realism has a truly satisfactory response to the charge of absurdity"
- **Relation to site tenets**: matches the Map's *compatible* verdict.

## Key Debates

1. **Is the felt gap evidence or symptom?** Chalmers treats the epistemic situation as data that PCS must explain; Papineau treats the felt gap as a product of an implicit dualism that science shows false. Ongoing.
2. **Does the antipathetic fallacy over-generate?** Sundström 2008; Papineau 2011 ("well-taken" but not "conclusive"); Kammerer 2018 (Croatian J. Phil., not read); Chalmers 2018 (sets it aside). Unresolved, and untested experimentally.
3. **Is the intuition a fallacy at all?** Kammerer 2019 says no, on the evidence of its psychological profile, and moves the physicalist to illusion or lack of understanding.
4. **Can any concept-based explanation escape the master argument?** Chalmers 2007; Díaz-León 2010; Carruthers & Veillet 2007; Sasaki 2025 (on [[phenomenal-concepts-strategy]]). Open.
5. **Who owes the meta-problem?** Chalmers 2018 asks every theory to meet the meta-problem challenge. Type-B meets it cheaply, and dualism meets it only through a causal role for consciousness that the Map holds as owed.

## Map Relevance

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]].** Against Type-B, Tenet 1 stays a commitment rather than a result. The comparison shows where the dualist case is strongest (the primitive-cost asymmetry, conditional on the master argument) and where it is weakest (the reports, which Type-B explains without remainder).

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]].** The one empirical criterion (C3) is where Tenet 3 matters. Interactionism is the form of dualism that can meet Chalmers's meta-problem challenge at all, because on it consciousness helps produce judgments about consciousness. The Map holds that role as available rather than actual ([[inference-to-the-best-explanation-against-dualism]] L66), so the tenet turns a dualist failure on C3 into an unpaid debt. It removes a defeater and raises no tier.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]].** The tenet discounts the count-of-kinds strand of C5 on both sides and leaves C2, C3 and C6 untouched, since none of them is a simplicity criterion. Kind's charge that physicalism is treated as a default ([[ignorance-hypothesis]] L102) binds here too: neither rival is entitled to win ties.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]].** No direct bearing. The mechanism debt (P-Q10) is the price dualism pays to compete on C3.

**[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]].** Orthogonal.

**The register.** The dualist advantages on C2 and C6 are shared with ignorance and illusion accounts. Like the routing page's Type-C verdict, they move credence toward several views together, and under [[positions/methodology-and-calibration#^p-m2|P-M2]]'s convergence discount they are not separate evidence. Kammerer's taxonomy makes [[positions/arguments-for-dualism#^p-d1|P-D1]]'s description of PCS as "the mainstream physicalist statement of exactly this shared premise" (L46) one of three physicalist diagnoses. The open positions-evolve P3 on Stoljar is the place to record that.
