## Executive Summary

Strawson's "Persons" (1958; revised as *Individuals* ch. 3, 1959) is the classic statement of the **conceptual** problem of other minds turned into an argument. One can ascribe states of consciousness to oneself only if one can ascribe them to others. One can ascribe them to others only if one can identify other subjects. Subjects cannot be identified "only as subjects of experience". So the concept of a *person*, a single type of thing that bears both mental ("P-") and corporeal ("M-") predicates, is primitive, and the concept of a pure individual consciousness can exist at most as "a secondary, non-primitive concept". Behaviour then supplies "logically adequate" criteria for other-ascription. Those criteria do not exhaust the meaning of the predicates, so the view is not behaviourism. The sceptical problem cannot coherently be stated.

**For the Map this is a challenge, not support.** It does not touch Tenet 1 at tenet level: Strawson's P-predicates are not analysable into M-predicates, and he never argues physicalism. It does attack Cartesian two-subject dualism directly. It also attacks **Posit One's** "primitive subject individuation … prior to and independent of any physical or functional criterion". Ayer, who rejected the argument, still judged that its reasons "apply equally to any idea of a non-physical subject of consciousness".

The Map has a principled way out. Gomes (2011) shows that Strawson's identification requirement rests on a verificationist principle of significance. Ayer (1963) shows that grasping a general concept requires only that it *could* apply to others, not knowing that it does. That way out is a rejection of a premise, though, not a refutation. It leaves a framework-boundary disagreement.

The residue the Map has *not* answered is Gomes's own non-verificationist version. A causal bridge from behaviour to experience "can [be grasped] only if one understands the items which are so related". So the interactionist's "behaviour as causal trace" advantage is evidential, and it presupposes the concept of another's experience rather than supplying it. Gomes's embodiment answer also asks for a "non-contingent link between sensation and behaviour" at the level of presentation. `concepts/problem-of-other-minds` says dualism treats that link as contingent. Whether the two can be held together is open.

## Key Sources

### Strawson, P. F., "Persons" (1958) — the primary text
- **URL**: https://hdl.handle.net/11299/184616 (UMN Digital Conservancy; PDF and extracted text are open).
- **Publication of record**: H. Feigl, M. Scriven & G. Maxwell (eds.), *Concepts, Theories, and the Mind-Body Problem*, Minnesota Studies in the Philosophy of Science vol. 2. University of Minnesota Press, 1958, pp. 330–353. The editors and publisher were checked against the volume's title page in the Conservancy "preliminaries" item, 11299/184623.
- **Page mapping**: the extracted text puts each folio at the *foot* of its page. The last folio, 353, closes the paper, which confirms the mapping. Page numbers below are 1958 pages.
- **Key points and verbatim text**:
  - The two opening questions (p. 330): why are states of consciousness ascribed to anything at all, and why to the same thing as corporeal characteristics?
  - Cartesianism is "a dualism of two subjects (two types of subject)" (p. 336). The "no-ownership" doctrine, which Strawson constructs from remarks by Wittgenstein (1930–33, per Moore) and Schlick, is "a dualism of one subject (the body) and one non-subject" (pp. 336–337). Strawson argues it is incoherent, because states "owe their identity as particulars to the identity of the person whose states or experiences they are" (p. 336). That is his ground for non-transferable ownership.
  - The core premiss, as published in 1958: "it is a necessary condition of one's ascribing states of consciousness, experiences, to oneself, in the way one does, that one should also ascribe them (or be prepared to ascribe them) to others who are not oneself" (pp. 337–338). The footnote on that sentence concedes that only a "lesser claim" is needed. On that claim one need only be prepared to ascribe the predicates to others "on appropriate occasions". The footnote adds: "The main point here is a purely logical one" (p. 338).
  - The chain: "one can ascribe states of consciousness to oneself only if one can ascribe them to others; one can ascribe them to others only if one can identify other subjects of experience; and one cannot identify others if one can identify them only as subjects of experience, possessors of states of consciousness" (p. 339).
  - "What we have to acknowledge, in order to begin to free ourselves from these difficulties, is the primitiveness of the concept of a person" (p. 340). A person is "a type of entity such that both predicates ascribing states of consciousness and predicates ascribing corporeal characteristics, a physical situation, etc. are equally applicable to a single individual of that single type" (p. 340).
  - "So the concept of the pure individual consciousness—the pure ego—is a concept that cannot exist; or, at least, cannot exist as a primary concept in terms of which the concept of a person can be explained or analyzed. It can only exist, if at all, as a secondary, non-primitive concept" (p. 341). The OCR prints the dashes as hyphens. Also on p. 341: "The concept of a person is not to be analyzed as that of an animated body or of an embodied anima."
  - The disembodiment concession: "we might at least think of a disembodied person, retaining the logical benefit of individuality from having been a person" (pp. 341–342). Its footnote reads: "A little further thought will show how limited this concession is."
  - M-predicates are those also applied to material bodies ("weighs 10 stone"). P-predicates are "all the other predicates we apply to persons" ("is smiling", "is in pain", "believes in God") (p. 342).
  - Criteria: "the behavior criteria one goes on are not just signs of the presence of what is meant by the P-predicate, but are criteria of a logically adequate kind for the ascription of the P-predicate" (p. 344). The anti-sceptical status is stated carefully: "The point is not that we must accept this conclusion in order to avoid skepticism, but that we must accept it in order to explain the existence of the conceptual scheme in terms of which the skeptical problem is stated" (p. 344). He also says "The conclusion here is, of course, not new" (p. 344). That is the Wittgensteinian inheritance; he claims a new argument for it.
  - Anti-behaviourism: "In order to have this type of concept, one must be both a self-ascriber and an other-ascriber of such predicates, and must see every other as a self-ascriber" (p. 346). "And so we oscillate between philosophical skepticism and philosophical behaviorism" (p. 347). The gap argument runs: "But if only mine, then not mine at all"; "not even the syntax of the premises of the inference exists if the gap exists" (p. 347). The Queen of Hearts analogy follows. To call the criteria logically adequate "is not to say that all there is to the ascriptive meaning of these predicates is these criteria" (p. 348).
  - On other minds: "I have already argued that such a 'solution' or 'justification' is impossible, that the demand for it cannot be coherently stated" (p. 349).
  - On group minds: "there is no sense in speaking of the individual consciousness just as such, of the individual subject of experience just as such: for there is no way of identifying such pure entities" (p. 351).
  - Closing (p. 353): Wittgenstein's remarks "seem needlessly to flout the conceptual scheme we actually employ". Strawson proposes instead: "For each user of the language, there is just one person in ascribing to whom states of consciousness he does not need to use the criteria of the observed behavior of that person (though he does not necessarily not do so); and that person is himself."
- **Tenet alignment**: conflicts with Posit One and with Cartesian substance dualism. It is neutral to congenial on Tenet 1's bare irreducibility claim (see "What Strawson's view does to the Map's dualism" below).

### Ayer, A. J., "The Concept of a Person" (1963)
- **Publication**: *The Concept of a Person and Other Essays*, London: Macmillan (New York: St Martin's Press), 1963. The essay runs pp. 82–128 (contents page: essay 4 at p. 82, essay 5 at p. 129). Full text: https://archive.org/details/conceptofpersono00ayer
- **Key points (verbatim, pages from the running heads)**:
  - **Scope** (p. 86): "the reasons which Mr. Strawson has for denying that there can be a primary concept of the pure ego apply equally to any idea of a non-physical subject of consciousness, whatever its composition may be thought to be." **This is the sentence that reaches the Map.** Strawson's argument does not stop at the pure ego.
  - Ayer notes that Strawson "does not in fact discuss the thesis of physicalism" (p. 89). Ayer argues that physicalism escapes the no-ownership incoherence charge (p. 90).
  - **Logical adequacy** (p. 95): it is not entailment, which would collapse into physicalism, and not inductive support, which would collapse back into analogy. "What is envisaged is something between the two, but what can this be?"
  - **The circularity charge fails** (pp. 104–106). The generality of a concept only requires that one admit others *could* satisfy it. That "is perfectly compatible with the view that one does not know, or even with the view that one cannot know, that such an ascription is ever true in fact" (p. 105). "It seems to me, therefore, that Mr. Strawson's argument needs a stronger premiss than the one that he states" (p. 106): that one cannot be invariably mistaken. Ayer then argues that premiss is false.
  - **The automata child** (pp. 106–108). A child raised among responsive automata, and taught language by a machine voice, learns to apply the concept of a person to himself and to the automata. So the criteria "may determine no more than that some locution is correct, that in such and such conditions this is the proper thing to say; it does not necessarily follow that what is said is true" (p. 108).
  - Verdict (p. 109): Ayer agrees "that we ought to try to find a middle way", but "his notion of logical adequacy is rather too nebulous to bear the weight which he puts upon it."
  - Ayer's own view (p. 116): "I am, however, inclined to think that personal identity depends upon the identity of the body". He then defends a form of the no-ownership doctrine against Strawson's incoherence charge (pp. 116–117).
- **Tenet alignment**: Ayer is no dualist, but his two objections are the Map's resources. Concept grasp is not knowledge of instantiation, and the criteria may be satisfied by non-conscious systems. The automata case also anticipates the Map's AI stance (see Side findings).

### Gomes, A. (2011), "Is There a Problem of Other Minds?"
- **Publication**: *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 111(3pt3): 353–373. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9264.2011.00314.x (Crossref-verified). Read via the preprint, https://web.archive.org/web/2024id_/https://philarchive.org/archive/GOMITA. The abstract was also matched against OpenAlex.
- **Key points**:
  - It states the conceptual problem as "(CP): How is it possible for us to think about other minds?" (§2). The phrasing is generic, but Gomes uses it as a labelled formulation.
  - The **Knowledge Obstacle**, which Gomes reads in Strawson 1959 pp. 99–100, and the **Separation Obstacle**, from Wittgenstein PI §§281–307, each rely on a link between meaning and verification (§2).
  - Strawson's identification requirement is needed for the "Mental Placement" argument. Gomes's point: "if identification simply means the capacity to single out an individual in thought, then it is not clear why thoughts about Cartesian souls or causally relevant bodily locations will not suffice." Strawson's stronger requirement comes from the principle of significance (*Bounds of Sense* 1966, p. 164, as quoted by Gomes): "It is this principle of significance which places an identification condition on mental ascription incompatible with the ascription of mental predicates to Cartesian souls … Without this expression of verificationism, the Mental Placement argument falls short." Footnote 18 adds that Strawson admitted "contentment with a certain weak form of verificationism" (Strawson 1976). This note has that only via Gomes.
  - **Gomes's own non-verificationist version** (§3). If sensation and behaviour are wholly detached, the presentation of sensation leaves no room to distinguish an event happening from its happening to me. He considers the causal repair: think of others' sensations as the causes of their behaviour. He rejects it for the conceptual problem: "And one can grasp this causal relation only if one understands the items which are so related." Theory-theory avoids the obstacle, but "such a divorcement seems to remove from our understanding of the mental any aspect of its phenomenology". His answer is **Embodiment**: mental occurrences are *presented* as spatial and temporal aspects of one's person (Brewer on bodily sensation).
  - §4 has the minimal form: "A minimal rejection of the gap between behaviour and sensation requires only that, in one's own case, sensations are presented as aspects of a spatially located person and thereby as intimately bound up in their behavioural manifestations." Also: "there may be ways of accepting this non-contingent link between sensation and behaviour which fall short of according perceptibility to the mental lives of others."
- **Tenet alignment**: mixed. The verificationism diagnosis gives Posit One a principled exit from Strawson. The §3 argument presses the interactionist's evidential reply in `concepts/problem-of-other-minds`. Its "non-contingent link" sits against that page's "dualism treats it as contingent" (L118).

## Major Positions

### Strawson's primitive-person view (1958/1959)
- **Core claim**: the person, the bearer of P- and M-predicates, is conceptually prior to "an individual consciousness". Behavioural criteria are logically adequate but do not exhaust meaning. Self-ascription is non-observational. Scepticism cannot be stated coherently.
- **Relation to tenets**: it targets the Cartesian compound and any subject individuated without criteria. It makes no reductive claim, so Tenet 1's bare irreducibility survives it (SEP: "dual-aspect" structure).

### Ayer's empiricist reply (1963)
- **Core claim**: the conceptual condition shows only that a mental concept must be *applicable* to others. "Logically adequate" is too nebulous. The argument from analogy is weak, but the analogical problem is not senseless.
- **Relation to tenets**: Ayer's conclusion (bodily identity, a no-ownership view) is not the Map's. His two objections are what the Map would borrow.

### Gomes's embodiment reformulation (2011)
- **Core claim**: drop verificationism and Strawson's version falls. A real conceptual problem survives for anyone who holds that sensation is presented as detached from behaviour. Recognising that mental life is *presented* as an aspect of an embodied person removes it.
- **Relation to tenets**: this is the strongest live form for the Map. It is answerable only if the Map can keep presentation-level embodiment apart from metaphysical identity. The Map has not argued that.

### Non-Cartesian substance dualism (Lowe)
- **Core claim**: a substantial self, distinct from the body, bears both mental and physical characteristics. Its identity is primitive.
- **Relation to tenets**: `topics/philosophy-of-action-under-dualism` already says the Map's action theory is "closest to E. J. Lowe's non-Cartesian substance dualism". This is the Map's best available *shape* against Strawson's two-subject target. Its primitive-identity clause still meets Strawson's identification requirement head on.

## Key Debates

### Does the conceptual problem dissolve the epistemological one?
- **Sides**: Strawson ("once the conclusion is accepted, the skeptical problem does not arise", p. 344) and Malcolm, against Ayer (p. 105), SEP §2 ("not yet to show that that concept is instantiated") and Gomes §4, who is wary of a "tight" connection.
- **Current state**: no consensus. Most now grant that the problems are distinct and that solving the conceptual one does not by itself settle knowledge.

### Is Strawson a behaviourist after all?
- **Sides**: critics see "a retreat into behaviorism" (SEP). Strawson's own text answers with the Queen of Hearts passage and the "oscillate" diagnosis. Ayer presses that "logical adequacy" is unstable between entailment and induction.
- **Current state**: open. The criteria literature (McDowell 1982, Wright) carries it on.

### Does the argument depend on verificationism?
- **Sides**: Gomes (it does, via the principle of significance), against Strawson, who accepted "a certain weak form of verificationism" (per Gomes fn 18). Stroud 1968 attacks the general transcendental-argument form; cited via Gomes, not read.
- **Current state**: most contemporary readers reject strong verificationism (Gomes §2), so the identification argument is widely judged to fall short as stated.

## What Strawson's View Does to the Map's Dualism (graded)

Calibration first: none of the following is evidence *for* dualism. Each item is a point of contact, graded by how directly it engages a Map commitment.

1. **Tenet 1, at tenet level (irreducibility only): no conflict; congenial, not supportive.** P-predicates are not analysable into M-predicates, and Strawson does not discuss physicalism (Ayer p. 89). That conceptual irreducibility is silent on ontology. A physicalist can hold it, and Strawson nowhere infers non-physicality. Do not enlist Strawson for Tenet 1.
2. **Cartesian two-subject dualism: direct hit, and the Map need not defend it.** The target is "a dualism of two subjects". The Map's operational subject is Lowe-shaped (philosophy-of-action L29), and Lowe's self has physical characteristics. So the "compound of two subjects" picture is not obligatory for the Map. That is this note's reconstruction.
3. **Posit One, primitive subject individuation "prior to and independent of any physical or functional criterion": direct challenge, at the framework boundary.** Ayer p. 86 confirms that the reasons reach any non-physical subject. The Map's exit is to reject the principle of significance. Gomes shows the identification requirement needs it, and Ayer shows concept grasp needs only applicability. Posit One *must* reject that principle, because it asserts a fact that has no criterion of application. That is a respectable rejection, held by most contemporary philosophers, but it does not refute Strawson. The Map's record on deflation has the same form ("cannot compel anyone to abandon"). This is also consistent with `topics/kants-paralogisms-and-the-maps-subject` §posit-not-paralogism. A posit escapes inference-based objections, but Strawson's objection is not inference-based. It concerns whether the posited item is a possible object of thought, so the posit does not escape it automatically.
4. **The interactionist's evidential advantage (`concepts/problem-of-other-minds` §The Interactionist's Advantage, L126): partial hit, unanswered.** Strawson p. 344 and Gomes §3 both say that a causal-sign reading of behaviour presupposes the general concept of another's experience. Bidirectional Interaction can therefore help with the *epistemological* problem only. Gomes's embodiment answer requires a presentation-level "non-contingent link"; the page says dualism treats the link as contingent (L118). The Map can reply that the link is non-contingent in how it is *presented* and contingent in what it *is*. That reply mirrors the Map's own rule that a phenomenological datum is not by itself a metaphysical one. It is **not argued anywhere in the corpus**; it is owed.
5. **Disembodied existence: low relevance.** Strawson grants only a "logically secondary" disembodied person (p. 341) with "limited" scope (p. 342 n.). The "former person" gloss comes via Ayer. The Map does not posit unembodied subjects, so nothing here needs a fold.
6. **Partial ally against no-self views, with care.** Strawson's case against the no-ownership doctrine (experiences "owe their identity as particulars to the identity of the person", p. 336) supports the Map's insistence that experience has a subject. But for Strawson the subject is the embodied person, not a subject prior to the body. **Do not cite it for Posit One.**

## Guards for the Writer

- **P. F., never bare "Strawson", wherever Galen is nearby.** Galen Strawson's "thin subject" is used on `topics/the-self-minimal-narrative-and-substantial` L89 and `concepts/no-self-objection-to-phenomenal-value`. P. F.'s *other* work on the Map is "Freedom and Resentment" (`research/reactive-attitudes-and-strawsonian-responsibility-2026-07-09`; `topics/reactive-attitudes-and-strawsonian-responsibility`). Keep "Persons" (1958) and "Freedom and Resentment" (1962) distinct.
- **Edition discipline.** Every quotation above is the 1958 text with 1958 pages. *Individuals* wording differs, at least at p. 99. Never cite the 1958 wording with a 1959 page.
- **The premiss has a weaker official form.** Strawson's own footnote (p. 338) grants that only *readiness* to ascribe to others "on appropriate occasions" is needed. Ayer's objection is aimed at the gap between that and what the argument needs. Do not strengthen the premiss into "one must actually ascribe to others".
- **Coiners.** "P-predicate", "M-predicate", "no-ownership doctrine" and "the primitiveness of the concept of a person" are Strawson's. "Generality Constraint" is Evans's (1982). "Knowledge Obstacle", "Separation Obstacle", "Mental Placement argument" and "Embodiment response" are Gomes's labels. The "dual-aspect" description is SEP's (Snowdon & Gomes), not Strawson's. The criteria conclusion is Wittgensteinian, and Strawson says so himself (p. 344).
- **Strawson is not a simple Wittgensteinian.** `research/problem-of-other-minds-2026-01-14` L113 files him among the Wittgensteinian dissolvers. That is half right. He shares the criteria conclusion, but he rejects the 1930–33 Wittgensteinian no-ownership view as incoherent (pp. 334–336). He says Wittgenstein's remarks "seem needlessly to flout the conceptual scheme we actually employ" (p. 353).
- **No over-concession.** The Map's exit from Strawson is a premise rejection, and Fold 1 says so ("unrefuted on both sides"). Do not upgrade it to "Strawson's argument fails". Do not downgrade it to "Posit One is incoherent" either.
- **Lowe** may be named as a structural option only. He does not reply to Strawson in the text read.
- **Avramides 2001** is abstract-only. Credit her with the SEP framing (2019/2023), not with claims from the book.
- **Nagel 1986: 19–20** is quoted only via SEP.
