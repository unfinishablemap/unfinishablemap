## The Genuine Yield: `concepts/semantic-memory` L101 Overstates the Electrophysiology Twice

L101 currently reads, verbatim:

> Electrophysiological research shows personal semantic retrieval produces neural signatures intermediate between general semantics and unique episodes. This suggests a gradient rather than a dichotomy: general facts → autobiographical facts → repeated events → unique events, with increasing autonoetic character as you move right.

Both sentences are defective, and the second is defective in the way that matters most to the Map.

### Defect 1 — the four-step ordering is not established at its two middle steps

The ERP study this claim rests on is Renoult, Tanguay, Beaudry, Tavakoli, Rabipour, Campbell, Moscovitch, Levine & Davidson (2016), "Personal semantics: Is it distinct from episodic and semantic memory? An electrophysiological study of memory for autobiographical facts and repeated events in honor of Shlomo Bentin", *Neuropsychologia* 83: 242–256, `10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2015.08.013`. Its abstract (Europe PMC, MED/26277459) states:

> "the N400 (which is classically associated with retrieval from semantic memory) was maximal for general facts and the LPC (which is classically associated with retrieval from episodic memory) was maximal for unique events. For both ERP components, the two personal semantic conditions (i.e., autobiographical facts and repeated events) systematically differed from semantic memory. In addition, N400 amplitudes also differentiated autobiographical facts from unique events. **Autobiographical facts and repeated events did not differ significantly from each other** but their corresponding scalp distributions differed from those associated with general facts."

The endpoints of the Map's ordering are supported. The **two middle steps are not**: the study explicitly failed to separate autobiographical facts from repeated events. L101 presents a four-term monotonic ordering where the source resolved three positions, not four.

There is a second, subtler point in the Map's favour that L101 discards. A **scalp-distribution** difference is topographic, not amplitude-graded — it indicates partly distinct generators rather than a point on a single continuum. The source's own conclusion is that "the neural correlates of personal semantics **can be distinguished from** those of semantic and episodic memory" — a distinctness claim. L101 converts a distinctness finding into a continuum finding, which concedes ground to the deflationary reading the Map elsewhere resists.

### Defect 2 — "with increasing autonoetic character" attaches a phenomenological gradient to an electrophysiological result

No ERP study measures autonoetic character. Renoult et al. (2016) recorded N400 and LPC amplitudes and scalp distributions from 27 participants verifying sentence veracity; nothing in the design indexes felt re-experiencing. L101's closing clause reads as though the autonoetic gradient were part of the reported finding.

**This is precisely the error pattern the 2026-08-04 note nominated as its own most likely failure — "the continuum literature grades the category but not the quale" — except that it is being committed inside the Map's own article rather than by the literature.** That is a more actionable finding than the original suspicion, and it is the single thing a `refine-draft` should fix.

### The operationalisation caveat that explains both defects

Renoult et al.'s own 2012 abstract contains the warning L101 ignores (Europe PMC, MED; *TiCS* 16(11): 550–558):

> "Between these two poles, however, may lie a third entity: personal semantics (PS). ... We show that, **depending on how it is operationalized, the neural correlates of PS can look more similar to semantic memory, more similar to EM, or dissimilar to both.**"

Note also "**may** lie a third entity" — hedged, and "third entity" rather than "midpoint". L101 states as a settled result ("Electrophysiological research shows...") something the canonical source says is contingent on operationalisation and can come out non-intermediate in either direction.

An independent full-text source confirms the caveat empirically, and it bears directly on the Map's own third listed subtype.

## New Full-Text Source: Coronel & Federmeier (2016) — the Map's Third Subtype Sits at the Semantic Pole

**Retrieved at full text** (12-page publisher PDF, `commcogsystems.com`, title page verified as *Neuropsychologia* 84 (2016) 36–43): Coronel, J. C. & Federmeier, K. D. (2016), "The N400 reveals how personal semantics is processed: Insights into the nature and organization of self-knowledge", *Neuropsychologia* 84: 36–43, `10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2016.01.029`.

⚠️ **Attribution guard**: WebSearch surfaced this paper inside a result set for a Renoult query, and its title contains "personal semantics". **It is not a Renoult paper.** Crossref and the PDF title page both give Coronel (Ohio State) and Federmeier (Illinois) as the only two authors. Do not let the corpus acquire this as "Renoult et al."

They tested **personal preferences** — self-evaluative judgments, the category the Map's L97 lists as "Self-relevant general knowledge ('I am good at chess')". Their result (grep-verified in the PDF at offset 34927):

> "Our data suggest that, on this kind of continuum, knowledge of personal preferences is closer to general semantics and suggest that they **share important functional and neurobiological features**."

And from the abstract, "congruency effects that were **similar in latency and distribution** to those for general semantic statements elicited from the same participants."

They also flag the subtype split explicitly (offset ~6290):

> "The term personal semantics has also been applied to other types of knowledge, such as autobiographical facts (e.g., 'My brother's name is Brian.') or repeated events (e.g., 'I pick up my brother from school every day.'), **which may rely on a distinct set of neural networks**"

**Consequence for L101.** The Map lists three subtypes at L95–97, then offers a gradient containing only two of them. Its third subtype — self-relevant general knowledge — does not sit between autobiographical facts and repeated events; the best-powered N400 study of that subtype places it **at the general-semantic pole**, similar in both latency and scalp distribution. The gradient as written silently drops one of its own three terms and would misplace it if included.

These two 2016 papers are **complementary, not contradictory** — a point worth stating plainly because the surface reading invites the opposite conclusion. They appeared in consecutive volumes of the same journal (83 and 84), and Coronel & Federmeier cite the Renoult study approvingly. Renoult et al. found personal semantics separable from general semantics using *autobiographical facts and repeated events*; Coronel & Federmeier found it inseparable using *preferences*. Both outcomes are exactly what Renoult et al. (2012) predicted would happen as operationalisation varies. **No convergence claim should be built on either in isolation.**

## The Prior Note's Self-Nominated Likely Error: Splits by Sub-Literature, and Is False Where It Was Aimed

The 2026-08-04 note wrote: "The central interpretive claim of this note — that the continuum literature grades the category but not the quale — is inferred from abstracts. Gentry & Buckner's full text may well discuss phenomenal character... Treat this as the note's most likely error and check it first."

**Checked, at full text. It does not survive for Gentry & Buckner.** Their paper is open access in PMC (PMC11449154, 10,639 words extracted); Gentry, H. & Buckner, C. (2024), "Transitional gradation and the distinction between episodic and semantic memory", *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 379(1913): 20230407, `10.1098/rstb.2023.0407`.

They do not merely discuss phenomenal character — they advance an **error theory** about it (grep-verified at offset 50996):

> "In humans, the phenomenal experience of remembering does offer apparently sharp boundaries in recollective experiences, but we argue that these differences are best explained in terms of an error theory—i.e. a theory that explains why subjects would mistakenly suppose there are two distinct kinds of memory accessed"

This is a **sharper threat to the Map than the prior note modelled**, and the Map's threat assessment should be upgraded accordingly. "Grades the category but not the quale" leaves the felt distinction untouched as evidence. An error theory does not: it concedes the phenomenal boundary is sharp *as a seeming* and then explains the seeming away as a mistake about kinds. Any Map argument that treats the felt episodic/semantic boundary as evidence for two kinds must answer this directly, and the answer cannot be "they only graded the category."

They further reject the phenomenological criterion as a criterion (offset 31627): "an empirically intractable phenomenological criterion", and at offset 32152, "in the absence of language, we have only very indirect access to animal phenomenology". Their positive view is that human distinctiveness "is not because they fail a phenomenological criterion" (offset ~33041).

**Where the prior note's claim does survive: the ERP literature.** Renoult et al. (2016) and Coronel & Federmeier (2016) grade the category via N400/LPC and measure no phenomenology whatsoever. So the claim should be **split, not retracted**: true of the electrophysiological continuum literature, false of the philosophical continuum literature, which engages the quale head-on and more aggressively than the Map has registered.

Two incidental finds from the same full text:

- **"Repisodic" memory** (offset 11203) — the philosophical literature already has a name for the repeated-events subtype: "even partially autonoetic components, such as the 'repisodic' memories described by Neisser of a stereotyped, repeated event, such as a traditional family holiday dinner". Source: Neisser, U. (1981), "John Dean's memory: a case study", *Cognition* 9: 1–22, `10.1016/0010-0277(81)90011-1`. This term appears **0 times** in the Map corpus and is the standard handle for the category L96 describes.
- ⚠️ **A published citation typo not to inherit.** Gentry & Buckner attribute a quotation — "a conjunction of familiar concepts and episode-specific information (such as sensory and spatial context)" — to "Renoult et al. [33]" but tag the page as "[30, p. 1046]". Their ref [30] is Neisser 1981 (*Cognition* 9: 1–22, which has no p. 1046); ref [33] is Renoult, Irish, Moscovitch & Rugg (2019), *TiCS* 23: 1041–1057, where p. 1046 falls. The intended cite is [33].
