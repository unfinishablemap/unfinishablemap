## Executive Summary

Six empirical studies and one experiment bear on the question. Three questionnaire studies (8, 10 and 122 experiencers) find that the memory of a near-death experience is rated at least as rich in sensory, emotional and self-referential detail as memories of real events, and richer than memories of imagined events. Supporting studies find the memory stable on a standard scale over about two decades (72 experiencers), more detailed in narrative than flashbulb memories (25), and more central to identity than other self-defining memories (41 of 71). One experiment (20 experiencers, 20 matched coma survivors) finds that experiencers produce false recall of word-list lures at the same rate as controls but more often label those false recalls as consciously recollected.

No study in the set checks the content of a near-death memory against an independent record. The questionnaire scores how a memory seems to the person who has it. The Liège group that produced most of this work reads the richness as showing the memories are not *imagined* and proposes they are memories of hallucinations; the Virginia group reports the same pattern as memories recalled as "realer" than real events and frames it as bearing on whether the experiences are imagined. Neither inference reaches veridicality, and the false-memory result does not show fabrication. Both authors' groups say as much in their own limitations.

What the research adds:

1. **The "more real than real" report has been measured.** It is a questionnaire result replicated in three samples, the largest of 122. The page asserts the phenomenon from slogans.
2. **The measuring instrument has a known scope.** The Memory Characteristics Questionnaire was built for reality monitoring: telling memories of perceived events from memories of imagined ones by their felt qualities. It was never a test of accuracy, and one of its items simply asks the rememberer whether the event "really occurred".
3. **The group that found the richness reads it as hallucination, not as perception of real events.** This is the most useful single fact for the page's calibration: the richness finding comes from researchers who take a physiological view and who conclude "flashbulb memories of hallucinations".
4. **There is one experimental result on source monitoring in experiencers**, small and marginal, with an explicit authorial disclaimer that it does not show the near-death memory is illusory.
5. **Stability over time has a number**: 72 experiencers, about two decades, no significant change in scale scores.
6. **A side finding touches the page's Van Lommel sentence** (L120). A 2024 prospective intensive-care study found no reduction in fear of death specific to experiencers at one year (2 of 11 experiencers against 12 of 50 non-experiencers). See [Follow-ups not folded](#follow-ups-not-folded).

## Key Sources

Every quotation below was matched as a verbatim span (whitespace-normalised) in fetched source text. The route is given with each. "Abstract only" means the body was not obtained.

### Johnson, Foley, Suengas and Raye (1988): the instrument

- **Citation**: *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 117(4), 371–376. DOI 10.1037/0096-3445.117.4.371 (Crossref verified).
- **Read**: abstract only, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2974863/
- **Type**: experimental psychology paper; origin of the Memory Characteristics Questionnaire (MCQ).
- **Key points**: two studies of naturally occurring autobiographical events. Memories of perceived events were rated higher than memories of imagined events on perceptual information, contextual information and supporting memories, more so for recent memories. The authors interpret this within the reality-monitoring framework (Johnson and Raye 1981): people judge a memory's origin from its qualitative characteristics.
- **Quote 1**: "Compared with imagined events, perceived events were given higher ratings on several characteristics, including perceptual information, contextual information, and supporting memories."
- **Scope**: the comparison is between group averages for events whose origin the subject already knows. The abstract makes no claim that a high score certifies an individual memory as perceived, and none about accuracy of content.
- **Tenet alignment**: neutral.

### Thonnard et al. (2013): richness, first study

- **Citation**: Thonnard, M., Charland-Verville, V., Brédart, S., Dehon, H., Ledoux, D., Laureys, S., & Vanhaudenhuyse, A. *PLoS ONE*, 8(3), e57620. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0057620 (Crossref verified).
- **Read**: full text, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3609762/ (PMC efetch XML).
- **Design**: 21 coma survivors in three groups (8 with a near-death experience by Greyson scale ≥ 7; 6 with coma memories but no near-death experience; 7 with no coma memory) and 18 healthy controls. Each rated five memories on a modified 15-item MCQ: a target memory, recent and old real events, recent and old imagined events. Non-parametric tests. The near-death experiences were on average 93 months old (SD 87).
- **Results**: within the near-death group, the target memory scored higher on MCQ total than all four comparison memories. Between groups, near-death memories scored higher than the other groups' target memories on clarity, self-referential and emotional information; on sensory detail they did not differ from other coma memories. No group effect for reactivation frequency or for confidence.
- **Quote 2** (abstract): "NDE memories have more characteristics than memories of imagined and real events (p<0.02)"
- **Quote 3** (abstract): "Thus, this suggests that they cannot be considered as imagined event memories."
- **Quote 4** (abstract): "their physiological origins could lead them to be really perceived although not lived in the reality"
- **Quote 5** (discussion): "NDEs can meet the definition of hallucinations"
- **Quote 6** (discussion): "suggest that the memories of NDEs are flashbulb memories of hallucinations"
- **Quote 7** (limitation, on emotional matching): "possibly biasing within-NDE-group comparisons"
- **Quote 8** (limitation): "this lack of time-effect could be explained by the low sample size in our study"
- **Quote 9** (the MCQ confidence item, Table 1): "I believe the event in my memory really occurred in the way I remember it and that I have not imagined or fabricated anything that did not occur"
- **Grade**: n = 8 in the group of interest; retrospective; self-selected memories; the comparison "real" memories were chosen by the participant; the authors flag that emotional intensity was not successfully matched. The finding is a difference in self-rated memory qualities. The inference the authors draw is negative (not like imagined-event memories) and their positive proposal is hallucination.
- **Tenet alignment**: neutral. The authors' reading is physicalist. The data do not discriminate.

### Palmieri et al. (2014): replication with hypnosis and EEG

- **Citation**: Palmieri, A., Calvo, V., Kleinbub, J. R., Meconi, F., Marangoni, M., Barilaro, P., Broggio, A., Sambin, M., & Sessa, P. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*, 8, 429. DOI 10.3389/fnhum.2014.00429 (Crossref verified; article number from PubMed).
- **Read**: full text, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4063168/
- **Affiliation**: University of Padova. This is **not** a Liège follow-up, although the task brief and some secondary descriptions group it with them.
- **Design**: 10 experiencers and 10 controls; MCQ ratings of near-death, real and imagined memories before and after a hypnotic recall protocol, with EEG during recall.
- **Quote 10**: "NDE memories were similar to real memories in terms of detail richness, self-referential, and emotional information"
- **Quote 11**: "at a neural level, NDE memories are stored as episodic memories of events experienced in a peculiar state of consciousness"
- **Quote 12**: "(although not necessarily in relation to the external physical world)"
- **Quote 13**: "The main limitation of our research is the relatively small sample size."
- **Grade**: n = 10 per group. The result is "similar to real", weaker than Thonnard's "more than real". The EEG conclusions rest on correlations between band power and questionnaire change scores in ten people, and the abstract's gloss on delta activity (trance states and "transpersonal experience") is interpretive. Hypnosis is described by the authors as improving recall and decreasing inaccuracy; that is their premise and the study does not test accuracy. Do not cite the EEG result as showing that the memories are of real events; the authors themselves bracket the external world (quote 12).
- **Tenet alignment**: neutral. The discussion says a neural counterpart is "not necessarily in contrast" with a spiritual reading; that is the authors' aside and no evidence for one.

### Moore and Greyson (2017): the large sample

- **Citation**: Moore, L. E., & Greyson, B. *Consciousness and Cognition*, 51, 116–124. DOI 10.1016/j.concog.2017.03.003 (Crossref verified).
- **Read**: **abstract only**, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28329717/. The University of Virginia PDF is indexed by search but returned an HTML shell to a plain fetch.
- **Design** (from the abstract): 122 survivors of a close brush with death who reported near-death experiences each completed the MCQ for three memories: the experience, a real event around the same time, an imagined event around the same time.
- **Quote 14**: "These data suggest that memories of near-death experiences are recalled as "realer" than real events or imagined events."
- **Grade**: the largest sample and a within-subject design; no control group of non-experiencers is mentioned in the abstract. Subscale results, recruitment route and limitations were not read. The abstract's wording is careful: "recalled as" realer. The opening sentences frame the stakes as whether the experiences "are imagined rather than real events", which leans on the reality-monitoring inference (see [Key Debates](#key-debates)).
- **Tenet alignment**: the authors are sympathetic to non-reductive readings (Greyson is at the Division of Perceptual Studies), and the abstract mentions "enhanced cognition despite compromised brain function". The data reported are ratings.

### Greyson (2007): stability over two decades

- **Citation**: Greyson, B. *Resuscitation*, 73(3), 407–411. DOI 10.1016/j.resuscitation.2006.10.013 (Crossref verified).
- **Read**: **abstract only**, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17289247/
- **Quote 15**: "Seventy-two patients with near-death experience who had completed the NDE scale in the 1980s (63% of the original cohort still alive) completed the scale a second time, without reference to the original scale administration."
- **Quote 16**: "Mean scores did not change significantly on the total NDE scale, its 4 factors, or its 16 items."
- **Quote 17**: "Contrary to expectation, accounts of near-death experiences, and particularly reports of their positive affect, were not embellished over a period of almost two decades."
- **Grade**: what was compared is two administrations of a 16-item scale, not two narratives. The first administration was itself retrospective (how long after the event is not in the abstract), so the study speaks to drift after the 1980s and is silent on formation between the event and the first report. Consistency of report is a different property from accuracy of content.

### Martial et al. (2018): the false-memory experiment

- **Citation**: Martial, C., Charland-Verville, V., Dehon, H., & Laureys, S. *Psychological Research*, 82(4), 806–818. DOI 10.1007/s00426-017-0855-9 (Crossref verified: online 16 March 2017, issue July 2018).
- **Read**: full text (publisher offprint) at https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/208291/1/Martial_et_al_2017.pdf ; abstract at https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28303355/
- **Disambiguation**: the group's own later papers cite this as "Martial et al., 2017c" (online-first year) and the repository file is named 2017. It is one paper. The Map should cite 2018 with the volume and pages.
- **Design**: 20 experiencers (Greyson ≥ 7, after coma: 9 anoxic, 3 traumatic, 8 other) and 20 volunteers with comparable life-threatening events and coma but no near-death experience, matched on age, gender, education and time since the event. Mean age about 60; mean time since the event 29 and 21 years. Twelve French Deese–Roediger–McDermott (DRM) lists of 15 words, each converging on an unpresented "critical lure"; free recall with a Remember/Know/Guess judgement for every recalled word; then a post-recall test asking for words that came to mind but were withheld, used to estimate activation and monitoring separately.
- **Results**:
  - False recall of critical lures: no significant group difference. Quote 18: "Numerically, NDErs falsely recalled critical lures more than volunteers in the free recall phase, but this did not reached significance [F(1,38) = 2.569, p = 0.117"
  - "Remember" judgements: group main effect not significant (p = 0.061). Quote 19: "The Group type × Item type interaction was not significant [F(1,38) = 3.638, p = 0.064"
  - The headline effect is a planned pairwise comparison. Quote 20: "NDErs obtained higher proportions regarding to “Remember” responses attributed to critical items than those of volunteers [F(1,38) = 4.377, p = 0.04"
  - Post-recall test: volunteers produced more withheld lures (Table 3: 0.12 against 0.02, p = 0.04); summed activation was equal (0.52 in both groups). The authors read this as equal activation with weaker source monitoring in experiencers.
  - The Remember-to-lure group means are in a rotated table that did not extract unambiguously; they are not reported here.
- **Quote 21** (abstract): "NDErs and volunteers were equally likely to produce false memories, but that NDErs recalled them more frequently associated with compelling illusory recollection"
- **Quote 22** (abstract): "source monitoring was less successful in NDErs compared to volunteers"
- **Quote 23** (discussion): "Our study cannot conclude that the subjective experience associated with NDE is illusory recollection"
- **Quote 24** (discussion): "future studies are needed to know if perceived and reported phenomenological characteristics of NDE are associated with an actual event"
- **Quote 25** (limitation): "memories of word lists are, by design, less complex than autobiographical memories"
- **Quote 26** (limitation; the offprint's page number falls inside this sentence in the extracted text and has been removed): "The retrospective design of this study does not allow us to say whether NDEs occur more frequently in people with increased illusory recollection or whether such experiences induce increased illusory recollection in people who were previously not prone to this."
- **Quote 27** (authors' speculation): "might be explained, at least partly, by psychological mechanisms"
- **Grade**: one study, n = 20 per group, participants tested decades after the event, recruited through the group's website and media. The key effect is one planned comparison at p = 0.04 under an omnibus interaction that missed significance, and the paper mentions no correction for the several planned comparisons it runs. No replication surfaced: a PubMed search for "near-death" with DRM, false memory or illusory recollection returns three records, of which this is the only DRM study (the others are Ruiz et al. 2026 and the trial listed first under Unverified leads). Treat it as a lead on cognitive style in experiencers. It is a finding about word lists.
- **Tenet alignment**: the authors' framing is that the rich phenomenology may be partly psychological in origin. Neutral as data.

### Supporting studies from the Liège group

- **Martial et al. (2017)**, "Intensity and memory characteristics of near-death experiences", *Consciousness and Cognition*, 56, 120–127, DOI 10.1016/j.concog.2017.06.018 (Crossref verified). Full text at https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/214239/1/1-s2.0-S1053810016303804-main.pdf . 152 self-reported experiencers by mailed questionnaire. Quote 28: "Greyson NDE scale total score was positively correlated with MCQ total score (r = 0.29, p < 0.0005)". Intensity correlated with other-sensory detail, personal importance and reactivation frequency. Quote 29: "Intense events are more likely to be rehearsed which in turn can enhance the amount of phenomenological characteristics in memory". Quote 30: "this investigation may be subject to selection bias and hence may not be representative of NDErs in general". - **Cassol et al. (2019)**, "Memories of near-death experiences: are they self-defining?", *Neuroscience of Consciousness*, 2019(1), niz002, DOI 10.1093/nc/niz002 (Crossref verified). Abstract at https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30842887/ ; full text fetched (PMC6397322) but only the abstract was used. 71 participants (48 after a life-threatening event, 23 "NDE-like"); 41 named the experience among their two main self-defining memories and rated it more central to identity than their other one. Quote 31: "the richness of the NDE memory was positively associated to its centrality (CES scores)".
- **Cassol et al. - **Martial et al. (2024)**, "Phenomenological memory characteristics and impact of near-death experience in critically ill survivors", *International Journal of Clinical and Health Psychology*, 24(3), 100478, DOI 10.1016/j.ijchp.2024.100478 (Crossref verified). Full text at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11231695/ . The only **prospective** study in the set: 126 survivors of an intensive-care stay over seven days, 19 (15%) meeting the Greyson criterion within a week of discharge; MCQ at one month for the 19; follow-up at one year reached 11 of the 19 and 50 of 107 others. MCQ medians were at ceiling for visual detail, location, own actions and thoughts; the confidence item was at ceiling with no spread. Quote 34: "real/imagine (median=7; IQR=7-7)". ## Major Positions

### "Not imagined, but hallucinated" (Liège: Thonnard, Laureys, Martial, Cassol)
- **Core claim**: the richness shows the memory is of something perceptually experienced, and the experience was a physiologically driven hallucination. Emotional intensity, self-relevance and rehearsal add to the richness.
- **Later additions**: experiencers may have a cognitive style that favours illusory recollection and weaker source monitoring (2018); the memory is central to identity (2019) and is richer in episodic detail than a flashbulb memory (2020).
- **Relation to tenets**: a physicalist account. It conflicts with a dualist reading of near-death experiences as glimpses of mind without brain, and has no bearing on the Dualism tenet itself, which does not rest on near-death evidence. The Map should state this position at full strength.

### "Recalled as realer than real" (Virginia: Moore, Greyson)
- **Core claim**: the memory outscores a real event of the same period, which outscores an imagined one, and the account does not inflate over two decades. Offered against the suggestion that the experiences are imagined or embellished.
- **Relation to tenets**: friendlier to filter readings in its framing. The data are the same kind as the Liège data and license the same conclusion and no more.

### Reality monitoring as a general framework (Johnson and colleagues)
- **Core claim**: people infer the origin of a memory from its qualities; perceived events typically leave more perceptual and contextual detail than imagined ones.
- **Relation to the question**: the framework predicts that a vivid hallucination, being perceptual in character, will be stored with the marks of perception. So within the framework itself a high score cannot separate "perceived the world" from "had a percept-like experience". Thonnard et al. draw this consequence explicitly.

### Sceptical readings (Blackmore; French; Braithwaite, as reported by the Liège papers)
- **Core claim**: the memories are wholly or partly reconstructions shaped by prior knowledge and expectation. These authors were not read for this note; see [Unverified leads](#unverified-leads-do-not-quote).

## Key Debates

### Does a high MCQ score show the remembered event was real?
- **Sides**: the abstracts of Thonnard and of Moore and Greyson both frame the study as a test of whether the experiences are "imagined". Thonnard concludes hallucination. Moore and Greyson's abstract stops at "recalled as".
- **Core point**: the inference from richness to origin was validated on group averages for ordinary events of known origin. Applying it to a single memory formed in an abnormal brain state stretches it past its validation, and its own logic lets hallucination in. Separately, origin (was something experienced?) is a different question from accuracy (does the content match what happened in the room?). No study here addresses the second.
- **State**: settled as far as the published data go. Nobody in the literature read claims the questionnaire establishes veridical perception.

### Is the richness a product of rehearsal and emotion?
- Thonnard found no reactivation effect (n = 8); Martial 2017 found intensity correlates with reactivation frequency (n = 152, r = 0.28 for that item, as extracted from the results text); Heaps and Nash show in other populations that rehearsal can close the gap between false and true memories. The 2024 prospective study finds ceiling ratings at one month, which limits how much of the effect can be late accretion. Open.

### Does illusory recollection in experiencers explain the experience?
- The 2018 authors float it and disclaim it in the same discussion. Direction of causation is unknown (quote 26), the task is word lists (quote 25), and the effect is marginal. Open, with one unreplicated study.

### Are they flashbulb memories?
- Thonnard proposed it; Cassol 2020 found they are richer in episodic detail and more central than flashbulb memories. The flashbulb literature's lesson (confidence without accuracy) therefore transfers by analogy only.

### Overflow: `concepts/simulation-theory-of-memory`, new H3 at the end of §The Discrimination Problem

**Why this host**: the section (anchor `#the-discrimination-problem`) asks how a subject tells remembering from imagining and says the simulationist appeal to a "feeling of remembering" is underexplained. Reality monitoring and the MCQ are the empirical form of that feeling, and near-death memories are a field case in which it is at ceiling while the origin of the content is disputed. The page has no mention of near-death experiences, reality monitoring or Johnson (each counts 0).

**Placement**: insert as a new subsection after the paragraph that ends "sets out the rival in full, and [[predictive-processing-and-dualism]] the wider programme it belongs to." (the close of `### The self-model reply`) and before `## Relation to Site Perspective` (counts 1).

**NEW** (381 words by `count_words`, heading included):

> ### A Field Case: Near-Death Memories {#near-death-memories}
>
> The discrimination problem has an empirical counterpart. Reality-monitoring research found that memories of perceived events carry more perceptual and contextual detail than memories of imagined ones, and proposed that subjects use the difference to judge where a memory came from (Johnson et al., 1988). The Memory Characteristics Questionnaire built on that work turns the feeling of remembering into a score.
>
> Memories of [[near-death-experiences|near-death experiences]] sit at the top of that score. In three samples (8, 10 and 122 experiencers) the near-death memory was rated at least as rich as memories of real events and richer than memories of imagined ones (Thonnard et al., 2013; Palmieri et al., 2014; Moore & Greyson, 2017). The reports are also stable: 72 experiencers re-scored their experience after about two decades without significant change (Greyson, 2007). The first of these teams, which works within a neurophysiological account, did not read richness as a sign that the events took place. It concluded that the memories "cannot be considered as imagined event memories" and proposed that they are "flashbulb memories of hallucinations": episodes perceived, on its account, without having occurred.
>
> The score cannot carry more than that. The questionnaire records how a memory seems to its owner, and a stable report is a consistent one; neither checks content against a record. The felt marker has also been pulled apart from origin in this population. Twenty coma survivors with near-death experiences and twenty matched survivors without recalled never-presented words from associative word lists at similar rates, but the experiencers more often judged that they consciously recollected them (Martial et al., 2018). The finding is a single planned comparison (p = 0.04) in one small retrospective study, and its authors state that it "cannot conclude that the subjective experience associated with NDE is illusory recollection."
>
> The Map takes both halves. Richness is no evidence that perception occurred during arrest, and a tendency to illusory recollection on word lists is no evidence that the near-death memory is fabricated. What the case shows is the discrimination problem at full stretch: the feeling of remembering can be at its maximum where the origin of the content is the very thing in dispute, which is the Map's standing reason for treating [[introspection|introspective reports]] as evidence about phenomenology.

## Gaps in Research

- Two of the seven central sources (Moore and Greyson; Greyson 2007) were read as abstracts only.
- No published critique aimed specifically at the MCQ inference in the near-death literature was found; the critique in this note is assembled from the instrument's stated purpose, Thonnard's own conclusion, and the general false-memory literature (Heaps and Nash; Talarico and Rubin). It should be presented as the Map's reading, not attributed to a named critic.
- Replication status of the DRM result was checked in PubMed only.
