## Executive Summary

The reviewers' objection runs together two questions that the emotion literature keeps apart. *Are discrete emotion categories natural kinds?* Barrett's answer is no, and that is the whole of what "the theory of constructed emotion" denies. *Is valence — the pleasant/unpleasant dimension of core affect — a natural kind?* Barrett's answer, in every canonical statement from 1999 to 2017, is yes: core affect is "a psychological primitive" (Barrett & Bliss-Moreau 2009), "a fundamental, psychologically irreducible property of the human mind" [abstract], and valence and arousal "are basic features of consciousness … that, importantly, are not unique to instances of emotion" (Barrett 2017 SCAN, verbatim [raw]). Russell (2003) calls core affect "primitive, universal, and simple (irreducible on the mental plane)" [snippet], and Barrett's 2006 *J. Res. Pers.* paper is titled "Valence is a basic building block of emotional life" [metadata]. So constructionism in its canonical form does not deprive P-VS3 of its relatum; it hands the Map the relatum and argues only that *fear*, *anger* and *grief* are conceptual categorisations laid over it. `topics/emotion-and-dualism` L111–113 already says this in passing ("This constructionist view preserves phenomenal realism about affect even while treating discrete emotions as conceptually constructed") and never tests it — which is the real gap.

Three findings make the easy reply insufficient, and they are what an article should be about. **(1) The natural-kind status of core affect has been directly challenged from inside philosophy of science.** Scarantino (2009, *Philosophy of Science*) argues that "the empirical evidence does not support the thesis that core affect is a more 'natural' category than discrete emotions" and recommends "a splitting strategy in our search for natural affective kinds" [abstract]. Panksepp, whom `concepts/valence` cites for valence being phylogenetically ancient, himself splits affect into three kinds — "sensory, homeostatic, and emotional" (Panksepp 2011 [abstract]) — so the Map's own affective-neuroscience witness does not underwrite *one* valence kind. **(2) Barrett's 2025 restatement has drifted away from the primitive.** The 2025 *Perspectives* paper (Barrett, Atzil, Bliss-Moreau, Chanes et al.) uses "primitive" zero times and "core affect" only inside a quotation from its critics; "pleasant, unpleasant, arousing" are now "mental features" that "are not properties of the intrinsic brain signals alone or of the incoming sensory signals alone but of their relations," "lower dimensional summaries of higher dimensional sensory and motor signals," and "unpleasant" is given as an example of a "relational" and "abstract" feature that "make[s] the different patterns of sensory-motor signals functionally equivalent in a specific situation" [raw]. That is the reading on which the reviewer's objection bites — valence as a constructed compression rather than a felt primitive — and it is a 2025 development, not the theory the corpus has been citing. **(3) Philosophy of valence presupposes the unity the reviewers question, and the Map's named rival is on the Map's side.** Carruthers (2018), the evaluativist `concepts/valence` argues against, opens by claiming that "enough is now known about the causes, consequences, and properties of valence to indicate that it forms a unitary natural-psychological kind, one that seemingly plays a fundamental role in motivating all kinds of intentional action" [abstract]. The hedonic, evaluativist, imperativist and functionalist accounts all compete to say what *the* valence property is. The live challenge to unity comes from elsewhere: the heterogeneity problem for pleasure (Sidgwick to Labukt 2012, Bramble 2013, Lin 2020, Pallies 2021), Colombetti's (2005) and Walle & Dukes's (2023) findings that "valence" is polysemous and "ill-defined and poorly operationalized," Corns's (2014) double dissociation of unpleasantness from "motivational oomph," and Scherer's appraisal-theoretic micro-valences, on which one-dimensional "macro-valence" is a choice-level integration serving as "common currency" rather than a felt primitive (Shuman, Sander & Scherer 2013 [raw]).

The neuroscience is split along the same seam. Lindquist et al.'s (2016) 397-study meta-analysis found "little evidence for the bipolar or bivalent hypotheses" and supported an "affective workspace" in which valence "is flexibly implemented across instances by a set of valence-general limbic and paralimbic brain regions" [abstract]; Berridge (2019) argues limbic valence coding is a matter of dynamic "modes" rather than fixed "modules" [abstract]; Tye (2018) finds valence substrates "dynamic, context-dependent, and changing" [abstract]. Against this, Chikazoe et al. (2014) decoded "a continuous axis of pleasant-to-unpleasant valence" in orbitofrontal cortex "independent of sensory origin" that alone "could classify experienced affect across participants" [raw], and Wilson-Mendenhall, Barrett & Barsalou (2013) — Barrett's own lab — found valence ratings tracking orbitofrontal activity "across instances from all three categories" of emotion [abstract]. Neural degeneracy at the *implementation* level is compatible with a psychological-level kind, which is exactly the position Barrett's 2009 chapter took ("realized within a broadly distributed neuronal workspace" [abstract]).

**Upshot for the Map.** P-VS3 survives constructionism on two of the three readings of what valence is, and is threatened only by the third. If valence is a primitive kind at the core-affect level (Barrett 2009/2017, Russell 2003, Carruthers 2018), the identity keeps its relatum and the rival supplies it. If valence is plural — several hedonic tones (Labukt), several micro-valences (Scherer), three classes of affect (Panksepp) — the identity holds distributively between intrinsic value and *evaluative phenomenal character* at whatever grain the phenomenology has, which is already the Map's move in P-VS4; what fails is P-VS1's *scalar currency*, since macro-valence is then an integration performed for choice rather than a felt quantity consulted at choice. Only if valence is a relational compression with no felt primitive beneath it (the 2025 drift, read as an ontology rather than a description of implementation) does "the identity claim lose its relatum" — and that reading is the predictive-construction defeater the Map already registers at `evaluative-phenomenal-character` L170–172 and `phenomenal-value-realism` L200–202, restated in constructionist vocabulary. The Map's existing reply (the construction explains how valence is computed and accessed, not why it is felt) transfers unchanged; what the corpus lacks is the demonstration that it transfers.

## Key Sources

### Barrett (2017), "The theory of constructed emotion: an active inference account of interoception and categorization" — *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience* 12(1):1–23 [raw]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/scan/nsw154 (PMC5390700; PMID 27798257; open access)
- **Type**: Paper (theoretical review)
- **Key points**:
  - Emotions are constructed by categorising interoceptive predictions under concepts; the categories have no essence and are "degenerate" (multiply realised) at the neural level.
  - Affect is *not* what is constructed. Verbatim: "Interoceptive sensations are usually experienced as lower dimensional feelings of affect (Barrett and Bliss-Moreau, 2009; Barrett, 2017). As such, the properties of affect—valence and arousal (Barrett and Russell, 1999; Kuppens et al., 2013)—are basic features of consciousness (Damasio, 1999; Dreyfus and Thompson, 2007; Edelman and Tononi, 2000; James, 1890/2007; Searle, 1992, 2004; Wundt, 1897) that, importantly, are not unique to instances of emotion."

A response to Panksepp and Izard."
- **Tenet alignment**: The affect claim is congenial to Tenet 1 in exactly the way `emotion-and-dualism` L113 says — valence is treated as a basic feature of consciousness, not a conceptual product. The active-inference *implementation* is the physicalist rival the Map already engages under "predictive construction."

### Russell (2003), "Core affect and the psychological construction of emotion" — *Psychological Review* 110(1):145–172 [abstract; one phrase snippet]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.110.1.145
- **Key points**: Core affect is "states experienced as simply feeling good or bad, energized or enervated"; it "can therefore be experienced as free-floating (mood) or can be attributed to some cause (and thereby begin an emotional episode)"; prototypical emotions are "core affect attributed to something plus various nonemotional processes" [abstract]. ### Lindquist, Satpute, Wager, Weber & Barrett (2016), "The brain basis of positive and negative affect: evidence from a meta-analysis of the human neuroimaging literature" — *Cerebral Cortex* 26(5):1910–1922 [abstract]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhv001 (PMC4830281)
- **Key points**: "Although scientists overwhelmingly agree that valence is a basic psychological phenomenon, debate continues about how to best conceptualize it scientifically." Three hypotheses tested over 397 studies, 914 contrasts, 6,827 participants: bipolarity (one monotonic system), bivalence (independent positive and negative systems), affective workspace (a flexible valence-general set of regions). "We found little evidence for the bipolar or bivalent hypotheses. Findings instead supported the hypothesis that, at the level of brain activity measurable by fMRI, valence is flexibly implemented across instances by a set of valence-general limbic and paralimbic brain regions."
- **Tenet alignment**: Two readings. The constructionist one: no dedicated valence module, so valence is not a neural kind. The one Barrett's own 2009 chapter licenses: a psychological primitive "realized within a broadly distributed neuronal workspace." The article should say which inference the Map draws and why — the second is the one Boyd-style multiple realisability permits, and it is the same structure `is-conscious-being-a-natural-kind` uses.
- **Predecessor**: Lindquist, Wager, Kober, Bliss-Moreau & Barrett (2012), "The brain basis of emotion: a meta-analytic review," *BBS* 35(3):121–143 [abstract] — the same result for discrete emotion categories.

### Chikazoe, Lee, Kriegeskorte & Anderson (2014), "Population coding of affect across stimuli, modalities and individuals" — *Nature Neuroscience* 17(8):1114–1122 [raw]
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3749 (PMC4317366)
- **Key points**: "Representational mapping of population level activity evoked by complex scenes and basic tastes uncovered a neural code supporting a continuous axis of pleasant-to-unpleasant valence. This valence code was distinct from low-level physical and high-level object properties. While ventral temporal and anterior insular cortices supported valence codes specific to vision and taste, both the medial and lateral orbitofrontal cortices (OFC), maintained a valence code independent of sensory origin. Further only the OFC code could classify experienced affect across participants."
- **Tenet alignment**: The best empirical support for valence as a domain-general, cross-subject dimension — a supramodal code is what a natural-kind reading predicts. It is a *population* code in a region the workspace hypothesis also names, so it does not contradict Lindquist 2016 so much as locate the kind at a different level of description. (The corpus should note the "Anderson" here is Adam K. Anderson of Cornell, not David J. Anderson of Adolphs & Anderson.)

## Major Positions

### Psychological constructionism (Russell; Barrett and the Northeastern school)
- **Proponents**: James Russell; Lisa Feldman Barrett; Kristen Lindquist; Eliza Bliss-Moreau; Maria Gendron.
- **Core claim**: Discrete emotions are conceptual categorisations of core affect and are not natural kinds; core affect (valence × arousal) is a psychological primitive — "basic features of consciousness … not unique to instances of emotion."
- **Key arguments**: No consistent neural, physiological, facial or experiential signature per category (Barrett 2006; Lindquist et al. 2012); degeneracy; cross-cultural variation; concept-dependence of categorisation.
- **Relation to site tenets**: On the *affect* claim, closer to the Map than the reviewers assume: valence is treated as a feature of consciousness, prior to concepts, present in mood without an object. On the *implementation* claim (active inference; "relational features," 2025), it is the predictive-construction rival already registered. The 2025 drift is where the Map's engagement must be updated.

### Basic-emotion and affect-program theory
- **Proponents**: Paul Ekman and Daniel Cordaro; Carroll Izard; Jaak Panksepp; Griffiths and Scarantino (for affect programs only).
- **Core claim**: A small set of emotions are evolved, universal, automatic response programmes — natural kinds.
- **Key arguments**: Cross-cultural facial recognition; subcortical stimulation eliciting conditioned place preference/aversion (Panksepp); response coherence under adaptive specification (Sznycer & Cohen 2021).
- **Relation to site tenets**: Panksepp is a phenomenal realist about primary-process affect and the Map's stated ally; but he is a pluralist about affect (sensory/homeostatic/emotional), so he supports "valence is ancient and real," not "valence is one kind."

### Functionalist emotion states
- **Proponents**: Ralph Adolphs and David J. Anderson.
- **Core claim**: Emotions are evolved functional states defined by behavioural features (valence, scalability, persistence, generalisation, and others), separable from conscious feeling, studiable across species.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Treats valence as a functional feature of internal states rather than of experience — the reading the Map's hard-problem commitment declines. Barrett's charge that this is "psychological essentialism" is a dispute between two physicalist camps and does not bear on Tenet 1 either way.

### Splitting (Griffiths 1997; Scarantino 2009, 2012)
- **Core claim**: Neither the folk categories nor core affect is the natural affective kind; science should split the domain into whatever projectable clusters the evidence supports.
- **Relation to site tenets**: The most serious position for the Map to answer, because it targets core affect on its own terms. The Map's HPC-style framing in `is-conscious-being-a-natural-kind` supplies the tools: a natural kind can be a Boyd cluster with vague boundaries and no essence, and the question becomes whether felt valence projects across contexts, which Chikazoe 2014 and Wilson-Mendenhall 2013 suggest it does at the population-code level.

### Philosophical accounts of valence as a unified property
- **Proponents**: Hedonic/intrinsic (Rachels 2000; Bramble 2013; Labukt 2012; Rawlette 2016; the Map); evaluativist (Carruthers 2018, 2023; Bain 2013); imperativist (Klein 2015; Barlassina & Hayward 2019; Kauppinen 2021); functionalist (Aydede & Fulkerson 2014, 2018).
- **Core claim**: There is one thing, affective phenomenal character or valence, and the dispute is over its nature.
- **Relation to site tenets**: The unity premise is shared across the whole debate; the reviewers' doubt is an outlier within philosophy of valence. Where unity is questioned (heterogeneity problem), the pluralist-hedonic-tone response (Labukt) preserves intrinsic phenomenal value.

### Appraisal and enactive fragmentation
- **Proponents**: Klaus Scherer (component process model; Shuman, Sander & Scherer 2013); Giovanna Colombetti (2005 polysemy; 2014 *The Feeling Body*, on which cognition is "intrinsically affective"); Louis Charland (2005); Robert Solomon (2002).
- **Core claim**: "Valence" names several distinct oppositions (pleasantness, goal conduciveness, power, self-congruence, moral goodness) and several bearers (emotion, feeling, object, appraisal, behaviour); the one-dimensional scalar is an integration for choice or communication, not a datum.
- **Relation to site tenets**: The direct rival to P-VS1's scalar currency. Colombetti's enactivism is physicalist but treats affectivity as primordial to living sense-making rather than conceptually constructed — a second physicalist source on which valence is prior to construction.

## Key Debates

### Are emotions natural kinds?
- **Sides**: Barrett 2006 / Lindquist et al. 2013 (no) vs Panksepp 2007, Izard 2007, Ekman & Cordaro 2011, Scarantino & Griffiths 2011 (for affect programs), Adolphs & Anderson 2018, Sznycer & Cohen 2021 (yes, for some).
- **Core disagreement**: Whether projectable property clusters exist for the folk categories, and whether functional definition rescues them (Barrett 2017 reply: "Functionalism cannot save the classical view").
- **Current state**: Ongoing; Barrett 2019 concedes the book-length functionalist case "cannot resolve the great emotion debate" and that "ontological commitments are not discovered by scientists: they are stipulated." The Map has no stake in the outcome *except* through what each side says about valence.

### Is core affect — or valence alone — a natural kind?
- **Sides**: Barrett & Bliss-Moreau 2009, Russell 2003, Carruthers 2018, Lindquist et al. 2016 ("scientists overwhelmingly agree that valence is a basic psychological phenomenon") vs Scarantino 2009 (core affect no more natural than discrete emotions; split), Colombetti 2005 and Walle & Dukes 2023 (the concept is polysemous), Corns 2014/2020 (unpleasantness and motivation dissociate; pain not a kind), Labukt 2012 (several hedonic tones).
- **Core disagreement**: Whether the pleasant/unpleasant dimension projects across contexts as one property or fragments into several, and whether neural degeneracy (Lindquist 2016; Berridge 2019; Tye 2018) counts against a psychological-level kind (Chikazoe 2014 and Wilson-Mendenhall 2013 suggest a domain-general code).
- **Current state**: Open, and never addressed in the corpus. The Map's exposure is asymmetric: P-VS3 needs only that felt evaluative character be real and intrinsic at whatever grain; P-VS1 needs a single felt scalar at the moment of choice.

### Has the theory of constructed emotion retired its own primitive?
- **Sides**: Barrett 2009/2017 (affect as primitive; valence/arousal "basic features of consciousness") vs Barrett et al. 2025 ("pleasant, unpleasant … not properties of the intrinsic brain signals alone or of the incoming sensory signals alone but of their relations"; "unpleasant" as an "abstract feature"; "primitive" and "core affect" absent from the authors' own voice).
- **Core disagreement**: Whether the 2025 "relational features" ontology is a claim about *implementation* (compatible with a felt primitive, as 2009's "realized within a broadly distributed neuronal workspace" was) or about *constitution* (the felt quality is nothing over the relation — which is the illusionist-adjacent reading on which P-VS3 loses its relatum).
- **Current state**: Undecided in the text itself; the cited Barrett & Theriault 2025 is where it would be settled and was not located. This is the most valuable thing a new article could establish, and the one place the reviewers' objection has a live textual basis.

### Bipolar, bivariate, or workspace?
- **Sides**: Russell & Carroll 1999 (bipolar) vs Cacioppo et al. 1999 and Larsen & McGraw 2011 (bivariate/mixed) vs Lindquist et al. 2016 (neither at the fMRI level; workspace); Kron et al. 2013 (the answer depends on the scale used).
- **Current state**: Unresolved; the felt structure of valence is not known to be a single axis. Relevant to `negative-valence-asymmetry-and-the-selection-weighting-function` as well as P-VS1.

### Is there a common currency, and is it felt?
- **Sides**: P-VS1 (felt valence as the commensurating currency) vs Shuman, Sander & Scherer 2013 (macro-valence as an integrative "common currency" for choice, built from qualitatively distinct micro-valences) vs the neuroeconomic common-currency literature (the sibling harvest task on vmPFC/drift-diffusion).
- **Current state**: The appraisal-theory version of the objection is distinct from the neuroeconomic one and should be handled in this article, not the sibling: it grants that the micro-valences are felt and denies that the scalar is.
