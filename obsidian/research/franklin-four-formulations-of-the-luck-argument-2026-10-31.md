## Executive Summary

Franklin defends a **reductive, event-causal** libertarianism. His theory analyses the agent's role "solely in terms of events and states of the agent, eschewing any appeal to irreducible agent-causation" (p. 203). He then rejects four formulations of the luck argument, and the Mind argument as well. His diagnosis is that "A recurring mistake in these arguments concerns the exact location of indeterminism" (p. 228). In his view the ensurance and rollback formulations, and the Mind argument, place indeterminism *after* the agent's act (Kane's location), while his own account places it between the agent's reasons and her choice. He also agrees with van Inwagen "that agent-causation is of no help here" against the replay (p. 217). He never considers an agent-causal reply, and he never considers the disappearing-agent form, which is the form the Map's verdict against the event-causal wing actually uses. The Map's owed-primitive reply is therefore one Franklin does not consider. The Map, for its part, does not consider Franklin's two strongest moves: the denial that chance removes ability, and the claim that control does not track explanation. Both gaps are on the Map's side. **Verdict: FOLD** into `topics/event-causal-libertarianism` §The Luck and Disappearing-Agent Objection (passage drafted below, +288 words by `analyze_length`), with a one-clause pointer and two small corrections on `concepts/rollback-argument-against-agent-causation`.

## The Four Formulations (with Franklin's verdicts)

Franklin names them himself: "four different formulations: the Hume–Hobart formulation, the ensurance formulation, the rollback formulation, and the explanatory formulation" (p. 203). He first frames the core of the luck argument as two claims (p. 201): (i) an undetermined action is a matter of luck, and (ii) an action that is a matter of luck is not free.

| # | Formulation | Pages | Target / source | Franklin's verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hume–Hobart** | 208–209 | Hume's *Treatise*; Hobart 1934 | Fails. The argument's "key premise ... was that an undetermined action is an uncaused action" (p. 209). That premise is false because "causation can exist in the absence of necessitation" (p. 209; Anscombe 1971; quantum mechanics). "Once necessitation and causation are pried apart, the Hume–Hobart formulation crumbles" (p. 209). |
| 2 | **Ensurance** | 209–215 | Haji (1999, 2001), aimed at Kane (p. 214) | Fails on either reading. Premises: "(1) undetermined actions are not ensured, and (2) if an action is not ensured, then the action is a matter of luck" (p. 210). Read as *possessing* antecedent control, (1) is false: "agents can possess antecedent control over undetermined actions" (p. 211). Read as *exercising* it, (1) is true, but (2) is then either unmotivated (mere antecedent control) or produces a regress that makes freedom impossible for finite agents (free antecedent control). "Requiring mere antecedent control is unmotivated, and requiring free antecedent control makes freedom impossible for finite agents" (p. 213). Even so, "The obstacle to freedom so construed is our finitude, not determinism or indeterminism" (p. 213). |
| 3 | **Rollback** | 215–219 | van Inwagen 2000 (Alice; the promise case) | Premise (1) is granted. Premise (2) is denied. The premises are "(1) if a choice is undetermined, then it is a matter of luck, and (2) if it is a matter of luck that event E occurred, then no one was able to prevent or bring about E at that time" (p. 216). On van Inwagen's definition, "an event is a matter of chance just in case the objective probability of its occurring is less than 1", so "(1) is certainly true" (p. 216). Against (2): the promise case is disanalogous, because there the indeterminism falls after the choice (p. 217). On Franklin's ability account (A*), Alice has both abilities (p. 218). "What is undetermined is Alice's exercising her ability to choose to tell the truth" (p. 218). |
| 4 | **Explanatory** | 219–224 | Mele 1999a, 1999b, 2005, 2006 (Diana; the cross-world difference) | Fails. Mele's premise (2) is close to a tautology under his stipulated definition of luck, and the stipulation then gives no reason to think control is diminished (pp. 221–222). In Franklin's recast version, (1*) (nothing accounts for the cross-world difference) is "questionable": Hitchcock's probability-raising contrastive explanation is available, with the hedge "I do not take myself to have shown beyond doubt that contrastive explanations are available for undetermined events" (p. 224). (2*) is "dubious": "explanation does not tightly track control" (p. 224), and "it is possible for one to control an event even though the event's occurrence does not admit of a certain explanation" (p. 224). |

**The Mind argument** (pp. 225–228) is not one of the four. Franklin sets aside the dispute over BETA's validity (n. 34). He attacks the premise that the agent has no choice about whether R follows DB, which van Inwagen's button device does not establish because there the indeterminism falls after an action (p. 227). He then argues that having a choice about R and having a choice about R's following DB must be treated symmetrically (p. 227).

**Calibration of the whole paper (do not overstate it):**

- The rebuttal is **account-relative**. Franklin allows that accounts which mislocate indeterminism may be hit: "If van Inwagen has such accounts in mind, then his argument, for all I have argued, may well have purchase on these theories. My claim is that it leaves my own account untouched" (p. 228, n. 37).
- On Kane, Franklin is guarded: the point is "not that Kane's account fails" but "that he faces a unique charge of luck because he places indeterminism between actions" (p. 205).
- The Hitchcock move is hedged: "Hitchcock's account is admittedly controversial" (p. 223). See also the "beyond doubt" disclaimer quoted above.
- The paper defends only compatibility. "What remains to be seen is whether indeterminism is necessary for free will and moral responsibility, but this must wait for another occasion" (p. 229).

## Franklin on Agent Causation and on the Replay's Aim

- The replay's aim, as Franklin reads it: "the aim of this argument is not to show that free will is incompatible with indeterminism ... but to show that agent-causation is of no help in showing that indeterminism is compatible with free will" (p. 217). This agrees with the rollback page's own account (L33, L39).
- His own stance: "I agree with both these points—that agent-causation is of no help here and that this argument fails" (p. 217). He immediately adds that he "will not press the first, except insofar as I will show that event-causal libertarians do not have to appeal to agent-causation to handle this objection" (p. 217; the printed "event-causal" is broken across a line). So he *asserts* that agent causation does not help against the replay, but gives **no argument** for it.
- He also reports the dialectic the Map belongs to: "Agent-causalists sometimes offer the luck argument against their event-causal counterparts" (p. 217).
- His only reference to an agent-causal exercise comes inside the Mele quotation (see below). The terms "disappear(ing) agent" and "settle" have **0 hits** in the body. Pereboom is cited only for the problem of enhanced control (p. 202) and among Mele's critics (n. 28).

## Which Formulation the Map's Owed-Primitive Reply Answers, and Whether Franklin Considers It

**The Map's reply** (rollback L35, L63, L98; QIFW §Where Explanation Terminates L131; agent-causation L101): the agent's settling is a primitive. The Map says *who* settles the outcome and has no further answer to *why* it went one way, and it defends that stopping point as a legitimate terminus.

**Which formulation it answers.** It answers the rollback form (Franklin's no. 3) and Mele's explanatory or present-luck form (Franklin's no. 4). QIFW L86 frames the Map's luck reply against Mele's present luck. Mapped onto Franklin's schemas:

- **Rollback.** The Map grants the token-level chance, as Franklin does with premise (1). Its rollback page then works on the probability premise (the probability fork) and never states premise (2), the step from chance to inability (van Inwagen 2000: 16–17, the promise case). The page summarises the conclusion as "a mere matter of chance" (L33) and stops there.
- **Explanatory.** The Map concedes Mele's (1*) on its *why* reading, since there is no contrastive answer, and denies it on a *who* reading (agent-causation L101: "the agent *does* explain the difference through exercising causal power"). Its refusal to infer unfreedom from that terminus does the work of a denial of Franklin's (2*). The Map makes that refusal by **parity** ("event-causal explanations terminate at brute laws", QIFW L131). Franklin makes it by arguing that "Control is fundamentally a metaphysical matter and thus does not depend on the presence or absence of these pragmatic or epistemic conditions" (p. 221).

**Does Franklin consider the Map's reply? No.**

- He considers no agent-causal reply to any of the four formulations.
- He sets agent causation aside in one asserted, unargued clause (p. 217).
- The single point of contact is hostile. Mele's definition of luck, which Franklin quotes, is stated over the exercise of an agent-causal power: "if the question why an agent exercised his agent-causal power at t in deciding to A rather than exercising it at t in any of the alternative ways he does in other possible worlds with the same past and laws of nature is, in principle, unanswerable ... I count that as luck for the agent" (Mele 2006: 70, as quoted in Franklin 2011: 221). On that stipulation the Map's admitted terminus is luck **by definition**.
- Franklin's rejoinder is that "as the stipulative notion only indicates a lack of explanation, there is no reason to think that control is diminished" (p. 222). He also notes that "Mele (2006, p. 70) is willing to concede that 'luck' is not the right label" (n. 30). This is the defence the Map's terminus needs. But it comes from an event-causalist, and it protects the event-causal wing just as well.

**The gaps are on the Map's side.** Stated plainly:

1. **The rollback page never engages the premise Franklin attacks.** It does not state van Inwagen's chance-to-inability step, so it cannot say that Franklin's reply to the replay is ability-based. It also cannot say whether the Map's terminus needs that reply. Rollback L53's next sentence ("The contrastive explanation these replies use") implies that Franklin's reply is contrastive explanation. In the 2011 paper, contrastive explanation is his answer to Mele (pp. 222–224); his answer to the replay is the ability account (pp. 216–218).
2. **The ensurance formulation has zero corpus coverage.** `grep -rliF` on topics, concepts, apex, voids, positions and tenets gives "antecedent control" 0 live and 0 research hits, and "ensurance" 0 and 0. "Hume–Hobart" is also 0 and 0, although Hobart himself is named on rollback L45 for the Mind argument's lineage.
3. **ECL pairs Franklin 2011 with a verdict it does not address.** ECL L86 states the Map's verdict as the disappearing-agent gloss ("the undetermined event is not settled by anyone") and cites "Franklin's (2011) rebuttal of four formulations" as a rival reply that remains live. None of the four is the disappearing-agent form. Franklin takes that form up by name in a later paper (2014; title and metadata only, abstract elided by the publisher, body not read). The L86 calibration ("remain live") is sound. The defect is the pairing.
4. **Franklin's location test, applied to the Map. This is the Map's own analysis; no author makes it.** Franklin grants that "It is reasonable to suppose that indeterminism introduces an element of luck here: the agent does all he can, and then just has to wait to see what the outcome is" (p. 205), that is, where indeterminism falls between an act and its result. The Map's luck reply leans in part on "the phenomenology of effort" (QIFW L117 and L137; agent-causation L101, point 2). On the rollback page's propensity branch, outcomes have objective probabilities given the prior conscious state (rollback L81, L85). Whether the Map has Kane's location or Franklin's then depends on one question: is the effort an act of the agent inside that conditioning state, with the outcome still chancy given it, or is the effort the settling itself? The Map's wording ("choosing feels effortful") points to the second, but no page says so. This is a low-priority clarification, not a defect, and it should not be put into an article as Franklin's claim.

## The "Ensurance" Term: Who Coined It

- Franklin uses the noun in scare quotes: "From this lack of ''ensurance,'' it is inferred that the action in question is a matter of luck" (p. 210). He introduces "the ensurance formulation" as his label for the argument (pp. 203, 209).
- He credits the **argument**, not the word, to Haji: "The ensurance formulation of the luck argument has been most clearly and forcefully presented by Haji (1999, 2001)" (p. 209). Haji's own notion, as Franklin reports it, is "antecedent control", which Franklin glosses with a quotation from Haji 2001, p. 190 (p. 210).
- **Coiner of the noun: UNVERIFIED.** Haji 2001's body was not reached: Wiley, no OpenAlex abstract, and WebSearch found no pre-2011 use. Safe wording: "what Franklin calls the ensurance formulation", argued "most forcefully" by Haji. Do **not** write that Haji coined "ensurance", and do not write that Franklin did.
- Kane concedes the underlying point. Franklin quotes Kane 1996: 144 (p. 210) to the effect that libertarian agents cannot guarantee outcomes in advance. Kane's strategy is "to concede that libertarian agents lack antecedent control, but deny that this entails that their actions are a matter of luck" (p. 210). That makes the Kane passage at ECL L78 a natural neighbour for the fold.
