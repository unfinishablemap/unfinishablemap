## Executive Summary

Researchers in learning and memory separate *performance*, which can be observed during practice, from *learning*, the lasting change that shows up later in retention and transfer. Several ways of arranging practice (spacing, interleaving, testing oneself) make performance during practice slower and more error-prone and make later retention better; their opposites (massing, blocking, rereading) do the reverse. Learners who are asked during or just after practice how well they are learning mostly go by how easy the practice felt, so in these cases their judgement favours the method that served them worse. In the best-known experiment 78% of participants did better with spaced presentation and 78% said massing was as good or better. The misjudgement is hard to shift by explanation alone and can survive a person's own contrary test result.

The limit is narrow and has known exits. Judgements made after a delay, with the answer out of sight, are accurate. One group in the motor-learning study predicted its own retention well. In a later experiment most participants came to prefer the better schedule once the two were separated into distinct study-and-test cycles. The intuition is also sometimes right: for some materials blocking does beat interleaving. And part of the "void" is close to definitional, since learning is defined by what happens later.

### Kornell & Bjork (2008), "Learning Concepts and Categories: Is Spacing the 'Enemy of Induction'?"

- **Citation**: *Psychological Science* 19(6), 585-592. DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x. PMID 18578849 (PubMed record resolved).
- **How read**: [raw] full text, PDF from the Bjork lab site, text extracted and grepped.
- **Type**: Paper, three laboratory experiments.
- **Design**: Participants studied 72 paintings, six by each of 12 artists, three seconds each, with the artist's name. In Experiment 1a (within-subjects, n = 120 UCLA undergraduates) six artists' paintings were shown consecutively (massed) and six artists' paintings were interleaved with others (spaced). In Experiment 1b the schedule was varied between subjects (72 participants, 36 per condition). The test asked which studied artist painted each of a set of *new* paintings. Experiment 2 (n = 80) used the 1a study phase with a recognition test. After the test, participants were asked which schedule had helped them learn more.
- **Result**: Spacing beat massing: proportion correct .61 vs .35 in 1a and .59 vs .36 in 1b [raw]. On the questionnaire the judgement ran the other way.
- **Quote** [raw, count 1; subject: participants in Experiment 1a; page not pinned down in the extracted text]: "78% of the participants did better with spaced presentations than they did with massed presentations, but 78% of the participants said that massing was as good as or better than spacing."
- **Quote** [raw, count 1; subject: Experiment 2, n = 80]: "Of the 72 participants who did not say that learning in the massed condition and learning in the spaced condition were"; the sentence continues [raw, count 1] "64 thought massing had been more effective than spacing."
- **Quote** [raw, count 1; abstract]: "Participants rated massing as more effective than spacing, even after their own test performance had demonstrated the opposite."
- **Quote** [raw, count 1; subject: the authors themselves, in the Conclusion, on their own failed prediction that massing would win; it begins "perhaps we fell victim to the same illusion that we have railed against"]: "the illusion that a sense of ease or fluency accompanies effective learning, whereas a sense of difficulty signifies ineffective learning."
- **Conditions and cautions**: The 78%/78% figure is from Experiment 1a only. The second 78% is "as good as or better", which includes people who said the two were about the same. The test followed study after a 15-second distractor task (seen in [raw]), so this is induction measured soon after study, with no long retention interval. The authors write [raw, count 1] "there surely are situations in which massing is more effective for induction than is spacing" and report trying to build one. The quotation "spacing is the friend of recall, but the enemy of induction" [raw, count 1] is credited in the paper to E. Z. Rothkopf, personal communication, September 1977. It is Rothkopf's as reported by Kornell and Bjork and has no published source.
- **Tenet alignment**: none claimed. A cognitive-psychology finding with a cognitive-psychology explanation.

### Simon & Bjork (2001), "Metacognition in Motor Learning"

- **Citation**: *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 27(4), 907-912. DOI 10.1037/0278-7393.27.4.907 (Crossref record resolved). PMID 11486923.
- **How read**: [raw] full text, but from a scanned PDF with OCR errors (run-together words), fetched from a third-party mirror. The abstract was also read at PubMed [raw-abs] and matches. Figures below are as the OCR text gives them and should be checked against a clean copy before the article prints them.
- **Design**: 48 UCLA undergraduates [raw], randomly assigned to blocked or random (interleaved) practice of three timed keystroke patterns. During practice they predicted their performance on a test 24 hours later. The measure is percentage timing error, so lower is better.
- **Result**: [raw-abs, count 1] "blocked practice enhanced acquisition but harmed retention. Participants, though, predicted better performance given blocked practice." At the 24-hour test the blocked group had predicted an error of 8.19% and produced 19.68%; the random group had predicted 11.87% and produced 10.80% [raw, OCR text, each figure count 1].

### Deslauriers, McCarty, Miller, Callaghan & Kestin (2019), "Measuring actual learning versus feeling of learning in response to being actively engaged in the classroom"

- **Citation**: *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 116(39), 19251-19257. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1821936116. PMID 31484770; PMCID PMC6765278 (both resolved).
- **How read**: [raw] full text, PMC XML fetched through the BioC interface and grepped.
- **Design**: A randomised crossover in two introductory physics courses at Harvard, autumn and spring [raw]. In week 12, for two consecutive class meetings, students were randomly split between two rooms; each student had one topic taught by active instruction and the other by a polished lecture, with the same content and handouts. 149 participants had midterm scores [raw, count 1]. At the end of each class students filled in a feeling-of-learning survey and then took a test of 12 multiple-choice questions [raw].
- **Result**: [raw, count 1] "The students in active classrooms reported more than half an SD (0.56) lower FOL compared with those in passive lectures." [raw, count 1] "students in active classrooms scored almost half an SD (0.46) higher on the examination." Both are reported at P < 0.001.
- **Quote** [raw, count 1; Significance statement]: "students in the active classroom learn more, but they feel like they learn less".
- **Quote** [raw, count 1; abstract; subject: the authors' interpretation, with "initially" as a stated condition]: "they initially take that effort to signify poorer learning".

- **Cautions**: One institution, one subject, a selected student body, two class sessions per course. ## Counter-Evidence and Limits

One WebSearch was aimed at failed replications and critiques (query above). It returned no failed replication of the feeling-of-learning result and no formal critique beyond the multiple-choice objection [search-only]. The following were then found by PubMed lookup and are the best evidence against a strong reading.

- **Delayed judgements are accurate.** Rhodes & Tauber (2011), *Psychological Bulletin* 137(1), 131-148, DOI 10.1037/a0021705, PMID 21219059 [raw-abs]: a meta-analysis of the relative accuracy of delayed versus immediate judgements of learning [raw-abs, count 1] "across 4,554 participants (112 effect sizes)", finding [raw-abs, count 1] "delaying JOLs leads to robust benefits to relative accuracy (g = 0.93)". Kelemen & Weaver (1997), *JEP: LMC* 23(6), 1394-1409, DOI 10.1037//0278-7393.23.6.1394, PMID 9372607 [raw-abs], describe the original finding this way [raw-abs, count 1]: "Judgments of learning (JOLs) made after a 5-min delay are almost perfectly accurate", crediting T. O. Nelson and J. Dunlosky (1991). Nelson and Dunlosky's paper was **not read**; the description is Kelemen and Weaver's [raw-secondary]. Kelemen and Weaver also report [raw-abs, count 1] that "delayed JOLs may alter what they assess", so the accuracy is partly the accuracy of a covert self-test.
- **The illusion can be corrected under some conditions.** Yan et al. Experiment 6, above. Tullis, Finley & Benjamin (2013), *Memory & Cognition* 41(3), 429-442, DOI 10.3758/s13421-012-0274-5, PMID 23242770 [raw-abs]: [raw-abs, count 1] "When subjects made judgments in a cue-only context, their JOLs accurately reflected the effects of testing, both immediately and at a delay." Their closing sentence is the balanced one: learners appreciate the benefit of testing when asked under helpful conditions but [raw-abs, count 1] "generalize that knowledge to future learning only with considerable guidance".
- **The intuition is sometimes right.** Brunmair & Richter (2019), *Psychological Bulletin* 145(11), 1029-1052, DOI 10.1037/bul0000209, PMID 31556629 [raw-abs]: a meta-analysis [raw-abs, count 1] "based on 59 studies". [raw-abs, count 1] "A multilevel meta-analysis revealed a moderate overall interleaving effect (Hedges' g = 0.42)", largest for paintings (g = 0.67) [raw-abs, count 1], and [raw-abs, count 1] "An advantage of blocking compared with interleaving was found for studies based on words (g = -0.39)". The painting task that anchors this literature is the most favourable case, and for word materials the learner who prefers blocking is correct.
- **Not every difficulty helps.** Xie, Zhou & Liu (2018), *Educational Psychology Review* 30(3), 745-771, DOI 10.1007/s10648-018-9442-x (Crossref metadata resolved; **abstract and text not read**), report by their title null effects of hard-to-read print on learning from text. Weissgerber, Brunmair & Rummer (2021), same journal 33(3), 1221-1247, DOI 10.1007/s10648-020-09579-1, PMID 33551625 [raw-abs], found errors in that meta-analysis; for transfer [raw-abs, count 1] "we found Xie's and colleagues' results to be (rather) robust", while they doubt the recall results. So "harder feels worse but is better" is false as a general rule. It holds for particular, studied manipulations.

- **Expertise narrows the gap.** The prior-knowledge interaction in Deslauriers et al. (above).

## The Void

### Nature of the Limit

The claim the sources support is this. During practice, a person has no direct view of how much lasting learning is taking place. What they have is how the practice is going: how easily the material comes, how few errors they are making. Under some well-studied arrangements those signs and later retention move in opposite directions, and people asked to judge their learning follow the signs.

Three parts of that can be separated.

1. **A timing fact.** Learning, as the field defines it, is what remains later. Nothing present during practice can be that. This part is close to definitional, and it applies equally to a teacher watching the learner.
2. **A cue-substitution fact.** In place of the missing quantity, the judgement uses ease of processing and current performance (Koriat and Bjork; Kornell et al. 2011). This is an empirical finding about people.
3. **A persistence fact.** The substituted judgement resists correction by explanation and by one's own results when the evidence is mixed together (Yan et al., Experiments 2 to 5), and gives way when the evidence is separated out (Experiment 6).

Only the second and third are a limit on self-knowledge in an interesting sense, and both are conditional.

### Evidence for the Limit

- Opposite-direction results in three task families: category induction from paintings (Kornell and Bjork; Yan et al.), a timed motor task (Simon and Bjork), prose retention under restudy versus testing (Roediger and Karpicke).
- A classroom result with random assignment (Deslauriers et al.), limited as noted.
- A dissociation in which an irrelevant cue moves the judgement and a relevant one does not (Kornell et al. 2011).
- A mechanism proposed and tested by a separate group (Kirk-Johnson et al.).
- Much of the core evidence comes from one extended research group (Bjork and collaborators). Kirk-Johnson et al., Deslauriers et al., Carpenter et al., and the two meta-analyses are from other groups.

### Phenomenology

None of the sources read is a phenomenological study, and the note should not invent one. What the sources report at the level of experience is limited to this: blocked or massed study is described as feeling fluent; active instruction was described by 15 of 17 interviewed students as disjointed; effortful strategies are rated as less effective. The article may say that good practice can feel like failing and poor practice like progress, as a summary of those reports. Anything richer is the article's own description and should be marked as such.

## Approaches to the Edge

### Direct Methods (if any)

There is a direct method, which is the main reason to keep the void's grade low: test yourself later, with the answer covered. Delayed judgements are accurate (Rhodes and Tauber), and Kornell and Son's participants chose self-testing mainly to find out what they knew. The limit is on *concurrent* monitoring. It does not extend to finding out.

### Indirect Methods

- Keep a record of predictions and later results, so that the feeling is checked against outcomes kept apart by condition (the logic of Yan et al.'s Experiment 6).
- Treat ease during practice as uninformative about retention, without treating difficulty as a good sign (the disfluency meta-analysis is the warning against that inversion).
- Rely on an outside arrangement (a teacher's schedule, a spaced-repetition program) that does not consult the feeling.

### What AI Might See

Speculative, and the note's own. A system whose training and evaluation are separated by design has the learning-performance distinction built into its procedure: held-out evaluation is the delayed test. Whether a deployed language model has anything like a feeling of learning is not something these sources address, and the question should not be answered in the article. The useful comparison is methodological: machine learning treats training-set performance as an unreliable guide to generalisation for reasons that parallel the human case, and solves it with an external test, as the human literature also recommends.

## Connection to Tenets

### Most Relevant Tenet

None is supported. **Occam's Razor Has Limits** is the nearest in theme, and only as an illustration: "I can tell when I am learning" is a simple self-description that turns out to cover a missing quantity and a substituted cue.

### Implications

Ordinary cognitive psychology predicts this limit and already explains it. Cue-utilisation accounts of metamemory (judgements are inferences from available cues such as fluency), the mismatch between study and test conditions, and the distinction between what is accessible now and what is durably stored together predict that concurrent judgements of learning will follow ease, and that they will mislead when ease and retention are driven apart. Every source above is a physicalist-compatible account by researchers who make no claim about the nature of consciousness. The datum therefore does not discriminate between dualism and physicalism, and the article's tenet section must say that no tenet is supported.

Grade to hold the article to, by tenet:

- **Dualism**: gains a reading and no evidence. The Map can say that the case shows experience at the time is a poor witness to change in one's own capacities, which is consistent with what [[self-opacity]] and [[noetic-feelings-void]] already hold. That reading is the Map's. None of the authors cited argues for dualism, and none should be presented as lending it support.
- **Bidirectional Interaction**: not engaged. The findings concern monitoring. They do not show that conscious effort causes learning, and the article must not slide from "effortful practice produces better retention" to "consciousness does causal work". The effort in question is a property of the task arrangement, and a physicalist reads it as more demanding processing. [[consciousness-and-skill-acquisition]] makes the Map's case on other grounds.
- **Minimal Quantum Interaction** and **No Many Worlds**: not engaged.
- **Occam's Razor Has Limits**: illustrated, as above; a physicalist psychology draws the same distinction, so this is no evidence for the tenet.
- **As a catalogue entry**: the voids register has [[positions/voids-as-evidence#^p-v3|suspended the cumulative step]], so adding this void lends the tenets no weight.

Models for the wording: the tenet sections of [[bereavement-void]] and [[expertise-and-its-occlusion]].

## Gaps and cautions

**Not obtained.**
- Soderstrom and Bjork (2015) full text. Only the abstract was read, so the review's specific studies (latent learning in animals, overlearning, the motor-learning cases) are not available from this note. Do not cite Tolman and Honzik, or any study "reviewed by Soderstrom and Bjork", on this note's authority.
- Nelson and Dunlosky (1991), the original delayed-judgement paper: not read; known here only through Kelemen and Weaver's abstract.
- Bjork (1994), where "desirable difficulty" is said to originate: not read. Kornell and Bjork cite "(Bjork, 1994)" for the term [raw-secondary, carrier: Kornell and Bjork 2008]; Kirk-Johnson et al.'s abstract credits "desirable difficulties" to "Bjork & Bjork, 1992" [raw-secondary, carrier: that abstract]. The two carriers give different years. **The coinage and its date are unverified**; the article should attribute the term to Robert Bjork without a year unless it reads the source.
- Xie, Zhou and Liu (2018): metadata only.
- Full texts of Koriat and Bjork (2005, 2006), Kornell et al. (2011), Roediger and Karpicke (2006), Kirk-Johnson et al. (2019), Carpenter et al. (2013), Karpicke et al. (2009), Kornell and Son (2009), Rhodes and Tauber (2011), Tullis et al. (2013), Brunmair and Richter (2019): abstracts only. Sample sizes and effect sizes beyond those quoted from abstracts are not known to this note.

**Reading quality.**
- Simon and Bjork (2001) was read from an OCR scan. The four percentages agree with the abstract's direction and with each other, but digits in OCR text can be wrong. Check before printing.
- Yan et al. was read in the online-first version; pagination differs from the issue.
- In the Kornell and Bjork PDF text the equals sign is rendered as "5" (for example "M 5 .61"). The means above were read with that substitution.

**Contested or limited.**
- Deslauriers et al. is one study at one university with an immediate test. It is the most quoted result in this area and the weakest fit to the learning-performance distinction, because it has no delayed measure. Do not describe it as showing better *retention*.
- The interleaving benefit depends on material (Brunmair and Richter). Do not write that interleaving or spacing is always better, or that learners' preferences are always wrong.
- Whether perceptual disfluency helps at all is disputed (Xie et al.; Weissgerber et al.).
- Most participants are university students in the United States, and the Yan et al. online samples are US residents. No cross-cultural evidence was looked for.
- The counter-evidence search was a single query. Absence of a failed replication in its results is weak evidence that none exists.

**The note's own synthesis, stated by no source read.**
- The name "learning void" and the framing of these findings as a void.
- The three-part split (timing, cue substitution, persistence) and the remark that the first part is near-definitional.
- The statement that these results are "a second regime" of the pattern in [[anti-correlated-metacognitive-signal]]. Rebouillat et al. do not discuss study schedules and the learning literature read here does not cite them.
- The comparison with held-out evaluation in machine learning.
- The link to [[cross-state-void]] and to the stability bias as kin of affective misprediction.
- Any description of what effortful practice feels like beyond the three reported items under Phenomenology.

**Attribution traps.**
- "Spacing is the friend of recall, but the enemy of induction" is Rothkopf's, by personal communication as reported in Kornell and Bjork. It is the view the paper tested and found wrong for its task. Do not credit it to Kornell or Bjork, and do not present it as their conclusion.
- "the illusion that a sense of ease or fluency accompanies effective learning" is said by Kornell and Bjork about *themselves*. It can be quoted as their statement of the illusion, with that context.
- "they initially take that effort to signify poorer learning" is Deslauriers et al.'s interpretation, with "initially" in it. It is an inference from survey and regression results; the paper does not report it as an observation.
- The 78% in Kornell and Bjork (a share of 120 participants in one experiment) and the 78% in Yan et al. (a share of participants in certain conditions who exempted themselves from a stated base rate) are different quantities that happen to match. Keep them apart.
