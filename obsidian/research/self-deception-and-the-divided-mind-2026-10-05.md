# Research: Self-Deception and the Divided Mind

## Executive Summary

Davidson treats self-deception as the belief-side twin of akrasia: "weakness of the warrant" has "the same logical structure (or, better, illogical structure) as weakness of the will". His solution is the same partition, with one addition that matters for the Map. The boundary between the conflicting beliefs is "not discovered by introspection"; it is a "conceptual aid" to describing the agent coherently. Pears goes further and makes the deceiving part "a separate centre of agency". Mele's deflation denies the datum both are built to explain: garden-variety self-deception needs neither an intention to deceive nor two contradictory beliefs, only motivationally biased handling of evidence, and he reports that no one has met his challenge to produce a real dual-belief case. The live dispute is now over "deep-conflict" cases, where avowal and behaviour come apart, and over whether self-deception is essentially a failure of self-knowledge. The Map's stake is narrow: nothing here bears on dualism, Mele's account is friendly to a single subject, and the verification limit of P-A4 has a doxastic analogue that the literature states almost in so many words.

## Key Sources

### Davidson, "Deception and Division" [S]

Pages are those of Elster (ed.), *The Multiple Self* (CUP 1986), pp. 79–92. The essay also appeared in LePore & McLaughlin (eds.), *Actions and Events* (Blackwell 1985); Mele cites that printing as "Davidson 1985, p. 146", and the SEP's own in-text "Davidson 1985" in §4 has no bibliography entry. This resolves the date lead left open on 2026-10-04. The reprint is *Problems of Rationality* (2004), pp. 199–212.

- **The twin.** "Weakness of the warrant can occur only when a person has evidence both for and against a hypothesis" (p. 81). It "obviously has the same logical structure (or, better, illogical structure) as weakness of the will; the former involves an irrational belief in the face of conflicting evidence, the latter an irrational intention (and perhaps also action)" (p. 82; the sentence continues past the snippet). The norm violated is the Hempel–Carnap "requirement of total evidence for inductive reasoning" (Crossref abstract of the reprint [A]).
- **Self-deception is more than weak warrant.** "the person who is self-deceived must have a reason for his weakness of the warrant, and he must have played a part in bringing it about" (reprint p. 205). "Not all wishful thinking is self-deception, since the latter but not the former requires intervention by the agent" (reprint p. 206).
- **The definition** (p. 88, two overlapping snippets stitched): "A has evidence on the basis of which he believes that p is more apt to be true than its negation; the thought that p, or the thought that he ought rationally to believe p, motivates A to act in such a way as to cause himself to believe the negation of p. The action involved may be no more than an intentional directing of attention away from the evidence in favour of p; or it may involve the active search for evidence against p."
- **Not lying to oneself.** "lying to oneself would entail the existence of a self-defeating intention, while self-deception pits intention and desire against belief, and belief against belief" (p. 88).
- **Coexistence.** "the state that motivates self-deception and the state it produces coexist; in the strongest case, the belief that p not only causes a belief in the negation of p, but also sustains it" (p. 89). "Self-deception is thus a form of self-induced weakness of the warrant, where the motive for inducing a belief is a contradictory belief (or what is deemed to be sufficient evidence in favour of the contradictory belief)" (p. 89). The parenthesis is a hedge worth keeping: Davidson's own formula allows the motivating state to be an assessment of evidence instead of an outright belief, which is close to where Mele ends up.
- **The boundary.** One can believe each of a set of inconsistent propositions, but "it is not possible to believe the conjunction when the inconsistency is obvious" (p. 91). So: "we must accept the idea that there can be boundaries between parts of the mind; I postulate such a boundary somewhere between any (obviously) conflicting beliefs. Such boundaries are not discovered by introspection" (p. 91); "they are conceptual aids to the coherent description of genuine irrationalities" (pp. 91–92; a footnote intervenes in the scan, and the reprint abstract confirms the two clauses belong to one sentence).
- **The parts overlap.** "Contradictory beliefs about passing a test must each belong to a vast and identical network of beliefs about tests and related matters if they are to be contradictory" (p. 92). The parts cannot be separate believers, because the beliefs would then fail to contradict.
- **Where the irrationality sits.** "the irrational step is therefore the step that makes this possible, the drawing of the boundary that keeps the inconsistent beliefs apart" (p. 92). The desire to avoid what the total-evidence requirement counsels "cannot be a reason for neglecting the requirement. Nothing can be viewed as a good reason for failing to reason according to one's best standards of rationality" (p. 92). And "being out of bounds does not make the exiled thought powerless; on the contrary, since reason has no jurisdiction across the boundary" (p. 92).
- **Davidson on Pears.** "The differences between my view and Pears' are small compared" (p. 90, n. 7, truncated). He attributes to Pears the view that the self-deceiver must "'forget' or otherwise conceal from himself how he came to believe what he does", and puts the residue down to "different choices as to how to describe self-deception rather than to substantive differences. To me it seems important to identify an incoherence or inconsistency in the thought of the self-deceiver" (pp. 90–91).

**Tenet alignment**: neutral. Davidson is a naturalist and the boundary is an interpretive posit.

### Pears, *Motivated Irrationality* (OUP 1984) [S via Mele; 2nd]

Not reached directly. Mele (2001, p. 21) quotes Pears: "[There is a] sub-system built around the nucleus of the wish for the irrational belief and it is organized like a person. Although it is a separate centre of agency within the whole person, it is, from its own point of view, entirely rational." The Pears page number was outside the snippet. The SEP grades the options: Rorty's "relatively autonomous subagency", Pears's "separate center of agency", and "the relatively modest division of Davidson (1982, 1986), where there need only be a boundary between conflicting attitudes and intentions". Compare Davidson 1982, already quoted in [[the-divided-will]]: the division does not demand "a little agent".

### Mele, *Self-Deception Unmasked* (Princeton UP 2001) [S]; "Real self-deception" (*BBS* 20(1): 91–102, 1997) [A]

- **Thesis.** Self-deception "is explicable without the assistance of mental exotica. The key to understanding its dynamics is a proper appreciation of our capacity for acquiring and retaining motivationally biased beliefs" (1997 abstract). The book uses "mental exotica" at p. 4. **Fidelity flag:** the SEP puts 'psychological exotica' in quote marks, once beside "(Mele 2001)" and once unattributed; the only "exotica" the book's index returned is "mental exotica". Quote Mele's phrase, not the SEP's.
- **Two puzzles.** A *static* puzzle (the state: believing p and not-p) and a *dynamic* puzzle (the process), stated at p. 60: "if prospective self-deceivers have no strategy, how can they succeed? And if they do have a strategy, how can their attempt to carry it out fail to be self-undermining".
- **Sufficient conditions** for entering self-deception in acquiring a belief that p (pp. 50–51, stitched): "1. The belief that p which S acquires is false. 2. S treats data relevant, or at least seemingly relevant, to the truth value of p in a motivationally biased way. 3. This biased treatment is a nondeviant cause of S's acquiring the belief that p. 4. The body of data possessed by S at the time provides greater warrant for [not-]p than for p." They are offered as sufficient, not necessary (p. 119). The SEP reports two conditions added in 2012 [2nd]: S consciously believes there is a significant chance that not-p, and the belief is a product of "reflective, critical reasoning" that S wrongly regards as properly directed.
- **Mechanisms.** Negative and positive misinterpretation, selective focusing and selective evidence-gathering (pp. 26–28), driven by an error-cost ("FTL") model of lay hypothesis testing: "People tend to test hypotheses in ways that minimize costly errors" (p. 40).
- **Against agency views.** Pears and Davidson are his two exhibits of the "agency" view (p. 21). His verdict: "Stereotypical interpersonal deception is intentional deception; ordinary self-deception, I have argued, probably is not" (p. 74); "we lack good grounds for holding that such intentions or attempts are operative in garden-variety self-deception" (p. 74).
- **The challenge.** "I challenge critics to provide convincing evidence of the existence of instances of self-deception that satisfy the dual-belief requirement" (p. 76). After examining Gur & Sackeim (1979) and Quattrone & Tversky (1984): "The most influential empirical work on the topic has not met the challenge, as I have shown" (p. 93). His arguments against those studies were not read beyond snippets [Lead].
- **A third-person criterion.** Bias is gauged by an "impartial observer test" (pp. 106–110, 120–121): roughly, whether cognitive peers without the desire would reach the belief on the same data. Only the name and page range were verified; the gloss is a lead.

**Tenet alignment**: neutral, and locally convenient. See Map Relevance.

### SEP, "Self-Deception" (DeWeese-Boyd; rev. 13 Mar 2023) [2nd]

- Taxonomy: *intentionalist* accounts (temporal or psychological partitioning) against *revisionist* ones, which revise the intention requirement (deflation) or the belief requirement.
- On partitions: "there appears to be consensus even among intentionalists that self-deception can and should be accounted for without invoking speculative or stipulative divisions not already used to explain non-self-deceptive behavior, what Talbott (1995) calls 'innocent' divisions."
- Four standing objections to deflation: it cannot separate self-deception from wishful thinking; the *selectivity problem* (Bermúdez: strong desire often fails to bias); the *tension* problem; and the self-knowledge problem.
- **Deep conflict.** Critics hold that suspicion that not-p cannot explain "deep-conflict cases in which what self-deceivers say about p is seriously at odds with non-verbal behavior that justifies the attribution of the belief that ~p". Some conclude deflationary accounts describe "self-delusion" (Audi, Funkhouser, Gendler). Deflationists reply that someone who unconsciously believes the truth and merely avows otherwise is not deceived (Lynch 2012).
- **Self-knowledge.** Holton (2001) argues Mele's conditions are insufficient because they do not require a false belief about oneself: a person who knowingly ignores evidence is "willfully ignorant but not self-deceived". Funkhouser (2005) and Fernández (2013) relocate the product to the second order: the self-deceiver believes not-p and falsely believes that she believes p.
- **Belief may be the wrong category.** Schwitzgebel's "in-between believing"; Funkhouser (2009): "the self-deceived produce a confused belief-like condition so that it is genuinely indeterminate what they believe regarding p."
- **Unconscious goal pursuit.** Bargh-style research "seems to support the possibility of the sort of robust unconscious but flexible goal pursuit" that intentionalists need; the entry calls it "controversial" and cites Funkhouser & Barrett and Doody as sceptics.

### von Hippel & Trivers, "The evolution and psychology of self-deception" (*BBS* 34(1): 1–16, 2011) [A]

"self-deception evolved to facilitate interpersonal deception by allowing people to avoid the cues to conscious deception that might reveal deceptive intent." On how the self can be deceiver and deceived: "this is achieved through dissociations of mental processes, including conscious versus unconscious memories, conscious versus unconscious attitudes, and automatic versus controlled processes." These are Talbott-style innocent divisions, already posited for other reasons. Mele (2001, p. 89) answers the earlier Trivers (1985), saying of some alternative to dual belief that it "is no less effective a tool for deceiving others"; the snippet cuts the sentence's subject, so what exactly he substitutes is a lead. The Map currently cites only Trivers's 2011 trade book, in [[self-maintained-cognitive-limits]].

## Key Debates

1. **Is the dual-belief datum real?** Mele says no case has been shown. Deep-conflict theorists say Ellen-type cases (confident avowal, dread, systematic avoidance) show it. Funkhouser (2019, per SEP) suggests the dispute is partly over which cases are central. Unresolved.
2. **Selectivity.** Intention explains why desire biases belief only sometimes; error-cost models explain some of it; Jurjako (2013, per SEP) notes intentionalists have a selectivity problem of their own.
3. **Is self-deception a failure of self-knowledge?** Holton's willful-ignorance case says yes. Mele's 2012 condition 6 concedes a version of it.
4. **Kind or cluster.** Whether deflationary and deep-conflict cases are one psychological kind.

## Map Relevance

**1. Does belief press the single subject harder than action?** Partly, and the pressure is on self-knowledge. The clear-eyed akratic knows she is acting against her judgement; the self-deceiver cannot occupy the matching thought. Davidson's line that one cannot "believe the conjunction when the inconsistency is obvious" makes the boundary do for belief what no boundary needs to do for action. But his overlap requirement (p. 92) blocks the reading on which there are two believers: the conflicting beliefs must share one network to conflict at all. The partition divides what the subject can bring together, and leaves the count of subjects at one. [[the-divided-will]] already records that Davidson's parts are functional; the new material is the overlap argument and the introspection sentence.

**2. Mele's deflation is friendly to a single subject and indifferent to its nature.** If garden-variety self-deception is biased evidence-handling by one believer, no partition is needed. This repeats the result [[the-divided-will]] drew from Mele on akrasia: a unitary *physical* agent is available, so unity gives the Map's selector nothing. The article should not present Mele as an ally of dualism.

**3. The verification limit, applied to belief.** P-A4 in [[positions/agency-and-will]] says no agent can certify its own causal efficacy by introspection, because the check is run by the faculty in question. The literature supplies a structural parallel for belief, with three independent anchors: Davidson's boundaries "are not discovered by introspection"; Mele's criterion of bias is an impartial *observer* test; Holton, Funkhouser and Fernández make a false belief about one's own belief or reasoning essential to the phenomenon. On all three, the question "is this belief of mine motivated?" is answered by the reasoning whose bias is at issue. Two cautions. First, this is an analogy to P-A4, not an instance of it: P-A4 concerns causal efficacy and is meta-epistemic; the doxastic limit is contingent and partial, since people do catch their own self-deception afterwards and third parties catch it routinely. Second, it cuts against the Map's own practice. The Map leans on first-person report for phenomenal claims; the self-deception literature concerns propositional attitudes and one's access to them, so it does not directly impugn phenomenal report, but the article should say why the line holds and cite [[introspection]] for where the Map already draws it.

**4. Davidson's surd recurs.** In [[the-divided-will]] a selector that departs from supplied values moves Davidson's surd into the subject. The doxastic version: "Nothing can be viewed as a good reason for failing to reason according to one's best standards." A single believer who walls off the total-evidence requirement does so for a cause that cannot be its reason. Either the irrational step is sub-personal (and the conscious subject did not do it) or it is the subject's (and is inexplicable from the subject's own view). Mele escapes by denying any such step is taken intentionally.

**5. Bad faith.** [[authentic-vs-inauthentic-choice]] calls bad faith "self-deception about one's freedom" without engaging this literature. Sartre's objection to Freud's censor (the censor must know what it represses) is the classic anti-partition argument and belongs in the article, but it was **not sourced in this session** [Lead: *Being and Nothingness*, Part One, ch. 2].

**Tenets.** Dualism: untouched. Bidirectional interaction: untouched; self-deception is a case where motivation shapes belief by routes that are fully describable psychologically. Occam's limits: Mele's argument is an explicit parsimony argument, and the Map cannot wave it off with the fifth tenet, because Mele's simpler account is backed by independent evidence about biased hypothesis testing and not by simplicity alone.
