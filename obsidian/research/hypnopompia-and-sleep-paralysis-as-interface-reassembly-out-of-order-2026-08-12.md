## Executive Summary

The subject is genuinely uncovered and worth writing, but **the framing proposed by the commissioning review needs correction before an article is drafted**. The review's stated premise — that the anaesthesia article's induction/emergence asymmetry "predicts hypnopompia should differ structurally from hypnagogia" — runs into a well-established contrary consensus in the sleep literature: following Mavromatis (1987), most researchers report no clear phenomenological or physiological difference between hypnagogic and hypnopompic hallucinations and treat them as a single phenomenon (Ghibellini & Meier, 2023). An article that leads with "hypnopompia differs structurally from hypnagogia" would be asserting the opposite of the field's default position on thin evidence.

The stronger and better-evidenced version of the same thesis does not rest on the hypnagogic/hypnopompic phenomenological contrast at all. It rests on three independent literatures that *do* support ordered, channel-specific, direction-asymmetric transition:

1. **Sleep inertia shows demonstrably asynchronous network recovery.** Wang et al. (2024, *Scientific Reports*, n=21, simultaneous EEG-fMRI) found the thalamus restores its neurovascular coupling timing fastest after awakening (peak-correlation lag ~8 s → ~2.5 s by the third post-waking scan), the anterior cingulate changing significantly but *non-monotonically* (lag running earlier through the second scan, then settling back to ~6 s by the third — NB: not "an intermediate rate"; that gloss misdescribes the paper), and the sensorimotor cortex not changing at all across the measured window. Related work reports the sensorimotor network still looking disconnected post-awakening while the default-mode network has already recovered. Channels come back at different rates — this is the empirical core of "out of order," and it is measured rather than inferred from retrospective report.
2. **The transition is hysteretic, not reversible.** Kim, Moon, Mashour & Lee (2018, *PLOS Computational Biology*) argue that loss and recovery of consciousness follow distinct pathways as a network-level property, not a pharmacological artefact — the same claim the Map's anaesthesia article already makes via neural inertia, but generalised to a mechanism that should apply to sleep as well as anaesthesia. This is the honest bridge from the anaesthesia wing to the sleep wing.
3. **Sleep paralysis has its own electrophysiological signature, intermediate between REM and wake.** Mainieri et al. (2020/2021, *J Clin Sleep Med*) found sleep paralysis showing reduced theta and increased alpha relative to REM; Herrero et al. (2025, *Scientific Reports*, n=7) report reduced theta plus increased beta and low-gamma versus both REM and stage 1. Sleep paralysis is not "REM with the lights on" — it is a third configuration, which is exactly what a partial, out-of-order reassembly should look like.

Sleep paralysis is a strong natural experiment for the Map's thesis because in it the motor channel, the reality-monitoring channel and the environmental-access channel demonstrably come back in the wrong order relative to phenomenal awareness. It is also the case that in **isolated** sleep paralysis roughly 64% of episodes occur on awakening rather than at sleep onset, so the phenomenon really is predominantly ascent-side — though in narcolepsy and familial sleep paralysis the balance reverses toward sleep onset, which is itself a finding worth reporting.

The strongest counter-position is well developed and must be stated at full strength: the entire pattern is explicable as REM-wake dissociation from known neurochemistry (GABA/glycine-mediated motoneuron hyperpolarisation persisting past arousal, with orexin and serotonergic involvement), with hallucination content generated by a threat-activated vigilance system and a disturbed body-map at the temporoparietal junction. Nothing in that account requires non-physical consciousness. Further, the *content* of sleep-paralysis hallucinations is heavily culturally shaped — the same neural event is a jinn in Egypt, a witch in Italy, an alien in the US — which argues against reading hallucinatory content as a window onto interface architecture. The defensible dualist claim is about the **order and independence of channel restoration**, not about what the hallucinations depict.

## Key Sources

### Cheyne, Rueffer & Newby-Clark (1999) — the three-factor taxonomy
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1006/ccog.1999.0404 (also https://philpapers.org/rec/CHEHAH)
- **Type**: Peer-reviewed paper, *Consciousness and Cognition* 8(3), 319–337
- **Key points**:
  - Sleep-paralysis hallucinations sort into three factors: **Intruder** (sensed presence, fear, auditory and visual hallucination), **Incubus** (chest pressure, breathing difficulty, pain), and — in this paper's own wording — **Unusual Bodily Experiences** (floating/flying sensations, out-of-body experience, bliss). NB: the familiar *vestibular-motor* label for this third cluster is NOT from this 1999 paper; it is introduced in Cheyne's later work with Girard (Girard & Cheyne 2004, *Laterality* 9(1); Cheyne & Girard 2009, *Cortex* 45(2)). Cite the 1999 paper only for "Unusual Bodily Experiences".
  - Intruder experiences are attributed to a hypervigilant state initiated in the midbrain; Incubus experiences to the effect of motoneuron hyperpolarisation on the perception of respiration; the bodily-experience cluster to vestibular and body-schema systems operating without confirming afferent input.
  - The paper is explicitly a *dual* account: neurological generation plus cultural construction of the "night-mare."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral-to-supportive for Dualism *if* read as channel decomposition; the paper itself is a physicalist account and should not be cited as dualist support.
- **Note**: This is the taxonomy the commissioning review named. It is genuinely canonical and still the organising framework in 2024–2025 reviews.

### Ohayon & Priest et al. (1996) — prevalence asymmetry
- **URL**: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8894197/
- **Type**: Epidemiological survey, *British Journal of Psychiatry* 169(4), 459–467
- **Key points**:
  - Representative UK community sample, n=4,972, ages 15–100, telephone interview, 79.6% response rate.
  - **37% reported hypnagogic hallucinations; 12.5% reported hypnopompic hallucinations.** Both more common in those with insomnia symptoms.
- **Why it matters**: A roughly threefold prevalence gap is the one hard quantitative asymmetry between descent and ascent. It is *not* by itself a structural difference — but note the puzzle it creates: recall should favour hypnopompic experiences (the subject wakes immediately afterwards) yet they are reported far less. Either the ascent generates less hallucinatory content, or it is systematically less well encoded, and those two readings have different implications for the Map's occlusion vocabulary.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral; useful for Occam's Razor Has Limits (the "waking is falling asleep in reverse" simplicity fails a basic frequency test).

### Ghibellini & Meier (2023) — the hypnagogic state update, and the equivalence claim
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13719
- **Type**: Review, *Journal of Sleep Research* 32(1), e13719
- **Key points**: Surveys contemporary findings on the sleep-onset state, terminology, prevalence; evaluates six research directions proposed by Schacter (1976) and adds three (emotional dimensions; why visual and kinaesthetic modalities predominate; the functional purpose of the state).
- **Load-bearing for this article**: The field's working position, traced to Mavromatis (1987), is that **no clear phenomenological or physiological differences exist between hypnagogic and hypnopompic hallucinations, and they are often treated as one phenomenon.** This is the claim any "hypnopompia is structurally different" article must engage rather than ignore.
- **Note**: The equivalence claim was retrieved through secondary summary, not from the paper body. Verify at primary text before quoting or attributing.

### Mainieri, Maranci, Champetier, Leu-Semenescu, Gales, Dodet & Arnulf (2020) — SP as a distinct EEG state
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.9056
- **Type**: Peer-reviewed paper, *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine* 17(4), 719–727
- **Key points**:
  - Spectral EEG comparison of sleep paralysis and false awakenings against REM, lucid REM and wake.
  - Sleep paralysis: reduced relative theta and increased relative alpha versus REM; reported figure of 70.8% of mini-epochs containing theta rhythm during SP, against 89.7% in REM and 21.2% in wake.
  - False awakenings resemble sleep paralysis in profile.
  - Introduces **"lucid paralysis"** for episodes with heightened self-awareness and cognitive clarity.
- **Tenet alignment**: Supports the multi-channel reading — metacognition can be at waking levels while motor and perceptual channels are not.
- **Note**: Percentages came from secondary summary. Re-verify at primary text; the paper is paywalled at jcsm.aasm.org and the certificate on the PDF host failed during this pass.

### Herrero, Corfdir, Vázquez-Chenlo, Capurro & Forcato (2025) — first EEG of sleep OBEs
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-18748-7 (preprint: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.02.10.637510)
- **Type**: Peer-reviewed paper, *Scientific Reports*
- **Key points**:
  - n=7 participants with frequent conscious sleep experiences, overnight PSG, trained eye-movement sequences used to time-stamp awareness during sleep.
  - Sleep paralysis: reduced theta with **increased beta and low-gamma** versus REM and stage 1 — read by the authors as heightened cortical activation consistent with "lucid paralysis."
  - Out-of-body experiences (n=2 episodes): increased delta and theta, reduced alpha, beta and low-gamma relative to REM and wake — read as reduced sensory integration.
  - Reported as the first EEG recordings of out-of-body experiences occurring during sleep.
- **Tenet alignment**: Supportive of channel independence; **but n=7 with 2 OBE episodes is exploratory**, and the article must say so. The paper's own title calls it exploratory.
- **Corpus link**: This is the only known electrophysiology of sleep OBEs and directly upgrades `topics/out-of-body-experiences`, which currently carries the corpus's single passing mention of sleep paralysis.

### Wang et al. (2024) — asynchronous network recovery on awakening
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-51694-4 (open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10794702/)
- **Type**: Peer-reviewed paper, *Scientific Reports* 14, 1537
- **Key points**:
  - n=21 healthy subjects; simultaneous EEG-fMRI during nocturnal sleep plus three consecutive 5-minute resting-state scans post-awakening at 20-minute intervals.
  - Time-lagged cross-correlation between EEG features (alpha-vigilance, spectral slope, theta/beta ratio) and BOLD in thalamus, ACC and sensorimotor cortex.
  - **Thalamus recovers fastest**: peak correlation lag shifts from ~8 s immediately on waking to ~2.5 s by the third scan.
  - **ACC intermediate**; **sensorimotor cortex shows no significant change in coupling timing across sessions.**
- **Why it matters**: This is the best direct evidence that reassembly is channel-staggered on a measurable clock, and it is third-person data — it does not depend on retrospective introspective report, which is the standing methodological weakness of the hypnagogia article's own disengagement sequence.
- **Tenet alignment**: Supportive of the multi-channel interface; **neutral between filter and production readings** — a production model can equally say that different generators restart at different rates. The article must not overclaim here.
- **Companion**: "Reconfigurations in brain networks upon awakening from slow wave sleep" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10270716/) reports the sensorimotor network appearing still disconnected post-awakening while default-mode connectivity has already recovered.

### Kim, Moon, Mashour & Lee (2018) — hysteresis as a network mechanism
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1006424
- **Type**: Peer-reviewed paper, *PLOS Computational Biology* 14(8), e1006424
- **Key points**:
  - Argues hysteresis in consciousness transitions is a universal network property rather than a pharmacological effect: loss and recovery follow distinct pathways.
  - Reported quotes (**re-verify at primary text before use**): "the topographic similarities during emergence are always higher than during the induction path at the same connection strength"; "the critical coupling strength more steeply increases during emergence compared to induction."
  - Mechanism: asymmetry in how many nodes can join a synchronisation cluster along forward versus reverse pathways.
- **Why it matters**: Generalises the anaesthesia article's neural-inertia argument beyond pharmacology, licensing its extension to the sleep-wake transition. This is the cleanest way to connect the two wings without new empirical claims.
- **Tenet alignment**: The Map's anaesthesia article already reads hysteresis as interface-structural resistance; note that Kim et al. give a purely network-dynamical explanation, so the finding does not by itself favour the interface reading.

### Stevner et al. (2019) — whole-brain transition modules
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-08934-3
- **Type**: Peer-reviewed paper, *Nature Communications* 10, 1035
- **Key points**: Hidden Markov Model analysis of simultaneous fMRI-EEG in 57 participants falling asleep; recovers dynamic trajectories between whole-brain network states across the wake–NREM cycle and highlights the heterogeneity of stage N1 and of wakefulness before versus after sleep.
- **Caution**: A secondary summary attributed to this paper the claim that "falling asleep and waking up are two asymmetric processes, leading to two separated transition modules." **That sentence is not in the abstract retrieved here.** It may be in the body, but it is currently **unverified** and must not be quoted or attributed without retrieving the full text.

### Alcaraz-Sánchez (2024) — a multidimensional framework for transitional states
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.33735/phimisci.2024.10233
- **Type**: Peer-reviewed philosophy paper, *Philosophy and the Mind Sciences* 5
- **Key points**:
  - Proposes five phenomenological dimensions for states with disrupted or reduced content: **Richness of Content, Bodily-Awareness, Passage of Time, Attentional Focus, Self-Revelation**.
  - Situates hypnagogia, hypnopompia, sleep paralysis and "clear light" sleep as regions in a multidimensional state space rather than points on a single wakefulness axis.
  - Demonstrates the framework on pure awareness during sleep as documented in Indo-Tibetan Buddhist traditions.
- **Why it matters**: This is the closest thing in current philosophy of mind to the Map's multi-channel interface thesis, arrived at independently and from phenomenology rather than metaphysics. It gives the article a serious, non-dualist interlocutor who already accepts that the relevant properties vary along separable axes — which is the premise the Map needs and the physicalist usually resists.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral-to-supportive. Alcaraz-Sánchez is not a dualist; the convergence is structural, and the article should present it as convergent structure rather than as endorsement.
- **Caution**: The framework's dimensions are proposed as a research heuristic. The retrieved text does **not** contain an explicit claim that the dimensions dissociate independently, so do not cite it for that.

### Jalal & Ramachandran (2014, 2017) — the body-map account of the intruder
- **URLs**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mehy.2014.10.002 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5329044/
- **Type**: Hypothesis paper (*Medical Hypotheses* 83(6), 755–757) and review (*Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 11:92)
- **Key points**: Disturbed multisensory processing of body and self at the temporoparietal junction, with the right superior parietal lobule implicated, produces a hallucinated projection of the subject's own body-map, which is then misattributed to a separate entity. Absent afferent sensory confirmation is the enabling condition.
- **Tenet alignment**: **Conflicts** with any strong reading of the intruder as evidence about interface structure. This is the deflationary account at full strength and it explains the phenomenology well.
- **Site response**: The Map should concede the content account entirely. What the account does not explain away is the *timing structure* — why phenomenal awareness and metacognition return before motor control and body-schema integrity rather than with them.

### Jalal, Romanelli & Hinton (2021) and the cultural-construction literature
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1177/1363461520909609 (*Transcultural Psychiatry*)
- **Key points**: Sleep paralysis in Italy — frequency, hallucinatory content, cultural framing (giant cats, witches), against Egyptian jinn attributions and US alien-abduction framings. Hufford's *The Terror That Comes in the Night* (1982) is the foundational work.
- **Tenet alignment**: **Conflicts** with content-based inference. Cultural variance in content is a strong reason to restrict the Map's claims to structural and temporal features.

### Denis, French & Gregory (2018) — the systematic review
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2017.05.005 (*Sleep Medicine Reviews* 38, 141–157)
- **Key points**: 42 studies on variables associated with sleep paralysis frequency and intensity; causes remain unknown; characteristic features are panic and fear, muscular paralysis, sensed presence, and vivid sensorimotor hallucination including OBE and chest pressure.
- **Companion**: Denis & Poerio (2017), "Terror and bliss? Commonalities and distinctions between sleep paralysis, lucid dreaming, and their associations with waking life experiences," *Journal of Sleep Research* 26(1), https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.12441 — the direct SP/lucid-dreaming comparison, which matters because the Map's lucid-dreaming article treats lucidity as the trainability exhibit.

### Reviews of mechanism (2023–2025)
- **URLs**: https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/12/12/3876 ("Awake or Sleeping? Maybe Both… A Review of Sleep-Related Dissociative States", *J. Clin. Med.* 2023); https://www.imrpress.com/journal/JIN/24/8/10.31083/JIN38979 (*J. Integr. Neurosci.* 2025); https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10847008/ ("REM parasomnias: Straddling the sleep-wake line")
- **Key points**:
  - Sleep paralysis is a **dissociated state**: REM muscle atonia persisting into wakefulness. The reported sequence is alpha EEG intrusion into REM, then an arousal response, then persistence of atonia into the waking state.
  - Neurochemistry: GABA and glycine neurons of the ventral medial medulla directly inhibiting spinal motoneurons; orexin and serotonin systems implicated in the failure to terminate atonia on arousal.
  - Recurrent isolated sleep paralysis: ~7.6% lifetime prevalence of at least one episode in the general population. Predisposing factors include irregular sleep-wake schedules, sleep deprivation, jetlag.
- **Tenet alignment**: **Conflicts** with any claim that the phenomenon is mechanistically mysterious. The mechanism of the *paralysis* is well characterised. The article must not imply otherwise.

### Etymology and lineage: Myers (1897)
- **URL**: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-hyp3.htm ; https://www.etymonline.com/word/hypnopompic
- **Key points**: "Hypnopompic" was coined in 1897 by **Frederic W. H. Myers**, from *hypno-* plus Greek *pompē* ("sending away"), explicitly as the complement to Alfred Maury's earlier "hypnagogique" (*agōgos*, "leading"). Myers introduced it for "similar illusions accompanying the departure of sleep, as when a dream-figure persists for a few moments into waking life."
- **Why it matters for the Map**: Myers is the historical founder of the filter/transmission tradition the Map's `topics/terminal-lucidity-and-filter-transmission-theory` already treats. The very vocabulary of the ascent side was minted by a filter theorist, for the purpose of marking that the departure of sleep is a distinct process from its arrival. That is a genuine historical point, not a rhetorical one — and it is the Map's own lineage.
- **Caution**: Retrieved from etymology references, not from Myers's text. Confirm against *Proceedings of the SPR* or *Human Personality* before quoting Myers directly.

## Major Positions

### REM-Wake Dissociation (mainstream sleep medicine)
- **Proponents**: Cheyne; Denis, French & Gregory; the 2023–2025 review literature
- **Core claim**: Sleep paralysis is the co-occurrence of waking consciousness with REM-generated atonia and REM-generated imagery, produced by desynchronised termination of REM components on arousal.
- **Key arguments**: Known brainstem circuitry for atonia; alpha intrusion followed by arousal followed by persisting atonia; the state's intermediate EEG signature; predisposition by sleep-schedule disruption.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Compatible with all five, and not evidence for any. It is a complete physical account of the *mechanism*. The Map's engagement should be that the account describes *which channels* dissociate without addressing why phenomenal awareness tracks one subset rather than another.

### Threat-Activated Vigilance / Body-Map Projection (content accounts)
- **Proponents**: Cheyne (TAVS and bodily-self neuromatrix); Jalal & Ramachandran (TPJ, right superior parietal, body-image projection)
- **Core claim**: Hallucinatory content is generated by hypervigilant threat detection plus a body-schema left running without afferent confirmation.
- **Key arguments**: The three-factor structure maps onto three distinct physiological substrates; V-M experiences increase most steeply with episode frequency (Cheyne, 2005); phantom-limb analogies for body-map projection.
- **Relation to site tenets**: **Conflicts** with content-based dualist inference. The Map should concede this ground explicitly and stake nothing on what the intruder looks like.

### Cultural Construction
- **Proponents**: Hufford (1982); Adler; Jalal, Romanelli & Hinton (2021)
- **Core claim**: The neurological event is cross-cultural and stable; the interpretation is locally supplied.
- **Relation to site tenets**: **Conflicts** with content inference, converges with the Map's own methodological caution elsewhere about culturally-shaped report.

### Multidimensional Phenomenology (philosophical)
- **Proponents**: Alcaraz-Sánchez (2024); Windt (SEP, *Dreams and Dreaming*) on minimal phenomenal experience and sleep-onset imagery
- **Core claim**: Sleep-wake states are not points on a single arousal axis; they occupy positions in a space of separable phenomenological dimensions.
- **Relation to site tenets**: **Structurally convergent with the Map's multi-channel interface thesis** while remaining metaphysically uncommitted. This is the article's most useful ally and should be presented as convergence, not endorsement.

### Interface Reassembly (the Map's candidate position)
- **Core claim**: The consciousness-brain interface reassembles on waking through the same semi-independent channels it disassembles through at sleep onset, but not in reverse order and not on a single clock — and sleep paralysis is the case where the mis-ordering is severe enough to be noticed from the inside.
- **Key arguments**: Asynchronous network recovery (Wang et al.); hysteresis as a network property (Kim et al.); sleep paralysis's distinct spectral signature (Mainieri et al.; Herrero et al.); lucid paralysis as metacognition returning ahead of motor and perceptual channels; the induction/emergence asymmetry already established in the corpus's anaesthesia article.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Dualism and Bidirectional Interaction (channel independence visible from the reassembly side); Occam's Razor Has Limits (the simple "waking is sleep onset reversed" story is empirically false in both prevalence and network dynamics).
- **What it must not claim**: That the physicalist cannot explain any of this. Every finding above is compatible with a production model in which different generators restart at different rates. The claim available is explanatory-debt asymmetry, matching the standard the wing's other six articles already hold.

## Key Debates

### Are hypnagogic and hypnopompic states phenomenologically distinct?
- **Sides**: The field's default, traced to Mavromatis (1987) and restated in Ghibellini & Meier (2023), is that no clear phenomenological or physiological difference has been established and the two are usually treated as one phenomenon. Against this: Myers coined the terms precisely to separate them; Ohayon & Priest's threefold prevalence gap is a real quantitative asymmetry; the hysteresis and sleep-inertia literatures predict the transitions should differ physiologically.
- **Core disagreement**: Whether the absence of a demonstrated difference reflects genuine equivalence or reflects that **hypnopompia is simply much less studied**. A targeted search for 2025–2026 neural-correlate work on the hypnopompic state specifically returned nothing — the most recent directly relevant material was from 2023.
- **Current state**: Open, and under-researched rather than settled. **This is the honest framing for the article**: the "no difference" position is a default rather than a finding, and the asymmetry literatures give a principled reason to expect it will not survive.

### Is sleep paralysis a distinct state or REM with awareness added?
- **Sides**: Mainieri et al. and Herrero et al. report spectral signatures distinguishing SP from both REM and wake. Against: sample sizes are small (Herrero n=7), and the "intermediate state" reading is consistent with simple mixture.
- **Current state**: Leaning distinct, but on thin data. The Map should say "distinct signature reported in two small studies," not "sleep paralysis is a third state."

### Does hysteresis reveal interface structure or network dynamics?
- **Sides**: The Map's anaesthesia article reads neural inertia as the interface's structural resistance to reconfiguration. Kim et al. (2018) explain the same asymmetry entirely within coupled-oscillator network dynamics, with no reference to consciousness at all.
- **Current state**: Unresolved and probably not resolvable by this evidence. The corpus already handles this correctly for anaesthesia; the sleep article should use the same formula rather than treating the extension as free.

### Is lucidity in sleep paralysis evidence of trainability?
- **Sides**: Meditation-relaxation therapy for sleep paralysis (Jalal et al., *Frontiers in Neurology* 2020;11:922) reports that reappraisal, emotional distancing, inward-focused meditation and muscle relaxation reduce distress; a 2023–2025 RCT tested lucid-dreaming training and imagery-control therapy for REM-related symptoms in narcolepsy type 1 (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT07609537). Against: distress reduction is not channel control, and the neuroplasticity reading covers the data.
- **Relevance**: The commissioning review flagged exactly this over-claim ("you cannot train an epiphenomenon") in `concepts/sleep-and-consciousness`. **A new article must not reintroduce it.**

## Potential Article Angles

1. **"Reassembly Out of Order" as a topics article** (preferred). Lead with the measured finding — channels restore on different clocks (Wang et al.) — then use sleep paralysis as the case where the mis-ordering becomes phenomenally conspicuous, then the hysteresis literature to argue the ascent is not the descent reversed. Tenet section: Dualism and Bidirectional Interaction via channel independence; Occam's Razor Has Limits via the empirical failure of the reversal assumption. Explicitly concede the content accounts (Cheyne's TAVS, Jalal's TPJ body-map, cultural construction) and stake the argument only on order and timing. Pair with the corpus's `direction-of-interface-change` concept, which already names this family of signatures.

2. **A section extending `topics/hypnagogic-phenomenology-and-interface-modulation`** (the review's stated fallback if `topics/` is at cap). 3. **A methodological angle**: hypnopompia as a case where the Map's own epistemics apply to the literature. The hypnagogia article names retrospective-report circularity against itself; the ascent side has the *opposite* problem — reports are made seconds after the event with waking metacognition partly restored, which should make hypnopompic report *better* evidence than hypnagogic report, yet hypnopompia is the far less studied of the two. ## Gaps in Research

- **Cheyne, Newby-Clark & Rueffer (1999), *J Sleep Res* 8(4) — abstract not retrieved (paywall).** This is the one source that directly addresses whether hypnagogic and hypnopompic SP experiences differ, which is the article's pivotal question. Retrieve before drafting; try OpenAlex, EuropePMC, or the ResearchGate record.
- **Ghibellini & Meier (2023) — paywalled (HTTP 402).** The HG/HP equivalence claim attributed to it here came from secondary summary. Verify at primary text before quoting or attributing.
- **Mainieri et al. — paywalled; the JCSM PDF host failed TLS verification.** The theta percentages (70.8% / 89.7% / 21.2%) are from secondary summary and must be re-verified before appearing in an article.
- **Stevner et al. (2019) — the "falling asleep and waking up are two asymmetric processes" sentence is unverified.** It is not in the abstract retrieved. Do not quote or attribute without the full text.
- **Wang et al. (2024) — the quoted sentence "the thalamus and brainstem were the first regions to restore normal CBF upon awakening" may be the authors citing prior work rather than reporting their own result.** Check its context in the open-access PMC version before quoting.
- **Kim et al. (2018) quotes** were obtained via summarisation and need verbatim re-verification at the PLOS page.
- **Myers (1897)** — the coinage and the gloss about a dream-figure persisting into waking life are from etymology references, not from Myers's own text.
- **No 2025–2026 study of hypnopompia specifically was found.** A targeted search returned nothing more recent than 2023 on hypnopompic neural correlates. This absence is itself reportable — but note it is a failed search, not established absence; the search was not exhaustive and did not cover PhilPapers or PubMed directly.
- **No SEP or IEP entry covers hypnopompia or sleep paralysis directly.** The nearest is Windt's SEP *Dreams and Dreaming*, which treats sleep-onset imagery and minimal phenomenal experience but was not read in full in this pass.
- **Unquantified**: whether the 37%/12.5% prevalence gap survives controls for recall opportunity. If it does, it is a substantive asymmetry; if it is a reporting artefact, one of the article's supporting planks goes.
