## Executive Summary

Arthur Holly Compton's amplifier model is a two-layer proposal, and the corpus currently carries only the first layer, through Popper. Layer one (Science, 14 Aug 1931; *The Freedom of Man*, 1935, pp. 37–65) is a consistency claim: a single photon diffracted toward one of two photocells, each driving an amplifier, one wired to a stick of dynamite and the other to a cut-out switch, shows that a macroscopic outcome can be made to hang on one quantum event; since nerve impulses are small-scale electrochemical events, deliberate actions are "events of this kind", so physical law is no longer evidence against freedom. Layer two, which Popper confirms is in the 1935 book, is a selector: Compton "very tentatively accepted the suggestion" that minds act "by influencing or selecting some quantum jumps" (Popper 1972, p. 233, with n. 44 citing *Freedom of Man* pp. 37–65), and a passage carried by the Information Philosopher has consciousness "select the desirable brain current ... without violating or modifying any physical law". Compton never claimed the quantum jump *is* the decision. His own caveats are explicit: in 1930 he did not claim to have solved "the old question of how mind acts on matter"; in 1956, "Whether mind may participate in determining the course of events simply cannot be answered by physical observations"; in 1957, "Science is incapable of telling whether a person's acts are free or not" and freedom "involve[s] the additional determining factor of choice, about which science tells us nothing".

Popper's penny-toss objection is fair to the amplifier *as a model of decision* and Popper says so while conceding most of Compton's ground: "Compton himself designed such a model, though he did not particularly like it" (p. 227); he "used it for one purpose only: to show that human indeterminism (or even 'freedom') does not necessarily contradict quantum physics. I think he was right in all this, including his dislike of master-switch theories" (p. 233); and Popper's own positive requirement, that freedom be "not just chance but, rather, the result of a subtle interplay between something almost random or haphazard, and something like a restrictive or selective control", is introduced as "Compton's postulate of freedom" (pp. 231–232). The Map's Popper page therefore under-describes Compton: it has Compton's model as the thing Popper refuted, where Popper's text has Compton as the source of the standard the refutation applies.

Lineage: the amplifier image in physiology is Ralph Lillie's (Science, 12 Aug 1927), whom Compton cites in 1931 and at *Freedom of Man* p. 50; Compton's contribution is the photocell–dynamite apparatus and the "any deliberate act is such an event" step. Eddington (1928, ch. XIV) had already drawn the Map's Tenet-2 line and then refused the amplifier route: "It is one thing to allow the mind to direct an atom between two courses neither of which would be improbable for an inorganic atom; it is another thing to allow it to direct a crowd of atoms into a configuration which the secondary laws of physics would set aside as 'too improbable'", and the hypothesis "that the mind operates through two or three key-atoms in the brain is too desperate a way of escape for us". Pascual Jordan's Verstärkertheorie (1932, 1938) is a contemporary parallel, not a source.

For the Map, Compton is a precedent for the selection posit and for its interface answer (selection among physically permitted outcomes), and his individuality argument (*Freedom of Man* pp. 63 f.) is an early reply to the luck objection that Popper accepts as avoiding the pure-chance horn. He is not a witness for substance dualism: he argued for the efficacy of consciousness from natural selection and placed the knowledge of freedom in inner awareness, outside physics.

### Compton, "The Uncertainty Principle and Free Will", *Science* 74 (1911): 172, 14 Aug 1931
- **Record**: Crossref-verified (title, volume, issue, page, date). A reply to C. G. Darwin, "The Uncertainty Principle", *Science* 73 (1903): 653–660, 19 June 1931 (Crossref-verified).
- **Text**: reproduced in full by the Information Philosopher (five paragraphs); the *Science* page itself is paywalled and no archive.org copy exists. **Quote status: carrier-only.**
- **Key points** (carrier text): "As a purely physical example, one might pass a ray of light through a pair of slits which will so diffract it that there is an equal chance for a photon to enter either of two photoelectric cells. By means of suitable amplifiers it may be arranged that if the first photon enters cell A, a stick of dynamite will be exploded (or any other large-scale event performed); if the first photon enters cell B a switch will be opened which will prevent the dynamite from being exploded. ... That is, the result is unpredictable from the physical conditions. Professor Ralph Lillie has pointed out that the nervous system of a living organism likewise acts as an amplifier". Its closing caveat: "Of course this does not necessarily mean that the living organism is free to determine its own actions. The uncertainty involved may merely correspond to the organism's lack of skill. Yet it does mean that living organisms are not subject to physical determinism of the kind indicated by Darwin."
- **Note**: the apparatus is described with "suitable amplifiers". The phrase "massive switch amplifier", which secondary sources attach to Compton, appears in Seth Lloyd's "A Turing test for free will" (arXiv:1310.3225) and was not found in any Compton text here; do not attribute it to Compton.

- **Page map, from two independent carriers**: the amplifier discussion is pp. 37–65 (Popper n. 44); the photon-diffraction figure is p. 39 (Davis, fig. 1 caption); the "human action which is definitely subject to Heisenberg uncertainty" passage is pp. 49–50 (Davis n. 63); Lillie is cited p. 50 (Popper n. 13, n. 44); the heat-motion vs Heisenberg comparison is pp. 51 f. (Popper n. 30); emergent evolution pp. 90 ff. (Popper n. 23).
- **Davis-carried passages** (**quote status: secondary, with page numbers, not grep-verified in the book**): quantum uncertainties affecting microscopic events "may result in an equal uncertainty in an event of great magnitude"; the physicist who "decides to go home for lunch when the next photon enters photocell A" gives "a human action which is definitely subject to Heisenberg uncertainty"; citing Lillie, "all deliberate actions of living organisms seem to be events of this kind"; nerve pulses are "presumably electrochemical reactions on a minute scale", so the small number of molecules yields "an appreciable uncertainty" (pp. 49–50). Natural selection has in higher animals "brought consciousness to an ever higher level of development", which should not happen "if consciousness were of no value to the life of the animal" or if "the animal were incapable of affecting its course of action" (pp. 41–4, 55–6). Differences in states of consciousness are "not detectable by any known type of physical test" yet must exist (same pages). With indeterminacy, "man is left by science in control of his own actions within the bounds set by natural law" (pp. 66–7).
- **Information-Philosopher-carried passage** (**quote status: carrier-only, and its dating is unresolved**, see Gaps): the two-photocell set-up with "a stick of dynamite" and a cut-out switch; then "A daemon controlling the shutter might ... close the shutter to all approaching 'bad' photons until a 'good' photon has passed through and saved the day. Has the daemon in this way contravened any physical laws? The point is that the event under consideration is really an individual act, to which, since it can be performed but once, the laws of statistics do not apply. ... Thus deliberate actions likewise are individual events, and are not therefore predictable from laws of probability." Then: "all one's physiological structure, his environment, and previous history determine what Warren Weaver describes as a 'spectrum of action probability,' within which 'spectrum' it is possible for any action to be chosen. ... It would be easy to outline a closely parallel scheme whereby consciousness could select the desirable brain current, even though the undesirable one might be equally probable from statistical considerations ... It is not necessary to elaborate any particular brain mechanism for performing the selection, for the example just given shows that it is possible to select one of a number of physically possible acts without violating or modifying any physical law. In this way the determination of a man's actions by his will is, I believe, shown to be consistent with the principles of physics as they are now understood." The individuality argument matches Popper's description of pp. 63 f.; the Weaver sentence cannot be 1935 unless Weaver used the phrase before his 1948 paper (see Gaps).
- **Tenet alignment**: Layer one aligns with the Minimal Quantum Interaction tenet's premise (a single quantum event can be made decisive); layer two aligns with Bidirectional Interaction as selection within physical law. Nothing on Dualism as an ontology, nothing on Many Worlds.

### Compton, "Science and Man's Freedom", *The Atlantic Monthly* 200 (4), Oct 1957, pp. 71–74
- **Record**: Davis 2009 n. 47 ("Science and Man's Freedom," *Atlantic Monthly* 200, no. 4 (October 1957): 71–4 (reprinted in *Cosmos*, 115–24)); a Zygon article cites the same issue at p. 74. The Atlantic's site is closed to this tool. The Information Philosopher reprints a long extract under the heading "Today's Physics Has Room for Freedom", which is a section heading, not the essay's title.
- **Quote status: carrier-only (Information Philosopher, from the *Cosmos* reprint).**
- **Key points** (carrier text): the amplifier reappears with a Geiger counter and radium in place of the photocells ("Whether in the following second a ray will be counted cannot be foretold"); "A set of known physical conditions is not adequate to specify precisely what a forthcoming event will be. These conditions, insofar as they can be known, define instead a range of possible events from among which some particular event will occur. When one exercises freedom, by his act of choice he is himself adding a factor not supplied by the physical conditions and is thus himself determining what will occur. That he does so is known only to the person himself. From the outside one can see in his act only the working of physical law." Summary: "It is not from scientific observation that we know man is free. Science is incapable of telling whether a person's acts are free or not. ... Freedom does, however, involve the additional determining factor of choice, about which science tells us nothing. For myself, at least, this answer satisfies. Yes, I am free."
- **Restates or softens?** It restates layer one (the amplifier as proof of room) and keeps layer two as "an act of choice ... adding a factor not supplied by the physical conditions", while making the epistemic ceiling explicit: freedom is known by "immediate awareness", invisible "from the outside". Whether the daemon and "select the desirable brain current" sketch recurs is not determinable from the extract. Davis n. 66 pairs the essay with Compton's 1956 American Philosophical Society address, which says the physical laws "are not inconsistent with the effectiveness of purpose in shaping the events in nature" and "Whether mind may participate in determining the course of events simply cannot be answered by physical observations" (*Proc. ### Eddington, *The Nature of the Physical World* (1928), ch. XIV "Causation"
- **URL**: https://archive.org/details/natureofphysical00eddi (djvu text, grep-verified; running heads "294 CAUSATION" and "CAUSATION AND TIME'S ARROW 295" located; the head on the key-atom page is OCR-garbled, the Information Philosopher gives pp. 310–315).
- **Key points** (verbatim): "It is a consequence of the advent of the quantum theory that physics is no longer pledged to a scheme of deterministic law" (p. 294); "science thereby withdraws its moral opposition to free-will" (p. 295); "If the laws of physics are not strictly causal the most that can be said is that the behaviour of the conscious brain is one of the possible behaviours of a mechanical brain. Precisely so; and the decision between the possible behaviours is what we call volition. Perhaps you will say, When the decision of an atom is made between its possible quantum jumps, is that also 'volition'? Scarcely; the analogy is altogether too remote." Then the refusal: "The indeterminacy recognised in modern quantum theory is only a partial step towards freeing our actions from deterministic control. ... But the hypothesis that the mind operates through two or three key-atoms in the brain is too desperate a way of escape for us, and I reject it for the reasons already stated. It is one thing to allow the mind to direct an atom between two courses neither of which would be improbable for an inorganic atom; it is another thing to allow it to direct a crowd of atoms into a configuration which the secondary laws of physics would set aside as 'too improbable'." He adds that an interference wide enough to produce observed effects "involves a genuine physical difference between inorganic and organic (or, at any rate, conscious) matter. I would prefer to avoid this hypothesis".
- **Significance**: Eddington is a critic of the amplifier route, not its originator: he states the magnitude problem (the "crowd of atoms") three years before Compton's reply, and the reply, via Lillie, is that the nervous system already does the amplifying, so no crowd needs steering. Davis's "he, too, stressed the role of consciousness in amplifying uncertainties" is not supported by this chapter. The sibling harvest task on the 1927–1940 wave owns Eddington's later statements (1932 "Mind and Indeterminism"; *New Pathways in Science*, 1935, where, per the Information Philosopher, he puts the smallest unit in which volition originates at "many billions of atoms").
- **Tenet alignment**: the "one thing ... another thing" sentence draws exactly the line the Minimal Quantum Interaction tenet draws (influence within what is already not improbable for the physics); Eddington then declines to walk it.

## Major Positions

### Compton's two-layer indeterminism (1931–1957)
- **Proponent**: A. H. Compton.
- **Core claim**: (1) macroscopic events, including deliberate acts, can hang on single quantum events, so physical law no longer excludes freedom; (2) an agent's choice is "a factor not supplied by the physical conditions" that selects among physically permitted outcomes "without violating or modifying any physical law"; (3) whether such selection occurs is not a physical question ("cannot be answered by physical observations").
- **Key arguments**: the photocell–dynamite apparatus; Lillie's nervous-system-as-amplifier; the individuality of deliberate acts (statistics do not govern a one-off event); natural selection would not have elaborated an inefficacious consciousness.
- **Relation to site tenets**: supports the premise of Minimal Quantum Interaction and the structure of Bidirectional Interaction; silent on Dualism as ontology and on Many Worlds; its epistemic ceiling (freedom known only "from the inside") is a limit the Map should quote rather than argue past.

### Eddington's refusal (1928)
- **Core claim**: indeterminacy removes physics' "moral opposition to free-will" but a mind acting through "two or three key-atoms" is "too desperate", and steering a "crowd of atoms" against statistics would require a physical difference between conscious and inorganic matter.
- **Relation to site tenets**: states the magnitude horn of the luck/interface problem that the Map's Tenet 2 is designed to answer; the Map's answer (amplification by neural dynamics plus selection within what is not improbable) is Compton's and Lillie's reply.

### Popper's intermediate category (1965)
- See [[research/popper-three-worlds-interactionism-and-eccles-trialism-2026-09-17]]. New here: Popper labels the "not just chance" requirement "Compton's postulate of freedom" and says Compton disliked the master-switch model and used it only for the consistency point.

## Key Debates

### Does Popper's penny-toss objection hit Compton's text?
- **Sides**: the Map's Popper page reads Popper as refuting "Compton's model of decision"; Popper's own text distinguishes the model (which Compton "did not particularly like") from Compton's aim (consistency) and standard (the postulate).
- **Core disagreement**: whether Compton claimed the jump is the decision. He did not: the 1931 note ends with "this does not necessarily mean that the living organism is free to determine its own actions", and the 1935 individuality argument plus the selector are his answer to "mere chance".
- **Current state**: the penny-toss is fair to the apparatus as a *model of deciding*; the grain objection (snap-decisions) and Schlick's objection remain live against the selector version, as the Popper page already says. The correction needed is to the attribution, not to Popper's verdict.

### Who originated the amplifier image?
- Lillie 1927 (nervous system as amplifier) → Compton 1931 (apparatus; "any deliberate act is such an event") → Compton 1935 (selector; individuality argument) → Jordan 1932/1938 (independent Verstärkertheorie) → Eddington 1935 (billions of atoms, per carrier) → Popper 1965 (penny-toss; postulate). Kane 1985 (*Free Will and Values*) is reported by the Information Philosopher as proposing an amplifier model "in the ... tradition of Compton"; not verified here.
