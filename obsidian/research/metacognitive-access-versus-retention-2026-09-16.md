## Executive Summary

**Verdict: worth covering, but not in the form the harvesting review framed it.** The review's claim that DID, dissociative amnesia, depersonalisation (DPDR) and anosognosia "all share" one pattern ("retained but inaccessible; retained but disowned") is too coarse. The literature supports a *four-level decomposition* — (1) **retention** (information is stored), (2) **performance-level use** (it shapes recognition, interference, reaction times, choices), (3) **metacognitive access** (the subject can report, believe, or feel that they know it), and (4) **felt ownership** (it presents as *mine*) — and the four conditions break at *different* levels. DID (Huntjens 2006, 2012; Marsh et al. 2021) shows levels 1–2 intact with a failure at level 3: patients use the "amnesic" information on objective tests while sincerely reporting they lack it — Huntjens et al. (2006) explicitly re-describe DID as a "meta-memory" disorder of "incorrect beliefs about their own memory functioning." Anosognosia shows level 2 intact (deficit-related interference) with level 3 absent (Nardone et al. 2007; Fotopoulou et al. 2010). DPDR shows levels 1–3 intact with level 4 attenuated. Dissociative amnesia is the weakest instance: whether level 1 survives is itself contested (Mangiulli et al. 2022), and the best imaging evidence (Kikuchi et al. 2010) points to an active *retrieval* inhibition at level 2, not a metacognitive failure. No existing Map page states this decomposition: `mine-ness` owns level 4, `metacognition` touches levels 2–3 through blindsight and feeling-of-knowing, `implicit-memory` covers anoetic performance, and the clinical data are split across `clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence`, `pharmacological-dissociation-as-evidence` and `erasure-void`. A concepts/ page is justified (concepts at **326/360** measured via `tools.evolution.state.count_section_files`). Its philosophical yield for the Map is modest and must be stated at the right tier: the decomposition is theory-neutral architecture; it does *not* by itself favour an interface over a production reading.

## Key Sources

### Marsh, Dorahy, Butler, Middleton, de Jong, Kemp & Huntjens (2021) — the review's lead, verified
- **URL**: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0245849 (full text via Europe PMC PMC7880432)
- **Type**: Empirical paper
- **Verification**: The review's "Marsh et al. 2021" is correct; Huntjens is **last** author, not first. Title, authors, *PLoS ONE* 16(2): e0245849, published 12 Feb 2021, all confirmed at the publisher. The corpus citation (ref 17 of the clinical-dissociation article) matches.
- **Design**: Recruited 19 DID, 16 simulators, 21 partial-information and 20 full-information comparison participants (analysed DID sample smaller after exclusions — the publisher-page summary gave 12; re-check the Methods table before quoting a number). Study 1: self-referential vignettes; Study 2: autobiographical material.
- **Key points**:
  - DID participants subjectively reported amnesia for events in the other identity in every case.
  - On recall, a *trend* toward inter-identity amnesia (Study 1).
  - "on tests of recognition, DID participants recognized significantly more of the event that occurred in another identity than simulator and partial information comparisons."
  - Authors' conclusion: the DID profile "was not accounted for by true or feigned amnesia, lending support to the idea that reported amnesia may be more of a perceived than actual memory impairment."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. It separates report from retention; it says nothing about what the reporter is.
- **Quote** (verbatim, abstract): "reported amnesia may be more of a perceived than actual memory impairment"

### Huntjens, Peters, Woertman, Bovenschen, Martin & Postma (2006) — the originating "meta-memory" framing
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291706007100 (PubMed 16438741)
- **Type**: Empirical paper, *Psychological Medicine* 36(6): 857–863
- **Key points**: 22 DID patients vs simulators, guessors and normal controls on multiple-choice recognition for material learned in another identity. Patients chose incorrect answers above chance, like simulators — i.e., they used knowledge of the correct answer — but did not show simulators' strategic answer-selection style.
- **Quote** (verbatim, abstract): "DID patients were found not to be characterized by an actual memory retrieval inability, in contrast to their subjective reports. Instead, it is suggested that DID may more accurately be considered a disorder characterized by meta-memory problems, holding incorrect beliefs about their own memory functioning."
- **Why it matters**: This is the cleanest literature statement of the concept the page would name. Any article should credit the framing to Huntjens et al. 2006, with Marsh et al. 2021 as a later extension to self-referential/autobiographical material.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral; a production-friendly redescription (false metabeliefs) is the authors' own.

### Huntjens, Verschuere & McNally (2012)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0040580
- **Type**: Empirical paper, *PLoS ONE* 7(7): e40580
- **Key points**: Concealed-information task on autobiographical details; 11 DID patients, 27 controls, 23 simulators. "Although patients subjectively reported amnesia for the autobiographical details included in the task, the results indicated transfer of information between identities."
- **Quote** (verbatim): "The amnesia criterion should be modified to emphasize its subjective nature."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral.

### Schacter, McAndrews & Moscovitch (1988) — the classic unifying treatment
- **URL**: https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1988-98273-010 ; https://philpapers.org/rec/SCHATC
- **Type**: Chapter in L. Weiskrantz (Ed.), *Thought without Language*, Oxford University Press, pp. 242–278 (page range from search summary; confirm before citing)
- **Key points**: The canonical cross-syndrome survey of *implicit/explicit dissociations* (amnesia, blindsight, prosopagnosia, neglect, aphasia, anosognosia) framed as failures of **access to consciousness** rather than loss of knowledge. Origin of the DICE ("Dissociable Interactions and Conscious Experience") style model in which a conscious-awareness system can be disconnected from intact knowledge modules.
- **Relevance**: Shows the "retained but inaccessible" pattern is a 1980s neuropsychological generalisation, not a new Map observation. The concept page must acknowledge this lineage rather than present the pattern as discovered in the dissociative wing. Abstract not retrieved — do not quote.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral; the disconnection architecture is compatible with both interface and production readings.

### Farah, O'Reilly & Vecera (1993) — the main rival to disconnection accounts
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.100.4.571
- **Type**: Paper, *Psychological Review* 100(4): 571–588
- **Key points**: Covert recognition in prosopagnosia had been read as "an intact visual face recognition system deprived of access to other brain systems necessary for consciousness." The authors propose instead "that the visual face recognition system is damaged but not obliterated" and that degraded networks show residual knowledge on exactly the indirect tasks used to measure covert recognition; a lesioned model reproduces covert recognition on three tasks.
- **Relevance**: The **degraded-representation alternative**: "retained but inaccessible" may be "weakly retained, so detectable only by sensitive tests." This is the most important defeater for the concept page. For DID it is partly answered by the comparison design (recognition *above* partial-information comparisons) but not eliminated — recognition is more sensitive than recall in everyone.
- **Tenet alignment**: Mildly conflicts with interface readings that lean on "intact but disconnected"; neutral on dualism as such.

### Nardone, Ward, Fotopoulou & Turnbull (2007)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1080/13554790701881749
- **Type**: Empirical paper, *Neurocase* 13(5): 438–445
- **Key points**: Attentional-capture paradigm with deficit-related words; non-anosognosic hemiplegics showed facilitation for threatening words, anosognosics showed interference, "a finding which supports the claim of implicit awareness." Small sample; the authors link it to a repression-like pattern.

### Fotopoulou, Pernigo, Maeda, Rudd & Kopelman (2010)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awq233
- **Type**: Empirical paper, *Brain* 133(12): 3564–3577
- **Key points**: 14 right-hemisphere stroke patients; implicit task (verbal inhibition of sentence completion) vs explicit self-relevance ratings of the same deficit-related sentences. Anosognosics were slowed by deficit-related sentences "despite their explicit denial of the self-relevance"; six of seven anosognosic patients showed the dissociation individually. Lesions in anterior insula, basal ganglia, limbic structures distinguished the anosognosic group; the patient *without* implicit awareness had more cortical frontal/parietal lesions.
- **Quote** (verbatim, abstract): "an explicit, affectively personalized sensorimotor awareness requires the re-representation of sensorimotor information in the insular cortex"
- **Relevance**: The title phrase — "unconscious interference without conscious re-representation" — is the anosognosia version of level 2 intact / level 3 absent, and the lesion data give a *physical* account of the missing level. The Map must engage this: it is a production-side mechanism for exactly the access failure.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral-to-conflicting with a strong interface reading.

### Moro, Pernigo, Zapparoli, Cordioli & Aglioti (2011)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bbr.2011.07.010
- **Type**: Empirical paper, *Behavioural Brain Research* 225(1): 259–269
- **Key points**: 12 anosognosic patients; implicit awareness (report/behaviour discrepancy) in five, *emergent* awareness (verbal awareness increased by attempting the action) in three; different lesion loci for lack of implicit vs lack of emergent awareness. Emergent awareness is a within-session *restoration* of level 3 — link to the queued anosognosia-reversal article.

### Kikuchi, Fujii, Abe, Suzuki, Takagi, Mugikura, Takahashi & Mori (2010)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn.2009.21212
- **Type**: Empirical paper (fMRI, n = 2), *Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience* 22(3): 602–613
- **Key points**: For faces/names of acquaintances the patients could not recognise, increased prefrontal and decreased hippocampal activity; the pattern disappeared in the patient whose memories recovered and persisted in the one whose did not.
- **Relevance**: Dissociative amnesia (where genuine) looks like an **active inhibition of retrieval** — a level-2 control failure — rather than intact use with failed metacognition. This is why it should not be lumped with DID. Two patients: suggestive only. Consistent with Anderson-style retrieval suppression already cited in the corpus.

### Mangiulli, Otgaar, Jelicic & Merckelbach (2022)
- Already cited in the corpus (*Clinical Psychological Science* 10(2): 191–211, doi 10.1177/21677026211018194). Contests that dissociative amnesia cases establish "successfully stored but inaccessible" memories at all. Any concept page must carry this as the level-1 doubt.

### Shanks (2017) and Vadillo, Linssen, Orgaz, Parsons & Shanks (2020) — measurement critique
- **URLs**: https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-016-1170-y ; https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000632
- **Type**: Methodological papers, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 24(3): 752–775; *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 149(1): 160–181
- **Key points**: Claims of "performance without awareness" are routinely inflated by unreliable, underpowered awareness measures and by post hoc selection of unaware participants (regression to the mean). Vadillo et al.: "false negatives can be just as concerning in areas of research that depend on finding support for the absence of an effect."
- **Relevance**: The DID and anosognosia evidence pairs a *sensitive* performance measure with a *verbal report* of unawareness — exactly the asymmetry these papers warn about. The concept page should state that "access absent" is an inference from report, and that report is the least sensitive measure in the design.

### Dienes & Perner (1999) — a ready-made hierarchy
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X99002186
- **Type**: Target article, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 22(5): 735–808 (note: **BBS**, not *Psychological Bulletin* — a from-memory attribution caught at Crossref)
- **Key points**: Knowledge as an attitude to a true proposition predicating a property of an entity; implicitness can occur at each level, and the hierarchy is partial ("If a higher aspect is known explicitly then each lower one must also be known explicitly"). "The clearest cases of explicit knowledge of a fact are representations of one's own attitude of knowing that fact."
- **Relevance**: Gives the concept page a respected philosophical scaffold for "level 3" — DID patients represent the content (recognition) without representing *their own attitude of knowing it*. Also predicts that ownership (level 4, a self-involving representation) can fail while content and even attitude survive, matching DPDR.

### Nelson & Narens (1990) — object-level / meta-level framework
- **DOI**: 10.1016/S0079-7421(08)60053-5, pp. 125–173 (Elsevier, ISBN 9780125433266)
- **Verification caveat**: the Crossref record is incomplete (lists only Nelson; container given as "Psychology of Learning and Motivation"). The paper is standardly cited as Nelson, T. O., & Narens, L. (1990), "Metamemory: A theoretical framework and new findings," in *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*. **Confirm volume and series at the publisher before citing.**
- **Relevance**: The monitoring/control distinction between an object level and a meta level. DID ≈ a monitoring failure (meta-level misrepresents the object level); dissociative amnesia (Kikuchi) ≈ a control-flow inhibition; anosognosia ≈ a monitoring failure with affective loading.

### Klein & Nichols (2012)
- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzs080
- **Type**: Paper, *Mind* 121(483): 677–702
- **Key points** (from publisher/PhilArchive summaries; abstract not retrieved verbatim): patient R.B., after a head injury, had accurate memories of scenes from his past that lacked the sense of mineness; the authors propose that the sense of identity has two components, one delivering memory content and one generating mineness.
- **Relevance**: A **non-dissociative, neurological** instance of level 4 failure with levels 1–3 intact — shows "retained but disowned" is not specific to dissociative disorders. Not currently cited in `mine-ness`; a candidate cross-link.

## Major Positions

### Disconnection (access-to-consciousness) account
- **Proponents**: Schacter, McAndrews & Moscovitch (1988); Weiskrantz's "disconnected awareness" work.
- **Core claim**: Intact knowledge systems are cut off from a system required for conscious awareness/report.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Structurally congenial to the Map's interface/channel vocabulary, but the account was built inside cognitive neuropsychology with a *physical* awareness system as the disconnected node. Adopting it gives no dualist advantage by itself.

### Degraded-representation account
- **Proponents**: Farah, O'Reilly & Vecera (1993).
- **Core claim**: Covert-without-overt performance is what weakened representations do; no separate awareness system need be disconnected.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Undercuts "intact but inaccessible" rhetoric. The Map should concede it where comparison groups cannot rule it out.

### Metamemory / false-metabelief account (DID)
- **Proponents**: Huntjens et al. (2006, 2012); Marsh et al. (2021).
- **Core claim**: DID amnesia is largely a disorder of beliefs about one's memory; information transfers across identities.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Fits the decomposition directly. It is sometimes marshalled by sociocognitive critics (Lynn et al. 2022, already in corpus) against the trauma model; the concept page should not take sides on DID aetiology.

### Implicit-awareness / re-representation account (anosognosia)
- **Proponents**: Fotopoulou and colleagues; Moro and colleagues.
- **Core claim**: Deficit information is processed and influences behaviour but is not re-represented (insula-dependent) as affectively personalised explicit awareness.
- **Relation to site tenets**: A concrete neural story for the level-3 failure. A dualist reading must be the claim that the re-representation step is where the interface lies — speculative-integration tier at best.

### Active-inference / precision account
- **Proponents**: Ciaunica, Seth, Limanowski, Hesp & Friston (2022) for DPDR (in corpus).
- **Core claim**: Ownership and felt reality are precision-weighting outputs; DPDR is a precision imbalance.
- **Relation to site tenets**: Explains level 4 on production terms. Already engaged in `clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence` and `mine-ness`.

## Key Debates

### Is "retained" established, or is it "weakly retained"?
- **Sides**: Disconnection theorists vs Farah et al.; for dissociative amnesia, clinical case literature vs Mangiulli et al. 2022.
- **Core disagreement**: Whether above-comparison performance on sensitive tests shows intact storage.
- **Current state**: For DID, comparison-group designs make "genuinely transferred" the better reading. For dissociative amnesia, unresolved and contested at the level of the data.

### Is "inaccessible" established, or is the report measure insensitive?
- **Sides**: Clinical dissociation researchers vs Shanks, Vadillo et al.
- **Core disagreement**: Whether verbal denial is a valid index of absent access.
- **Current state**: Ongoing; the critique is general and has not been applied specifically to DID transfer studies (gap).

### Does the DID pattern reflect genuine dissociation or sociocognitive enactment?
- **Sides**: Trauma model (Dorahy et al. 2014, *Aust. N. Z. J. Psychiatry* 48(5): 402–417, doi 10.1177/0004867414527523) vs sociocognitive model (Lynn et al. 2022).
- **Relevance**: Huntjens 2006 found patients did *not* show simulators' strategic answer style — weighing against straightforward feigning — but a metamemory disorder is compatible with both aetiologies.
