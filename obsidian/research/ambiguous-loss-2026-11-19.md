## Executive Summary

Pauline Boss's *ambiguous loss* is loss with no verified end. In her **Type One** a person is physically absent but psychologically present: the missing, the disappeared, the unrecovered dead. In **Type Two** a person is physically present but psychologically absent: dementia, brain injury, addiction. The term grew from family stress theory, where "boundary ambiguity" is "the family not knowing who is in and who is out of the system" (Boss & Greenberg 1984). Boss's strong claims are that ambiguous loss "freezes the grief process" (her university site) and is "the most stressful loss" (Boss 2004, p. 553, as quoted by Lenferink et al. 2019). The comparative record does not bear this out as stated. A systematic review found that "psychopathology levels did not differ between relatives of missing and deceased persons" (Lenferink et al. 2019). Outside armed conflict, homicide-bereaved relatives reported more prolonged grief than relatives of long-term missing persons (d = 0.86; Lenferink et al. 2017). In Colombia, 23% of relatives of the disappeared met prolonged-grief criteria against 31.5% of the bereaved, yet among the relatives of the disappeared hope that the person was alive predicted prolonged grief (Heeke et al. 2015). In the Norwegian tsunami-bereaved, a longer time to confirmation of the death was associated with complicated grief (Kristensen et al. 2010).

For the Map the useful result concerns self-knowledge. The bereavement void's lag needs a fact, the death, for expectation to catch up with. In Type One there is none, so the residual expectation cannot be called a lag, and may be warranted hope. The state also resists its own name: designers of an inventory for relatives of the missing dropped the word "grief" on experts' advice (Comtesse et al. 2023), and in one small qualitative study "the discovery of a corpse... allows mourners to fully recognise their grief" (Testoni et al. 2020). In Type Two, carers grieve before the death, rarely recognise the symptoms as grief (Blandin & Pepin 2017, summarising earlier studies), and in one cohort their grief scores were about the same before the death as seven months after it (Givens et al. 2011). Type Two bears on the Map's personal-identity commitments only in that the Map gives a determinate answer, that the subject persists, to a question families experience as open; neither the families' experience nor the clinical literature supports or undermines that answer. **Verdict: fold.** Topics and concepts are at cap (370/370) and voids is at 124/125. - **Tenet alignment**: neutral.

### Tests of the frozen-grief and most-stressful claims

**Lenferink, L. I. M., de Keijser, J., Wessel, I., de Vries, D., & Boelen, P. A. (2019)**, "Toward a better understanding of psychological symptoms in people confronted with the disappearance of a loved one: A systematic review", *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse* 20(3): 287–302, doi:10.1177/1524838017699602 (PMC6591515, CC BY-NC) [read, full text]
- Fifteen studies. "All reviewed studies were focused on disappearances due to war or state terrorism." "Comparative studies showed that psychopathology levels did not differ between relatives of missing and deceased persons." "Only 4 of the 24 comparisons across six studies yielded significantly higher levels of severity or prevalence of psychological symptoms in the disappeared group compared to the homicide group, and these differences had a small to medium effect size." On the earlier literature: "Boss's (1976, 2004) claim that the disappearance of a loved one is the most stressful type of loss does not seem to rest on a solid empirical basis."
- The review excluded Type Two ("physically present but psychologically absent (e.g., dementia patients...)").
- **Tenet alignment**: neutral.

**Lenferink, L. I. M., van Denderen, M. Y., de Keijser, J., Wessel, I., & Boelen, P. A. (2017)**, "Prolonged grief and post-traumatic stress among relatives of missing persons and homicidally bereaved individuals: A comparative study", *Journal of Affective Disorders* 209: 1–2, doi:10.1016/j.jad.2016.11.012 [abstract]
- Dutch sample, outside armed conflict: 134 relatives of long-term missing persons and 331 homicide-bereaved. "Homicidally bereaved individuals reported significantly higher levels of PGD (d=0.86) and PTSD (d=0.28) than relatives of missing persons." Self-report measures.

**Heeke, C., Stammel, N., & Knaevelsrud, C. (2015)**, "When hope and grief intersect: Rates and risks of prolonged grief disorder among bereaved individuals and relatives of disappeared persons in Colombia", *Journal of Affective Disorders* 173: 59–64, doi:10.1016/j.jad.2014.10.038 [abstract]
- 73 relatives and friends of the disappeared and 222 bereaved, all displaced by the armed conflict. "23% of participants who lost a significant other to disappearance met criteria for PGD as compared to 31.5% in bereaved participants. No differences were found between the two groups in terms of symptom severity." "Among relatives and friends of disappeared persons, the extent of hope predicted PGD above and beyond depression severity." The instruments "were not validated for use in Colombia".
- **The Map's caution (not the authors')**: the PG-13 scores yearning for the person, and hoping the person is alive may overlap with yearning, so the association may partly reflect overlap between measures. Not tested here. Lenferink et al. (2019) call Heeke et al. "the single empirical study" on hope and note that it runs against the expectation that hope eases distress.

**Kristensen, P., Weisæth, L., & Heir, T. (2010)**, "Predictors of complicated grief after a natural disaster: A population study two years after the 2004 South-East Asian tsunami", *Death Studies* 34(2): 137–150, doi:10.1080/07481180903492455 [abstract]
- 130 Norwegian respondents, 47.7% screening positive for complicated grief. Positive associations with "being female, losing a child or spouse, and time to death confirmation". This is a temporary Type One case: the deaths were eventually confirmed, and the longer the wait, the worse the grief. It is the clearest support found for Boss's view, and a correlate, not a causal result.

**Lenferink, L. I. M., de Keijser, J., Piersma, E., & Boelen, P. A. (2018)**, "I've changed, but I'm not less happy: Interview study among nonclinical relatives of long-term missing persons", *Death Studies* 42(6): 346–355, doi:10.1080/07481187.2017.1347213 [abstract]
- 23 nonclinical relatives drew their own functioning over time. "Patterns most frequently drawn were a recovery and resilient/stable pattern." The study is retrospective and self-selected.

**Comtesse, H., Killikelly, C., Hengst, S. M. C., Lenferink, L. I. M., de la Rie, S. M., Boelen, P. A., & Smid, G. E. (2023)**, "The Ambiguous Loss Inventory Plus (ALI+): Introduction of a Measure of Psychological Reactions to the Disappearance of a Loved One", *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* 20(6): 5117, doi:10.3390/ijerph20065117 (PMC10049427) [read, full text]
- Earlier studies "used validated grief measures referenced for the study purpose of a disappeared instead of a deceased person", and "established PGD measures may not capture precisely the distress after a disappearance". The experts "remarked that the word 'grief' included in these items was not suitable for the separation distress experience of relatives of disappeared persons", so "we were careful to refer to 'separation distress' rather than 'grief' throughout the ALI+." Items were reworded, e.g. "Longing or yearning for the person who disappeared".

**Testoni, I., Franco, C., Palazzo, L., Iacona, E., Zamperini, A., & Wieser, M. A. - **Eight participants** (one person found alive, three found dead, four still missing; disappearances lasting from 10 days to 26 years). A retired man whose daughter was never found says she "is always there even if she is in the dark and I don’t see her".

**Isuru, A., Bandumithra, P., & Williams, S. S. (2021)**, "Locked in grief: a qualitative study of grief among family members of missing persons in southern Sri Lanka", *BMC Psychology* 9: 167, doi:10.1186/s40359-021-00675-7 (PMC8555241) [read, full text]

### Type Two: dementia and pre-death grief

**Chan, D., Livingston, G., Jones, L., & Sampson, E. L. (2013)**, "Grief reactions in dementia carers: A systematic review", *International Journal of Geriatric Psychiatry* 28(1): 1–17, doi:10.1002/gps.3795 [abstract]
- 31 publications. "Carers experience anticipatory grief as multiple losses for themselves (companionship, personal freedom and control) and the person with dementia. Anticipation and ambiguity about the future, anger, frustration and guilt are core features." The evidence on prevalence was poor in quality: anticipatory grief was reported at "between 47% and 71%", and complicated grief after death at "around 20%".

**Crawley, S., Sampson, E. L., Moore, K. J., Kupeli, N., & West, E. (2023)**, "Grief in family carers of people living with dementia: A systematic review", *International Psychogeriatrics* 35(9): 477–508, doi:10.1017/S1041610221002787 [abstract]
- 55 quantitative studies. "17% met the Prolonged Grief Disorder criteria pre-death (n = 1) and 6-26% (n = 4) of participants met the Complicated Grief criteria post-death." "Pre-death factors found to influence post-death grief were grief and depression."
- **Note**: applying prolonged-grief criteria before the death extends a death-defined diagnosis to a living patient's family, and the 17% figure rests on one study. Do not repeat it without that qualifier.

**Lindauer, A., & Harvath, T. A. (2014)**, "Pre-death grief in the context of dementia caregiving: a concept analysis", *Journal of Advanced Nursing* 70(10): 2196–2207, doi:10.1111/jan.12411 [abstract; the definition via Blandin & Pepin 2017]
- Abstract: the concept "lacks a refined definition and blurs with similar constructs". The definition, as quoted by Blandin & Pepin: "Pre-death grief in the context of dementia family caregiving is the caregiver's emotional and physical response to the perceived losses in a valued care recipient." **Secondary quotation.** "Perceived" is in the definition.

**Blandin, K., & Pepin, R. (2017)**, "Dementia grief: A theoretical model of a unique grief experience", *Dementia* 16(1): 67–78, doi:10.1177/1471301215581081 (PMC4853283) [read, full text]
- Caregivers "rarely recognize symptoms of pre-death grief (Marwit & Meuser, 2001; Sanders & Corley, 2003; Silverberg, 2007)". "Ambiguous loss is rarely recognized by family members themselves or others." The "receding of the known self" is "the changes in the self, including identifying personality characteristics, memories, and abilities, observed in people with dementia". "Yet their loved one is still alive, and though they may not act like the same person caregivers once knew, they are the same person and family members often strive to remain connected to them." The model's three states are "separation, liminality, and re-emergence".
- **Guard**: the self-recognition claims are the authors' summary of earlier studies, not their own data; keep "by one model's summary". "They are the same person" is the authors' ordinary-language view and not a metaphysical argument. Do not enlist it for the Map's identity verdict.

**Givens, J. L., Prigerson, H. G., Kiely, D. K., Shaffer, M. L., & Mitchell, S. L. (2011)**, "Grief among family members of nursing home residents with advanced dementia", *American Journal of Geriatric Psychiatry* 19(6): 543–550, doi:10.1097/JGP.0b013e31820dcbe0 (PMC3101368) [read, full text]
- 123 family members of residents in 22 Boston-area nursing homes, all with very advanced dementia (Global Deterioration Scale 7). "Levels of preloss and postloss grief were relatively stable from baseline to 7 months postloss." In the discussion, this contrasts with "a community-based study ... which noted a sharp decline in grief symptoms in the months following patient death". "Overall, the frequency with which family members experienced grief symptoms was relatively low both before and after the resident's death"; "only one HCP in the cohort met the criteria for prolonged grief disorder". The authors' explanation: "the protracted course of illness and the loss of personhood that characterizes dementia".
- **Guards**: the pre-loss and post-loss scales differ in three items, the sample was "predominantly white, well-educated", and grief levels were low overall. "Loss of personhood" is the authors' phrase. The Map must not adopt it.
- **Why it matters for the Map**: for these families the death was not the turning point grief registered. Type Two splits the fact.

## Major Positions

### Ambiguous loss as a distinct, especially stressful stressor (Boss)
- **Proponents**: Boss; the family-stress and family-therapy literature that builds on her (Carroll, Olson & Buckmiller's 2007 30-year review of boundary ambiguity, abstract read: 37 studies in 11 domains).
- **Core claim**: the absence of verification, not the absence of the person, blocks the ordinary grief process. The problem lies in the situation, not the mourner. The therapeutic aim is tolerance of ambiguity and the six guidelines, which Paun et al. (2022) summarise as "a dialectical approach", not closure.
- **Relation to site tenets**: framework-independent. Boss's account is relational and situational and makes no claim about minds or their metaphysics.

### Ambiguous loss as one risk factor among others (the clinical-comparative literature)
- **Proponents**: Lenferink, Boelen and colleagues; Heeke and Knaevelsrud.
- **Core claim**: relatives of the missing show elevated distress, but not reliably more than comparable bereaved groups. Hope, kinship and trauma exposure are the correlates, and the instruments need adapting (ALI+).
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral.

### Pre-death grief as a distinct dementia-carer construct
- **Proponents**: Lindauer & Harvath; Blandin & Pepin; Chan, Sampson and colleagues.
- **Core claim**: carers grieve serial, perceived losses in a living person. Pre-death grief predicts post-death grief (Givens; Crawley; Chaudhry et al. 2025, *JAMDA*, title only).
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral. Its talk of the "receding of the known self" and "loss of personhood" is descriptive vocabulary, not a theory of identity.

## Key Debates

### Does ambiguity freeze grief?
- **Sides**: Boss (yes, as the defining effect) against Lenferink et al. (comparisons do not show more psychopathology) and Lenferink et al. 2018 (resilient and recovery patterns most often drawn). Kristensen et al. (time to confirmation predicts complicated grief) and Heeke et al. (hope predicts PGD) give partial support to Boss.
- **Core disagreement**: partly empirical and partly measurement. If death-normed instruments miss the distress (Comtesse et al.), the null comparisons are not decisive.
- **Current state**: open. Every comparative study is of war, state terror or homicide comparisons; non-conflict disappearances are under-studied.

### Is "grief" the right word?
- **Sides**: Boss writes of "unresolved grief" and "frozen grief"; the ALI+ team chose "separation distress"; Isuru et al. write "vacillating grief"; Testoni et al. see grief fully recognised only on the discovery of a body.
- **Map relevance**: high. See the next section.

## The Map-Specific Question {#map-question}

Everything in this section is the Map's analysis, built on the sources above. No source states it in these terms.

### 1. The lag needs a fact; Type One has none

The bereavement void separates three things that "She knows he is dead" runs together: assent to the fact, the expectations that should follow from it, and a valuation of what was lost. Its first face, the lag, is defined by a standard: a fact the expectation has not yet caught up with. Its evidence that belief is "largely intact" (the Yale acceptance item) presupposes that there is a death to accept.

When someone disappears, there is no fact. Three things follow.

- **The residual expectation is not a lag.** For the relative of someone who has disappeared, setting a place, keeping the phone line, or expecting a knock may be proportioned to an open question: warranted hope, not alief against belief. The bereavement void's tool, that sincere belief is in place while expectation trails behind, has nothing to grip. The mourner's own state supplies no way to tell which part of her expectation is residue and which is credence. Neither does anyone else, until the fact arrives.
- **The state resists its own name.** In Isuru et al., 21 of 24 relatives were "not sure" whether the person was alive, in some cases more than two decades after the disappearance. An instrument designed for them dropped "grief" on experts' advice (Comtesse et al.). In Testoni et al.'s eight cases, the discovery of a body "allows mourners to fully recognise their grief". On the Map's reading the mourner may be unable to say *which* emotion she is in, grief or waiting, because the classification depends on a fact outside her. That is a different limit from the bereavement void's. There the survivor knows she grieves and cannot yet absorb what the death means. Here she may not be able to settle that she grieves. Boss's "Normalizing Ambivalence" treats the mixed state as normal. The Map can agree: the ambivalence registers, accurately, a world that has not settled.
- **The lapse face inverts.** Marušić's puzzle (via [[bereavement-void]]) assumes that grief's reason stays fixed while grief fades. When someone disappears, the reason may *grow*: as years pass, death becomes more probable while hope persists. A fading of distress here could track a changing credence, and a deepening of grief could be an update rather than a failure to recover. No source read discusses this. It is the Map's observation and a candidate for any later article.

The empirical record cautions against overstating the first point's consequences. Relatives of the missing do not reliably show more disorder than the bereaved (Lenferink et al. 2017, 2019; Heeke et al. 2015), and many describe recovery (Lenferink et al. 2018). That an expectation cannot be classified does not mean the mourner suffers more. Heeke et al.'s finding that hope predicted PGD fits the Map's reading that the expectation is live and not residual. It is also open to the measurement-overlap caution above.

### 2. Type Two: the fact splits

In dementia the death, when it comes, is not the event grief has been waiting for. Givens et al. found grief stable from before the death to seven months after it, and Blandin & Pepin's "receding of the known self" locates the losses years before. The fact the bereavement void needs exists, but it arrives after much of what is mourned. Self-knowledge is limited in a new direction: carers "rarely recognize symptoms of pre-death grief" (Blandin & Pepin, summarising earlier studies). The category "grief" is socially tied to death, a tie Doka's disenfranchised grief names and Kauffman's "self-disenfranchisement" (LEAD) may extend inward. A carer can be grieving without counting it as grief. That is the reverse of the bereavement void's survivor, who has the belief and lacks the absorption. The carer has the feeling and lacks the label.

### 3. Does Type Two bear on the Map's personal-identity commitments?

Only in a limited way, and the limit should be stated.

- **What Boss claims.** Her types are framed as the *family's perception*: "The family may perceive ... a physically present member as psychologically absent" (Boss & Greenberg 1984). Psychological absence in her sense is absence from the family system, not a verdict that the person no longer exists. Her site's "emotionally or cognitively gone" is her idiom for that perception.
- **What the Map holds.** [[consciousness-and-neurodegenerative-disease]] holds that the patient is "the *same* conscious subject with drastically fewer cognitive tools", grounded in the determinate-subject posit ([[tenets/background-commitments]], Posit One: diachronic subject persistence). The Map therefore gives a determinate answer to a question the family experiences as open.
- **How the two fit.** What carers say they grieve is losses of companionship, roles, relationship and the person's recognisable qualities (Chan et al.; Nathanson & Rogers; Blandin & Pepin). Their judgments of identity change track moral change, not memory (Strohminger & Nichols). On the Map's reading these are losses of *qualities and reciprocity*. They are real and fitting objects of grief, and the Map's verdict leaves them standing. This is the inverse of the beloved void's duplicate case. A duplicate has every quality and is the wrong individual; the patient, on the Map's view, is the right individual and has lost qualities. Love, as the beloved void finds, tracks qualities and familiarity. The Map's identity verdict tracks the subject. Grief in Type Two is lived in the gap between them.
- **What does not follow.** The families' experience does not support the Map's verdict. A psychological-continuity theorist reads the same experience as partial loss of the person, and the second-person evidence families rely on (responsiveness, recognition, coupling) is, by the Map's own account in [[the-second-person-evidential-channel]], "no window into another's interior". Equally, a family's sense that the person is absent is no evidence against the Map's verdict. The case puts the posit to a practical test of meaning and does not test its truth. Blandin & Pepin's "they are the same person" and Givens et al.'s "loss of personhood" are ordinary-language glosses on opposite sides. Neither is an argument, and neither should be cited for the Map.
- **The Map must not say, in its own voice, that a person with dementia "is gone", "is no longer there" or has lost "personhood".** It may report that families perceive psychological absence and that Boss names this ambiguous loss.

### Relation to the tenets

- **Dualism**: not engaged. Boss's theory is family-systemic, the comparative studies are clinical, and the dementia literature is caregiving research. None bears on whether consciousness is physical. Do not enlist any of it.
- **No Many Worlds** (via [[haecceity]]): thematic contact only, through the inverse-duplicate point above, which illustrates the indexical commitment without supporting it.
- **Occam's Razor Has Limits**: illustrated only. "She is grieving" covers assent to a fact, expectation, valuation and the classification of one's own state, which come apart when the fact is missing. A physicalist psychology draws the same distinctions.
- **Bidirectional Interaction, Minimal Quantum Interaction**: not engaged.
