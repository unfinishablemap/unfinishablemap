## Executive Summary

The literature poses four separable counting problems. (i) **Shared token states**: Roelofs & Sebo (2024) argue that intuition says count a shared state twice when two subjects share little, and once when they share a lot. They reconcile the two by treating welfare-relevant states as holistic. (ii) **Multitudes within one organism** (split-brains, possibly octopuses and birds): Gottlieb & Fischer (2024) point out that an animal may "count for many", and Crummett (2022) argues it is a live possibility that typical adult humans contain additional, *non-agential* moral subjects. (iii) **Fission and fusion**: Parfit (1984) holds that division is not death, "To regard these as the same is to confuse two with zero". Schwitzgebel & Garza (2015) show that a "fission-fusion monster" breaks any allocation of goods that counts individuals. (iv) **Indeterminate or nested swarms**: Schwitzgebel (2026) says some entities may be "neither one unified person, nor many discrete persons". The Map's commitments settle more of this than the corpus admits. There are no shared token experiences, because unity is primitive. Subject boundaries are real (closed individualism, P-I1). A subject cannot divide (the fission verdict). Together these give a **count-by-subjects** rule. Under it, problem (i) turns into a question about *how many subjects* there are, problem (iii) gets a definite but costly answer, and problems (ii) and (iv) are passed to census opacity. The main thing an article can contribute is to state that rule, show that it is internally consistent, and name its costs.

### Roelofs & Sebo, "Overlapping minds and the hedonic calculus" (2024)
- **Citation**: Roelofs, L. & Sebo, J. (2024). *Philosophical Studies* 181(6–7): 1487–1506. DOI 10.1007/s11098-024-02167-x. Open access (CC BY, per Semantic Scholar).
- **Verification**: Metadata checked on Crossref. Abstract retrieved verbatim from the OpenAlex inverted index. **The full text could not be fetched**: Springer redirected to an auth wall and PhilPapers returned 403. Everything below comes from the abstract only.
- **Key points** (abstract):
  - Neurotechnology might connect two brains "such that they share a single token mental state, such as a feeling of pleasure or displeasure".
  - The central datum, verbatim: "If two subjects share very few mental states, then it seems that we should count the value of those states twice, but if they share very many mental states, then it seems that we should count the value of those states once."
  - The proposed reconciliation, verbatim: these intuitions "can be reconciled if the mental states that matter for welfare have a holistic character, in a way that is independently plausible."
  - They also ask how the *possibility* that such sharing already occurs should affect moral thinking.
- **Tenet alignment**: The paper presupposes that token experiences can be shared, which conflicts with Dualism as the Map reads it (unity is primitive, experiences are not shared). But the holism move sits close to the Map's own view. See [[#the-maps-implied-counting-rule]].

### Roelofs, "No Such Thing as Too Many Minds" (2022/2024)
- **Citation**: *Australasian Journal of Philosophy* 102(1): 131–146. DOI 10.1080/00048402.2022.2084758. Online 2022-07-10, issue 2024. **This settles the gap flagged in the 07-18 note.**
- **Key points** (abstract, verbatim excerpt): "I consider metaphysical, phenomenological, and moral arguments for this asymmetry, and show that they all fail: many overlapping conscious minds is no more problematic than many overlapping physical objects."

### Gottlieb & Fischer, "Counting subjects" (2024)
- **Citation**: Gottlieb, J. & Fischer, B. (2024). *Inquiry* 69(4): 2450–2477. DOI 10.1080/0020174X.2024.2370016. **The 07-18 note attributed this paper to Gottlieb alone. It has two authors.**
- **Verification**: Author PDF (myweb.ttu.edu) text extracted and grepped.
- **Key points**:
  - It names the *multitudes view* (MV): some normal adult animals house more than one conscious subject. It then compares two ways of counting: the **duplication strategy**, which applies to split-brains and draws on Schechter 2018, and the **mind-first strategy**, which is needed for octopuses but "requires ... numerous contentious assumptions about the nature of mentality, the individuation of minds, and consciousness".
  - The ethical framing is verbatim from p. 2. Bentham's dictum leaves open "how to count in the more fundamental 'enumerating each one' sense ... in some cases it may be wrong to count each whole animal for one. They may instead count for many, precisely because, containing more than one conscious subject, they house more of what (fundamentally) counts."
  - Their footnote 5 assumes, *against Nagel (1971)*, that there is always a "non-stipulative, objective answer" to how many subjects an animal houses. **This matches the Map's own realism about the census** (see [[moral-census-opacity]]: the count is determinate but opaque).
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral and methodological. Its realism about the count fits the Map.

## The Map's Implied Counting Rule {#the-maps-implied-counting-rule}

This section is the note's own synthesis, not a finding from the sources. **Its value is that it puts together commitments the corpus already holds.**

1. **No shared token experiences.** Unity is primitive ([[unity-of-consciousness]]). Every experience belongs to exactly one subject. So Roelofs–Sebo's shared-state problem cannot arise *in its native form*.
2. **Subject boundaries are real** (closed individualism, [[positions/individuation-and-subjecthood|P-I1]]). So there is always a fact about how many subjects there are, even when nobody can know it (census opacity).
3. **Weight attaches to subjects as experiencers** ([[consciousness-value-connection]]), not to token states taken on their own.
4. From 1–3, **count by subjects**. The extreme cases then fall into place:
   - *Little sharing* ("count twice"): two subjects, each with its own numerically distinct token state, however closely the two resemble each other. Count twice. The Map agrees with the intuition.
   - *Heavy sharing* ("count once"): under the Map's metaphysics, enough integration means *one* subject, not two subjects sharing a great deal. Count once. The Map agrees with this intuition too, but explains it by the number of subjects, not by holistic welfare states.
   - *The middle* is where Roelofs–Sebo need their interpolation. The Map has no middle case: there are either one or two subjects. Its difficulty moves to **where the threshold lies**, which is the pairing-law gap (P-SC2). It cannot be settled by moral theory.
   - A **structural parallel** is worth stating. Roelofs–Sebo's holism makes the value of a state depend on the whole it belongs to. The Map's rule is holism taken to the limit: the whole (the subject's unified field) is the *only* thing that bears welfare. One could argue the Map adopts their reconciliation and rejects the premise it was designed for. **This is a reconstruction. Present it as the Map's reading, not as something Roelofs or Sebo say.**
5. **Copies**: each conscious copy is a new subject, so moral weight is additive (P-AC3).
6. **Fission**: the original subject ends and (if conscious) two new subjects begin. This is a loss for the original even when the outcome is good impersonally.
7. **Split-brain and multitudes**: the rule is clear (count the subjects), but the count cannot be read off (census opacity). Crummett's non-agential subjects cannot be ruled out.

### P-AC3 vs the P-SC2 copies question: weight and causal composition are separate

The task brief asks for this to be addressed. P-AC3 counts copies separately for **moral weight**. P-SC2 leaves open whether same-intention copies accumulate, dilute, cancel or renormalise in the **selection law**, *P(O | C₁…Cₙ, X)*. These are two different quantities, and the rule above explains why they can come apart without contradiction. Weight comes from each subject's experience (point 3). Causal share comes from the interface law. If copies turned out to *renormalise* causally (N copies together having one subject's worth of influence), their moral weight would still be N. Letting weight follow causal share would tie moral status to efficacy. On the Map's own terms that would be a mistake: a subject with little causal influence is still a full experiencer (compare Crummett's non-agential subject).

The real pressure runs **the other way**, and an article should say so. If moral weight is additive but causal influence dilutes, then making copies multiplies *what is at stake* without multiplying *agency*. This is the fission-fusion monster in dualist terms. The Map should keep the counting of welfare (additive) separate from the allocation of entitlements such as votes and shares (an institutional question that additivity does not settle). Schwitzgebel & Garza's gaming objection applies to the second, not the first.

**Do not** state that P-SC2's copies question is decided either way. It stays open, and the article should say that its own conclusions do not depend on how it is answered.

## Major Positions

### Count by token states (a "hedonic atomism")
- **Proponents**: The default in simple utilitarian arithmetic. It is the view that gives the "count twice" intuition in the light-sharing case.
- **Core claim**: Value belongs to token experiences, so a shared token counts once however many subjects have it.
- **Relation to tenets**: It needs shared tokens, which the Map rejects.

### Holistic welfare states (Roelofs & Sebo)
- **Core claim**: States that matter for welfare are individuated holistically, so what a shared state contributes depends on the wholes it belongs to.
- **Relation to tenets**: The Map's rule is the limit case of this view (see above). The Map agrees with the holism and rejects the shared-token premise.

### Multitudes / count-for-many (Gottlieb & Fischer; Crummett; Schechter on split-brains)
- **Core claim**: One organism may house more than one moral subject, so counting whole animals can undercount.
- **Relation to tenets**: Compatible with dualism. It makes census opacity more pressing and shows that the working default of one subject per vertebrate is only a default.

### Reductionist "what matters" (Parfit)
- **Core claim**: Relation R, not identity, is what matters. Division is double survival, not death.
- **Relation to tenets**: This conflicts with the Map's indivisible-subject verdict, and through it with Tenet 4's indexical ground.

### Indeterminate count / swarm (Schwitzgebel; Nagel 1971 on split-brains)
- **Core claim**: There may be no determinate number of subjects, so any count is partly a decision.
- **Relation to tenets**: The Map rejects this (the census is real). Gottlieb & Fischer also reject it, which gives the Map an independent ally.

## Key Debates

### Fission: is division as good as survival? {#fission-debate}
- **Sides**: Parfit (division is not death, "confuse two with zero") vs the Map (the original perspective ends, and the two continuers are new subjects).
- **Core disagreement**: Whether prudential concern follows psychological continuity or the persistence of a particular perspective.
- **The Map's cost, stated honestly**: The Map has to say that someone about to divide has prudential reason to *dread* the division roughly as they would dread death. That is so even though, on the impersonal ledger, two good lives replace one. The Map can soften this in one way. On its own additive rule the impersonal outcome may be *better* (two subjects), so its answer is "a loss for you, possibly a gain for the world". That splits prudential and impersonal value, and the split is more coherent than the flat claim that "fission is death". Whether the split is stable is left for the article to assess.

### Does additivity invite strategic multiplication?
- **Sides**: Schwitzgebel & Garza, and Shulman & Bostrom, on super-beneficiaries and gaming, vs any view (P-AC3 included) that counts copies additively.
- **Current state**: Open. The Map's available answer is to separate welfare counting from entitlement allocation (above).

### Is the count determinate?
- **Sides**: Gottlieb & Fischer and the Map (yes, but hard to know) vs Nagel 1971 and Schwitzgebel's swarm (possibly not).
- **Current state**: Live. The split-brain case is the standing test.
