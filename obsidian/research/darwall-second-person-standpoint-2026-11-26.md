## Executive Summary

Stephen Darwall's *The Second-Person Standpoint: Morality, Respect, and Accountability* (Harvard UP 2006) is a work of metaethics. Its "second person" is the standpoint of making and acknowledging claims on one another. Its central concepts are valid demand, practical authority, second-personal reason and accountability. Darwall holds that these form an interdefinable and irreducible circle, and that addressing a demand presupposes "normative felicity conditions": the addresser's authority, the addressee's second-personal competence, and the accountability that results. Darwall's questions are about validity and obligation. They are not about evidence or knowledge of other minds.

The Map's host page, `topics/the-second-person-evidential-channel`, asks an evidential question: does live reciprocal engagement give a distinct channel of evidence about another mind? The two literatures share a vocabulary ("address", "you", "second person", "irreducible") but not a question. A citation check confirms they barely touch. Neither "Darwall" nor "Gomila" nor "Eilan" appears among the 294 Crossref-deposited references of Schilbach et al. 2013 (*BBS*). Eilan (2014) describes the "thinking" strand and the "normative" strand of second-person philosophy as two self-contained literatures.

**What Darwall adds to the host:**
1. A **disambiguation**. "Being addressed as a 'you'" (host L73) can mean being responded to (the host's sense) or being held answerable (Darwall's). The host never separates them. The optimistic review that seeded this task (`reviews/optimistic-2026-11-26-other-minds-wing` §Medium Priority) asked for exactly this separation, so that the evidential claim does not borrow moral weight.
2. A **boundary claim** with a source. Darwall's presupposition argument yields commitments the addresser incurs. It yields no findings about the addressee. Darwall makes the same concession for the epistemic case himself (testimony: second-personal authority "is not second-personal all the way down", 2006: 57, quoted via Eilan 2014).
3. A **direction of dependence**. Isern-Mas & Gomila (2020) argue that Darwall's normative standpoint *presupposes* the real-time attribution of intentions that second-person social cognition studies. If they are right, normative address is downstream of the evidential channel and cannot certify it.

**What Darwall does not add:**
- No evidence for other minds. The presupposition argument establishes what one is *committed to* in addressing someone, not that anyone is there, and not that the addressee is conscious.
- No support for the host's *sui generis* hypothesis. Darwall's irreducibility is conceptual and normative: second-personal reasons cannot be derived from the value of outcomes. The host's irreducibility is evidential: being responded to may supply evidence neither introspection nor observation can. Upgrading the first into the second is the error this note exists to block.
- No support for dualism or for libertarian agency. Darwall frames second-personal psychology "naturalistically" (ch. 7, as quoted by Lavin 2008). He reads Fichte's positing of free agents as made "from the practical point of view" (Darwall 2014: 14). His project is, in Isern-Mas & Gomila's words, "analytical and normative, rather than descriptive or explanatory".

**Secondary host:** `topics/reactive-attitudes-and-strawsonian-responsibility`. Darwall's account is the leading "second-personal" reading of Strawson. Its competence presupposition bears directly on Watson's capacity seam (that page, L71), and it cuts *against* the Map as much as for it. A short fold there is the honest placement for the normative content.

### Darwall (2014), "Why Fichte's Second-Personal Foundations Can Provide a More Adequate Account of the Relation of Right than Kant's"
- **Citation [META]**: *Grazer Philosophische Studien* 90: 5–20. DOI 10.1163/9789004298767_003. Open full text in the Graz-hosted volume PDF (`static.uni-graz.at/.../GPS_90.pdf`). Page numbers below are the printed folios.
- **Why it matters**: it is **Darwall's own words**, reachable in full, summarising the 2006 presupposition argument and his reading of Fichte's summons.
- **Quotes [RAW]**:
  - p. 15: "In The Second-Person Standpoint, I argue that any putative address of second-personal reasons is committed to both a shared second-personal competence to choose freely to act on such reasons and a shared basic second-personal authority to make claims and demands of one another from which such reasons flow (Darwall 2006)." Note "is committed to": the argument is about commitments.
  - p. 14: "So far, however, this might be no different from a third-personal, observer's awareness. That the other sees her as an agent, or even that she so sees herself, is but another aspect of the way things are anyhow." Darwall locates what is distinctively second-personal in the normative structure of address, *not* in any extra awareness of the other. This is the cleanest textual support for the "adds no evidence" point.
  - p. 14: the presuppositions of second-personal deliberation "require that the agent deliberate under the assumption that she (and her co-deliberator) are both free agents"; she posits herself and the other as free "from the practical point of view".
  - p. 13: "there is no question that he takes a reciprocally recognizing, and thus second-personal, summons from a free rational being to the agent (as a free rational being) to be necessary for self-consciousness."
- **Guard (Fichte)**: Fichte's summons argument (*Foundations of Natural Right*, 1796–97, §§1–4) is the one place in this lineage that purports to reach the *existence* of other rational beings, as a condition of self-consciousness. Darwall p. 14 calls it "Fichte's transcendental claim". Even granted, it would establish rational agents in general, posited practically. It would say nothing about whether *this* interlocutor is conscious, or about phenomenal consciousness at all. The Map must not import it through Darwall as an anti-sceptical result. The Fichte page numbers Darwall gives refer to the Neuhouser/Baur translation (Cambridge 2000). They were **not** checked against Fichte this run.
- **Guard (two standpoints)**: p. 14 says the summons "gives her a practical awareness of her agency that is irreducible to any consciousness she has of herself as part of a causal order". That sentence is a Kant–Fichte two-standpoints claim about the *practical* point of view. It is **not** an anti-physicalist or libertarian thesis, and must not be cited for agent causation or for Tenet 1.

### Lavin (2008), NDPR review of *The Second-Person Standpoint*
- **Citation [META]**: Douglas Lavin, *Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews* 2008.01.05. https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/the-second-person-standpoint-morality-respect-and-accountability/ (fetched in full).
- **Key points**:
  - Ch. 10 argues from the "normative felicity conditions" of address. In addressing a demand I presuppose my authority to hold you accountable, and that you can comply by recognising the demand's authority (Lavin's paraphrase, citing p. 249).
  - Ch. 11 confronts whether the whole apparatus is "rationally optional, or worse, illusory" (p. 277).
- **Coinage to credit to Lavin, not Darwall [RAW]**: "in it we have, I think, a problem about the efficacy of other minds". Lavin contrasts this with the classical problem of other minds, which he calls "a problem belonging to theoretical reason". This is the precise frame for the host. Darwall's subject is a *practical* problem of other minds (how another's demand can bind my will). The host's subject is the *theoretical* one. If a live page uses the contrast, credit Lavin 2008.
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral.

## Major Positions

### Darwall: morality as equal accountability
- **Proponent**: Darwall (2006; 2013a, b; 2014).
- **Core claim**: moral obligation is irreducibly second-personal. Making or acknowledging a claim presupposes a shared competence and a shared authority among free and rational agents. Morality as equal accountability is supposed to follow from those presuppositions, and contractualism is the favoured normative theory.
- **Key arguments**: the felicity conditions of address; "Strawson's Point" against consequentialist justifications of blame; rejection of the Kantian project of deriving morality from first-person deliberation (ch. 9, the "naïve practical reasoner"); a Fichtean argument that the second-person standpoint is inescapable.
- **Relation to site tenets**: metaethical and neutral on mind–body metaphysics. **Compatible** with Dualism (it is silent on it), **not suggestive**, **not discriminating**. It gives nothing for or against Bidirectional Interaction. It does not touch Minimal Quantum Interaction or No Many Worlds. Tenet 5 has no work to do here: Darwall's argument is not a parsimony judgement.

### Critics within the normative literature
- **Korsgaard** (2007): the standpoint looks optional unless the second person is already "within" the deliberating agent. This is a Kantian internalisation.
- **Wallace** (2007) and **Faulkner** (2014): an ought holds whether or not anyone demands it, so second-personal reasons are not "second-personal all the way down". Wallace is known here only via Eilan.
- **Yaffe** (2010): second-personal competence is neither sufficient (nor obviously necessary) for freedom of will.
- **Miller** (2014): "Strawson's Point" misreads Strawson.
- **Lavin** (2008, 2014): presenting the second-personal circle does not answer how another's view can bind my practical reason. The real interest lies in a practical problem of other minds and its link to "other wills".
- **Relation to site tenets**: all neutral. None engages consciousness as such.

### Naturalisers: second-person social cognition under the normative standpoint
- **Proponents**: Isern-Mas & Gomila (2020, 2022); in the developmental-evolutionary register, Tomasello (2016) (lead).
- **Core claim**: normative address presupposes an engaged, online, reciprocally contingent second-personal competence for attributing intentions and emotions.
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral metaphysically. Methodologically it is **compatible** with the host's *enabling* reading. It places the interactive capacity first, which fits "interaction enables evidence", and it makes no constitutive-of-consciousness claim. It does not bear on the enactivist dispute either way.

## Key Debates

### Does second-personal address bear on knowledge of other minds?
- **Sides**:
  - Implicitly *yes*, in the Fichtean lineage: the summons is a condition of self-consciousness, so other rational beings are presupposed a priori.
  - *Not evidentially*: Darwall's own framing makes the presuppositions commitments of the practical standpoint (2014: 14–15). He concedes that testimony's second-personal authority rests on epistemic authority (2006: 57).
  - *Interdependent*: Lavin and Eilan argue the normative and thinking strands need each other.
- **Map's reading**:
  - A presupposition shows what an addresser is committed to, not that the commitment is satisfied.
  - An addresser can address a demand to a recording, a chatbot or a sleeping person, and the felicity conditions then simply fail.
  - Nothing in the address detects the failure. Detecting it is the evidential channel's job (contingent response, repair, timing), and that job is prior.
  - Darwall's p. 14 remark that awareness of being regarded as an agent "might be no different from a third-personal, observer's awareness" puts his own distinguishing mark on the normative side.
- **Current state**: no source found that runs Darwall's presupposition argument as an anti-sceptical argument about other minds. There is a "transcendental argument from the second person" literature for interpersonal *morality* (seen in search results; not read).

### Is the second person irreducible?
- **Normative sense (Darwall)**: the concepts of demand, authority, reason and accountability can be defined only through one another, so second-personal reasons are not reducible to outcome-regarding reasons.
- **Evidential sense (host)**: being responded to may supply evidence neither introspection nor observation can. The host holds this as a hypothesis lacking its discriminating test (Pfeiffer et al. 2014).
- **Map's stake**: the two theses are logically independent. Either could be true while the other is false. **The fold must say so explicitly**, because the shared word "irreducible" invites the upgrade.

### Does second-personal competence require phenomenal consciousness?
- Darwall does not ask. His competence is a rational and agential capacity: to recognise a demand as a reason and hold oneself to it.
- The Map's reactive-attitudes page already cites Madva (2019, *JCS*) for the claim that the capacity for phenomenal consciousness is necessary for moral responsibility (that page L40). Madva was **not** re-verified this run.
- **Map's reading**: even if Madva is right, the presupposition would then *presuppose* consciousness in the addressee. It would still not be evidence that the presupposition is met. There is a possible bearing on AI addressees, but it is out of scope here and would need its own sources.

### Host: `topics/the-second-person-evidential-channel` (2,736 / 4,000 at 17:18Z), measured delta **+237** (2,736 → 2,973; under the +250 cap)

**H1. New paragraph**, appended as the fourth bold item of §"The Evidential-Status Discipline". Insert it after the sentence ending `and this article does not pretend otherwise.` (count 1; currently L61), separated by a blank line. 160 words:

> **The normative second person.** Stephen Darwall's *The Second-Person Standpoint* (2006) puts the same vocabulary to a different question: what making and acknowledging claims on one another presupposes. In his own summary, "any putative address of second-personal reasons is committed to" a shared competence to act freely on such reasons and a shared authority to make claims and demands (Darwall, 2014). A commitment incurred by addressing someone is not a finding about them. It does not show that anyone is there to be addressed, still less that they are conscious; if anything the dependence runs the other way, since address presupposes the real-time attribution of intentions this channel studies (Isern-Mas & Gomila, 2020). Darwall's irreducibility thesis concerns concepts (demand, authority, reason and accountability are definable only through one another), so it lends nothing to the evidential irreducibility held open above. What he adds is a boundary: being addressed can carry a claim on one's will, and a claim is not evidence.

## Writer Guards

1. **Never upgrade a normative presupposition into evidence.** "Address presupposes the addressee's competence" means the addresser is committed to that competence. It does not mean the competence is present, and it says nothing about consciousness.
2. **Keep the two irreducibilities apart.** Darwall's is conceptual and normative (second-personal reasons cannot be reduced to outcome-regarding reasons). The host's is evidential and held as a hypothesis. Neither supports the other.
3. **Keep the senses of "second-personal" in the Map's corpus distinct:**
   - (a) Darwall's normative sense: address, claims, accountability.
   - (b) León's "second-personal acquaintance" in `research/voids-intersubjective-void-2026-02-01` L147. This is an *epistemic* sense: shared experience as direct, unmediated contact with another's psychological life.
   - (c) Macarthur's persons "acknowledged as rational agencies in second-personal space" (`research/subject-naturalism-and-the-placement-problem-2026-09-15` L117). This is a Sellarsian, recognitional sense, closer to (a) than to (b).
   - (d) The host's interactive/evidential sense.
   Do not let (a) or (c) borrow the epistemic credentials of (b) or (d).
4. **Coiners**:
   - "Second-person standpoint" and "second-personal reason": Darwall (2006).
   - "Strawson's Point": Darwall's label for his reading of Strawson, contested by Miller (2014).
   - "Bipolarity": M. Thompson (2004), via Eilan.
   - The practical "problem about the efficacy of other minds": Lavin (2008).
   - The summons argument: Fichte (1796–97), in Darwall's interpretation.
5. **Darwall is not a libertarian ally.** His freedom is presupposed "from the practical point of view". His own stance on determinism in ch. 11 was **not** reached, so do not call him a compatibilist or an incompatibilist.
6. **Quotation status.** Darwall-2006 quotations here are second-hand (Lavin, Eilan, Isern-Mas & Gomila). Only the Darwall 2014 strings are first-hand. Prefer the 2014 text or paraphrase on live pages.
