## Executive Summary

Popper is the twentieth century's most prominent interactionist about mind and body, and he is the Map's most awkward ally. Five results matter, in descending order of importance to the Map:

1. **Popper was not a substance dualist, and said so in print.** In "Of Clouds and Clocks" he writes: "Thus, like Descartes, I propose the adoption of a dualistic outlook, though I do not of course recommend talking of two kinds of interacting substances. But I think it is helpful and legitimate to distinguish two kinds of interacting *states* (or events), physio-chemical and mental ones" (verified, *Objective Knowledge* ch. 6, p. 252). The Map is a two-substance dualism. Popper is a state/event interactionist who then adds a third realm. This is the divergence, and it is documented in Popper's own voice.

2. **Popper's case for World 2 runs through World 3, so the Map cannot inherit his argument.** John Gray's 1981 review lands this as a criticism: "Popper's philosophy contains no resources to resist physicalism, since his only arguments for World Two are arguments that invoke World Three. If … Popper's postulated World Three is unnecessary, he has no reason to move beyond the First World of physical objects and laws" (verified, *Reason Papers* 7: 121–124; the p. 124 running head did not survive text extraction, so the page is inferred from position). If the Map declines World 3 — and it must, since World 3 objects are neither physical nor experiential — then Popper's argument for an irreducible mind is unavailable to it. The Map can cite Popper for *provenance and for the shape of the interactionist programme*, never for the inference.

3. **Popper explicitly rejected the amplified-quantum-jump model of decision — the Map's own mechanism family.** On Compton's amplifier model: "in my opinion the model has no similarity to any rational decision. It is, rather, a model of a kind of decision-making where people who cannot make up their minds say: 'Let us toss a penny'" (verified, ch. 6, p. 228). And: "I do not think that we shall get much further with quantum jumps" (p. 228). He does concede the mechanism may be real but denies it is the interesting case: "I even admit that it is conceivable that something like the amplification of a quantum jump may actually happen in our brains if we make a snap-decision. But are snap-decisions really so very interesting?" (p. 228). This is a hostile witness from inside interactionism, and the corpus has never met him: **"clouds and clocks" and "plastic control" return zero hits across all of `obsidian/`.**

4. **Popper anticipated the Map's answer to the conservation objection by roughly sixty years.** In a footnote: biological processes characteristically involve "the release or triggering of stored energy", so "such formulae as 'the cause equals the effect' (and, with it, the traditional criticism of Cartesian interactionism) have long become obsolete, in spite of the continuing validity of the conservation laws" (verified, ch. 6, note 46, p. 234). The hub currently gives this reply to Broad (1925); Popper is an independent and mid-eclipse instance of the same move.

5. **Popper stated a version of Tenet 5 as a methodological rule, and a reviewer called it bizarre.** Gray, reporting Feyerabend: Popper "even elevates this game of hide-and-seek into a methodological principle, stipulating (bizarrely) that we are to resort to Occam's razor only after we have decided which entities are irreducible" (verified, *Reason Papers* 7: 123, page marker confirmed in the extraction). That stipulation is close to [[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]. It arrives with its own named critic attached, which is the useful part.

Popper's own summary of the position: "Thus I arrive at the view that a mind-body dualism is nearer to the truth than a materialist monism. But dualism is not enough. We have to recognize world 3" (verified, Tanner Lecture, p. 164).

## Key Sources

### Popper, K. R. (1980). "Three Worlds"

- **URL**: https://tannerlectures.org/lectures/three-worlds/ (PDF: https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/popper80.pdf)
- **Type**: Published lecture (primary), read in full
- **Citation, corrected**: The Tanner Lecture on Human Values was **delivered 7 April 1978** at the University of Michigan and **published 1980** in *The Tanner Lectures on Human Values*, vol. 1, S. M. McMurrin (ed.), Salt Lake City: University of Utah Press, 141–167. The SEP Popper bibliography gives it as **1980**; the driver brief and several citing sources give it as 1978. The PDF's running page numbers (144, 148, 149, 153, 154, 165, 166, 167) confirm the 141–167 range. **Cite as Popper (1980), delivered 1978** — or give both. Do not cite it as a 1978 publication.
- **Key points**:
  - The three worlds: World 1 = physical bodies, "of stones and of stars; of plants and of animals; but also of radiation, and of other forms of physical energy"; World 2 = "the mental or psychological world, the world of our feelings of pain and of pleasure, of our thoughts, of our decisions, of our perceptions and our observations"; World 3 = "the world of the products of the human mind, such as languages; tales and stories and religious myths; scientific conjectures or theories, and mathematical constructions; songs and symphonies; paintings and sculptures. But also aeroplanes and airports and other feats of engineering."
  - The lecture's target includes dualists: "In this lecture I intend to challenge those who uphold a monist **or even a dualist** view of the universe; and I will propose, instead, a pluralist view." Popper does not pretend to refute either: "I do not pretend that I can refute these two views; but I can challenge them, by offering and defending a pluralist view."
  - **The criterion of reality is causal**, and it is built by generalising the physicalist's own: "what is real or what exists is whatever may, directly or indirectly, have a causal effect upon physical things, and especially upon those primitive physical things that can be easily handled." World 3 then qualifies because theories change the physical world.
  - **World 2 is the indispensable intermediary.** "It seems to me that the intervention of the mind, and thus of world 2, is indispensable, and that only the intervention of the mental world 2 allows world 3 objects to exert, indirectly, a causal influence upon the physical world 1."
  - **Causal closure denied for all three worlds**: "this means that the worlds 1, 2, and 3, can interact and, therefore, that none of them is causally closed." And: "I think that it means shutting one's eyes to the obvious, and explaining away the obvious, if we deny that world 1 is causally open to world 2, and through it, to world 3."
  - **Emergence plus feedback**: "World 2, the world of conscious experience, emerges as an evolutionary product from the world of organisms. World 3, the world of the products of the human mind, emerges as an evolutionary product from world 2. In each of these cases, the emerging product has a tremendous feedback effect upon the world from which it emerged."
  - **The self is World-3-dependent**: "The very idea of a self depends on world 3 theories, especially upon a theory of time which underlies the identity of the self … the full consciousness of self is anchored in our human language."
  - **On machine consciousness**: "Can computers think? I do not hesitate to answer this question with an emphatic 'No'." And a functional argument the Map should notice: "Consciousness has a biological function in animals … It does not seem to me at all likely that a machine can be conscious unless it needs consciousness. Even we ourselves fall asleep when our consciousness has no function to fulfil."
  - Popper's own footnote gives the book's imprint as "K. R. Popper and J. C. Eccles, *The Self and Its Brain* (Berlin, Heidelberg, New York, London: Springer International, 1977)" — publisher of record, in the author's hand.
- **Tenet alignment**: Aligns with Tenet 3 (bidirectional interaction) and with the denial of causal closure. **Conflicts with the Map's two-substance framing** via World 3 and via Popper's explicit refusal of substance talk. Neutral-to-hostile on Tenet 2 (see "Of Clouds and Clocks" below).
- **Note on the "monist or even a dualist" framing**: the lecture does not argue against dualism by attacking mental causation; it argues that dualism has too few categories. That is a *different* line of attack from any the corpus currently records, and it does not threaten Tenet 1.

### Popper, K. R. (1972). "Of Clouds and Clocks", *Objective Knowledge* ch. 6

- **URL**: scan at http://www.the-rathouse.com/Of_Clouds_and_Clocks_Part_1.pdf and `..._Part_2.pdf`
- **Type**: Book chapter (primary), read in full; originally the second Arthur Holly Compton Memorial Lecture, Washington University, 21 April 1965, published separately 1966, reprinted as ch. 6 of *Objective Knowledge: An Evolutionary Approach* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1972). The scan carries the *Objective Knowledge* running heads and page numbers (207–255), so page citations below are to that reprint. **Every page attribution below was fixed by locating the quote between two surviving running-head page numbers in the extraction, not by eye.**
- **Delivery-date caveat**: the abebooks/Maggs and Blackwell's dealer listings both date the lecture to **21 April 1965** with 1966 publication; one search summary gave 1966 for the lecture itself. Prefer "delivered 1965, published 1966, reprinted 1972".
- **Key points** (all grep-verified in the NFKC-normalised extraction):
  - **The penny-toss objection** (p. 228): Compton's model "uses quantum indeterminacy, and the unpredictability of a quantum jump, as a model of a human decision of great moment. It consists of an amplifier which amplifies the effect of a single quantum jump in such a way that it may either cause an explosion or destroy the relay necessary for bringing the explosion about. In this way one single quantum jump may be equivalent to a major decision. But in my opinion the model has no similarity to any rational decision. It is, rather, a model of a kind of decision-making where people who cannot make up their minds say: 'Let us toss a penny.' In fact, the whole apparatus for amplifying a quantum jump seems rather unnecessary: tossing a penny, and deciding on the result of the toss whether or not to pull a trigger, would do just as well."
  - **The partial concession** (p. 228): "I admit that the quantum jump model may be a model for such snap-decisions; and I even admit that it is conceivable that something like the amplification of a quantum jump may actually happen in our brains if we make a snap-decision. But are snap-decisions really so very interesting? Are they characteristic of human behaviour — of rational human behaviour? I do not think so; and I do not think that we shall get much further with quantum jumps."
  - **What Popper wanted instead** (p. 228) — and this is where the Map and Popper may actually meet: "What we need for understanding rational human behaviour — and indeed, animal behaviour — is something intermediate in character between perfect chance and perfect determinism — something intermediate between perfect clouds and perfect clocks." He rejects the Hume/Schlick doctrine that "the only alternative to determinism is sheer chance" as "not only highly dogmatic … but clearly absurd".
  - **Plastic controls** are his positive proposal: "a 'plastic control', as I shall call it in contradistinction to a 'cast-iron control'"; organisms "establish a kind of plastic control" over their behaviour; "our actions by our theories and purposes is a plastic control". He argues such controls physically exist (the soap bubble example, p. 251) — "natural physical cloud-like systems which are plastically and softly controlled by other cloud-like systems do exist."
  - **The conservation reply** (note 46, p. 234): "This is a point of great importance, so much so that we should hardly describe any process as typically biological unless it involved the release or triggering of stored energy … though amplifiers and release processes did not play a great role in classical physics, they are most characteristic of quantum physics and of course of chemistry. (Radioactivity with a triggering energy equal to zero is an extreme case …) This is one of the reasons why such formulae as 'the cause equals the effect' (and, with it, the traditional criticism of Cartesian interactionism) have long become obsolete, in spite of the continuing validity of the conservation laws."
  - **The state-not-substance passage** (p. 252), quoted in full in the Executive Summary above, with its pluralist conclusion: "Thus we have to be not merely dualists, but pluralists."
  - **An anti-will-centric caution** (note 62, p. 252): Popper holds "that there exist mental states, or conscious states (for example in dreams) in which the consciousness of the ego … is very weak, or absent. It seems therefore reasonable to assume that full consciousness of the ego is a late development, and that it is a mistake to formulate the body-mind problem in such a way that this form of consciousness (or conscious 'will') is treated as if it were the only one."
  - **Interaction as the only satisfactory solution** (note 62): "I conjecture that the acceptance of an 'interaction' of mental and physical states offers the only satisfactory solution of Descartes's problem."
- **Tenet alignment**: **Conflicts with Tenet 2** as usually pressed (Popper thinks amplified quantum jumps model coin-flipping, not deciding). **Aligns** with the demand for a middle category between chance and determinism, which is structurally what the Map's biasing-within-the-Born-distribution proposal supplies. **Aligns strongly** with the Map's conservation-objection reply and with Tenet 3.

- **Structure** (Gray 1981, who had the book in hand): three parts. ### Gray, J. N. (1981). Review of *The Self and Its Brain*

- **URL**: https://reasonpapers.com/pdf/07/rp_7_10.pdf
- **Type**: Book review (secondary), read in full. *Reason Papers* No. 7 (Spring 1981): 121–124. Reviewer: John N. Gray, Jesus College, Oxford.
- **Why it matters more than the better-known reviews**: it is freely available, it is by a serious philosopher, and it states the structural dependence of Popper's dualism on World 3 more cleanly than anything else found.
- **Key points**:
  - The book's thrust: "At one level, the book may be seen as a sustained polemic against reductionism. According to Popper and Eccles, the conception of man as 'nothing but' a physico-chemical mechanism is at once scientifically unsupported, philosophically inadequate, and morally pernicious. Their aim is to reinstate 'the ghost in the machine' …" (the scan reads "philosophically-inadequate", a line-break hyphen)
  - Where Popper succeeds: "He contends that nothing can show a priori the superiority of the materialist position"; he "adduces some important and, in my view, wholly conclusive arguments against the doctrines of parallelism and epiphenomenalism".
  - **The World-3 dependence** (the load-bearing result, quoted in full in the Executive Summary): Popper's argument against the identity theory "presupposes the truth of the 'Three Worlds' doctrine. If we can avoid postulating World Three, there is every reason to suppose we can do without World Two, as well."
  - **Feyerabend's objection, as relayed**: "none of Popper's arguments for the autonomy of abstract objects establishes their irreducibility in terms of mental or physical states and processes. Pointing out, as Popper does, that such things as numbers, arguments, and theories exert a causal influence in the mental and physical realms cannot by itself show that such things do not themselves belong to those realms: **to show a causal connection is not to mark an ontological distinction.** Too often, as Feyerabend remarks, Popper proceeds by excluding certain things from the physical and mental realms and then triumphantly discovers them in World Three."
  - **The Occam's-razor stipulation** (Tenet 5 precedent + its critic), quoted in the Executive Summary.
  - Gray's verdict: "all philosophers should read it — even though few will find its argument finally persuasive."
- **Tenet alignment**: This review is the sharpest *anti*-Map instrument in the whole subject, because "to show a causal connection is not to mark an ontological distinction" is a general objection that transfers straight onto any argument that infers irreducibility from causal efficacy. The Map should absorb it, not just Popper's half.

## Major Positions

### Popper's trialist interactionism

- **Proponents**: Popper; Eccles from 1973 onward.
- **Core claim**: three interacting sub-universes — physical, mental, and the objective contents of thought — none causally closed. Reality is conferred by causal efficacy, generalised from the physicalist's own criterion. World 3 acts on World 1 *only* through World 2.
- **Key arguments**: (a) the Beethoven's-Fifth argument — there are objectively better and worse performances, which neither materialist monism nor dualism can explain, since both can only reduce "better" to how many people approve; (b) the argument from science-driven physical change — theories change the world, so theories are causally efficacious, so real; (c) the evolutionary emergence-plus-feedback story; (d) the anti-identity-theory argument, which Gray shows presupposes World 3.
- **Relation to site tenets**:
  - Tenet 1 (Dualism): **partial**. Popper denies reducibility of the mental, but explicitly declines substance talk and reframes the position as pluralism about interacting *states*. The Map's two-substance commitment is not his.
  - Tenet 2 (Minimal Quantum Interaction): **conflict, sharply**, via the penny-toss objection. But see "Potential Article Angles" — the conflict may be with the *bare-amplifier* version of MQI rather than with the Map's actual biasing proposal.
  - Tenet 3 (Bidirectional Interaction): **strong alignment**. "None of them is causally closed"; interaction is "the only satisfactory solution of Descartes's problem".
  - Tenet 4 (No Many Worlds): **no direct bearing found in the texts read.** Popper was a critic of subjectivist and, separately, of Everettian readings of quantum theory, but I did not verify anything on MWI. Do not assert an alignment here.
  - Tenet 5 (Occam's Razor Has Limits): **alignment, as a methodological stipulation** — razor only after irreducibility is settled — with Feyerabend and Gray both attacking it as question-begging.

### World 3 as the point of divergence

- **Core claim**: the objective contents of thought (theories, arguments, problems, institutions) form a third autonomous realm, man-made but not reducible to minds or to physical embodiments.
- **The Map's incompatibility** is structural, not stylistic. The Map's ontology has two kinds; World 3 objects would be a third that is neither. And per Gray, Popper's route to World 2 runs through World 3, so the Map cannot take the conclusion while dropping the premise.
- **Critics, with attribution**:
  - **Feyerabend (1974)**, via Gray: causal efficacy does not establish ontological distinctness.
  - **Cohen (1980)** questioned World 3's autonomy and ontological unity, treating it as "a metaphor rather than a reliable ontological view of reality" — **search-summary level only; not verified.** L. J. Cohen also reviewed *The Self and Its Brain* in *Mind* (1979), per a search summary; both need checking before citation.
  - **Feser (2010, blog)**: Popper's World 3 is "man-made", its objects "abstracted" from World 1 embodiments, which Feser reads as closer to Aristotelian moderate realism than to Platonic extreme realism. He also flags Popper's rejection of substance in favour of process, and judges Popper "better as a critic than as a positive theorist". Useful for locating World 3 on the universals map; a blog, so cite as such.
  - The standing general objection (multiple carriers): Popper knew he would be charged with hypostatising abstractions; "where is this world and how does the mind make contact with it" recurs — i.e. **World 3 inherits its own interaction problem**, which is a neat structural point for an article whose subject is the interaction problem.

### Eccles's trialism, and where it stops

- **Core claim**: Eccles adopted Popper's three worlds wholesale ("I was a dualist, now I am a trialist") and located World-1/World-2 interaction in the liaison areas of the dominant hemisphere.
- **The drift**: Eccles's later work (dendron/psychon, Beck–Eccles) is mechanistic and synaptic, and its ontology is a two-way mind/brain coupling. Gray records that Eccles, unlike Popper, wanted personal survival of bodily death. So trialism was, for Eccles, a framework he announced and then largely stopped using — his 1994 title is *How the Self Controls Its Brain*, not *How the Self and World 3 Control the Brain*. **This reading is mine, inferred from the source pattern; it is not asserted by any source I read. Flag it as a conjecture if used.**
- **Do not re-derive**: Beck–Eccles and the quantum-interface material have corpus coverage (`sherrington-dualist-lineage`, `penfield-interactionist-dualism`, `concepts/quantum-biology-and-neural-mechanisms`, P-Q4). Cross-reference only.

## Key Debates

### Does causal efficacy establish ontological distinctness?

- **Sides**: Popper (yes — it is the generalisation of the physicalist's own criterion of reality) vs Feyerabend and Gray (no — "to show a causal connection is not to mark an ontological distinction").
- **Core disagreement**: whether a criterion of *reality* can double as a criterion of *irreducibility*.
- **Current state**: unresolved, and the objection is the reason Popper's World 3 never became mainstream. **It bears on the Map directly**: any Map argument of the form "consciousness has effects, therefore it is not physical" is exposed to the same reply. Worth checking `topics/consciousness-and-causal-powers` and `concepts/type-token-causation` against it.

### Is quantum indeterminacy any use to the will?

- **Sides**: Popper (no — amplified quantum jumps model coin-flipping, not deciding; what is needed is "something intermediate between perfect chance and perfect determinism") vs the Map, Compton, Eccles-after-1992, Stapp.
- **Core disagreement**: whether indeterminism plus *selection* is a coherent third category, or whether every indeterministic mechanism collapses into chance. Popper affirms the third category exists (plastic controls) but denies quantum jumps deliver it.
- **Current state**: the corpus already treats the luck/randomness objection well (`concepts/quantum-indeterminacy-free-will`, `topics/trilemma-of-selection`, `topics/event-causal-libertarianism`, `concepts/agent-causation`). **What is missing is that the objection's most eminent twentieth-century statement comes from an interactionist** — and that his own positive answer (plastic controls, hierarchies of soft control) is a candidate the Map has never assessed. Zero corpus hits for "plastic control" or "clouds and clocks".

### Did Popper treat interactionism as a research programme?

- **Status: UNVERIFIED — do not assert.** The driver brief asks whether "Popper himself called psychophysical interactionism a research programme rather than a tested theory". Grep of the full Tanner Lecture text returns **zero** hits for "research programme" and "research program". What *is* documented is adjacent and weaker:
  - Popper's concept of a **"metaphysical research programme"** is real, and is developed in "A Metaphysical Epilogue" to *Quantum Theory and the Schism in Physics* (vol. III of the *Postscript to the Logic of Scientific Discovery*, Bartley III (ed.), 1982; Routledge reissue ISBN 9780415091121). Verified to chapter-title level; **the epilogue was not read**, and I did not verify that Popper applies the term to psychophysical interactionism.
  - Popper's falsifiability criterion demarcates *science*, and he did not treat metaphysics as meaningless — Gray: "philosophical problems grow naturally from difficulties in other areas of inquiry … genuine questions to which a diversity of meaningful answers may be proposed."
  - Popper does call his own proposal a conjecture: "I conjecture that the acceptance of an 'interaction' of mental and physical states offers the only satisfactory solution of Descartes's problem" (note 62, verified), and he closes the essay by saying "I am far from suggesting that my proposed solution is what philosophers have been looking for."
- **Correct way to use this**: the Map may claim Popperian licence for *running an untested metaphysical programme* (via the MRP concept, once someone reads the epilogue), but it may **not** claim that Popper certified interactionism as science, or that he called it a research programme. Anyone writing the article should verify the epilogue first or write around it.
