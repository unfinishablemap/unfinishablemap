## Executive Summary

The brief asked whether procrastination is akrasia, a judgement against delay that delay defeats, which it attributed to Stroud, or a distinct failure of diachronic agency, which it attributed to Andreou. **The attribution needs correcting. Stroud is on the second side.** Her chapter argues that procrastination is not "simply a variety of akrasia", because akrasia "is a purely synchronic phenomenon". Nor can Holton–McIntyre weakness of will cover all of it, because that is "exclusively a defect in plan implementation", whereas procrastination "is sometimes constituted by defects in plan drafting and adoption" (chapter abstract). Arpaly's review calls this conclusion "surprising" and reports Stroud's "anemic" intentions.

**Andreou's framing is verified, in her SEP entry rather than the 2007 paper.** A dynamic choice problem is one where "a series of choices do not serve one's concerns well even though each choice in the series seems perfectly well suited to serving one's concerns". Procrastination "seems to be, by its very nature, a dynamic choice problem (Stroud 2010)". The 2007 paper's own mechanism is "preference loops", stable or evanescent (abstract only).

**Tenenbaum goes furthest.** Of the novel left unwritten, "none of my momentary choices is irrational (at least, in respect to this project) when considered in isolation" (proof p. 139). Even adopting an intermediate policy does not move the failure into any one choice (pp. 140–142).

**The reductive reply** is MacIntosh's. Once Andreou's self-torturer model is made intelligible, the agent "would delay only from ordinary weakness of will". Ainslie calls procrastination "a species of impulse". Psychology's standard definition (Steel 2007, p. 66) builds an akratic-looking expectation into the concept: "despite expecting to be worse off for the delay".

**For the Map**, the analyses that deny procrastination is akrasia place its failure where the host's selection lens has nothing to read. This is a boundary on the lens, not a further fit ([below](#maps-question)). It belongs in the host's Relation to Site Perspective, next to the luck objection it qualifies.

**Genuinely new**:
1. **The classification debate exists, and its leading voices deny that procrastination is akrasia.** Stroud (2010), Andreou (SEP 2025) and Tenenbaum (2010) all say so; MacIntosh (2010) replies. The host has 0 hits for "procrastinat".
2. **Who holds what.**
   - **Not akrasia, nor wholly Holton's weakness of will**: Stroud. Procrastination can be "constituted by defects in plan drafting and adoption".
   - **A dynamic choice problem**: Andreou. Each choice "seems perfectly well suited".
   - **No momentary choice irrational**: Tenenbaum's "top-down independent" irrationality.
   - **Reductive replies**: MacIntosh ("ordinary weakness of will"); Ainslie ("a species of impulse").
   - **Psychology's definition**: Steel. Voluntary delay "despite expecting to be worse off for the delay".
3. **Steel's definition is ambiguous in a way that matters** (the Map's observation, not Steel's). "Expecting to be worse off for the delay" can mean worse off for *this* deferral, which gives an akratic shape, or for the delay as a whole, which fits Tenenbaum. The whole-delay reading makes the definition compatible with no momentary akrasia.
4. **The "series of choices" framing predates Andreou.** Akerlof's 1991 lecture (working-paper text) already has "the cumulative effect of a series of repeated errors". But his choices are each small *errors* ("close to maximizing"). Andreou's each "seems" well suited, and Tenenbaum's are each not irrational. **Three grades, three authors.**
5. **Akerlof's model is salience, not hyperbolic discounting.** "Undue salience or vividness"; 0 hits for "hyperbolic" in the working paper. Do not credit Akerlof with hyperbolic discounting ([coiner traps](#coiners)).
6. **Tenenbaum blocks the relocation repair.** Recognising the need for an intermediate policy "seem[s] to allow us to locate failures of rationality more precisely". Yet "even in those cases, it is possible that none of my momentary actions was irrational" (p. 141). A loose policy reproduces the problem, and a policy with fixed exceptions "is almost always a second best" (p. 142). This bears directly on `self-control` L86's "Ordinary success moves to moments of policy".
7. **The Map consequence** ([The Map's Question](#maps-question)): the host's lens has a boundary, and the luck objection's starkest form does not reach procrastination of the Tenenbaum and Stroud kinds.
8. **The retraction is confirmed by three independent channels** ([below](#retraction)).

### D. Tenenbaum, S. (2010). "The Vice of Procrastination." In Andreou & White (eds.), pp. 130–150. doi:10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195376685.003.0008 [V-xref; V-proof]

- **Source**: OUP uncorrected page proof (PhilPapers `TENTVO-2`), running heads 130–150. **Final wording unverified.** Pages match the published range.
- **Top-down independence** (p. 134):
  - "The rationality of a long-term activity A is top-down independent if and only if the following obtains: If an agent engages (or ought to engage) in A, there is a set of possible choices S for the agent such that: (1) No particular choice in S would be irrational. (2) If the agent were to make all of the choices in S, she would be for that very reason irrational."
  - "I might fail to exercise regularly while being rationally justified in each action in which I fail to exercise."
  - Such activities are not rare: "most policies, plans, and projects are like that".
- **The mechanism** (p. 138): "we procrastinate when we move imperceptibly from a clearly acceptable set of choices to a neither acceptable nor unacceptable set and then to an unacceptable one".
- **The draft's quotation** (p. 139): "when I procrastinate, none of my momentary choices is irrational (at least, in respect to this project) when considered in isolation". It is said of the project of writing a novel.
- **Intermediate policies do not relocate it** (pp. 140–142):
  - "if I can't expect to write a novel without an intermediate policy, the failure to adopt the intermediate policy is itself a failure of rationality". But "even in those cases, it is possible that none of my momentary actions was irrational" (p. 141).
  - A loose policy reproduces the problem: "none of these momentary choices on its own is instrumentally irrational" (p. 142).
  - A strict one, with exceptions built in, "is almost always a second best" (p. 142). Strict policies are mostly "intrinsically choiceworthy or rather desperate reactions to powerful temptations".

## Major Positions {#positions}

### Procrastination is (a form of) weakness of will
- **Proponents**: MacIntosh 2010 (reductive reply); Ainslie 2010 (a species of impulse, a temporary preference); implicitly the assumption Andreou's SEP reports ("It has perhaps been assumed…").
- **Core claim**: delay comes from ordinary weakness, whether akratic or a preference reversal.
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral. It is the case where the host's selection lens applies, though only where a momentary judgement against *this* deferral exists.

### Procrastination is a distinct, diachronic failure
- **Proponents**: Stroud 2010 (plan drafting and adoption); Andreou 2007 and 2025 (a dynamic choice problem; preference loops); Tenenbaum 2010 (top-down independent irrationality; the vice opposed to "practical judgment").
- **Core claim**: the failure belongs to the management of agency over time, or to a pattern. It need not be present in any single choice.
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral as to metaphysics. It marks the selection lens's boundary ([below](#maps-question)).

### Psychology's operational definition
- **Proponent**: Steel 2007.
- **Core claim**: voluntary delay of an intended action "despite expecting to be worse off for the delay". Its causes (task aversiveness, impulsiveness, conscientiousness) fit temporal motivation theory.
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral. The expectation clause is ambiguous between this deferral and the whole delay (What Is New, item 3).

### Economic models
- **Proponents**: Akerlof 1991 (salience; a naive agent in effect); O'Donoghue & Rabin 1999 (present bias; naive versus sophisticated).
- **Core claim**: repeated small, time-inconsistent decisions accumulate into large losses.
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral. The models are physical planners; no experiential term.

### Mood repair
- **Proponents**: Sirois & Pychyl 2013.
- **Core claim**: procrastination prioritises short-term mood regulation, and its costs fall on the "future self".
- **Relation to site tenets**: neutral. It is an emotion-regulation lead.

## Key Debates {#debates}

1. **Is procrastination akrasia?**
   - **Sides**: Stroud, Andreou and Tenenbaum say no, or not in general. MacIntosh, and Ainslie in another register, say it reduces to weakness or impulse.
   - **Current state**: open. The 2025 SEP "Weakness of Will" entry, co-authored by Stroud, has **0** hits for "procrastinat" (positive controls in the same text: "akrasia" 69, "Holton" 14, "Stroud" 12). So the debate has not entered that survey.
2. **Is a series irrational when no member is?**
   - **Sides**: Tenenbaum says yes (top-down independence). MacIntosh denies that intransitive or vague structures make delay irrational. Haase (2023) contests Tenenbaum's whole/part model of ends and means.
   - **Current state**: open.
3. **Is intransitivity rational?**
   - **Sides**: the money-pump tradition says no. MacIntosh says yes. Andreou 2023 (*Choosing Well*, ch. 3, per the SEP) makes rationality answer to "subjective appraisal responses" beyond preference [V-raw for the SEP's report; book not read].
4. **Is the cure a policy or a scaffold?**
   - **Positions**: Wieber & Gollwitzer, implementation intentions; Heath & Anderson, the extended will; Andreou, leveraging; White, Kantian willpower; Tenenbaum, strict policies are mostly "second best".

## The Map's Question {#maps-question}

This section is the Map's interpretation. No source above argues for it.

1. **What the host's lens needs.**
   - The selection reading treats "I chose X while judging Y better" as a report about selection against "the agent's own all-things-considered evaluative state", "indexed to the time of action" (host L98).
   - The luck objection is "at its starkest" when "selection runs against the agent's all-things-considered ranking" (host L100).
   - Both need a *moment*: a choice and a contemporaneous judgement it runs against.
2. **Where procrastination puts the failure.**
   - **Stroud**: sometimes in plan drafting and adoption, through an anemic or absent intention. There is no prior resolution to abandon, and no momentary all-things-considered judgement against this delay for selection to defy.
   - **Tenenbaum**: sometimes in the pattern alone. Each momentary choice is not irrational with respect to the project, and the slide is "imperceptible".
   - **The Map's gloss**: in neither case need a momentary choice run against the agent's judgement. So **there is no akratic selection to read**, and the luck objection's starkest form does not arise.
3. **Fit, for Stroud's half.**
   - Her defect lies at what `self-control` L86 calls a moment of policy ("forming an if-then plan, adopting a rule"). That is where the Map places a selector's ordinary work.
   - This is the location-wing review's "failure at the policy moment", and it is **fit, not evidence**. A physical planner that drafts plans badly fits equally. Wieber & Gollwitzer's implementation intentions are the matching remedy, and they work for any planner.
4. **A limit, for Tenenbaum's half.**
   - Tenenbaum argues that the failure "may" be in no momentary action even after intermediate policies are adopted (pp. 140–142). So the obvious repair, relocating the failure to a policy moment, is blocked on his account.
   - A selector whose contributions are momentary selections, of an action or of a policy, then finds **no moment that carries the failure**.
   - The lens can cover such procrastination only if selection can take an **extended action** as its object, roughly as Tenenbaum's Policy as Action Model lets a long-term action govern momentary ones.
   - The Map has not made that commitment. Making it would be a substantive extension of the selection model, not something procrastination supports. A physical planner can also run extended actions, so even the extension would be fit, not evidence.
5. **What does not follow.**
   - Procrastination is **not** a counterexample to the Map's selector. It shows the scope of the akrasia lens, not its falsity.
   - It is **not** evidence that consciousness is idle in procrastination. Felt reluctance and the "I'll do it later" phenomenology fit a selector and a physical planner alike. They do not evidence a non-physical selector, and their presence or absence does not show conscious contribution idle.
   - It does **not** settle Tenet 3's quantifier.
   - **Tenet 4**: the pattern's owner could be described as the Map's persisting subject. But that persistence is a **commitment** ([[tenets/background-commitments|Posit One]]), and a persisting organism owns the pattern equally. Tappolet's argument (Source K) cuts against using future-directed concern as evidence for a further-fact self; the Map does not currently use it (scoped probe, [absence](#absence) 5).
6. **Answer to the brief's question.**
   - Procrastination is sometimes akrasia or weakness of will (Ainslie, MacIntosh, and Steel's definition read per deferral). It is often a distinct diachronic failure (Stroud, Andreou, Tenenbaum).
   - What the Map adds is not a new account of procrastination. It is an exact statement of where its own lens stops: the lens covers the first kind, can locate Stroud's kind at a policy moment as fit only, and needs an untaken extension for Tenenbaum's kind.
