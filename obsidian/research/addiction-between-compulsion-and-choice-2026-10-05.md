# Research: Addiction Between Compulsion and Choice

**Date**: 2026-10-05

## Executive Summary

The Map's akrasia page makes the selection reading depend on a classification: it applies to weakness and not to compulsion. The addiction literature read here says that the classification, as a two-box sort, has no stable application to addiction. Holton and Berridge (2013) argue that addictive desires are "very difficult to resist" and not "impossible to resist", and split the explanation across two systems: one that forms desires and malfunctions, one that controls which desires are acted on and does not. Heyman (2013) and Pickard (2017) press the incentive-sensitivity evidence from the choice side; Henden, Melberg and Røgeberg (2013) reply that behaviour can be compulsive without irresistible desires; Levy (2014) relocates the loss of control to belief. Nobody read here defends literal irresistibility. Every position is naturalistic, and none lends the Map's dualism any support. What the literature does give the Map is a principled place for addiction on the responsibility gradient, and a hard question for the selection reading: where a failure runs through shifted judgement, what did the selector select?

## Key Sources

### Holton & Berridge (2013), "Addiction Between Compulsion and Choice"
- **URL**: https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2019/10/Holton-Berridge-2014-Addiction-between-compulsion-choice-chapter.pdf (publisher proof, 30 pp.); doi 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199862580.003.0012
- **Type**: Book chapter, in Levy (ed.), *Addiction and Self-Control* (OUP), pp. 239–268. **Read in full text.**
- **Metadata finding**: the chapter title page and Crossref give "Addiction Between Compulsion and Choice"; the chapter's own running head on odd pages reads "Addiction Between Choice and Compulsion", and Pickard 2017 cites it in that reversed order. Cite the title-page form. The Berridge-lab filename says 2014; the proof is dated 2013 and Crossref records 2013-11-29.
- **Key points**:
  - The stated aim is "a middle path" between a disease camp and an ordinary-choice camp (p. 241).
  - They accept that the mesolimbic dopamine system is not under direct conscious control and deny that this settles anything about action (p. 241, quoted below).
  - Addictive desire is insulated from the beliefs that would undermine an ordinary desire: addicts "need not even like the thing that they are addicted to" (p. 241). This is the wanting-without-liking dissociation applied to philosophy of action.
  - Addictive desire does not behave as an input to deliberation. It "functions as something more like an intention: as something that, unless checked, will lead, in a rather direct way, to action" (p. 241). Cravings "seem to come somewhere between" desires and intentions (p. 261).
  - Against the James cannonball story: addicts "go around or wait for a lull", and smokers on aeroplanes postpone (p. 242).
  - What an agent does "is not fully determined by the relative strength of the different sorts of desires. We also need to factor in a more active control on the part of the agent" (p. 261).
  - Four factors modulate control: the strength of the self-control system, the efficiency with which it is used, the agent's other desires (motives for giving up strengthen with age, while dopamine levels fall), and belief (pp. 262–263).
  - Belief about control is itself a cause: "If addicts think of the addiction as resulting in behavior that is quite outside their control, they will be far less motivated to try to control it" (p. 263, crediting Bandura).
  - Substance addicts are irrational twice over: frequently akratic, and subject to "the malfunctioning of a normally rational system for creating intrinsic desires" (p. 265). Behavioural addicts get only the first.
- **Quotes**:
  - "it is one thing to say that people cannot control their mesolimbic dopamine system, and quite another to say that they cannot control how it influences their actions." (p. 241)
  - "The experience of self-control that everyone has at certain moments is a veridical one: self-control is a real phenomenon" (p. 242)
  - "We should thus not be thinking of addictive desires as things that are impossible to resist, but as things that are very difficult to resist." (p. 242)
  - Footnote 7 to that sentence: "Could they sometimes be truly irresistible? It seems rash to rule that out, although it is hard to be sure quite what the claim means: That no incentive would overcome it? That no incentive could?" (p. 242)
  - "Addiction results from the malfunction of the first; insofar as it does not result in a complete loss of agency, that is thanks to the second." (p. 265; "the first" is the system that regulates desires, "the second" the one that controls which desires are acted on)
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The "more active control on the part of the agent" is, for the authors, prefrontal and wholly natural. The phrase is open to the Map's reading and gives it no evidence.
- **Caution for the article**: their account of self-control says it is "depleted by various factors including stress, fatigue, and its prior exercise" (p. 262). That is the ego-depletion resource model the akrasia page already records as weakened by Hagger et al. 2016. The two-system structure does not depend on it, but the article should not carry the depletion claim across unmarked.

### Heyman (2013), "Addiction and Choice: Theory and New Data"
- **URL**: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3644798/ ; doi 10.3389/fpsyt.2013.00031
- **Type**: Paper, *Frontiers in Psychiatry* 4:31. **Read in full text.** This is the author's own article-length restatement of the 2009 book.
- **Key points**:
  - Most people who meet criteria for dependence on an illicit drug stop by about age 30, usually without professional help; the correlates of quitting are legal, economic and familial.
  - Half-lives of dependence from the survey data he fits: cocaine about 4 years, marijuana 6, alcohol 16.
  - He does not claim addiction is rational. It is voluntary and suboptimal, as the matching law, melioration and hyperbolic discounting predict.
  - He states the methodological pitfalls himself: those in remission may relapse after interview, and survey rosters may over-represent quitters.
- **Quotes**:
  - "the correlates of quitting are the correlates of choice not compulsion."
  - "addiction is not compulsive drug use, but it also is not rational drug use."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. Heyman's choice is behaviourist choice: a pattern governed by quantitative laws. It is not libertarian or agent-causal choice, and the article must not borrow his word as if it were.

### Heyman (2009), *Addiction: A Disorder of Choice*
- **Type**: Book, Harvard UP, doi 10.4159/9780674053991. **Not reached**; Crossref metadata confirms title, subtitle, publisher. Holton and Berridge's footnotes attribute the remission argument to ch. 4 (drawn "from examination of national population surveys—not just surveys of addicts") and the failure-to-think-about-the-future model to ch. 6. ### Pickard (2017), "Responsibility without Blame for Addiction"
- **URL**: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5486507 ; doi 10.1007/s12152-016-9295-2
- **Type**: Paper, *Neuroethics* 10(1), 169–180. **Read in full text.**
- **Key points**:
  - Evidence for choice: remission in the late twenties and early thirties, cost-sensitivity, contingency management, laboratory choices of money over drug.
  - A second argument that is conceptual: recovery through agency presupposes the agency.
  - Responsibility and blame come apart. Clinical practice holds patients responsible and accountable while withholding "affective blame".
  - Agency can be diminished without being abolished.
- **Quotes**:
  - "Agency needs to exist to be mobilized"
  - "reduction is not extinction: choices may be limited and control hard to achieve, without either being nullified."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral on metaphysics. Directly relevant to the Map's deferral of desert: Pickard supplies a worked case where classification (responsible) does not fix the response (blame).

### Henden, Melberg & Røgeberg (2013), "Addiction: Choice or Compulsion?"
- **URL**: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3736117/ ; doi 10.3389/fpsyt.2013.00077
- **Type**: Paper, *Frontiers in Psychiatry* 4:77. **Read in part** (abstract, introduction, dual-process section, conclusion).
- **Key points**:
  - They concede the incentive evidence defeats the strong disease view: "Irresistible desires, however, should not be affected by the presence of competing incentives."
  - They keep compulsion by detaching it from irresistibility, on the ground that clinical practice already classifies behaviour as compulsive without that condition.
  - Their mechanism is dual-process: a drug-oriented type-1 process shapes "the content and locus of attention" of the agent's type-2 processes.
- **Quote**: "behavior can be compulsive even if it is not caused by irresistible desires."
- **Tenet alignment**: Neutral. The attentional mechanism is the closest contact in this literature with the Map's attention-based gradient.

### Levy (2014), "Addiction as a disorder of belief"
- **URL**: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3991824/ ; doi 10.1007/s10539-014-9434-2
- **Type**: Paper, *Biology & Philosophy* 29(3), 337–355. **Read in part** (abstract, introduction, judgement-shift passages).
- **Key points**:
  - Loss of control is real and runs through judgement: cue-driven prediction error presses the addict's all-things-considered judgement to shift, and the shifted judgement is unstable.
  - He credits Ainslie (2001) with the judgement-shift idea and calls it "very much a minority view".
  - He suggests ordinary weakness of will may work the same way, and leans on ego-depletion studies for that step (same caution as above).
- **Quote**: "addicts lose control over their actions by losing control, first, of their beliefs"
- **Tenet alignment**: In tension with the selection reading as stated; see the debate below.

### Gorman (2023), "What is the Difference between Weakness of Will and Compulsion?"
- **Type**: Paper, *Journal of the American Philosophical Association* 9(1), 37–52, doi 10.1017/apa.2021.43. **Abstract only** (Crossref). Metadata matches the akrasia page's reference. Crossref's issued date is 2022-04-22 (online first); the issue is 2023.
- **Key point from the abstract**: the orthodoxy she rejects is that the difference "is a matter of the resistibility of an agent's effective motivation". Her alternative: agents "would upon reflection give some conative weight to acting on their weak-willed desires for some aim other than to extinguish them, but not to their compulsive desires."

## Major Positions

All are naturalistic; none bears on dualism directly. Sources are as read above.

- **Strong disease model (literally irresistible desire).** Named by every paper read as the view to reject; no primary defence was read. It is the only position on which the akrasia page's compulsion clause applies cleanly, so if it fails, that clause has little to bite on in addiction.
- **Two-system middle path (Holton and Berridge).** A malfunctioning desire-formation system plus an intact, effortful, fallible control system. Structurally the most hospitable to the Map: the selector would sit with the second system and select against sensitized wanting. The authors give no reason to think that system non-physical.
- **Choice models (Heyman; Pickard).** Use is voluntary, incentive-sensitive and suboptimal. Supports [[moral-responsibility]] L77, with no commitment about who or what chooses.
- **Compulsion without irresistibility (Henden et al.; Watson, abstract only).** Compulsion names graded, compromised decision-making. This turns the akrasia page's binary into the continuum the gradient already assumes.
- **Judgement-shift (Levy; Ainslie as source).** The addict acts on a judgement that cues have shifted. The hardest case for the Map: it joins Holton's resolution-abandonment weakness of will and removes the gap between judgement and act that the selection reading describes.
- **Attributability by reflective weight (Gorman).** The weakness/compulsion line is drawn by what the agent would on reflection give weight to, with resistibility playing no part. This offers the Map a classification that does not wait on the resistibility question.

## Key Debates

### Does incentive-sensitivity refute compulsion?
- **Sides**: Heyman and Pickard say yes; Henden et al. say it refutes only irresistibility; Holton and Berridge say it shows a control system at work on a pathological desire.
- **Core disagreement**: whether "compulsive" means "could not have been otherwise under any incentive" or "produced by a compromised process".
- **Current state**: ongoing. Holton and Berridge's footnote 7 doubts the irresistibility claim is even well defined.

### Where does the failure occur: in control, or in judgement?
- **Sides**: Holton and Berridge place it between craving and control and say addicts "will frequently be akratic". Levy places it in belief.
- **Core disagreement**: whether the relapsing addict acts against a concurrent better judgement.
- **Why it matters for the Map**: the selection reading treats akrasia as selection diverging from evaluation. On Levy's account evaluation moved first and selection followed it faithfully. The Map then has to say whether the selector is answerable for the shift, which is an attention question (what was kept in view while the cue pressed), or concede that this class of failure is upstream of selection.

### Does the theory of addiction change the addiction?
- **Sides**: Holton and Berridge (via Bandura) and Pickard both hold that believing one's use is outside one's control reduces the motivation to control it.
- **Why it matters for the Map**: a first-person belief about agency has downstream effects on behaviour. This is ordinary belief-desire causation and shows nothing about non-physical causation. It does mean that the Map's classification of addiction is a claim with practical consequences for readers who are addicted, and should be written with that in view.

## Bearing on the Three Map Pages

1. **Akrasia page (classification precedes desert).** The literature supports keeping the dependency and changing its shape. Classification is graded, and the pure-compulsion pole may be nearly empty for addiction. The conscious wish to stop then becomes weak-to-moderate evidence about control, scaled by how incentive-responsive the behaviour is, where the page now treats it as either evidence or none.
2. **Responsibility gradient.** Stability is the right primary home: Holton and Berridge say addictive urges "can be controlled, at least for a short while". Scope is a second, separate route (Henden's attentional capture; Heyman's ch. 6 as reported). The article can state that addiction degrades stability and scope while leaving initiation largely intact. That last clause is an inference from the incentive evidence, and should be flagged as such.
3. **Wanting-liking fork.** Holton and Berridge add what the fork page lacks: the action-theoretic profile of sensitized "wanting", a state between desire and intention that bypasses deliberation unless checked. Their footnote 45 adds that incentive salience can operate unconsciously and still be open to self-control when "the subject doesn't see the need to exert it". For the Map, craving is therefore a poor guide to felt value, and the page's caution that sensitization is one theory among several still applies.
4. **Frankfurt's unwilling addict.** Frankfurt's three addicts share one craving stipulated to be effective. The empirical picture here suggests the "struggling helplessly" figure is closer to James's cannonball drinker than to clinical addicts. The article should treat him as a thought-experimental limit case.

## Potential Article Angles

1. **Addiction as the case that breaks the binary.** Lead with the claim that addiction is neither weakness nor compulsion on the akrasia page's terms, then place it on the gradient (stability first, scope second). State plainly that the placement is the Map's interpretation of a naturalistic literature, a lens as on the akrasia page, with no evidential weight for Tenet 3.
2. **Two systems, one selector.** Read Holton and Berridge's control system as the locus the selection model cares about, then let Levy's judgement-shift stand as the objection, answered or conceded in the open.

Either way the article should carry Pickard's separation of responsibility from blame into the Map's deferred desert question, and should mark the ego-depletion dependence in both Holton and Berridge and Levy.

- Sripada (2018; 2022) and Wallace (1999) are metadata only. Sripada's unreliability account looks like the nearest rival to Holton and Berridge and should be read before the article commits to the two-system framing.
- Gorman 2023 and Watson are abstract only; no body-text quotation from either is licensed.
