## Executive Summary

Butler's objection is that memory presupposes identity and so cannot constitute it. Reid separated memory as *evidence* of identity from what identity consists in.

Shoemaker's quasi-memory (1970) drops the condition that the rememberer had the experience. Ordinary memory then becomes a special case, and a memory criterion is no longer circular. Penelhum reached the same idea the same year as "retrocognition", and Parfit spread it.

The critics (Evans, McDowell, Collins, Schechtman, Slors) deny that quasi-memory is intelligible apart from memory. McDowell argues this from an anti-Cartesian standpoint.

For the Map, the result is compatibility without support. The posited subject loses no premise, but memory cannot certify it, and P-I4 commits the Map to quasi-memory being coherent.

## The Coiner Finding {#coiner}

**Term: Shoemaker 1970. Concept, the same year and apparently independently: Penelhum 1970, as "retrocognition". Populariser: Parfit 1984.**

**Shoemaker presents the term as his own:**
- "Quasi-remembering, as I shall use the term, includes remembering as a special case" (reprint p. 24) **[S]**.
- "I shall use the expressions 'quasi-remember' and 'quasi-memory' when speaking of…" (p. 35) **[S]**.

**Parfit credits Shoemaker.**
- §80 note 13 reads "I follow SHOEMAKER (2)." (p. 516) **[F]**.
- His bibliography gives SHOEMAKER (2) as "'Persons and Their Pasts', American Philosophical Quarterly 7, 1970" **[F]**. The OCR garbles the label, but its place between (1) and (3) fixes it.
- The in-text marker is lost in the OCR. The note sits between n. 12 (Butler, p. 219) and n. 14 (p. 221), which puts it at the definition on p. 220.

**Secondary attributions agree:**
- McDowell: Parfit "follows Sydney Shoemaker in offering Locke conceptual material to escape it" (p. 366) **[S]**.

- Bernecker: "Shoemaker and Parfit suggest solving the circularity objection by substituting the concept of quasi‐memory" **[A]**.

## Key Sources

### Butler, "Of Personal Identity" (1736) [F]
Appended to *The Analogy of Religion*; Gutenberg #53346 (Malcolm ed.); reprinted in Perry (ed.) 1975, 99–105 (per Slors **[F]**).
- **Identity cannot be defined**: "all attempts to define would but perplex it".
- **Circularity**: "And one should really think it self-evident, that consciousness of personal identity presupposes, and therefore cannot constitute, personal identity; any more than knowledge, in any other case, can constitute truth, which it presupposes."
- **Forgetting**: if memory made identity, "a person has not existed a single moment, nor done one action, but what he can remember".
- **Substance**: "The identity of these, therefore, cannot subsist with diversity of substance."
- **"Thirdly"**: consciousness of being the same person proves the same substance, "since the same property cannot be transferred from one substance to another."
- **Tenet alignment**: this is the historical Simple View, close to the Map's posit. Its weak point is the non-transfer premise.

### Locke, *Essay* II.xxvii.13 [F]
Gutenberg #10615. Numbered §15 there; II.xxvii.13 in Nidditch and in Parfit.
- Locke raises the transfer case: one substance might "have represented to it, as done by itself, what IT never did, and was perhaps done by some other agent".
- He cannot exclude it "from the nature of things" and rests on "the goodness of God".
- His concession: "if the same consciousness … can be transferred from one thinking substance to another, it will be possible that two thinking substances may make but one person."

### Reid, *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1785), III.4 and III.6 [F]
Woozley abridged ed. (Macmillan 1941).
- **Indivisible subject**: "A person is something indivisible, and is what Leibniz calls a monad" (p. 203).
- **Evidence**: "my memory testifies not only that this was done, but that it was done by me who now remember it" (p. 203).
- **Evidence versus constitution**:
  - "it is not my remembering any action of mine that makes me to be the person who did it" (p. 204).
  - Locke's doctrine is that "personal identity is confounded with the evidence which we have of our personal identity" (p. 214).
- **Transitivity**: the brave officer (p. 213).
- **Tenet alignment**: Reid's indivisible subject is the closest historical analogue to P-I4. Quasi-memory spares his metaphysical claim and attacks his evidential one.

### Shoemaker, "Persons and Their Pasts" (1970) [S]
*APQ* 7(4): 269–285; reprinted in *Identity, Cause, and Mind* (CUP 1984; OUP 2003), 19–48. Pages are reprint pages.
- **The weaker condition**: quasi-remembering "is subject to a weaker previous awareness condition" than remembering (p. 24).
- **Causal requirement**: the quasi-memory must "stand in an appropriate causal relationship to, a past cognitive and sensory state of someone or other" (p. 34).
- **Reply to Butler** (Butler quoted at p. 41): "Such objections assume that remembering involves the satisfaction of the strong previous awareness condition, and they can be avoided on the assumption that the previous awareness condition is weaker than this, e.g., is that given for quasi-remembering in section IV" (p. 42).
- **Remembering from the inside**: "it seems logically possible that one should remember an action or experience from the inside (i.e., quasi-remember it)" without its being one's own (p. 45; paraphrase after the parenthesis). Memory keeps only "de facto" immunity (p. 46).

### Penelhum, *Survival and Disembodied Existence* (1970) [S]
Ch. 7, "'Bodily Transfer'", on Locke's cobbler and prince.
- Bodily transfer is not a mandatory reading of the tale (p. 88). Retrocognition, each man knowing the other's past, is the alternative (pp. 85, 88).
- Penelhum keeps "the priority of the bodily criterion" (p. 67).

### Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* (1984), §§80–81 [F]
archive.org OCR.
- **Butler, first reading** (Parfit's voicing): "It is part of our concept of memory that we can remember only our own experiences" (p. 220).
- **The definition**:
  - "To answer this objection, we can define a wider concept, quasi-memory": I seem to remember having an experience, someone did have it, and "my apparent memory is causally dependent, in the right kind of way, on that past experience" (p. 220).
  - "ordinary memories are a sub-class of quasi-memories" (p. 220).
- **First-person mode**:
  - Jane's apparent memories "may come to her in what Peacocke calls the first-person mode of presentation" (p. 221).
  - Ownership "is a separable belief" (p. 222).
- **Reply**: "Since the continuity of quasi-memory does not presuppose personal identity, it may be part of what constitutes personal identity" (p. 222).
- **Butler, second reading** (Parfit's voicing): "In memory we are directly aware of our own identity through time, and aware that this is a separate, further fact" (p. 222).
  - Answer: "This is not a point that can be argued" (p. 223).
  - The relay: "As both Locke and Kant argued, there might be a series of such entities that were psychologically continuous. Memories might be passed from one to the next like a baton in a relay race" (p. 223).
  - n. 17 cites Locke II.xxvii.13 and "Kant, … 342, especially footnote a" (Kemp Smith tr.).
- **Replicas**: "her Replica will have full quasi-memories of our shared history" (p. 295).
- **Evans** (n. 15, **[2nd]**): Evans denies that identity-neutral memory could give knowledge. Parfit replies that Jane, who knows the cause, gains it.

### McDowell, "Reductionism and the First Person" (1997) [S]
*Reading Parfit* (ed. Dancy), 230–50; reprinted in *Mind, Value, and Reality* (Harvard 1998), 359–382. Pages are reprint pages.
- **Circularity is no defect**: "we ought to pause before we accept Butler's assumption that circularity would be a problem for what Locke intends" (p. 367).
- **Derivativeness**: "it is disastrous to conclude, from the fact that we can arrive at it by dropping a requirement, that what it is for the second notion to be applicable is intelligible otherwise than in terms of an appearance" (p. 374).
- **Fission**: he voices the opponent, "Here, surely, it may be said, we have quasi-memory in the form in which Reductionists want it" (p. 375), and then resists it.
- **The persisting subject**: it is "not a peculiar Cartesian item, but a person, of whose continued life that continuity is, precisely, an aspect" (p. 361).
- **Tenet alignment**: anti-Cartesian; no ally for a dualist.

### Slors (2001) [F]
- **On Butler**: "it is likely that Butler's point was merely to argue against the conflation of epistemic and metaphysical criteria of identity" (p. 187).
- **Objection**: "Q-memory is impossible to square with a holism of the mental" (p. 190).
- **Proposal**: memory is "only indicative rather than constitutive" of continuity (p. 187).

### Abstract-level sources [A]
- **Roache 2006**: "From my quasi-memory of doing x, I cannot infer that I did x; but I can infer that somebody did x."
- **Roache 2016**:
  - Klein and Nichols took R.B. to show "that the conception of quasi-memory that underpins many memory-based accounts is inadequate".
  - Roache finds their "characterization of R.B.'s experience is implausible."
- **Buford 2009**: answers McDowell using Coliva's two types of IEM.
- **Collins 1997**: apparent memories meeting the weak q-memory definition "turn out not to involve remembering anything at all."
- **Northoff 2000**: rejects Schechtman's "principal inseparability" and "principal indistinguishability" arguments. Quasi-memories are "compatible with current empirical knowledge".
- **Bernecker 2009**: quasi-memory "is indeed coherent and is not a derivative from the concept of memory".
- **Dranseika 2020**: "neither the factivity constraint nor the strong previous awareness condition is a necessary part of ordinary use of 'remembering'".
- **Nichols 2017**: memory as *evidence* of identity "fits naturally with animalist views".

## Key Debates

**1. What the circularity objection is.** There are three readings:
- (a) **Conceptual**: memory entails identity.
- (b) **Evidence confused with constitution**: Reid; Slors on Butler.
- (c) **Direct awareness of a further fact**: Parfit's second reading.

Quasi-memory answers only (a). Reading (b) is common ground. Parfit meets (c) with the relay, and (c) is where the Map's interest lies.

**2. Coherence.**
- *No*: Evans, McDowell, Collins, Schechtman, Slors, and Klein & Nichols.
- *Yes*: Parfit, Roache, Bernecker, Northoff and Dranseika.
- *State*: open.

**3. The first-person mode.** Parfit grants it to quasi-memories but makes ownership separable. R.B., with his own memories and no felt mine-ness, is the reverse dissociation; Roache contests that reading.

## Map Relevance (RQ3, at P-M1 tiers) {#map-relevance}

**The phenomenon.** No actual case is established; the cases are thought experiments. Status: a conceptual possibility whose coherence is disputed.

**Bearing on the posited subject: compatible, no support either way.**
- The Map posits diachronic persistence as "a real further fact" (`tenets/background-commitments` L32).
- The [[kants-paralogisms-and-the-maps-subject#posit-not-paralogism|Kant page]] holds that the Map "*posits* its subject rather than *inferring* it" (L35).
- Quasi-memory removes only a route the Map does not take: the inference from memory to a persisting subject (Reid's "testifies… by me"; Butler's "Thirdly").
- It does not show the subject absent, and it leaves both Parfit's constitution claim and the Map's further fact unsettled.

**The coherence debate gives the Map no ally.** McDowell rejects quasi-memory on behalf of "a person, of whose continued life that continuity is, precisely, an aspect". Nichols ties memory-as-evidence to animalism. Defeating quasi-memory would favour an embodied or animal subject, not a non-physical one. *[Map reconstruction]*

**The Map is committed to coherence.** *[Map reconstruction]*
- P-I4 makes a copy "a numerically distinct subject however complete the psychological continuity".
- The copy's accurate, rightly caused apparent memories are, in Parfit's words, "full quasi-memories" (p. 295).
- If quasi-memory were incoherent, those states would be either genuine memories, which would make the copy the original against P-I4, or confabulations, which ignores how they were caused.
- So the Map sides with Shoemaker and Parfit on coherence and against them on what continuity constitutes.

**A defeater for one Map inference: personal-identity L101.**
- The line speaks of "the 'pastness quale' marking a memory as genuinely *yours*".
- A quasi-memory from the inside would carry the same quale, since Parfit allows it the first-person mode, with ownership "a separable belief".
- The quale can *present* a memory as one's own; it cannot *certify* that it is.
- L101's argument from indexical non-recurrence survives once "genuinely" goes.
- This is the Kant page's [[kants-paralogisms-and-the-maps-subject#three-exposures|first exposure]] in the diachronic case. Felt mine-ness is not evidence of a persisting subject.

**The Kant page's third exposure.**
- The elastic-ball series is a chain of quasi-memory, and Parfit's relay passage cites it.
- Butler's "Thirdly" stands or falls with "the same property cannot be transferred from one substance to another". Locke left that premise open, and Kant's footnote denies it.
- The Kant page already shows that causal history individuates only "the already-posited particular".
- Quasi-memory adds that memory, however accurate and from the inside, cannot tell which particular had the experience.

**De facto immunity.** Memory stays defeasible evidence of persistence in the actual world (Shoemaker p. 46), but it does not separate the Map's posit from its rivals, which matches P-I1's grade D.

**Open on purpose.** Quasi-memory sharpens the question of what makes a subject one subject over time, on which the aspect ontology is silent (Kant page L90). This note leaves the tension between aspects and a persisting subject open. It makes no claim about whether consciousness, or all consciousness, is causally efficacious.
