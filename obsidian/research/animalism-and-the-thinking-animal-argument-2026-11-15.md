## Executive Summary

Animalism holds that each of us is a human organism and persists as organisms do. Its main argument is the thinking-animal argument: the animal in your chair has your brain and so thinks; you are the thinker in your chair; so you are the animal. Anyone who denies the conclusion must deny that the animal exists, deny that it thinks, or accept two thinkers who share every thought and cannot tell which of them is which. The current SEP survey (Blatti, revised 23 February 2026) and the SEP personal-identity entry (Olson, revised 30 June 2023) both present animalists as accepting the counterintuitive transplant verdict because these alternatives look worse, and because animalism fits every actual survival judgment and our having been foetuses.

The reviewer is right that **the Map's tenets do not refute animalism**. Tenet 1 is neutral between substance and property dualism by its own definition. A human animal that bears irreducible phenomenal properties satisfies it, and some animalists even give the animal an immaterial form. The conflict enters one layer down, with the substance-leaning posit ([[where-the-substance-commitment-enters]]; Posit One in [[tenets/background-commitments]]) and with P-I3, which holds that subject boundaries cannot be read off physical structure. The Map's strongest available reply is Lowe's: the self is a psychological substance whose persistence conditions differ from the organism's and are primitive. That reply denies the argument's second premise and therefore owes Olson's "Question 1 (for dualists)": "Why is it impossible for a material thing to think?" (Olson 2021). The best dualist counter-pressure is animalism's own multiplication problem, which Olson rates as worse than the transplant objections (Olson 2008). The Map has declined to borrow that problem as an argument for its own view (see [[manyism]]), and it returns against souls (Hershenov 2023) as the Map's unpaid pairing law.

## The Thinking-Animal Argument at Full Strength

Blatti's formulation (SEP §3.1, raw):

> (P1) Presently sitting in your chair is a human animal. (P2) The human animal sitting in your chair is thinking. (P3) You are the thinking being sitting in your chair. (C) Therefore, the human animal sitting in your chair is you.

- **Names**: "thinking animal argument" (Olson 1997, 2003), "too many minds objection" (S. Shoemaker 1999), "two lives objection" (S. Campbell 2006), "too many thinkers problem" (Parfit 2012: 7) (SEP).
- **Origin, a coiner guard**: SEP says the argument "was developed by Snowdon (1990: 91), Carter (1988), McDowell (1997: 237), and Ayers (1991, vol. 2: 283)" and "has since been sharpened and popularized by Olson". **Do not present Olson as its originator.**
- **Why each premise resists denial**: denying P1 needs something like idealism. Denying P2 means saying that human animals do not think while porpoises do, a view the SEP says strains "empirical credibility and/or depend[s] on fairly sophisticated metaphysical machinery". Denying P3 posits two thinkers, which raises practical, epistemic ("how do you determine which of these beings you are?"), linguistic and ontological questions.
- **The epistemic form** (SEP Personal Identity §6, raw): psychological-continuity views raise the question "Am I a nonanimal that would go with its brain if that organ were transplanted, or an animal that would stay behind with an empty head?" Here "there seem to be no grounds on which to answer". The duplicating-machine analogy carries this form.
- **Olson's own statement of the trilemma** (Olson 2015, raw accepted manuscript): if I were not an animal, then either "(1) there is no animal here", or "(2) that animal is not thinking", or "(3) I am not the only thinker here". "The case for animalism lies in the repugnance of these alternatives." He adds that the most common escape, denying that human animals think, "could reasonably be described as a sort of substance dualism. It is not Descartes’ dualism of mind and matter, but a dualism of mind and life." **This matters for the Map.** On Olson's own framing, the line that animalism cannot cross is the claim that no organism can think, which is a substance-dualist claim. Property dualism falls on animalism's side of that line.
- **What else animalists weigh** (SEP Personal Identity §7, raw): "that we appear to be organisms", "that it’s hard to say what sort of nonorganisms we might be", "that our being organisms would avoid the too-many-thinkers problem", and that "every actual case in which we take someone to survive or perish is a case where an organism does so". Psychological views "conflict with the appearance that each of us was once a foetus". Snowdon (2014: 234) argues that the transplant intuition "may be less compelling than it first appears", because a brain transplant might be "metaphysically analogous to a liver transplant".
- **Further arguments** (SEP Animalism §3.3): Blatti's (2012) "animal ancestors argument" (if we are not animals, neither were our ancestors, against evolutionary theory); Thornton and Bailey's (2021) "argument from emotion"; Bailey and Pruss's (2021) "argument from beasts". Snowdon (2014: 106) calls animalism "the default conception of ourselves".

## Varieties of Animalism

- **Organic** (life processes necessary and sufficient: Olson, van Inwagen) vs **somatic** (life-apt structure suffices, so the dead animal can persist: Ayers, D. Mackie, Carter, Feldman) (SEP §1.2). This disagreement is "not merely verbal".
- **Weak, strong and new animalism** (Olson 2015, raw). Weak animalism is the bare claim that we are animals. Strong animalism adds further claims (essentially animals, brute-physical persistence, animal as a fundamental kind). "We might call the conjunction of weak animalism with the denial of the further claims new animalism." On one version, a cerebrum transplant "moves an organism from one head to another", so "The transplant objection would not apply." Olson himself doubts that it works ("it will be hard to take new animalism seriously until" someone supplies a metaphysics of organisms that makes sense of it). He wrote "I don’t know whether anyone has ever actually accepted new animalism". By 2023 the SEP Personal Identity entry attributes it to "Madden 2016, Noonan 2021; see also Langford 2014, Olson 2015: 102–106".
- **Hylomorphic animalism** (Thornton 2019; Toner 2011a). These Aristotelian-Thomist animalists hold that "the animal each of us is comprises two parts", a body and "its form (or ‘soul’), i.e., the immaterial thing". Whether such views count as animalism is contested; Hershenov places them between animalism and neo-Lockeanism (SEP §2.3). **For the Map this is the clearest evidence that animalism is compatible with a non-physical constituent.**
- **Current literature** (SEP 2026 bibliography): formulation disputes in Duncan (2021, 2024), Bailey, Thornton and van Elswyk (2021) and Bailey and van Elswyk (2021, "Generic Animalism"); Madden (2024) *Personal Identity and the Self*; Hermida (2023, 2024); Rooney (2025) and Skrzypek (2024) on hylomorphism; Olson (2023) on partial twinning. Leads only: these entries were seen in the bibliography, and none was read.

## Main Rivals and Objections (materialist and otherwise)

- **Constitution view** (Baker 2000, 2007): persons are constituted by animals without being identical to them, and have the first-person perspective essentially. Baker's "Key Distinction" between derivative and nonderivative property-possession lets her reject P3 as ambiguous: "the constituted person who thinks nonderivatively and the constituting human animal who thinks derivatively" (SEP §3.2). Critics: Zimmerman 2002, Olson 2007 ch. 3, Hershenov 2009.
- **Personal pronoun revisionism** (Noonan 1998, 2001, 2010): "first-person thoughts and utterances refer only to persons, not to animals". This concedes two thinkers and blocks only the epistemic problem.
- **Shoemaker** (1984, 1999, 2004): no animal can think, because "mental properties ... can belong only to things having psychological persistence conditions". This denies P2 by way of functionalism.
- **Embodied-part views** (McMahan 2002, Parfit 2012, Hudson 2001): we are brains or parts of brains, so the animal thinks only derivatively.
- **Multiplication objections**: the *rival-candidates* problem (Zimmerman 2008) and the *thinking-parts* problem (heads, brains, "right-leg complements"). The standard animalist reply is van Inwagen's organicism: only organisms compose. The SEP adds that "Olson (2008: 38–42) is, however, quick to acknowledge that this riposte will be welcomed only by those antecedently persuaded of van Inwagen’s view". Olson's own replies (raw, Abstracta 2008) go further: "The rival-candidates problem worries me a lot more than the usual, familiar objections to my view", "I don’t have a good solution to the rival-candidates problem", and his sparse-ontology answer is "not a very good answer to the rival-candidates problem, but at least it’s an answer". He also says "Any sensible account of what we are faces its own version of the rival-candidates problem." **Dicephalus** (McMahan 2002: 35) is "two people but only one animal". The Map's own "conjoined twins share bodies" (individuation L59) is this objection. Animalist replies: Blatti 2007; Olson 2014, 2023; Campbell and McMahan 2016.
- **Remnant-person problem** (Johnston 2007; Olson 2016): a detached, functioning cerebrum looks like a person, which forces the animalist to say that cutting creates a person and implanting destroys one.
- **Transfer objections**: brain transplant, brain-state transfer, teletransport.

## Dualist Replies and What Each Costs

These names began as leads from the driver. Each was confirmed against a primary or reliable secondary text; the status of each is given here and in the ledger.

1. **Deny P2: organisms do not think; psychological substances do.** *Lowe* (verified, primary: Lowe 2012, raw). Lowe calls his view "non-Cartesian substance dualism". A human person is "a psychological substance", not "a living organism of any kind, even though a human person’s body is clearly just such an organism". "Persons are substances, as are their bodies. But the two are not identical substances, for persons and bodies have different persistence conditions". Since the self is simple, "there can be no diachronic criterion of identity which grounds its persistence through time", which makes its persistence primitive. Applied to animals: "mental states necessarily attach to psychological subjects which are not to be identified with their biological bodies", so "there are ‘animal souls’" (n. 25). The SEP Personal Identity entry lists "Lowe 1996: 1" among those holding that "There simply are no thinking animals". **This is the reply closest to the Map's own position.** It supplies exactly what the reviewer demands, persistence conditions that differ from the animal's, and its primitive persistence matches P-I1's haecceity ground and [[haecceity]]. *Cost:* Olson 2021 (verified, raw). If the brain's activity causes the soul to think, "Why is it that the soul thinks and not the body?" "Question 1 (for dualists): Why is it impossible for a material thing to think?" Olson argues that no argument for dualism answers this ("Showing that we (who think) are immaterial does nothing to explain why our bodies don’t think"). He also presses the remote-control objection: general anaesthesia stops all thought, which "would be baffling" if thinking went on in the soul. The Map's natural retort is that materialists owe "Question 2 (for materialists): How is it possible for a material thing to think?", which is the hard problem. Olson anticipates this and answers that the retort "misrepresents the dialectical situation", since material things evidently "produce thinking". [[anaesthesia-and-the-consciousness-interface]] does not cite Olson 2021, and it should be checked against that objection (see Deferred Items).
2. **Compound (composite) dualism: we are soul-plus-organism, thinking derivatively through the soul.** *Swinburne* (verified via SEP Personal Identity, "compound things made up of an immaterial soul and a biological organism (Swinburne 1984: 21)", and Olson 2021, raw, which says Swinburne "says that the compound thinks only in the derivative sense of having a thinking soul as a part"). *Cost:* Olson (2001, "A Compound of Two Substances"). As summarised by Zimmerman 2005 (raw), the view leaves it unclear how the soul could "think and not be a person?", and if both think, "each person includes two thinkers, neither of which can distinguish itself from the other". The too-many-thinkers problem thus turns against the compound view. Olson 2001 itself was not read; the summary is Zimmerman's.
3. **Emergent dualism: the brain generates a new subject.** *Hasker* (verified via Zimmerman 2005, raw: a sufficiently complex nervous system "automatically also generates a nonphysical substance to be the subject of that consciousness", an "emergent substance"; the Map's [[emergent-dualism]] page holds the primary detail). *Zimmerman* adopts it (verified, raw, Zimmerman 2017: he accepts the label "dualism" and calls Hasker's emergent dualism the kind he finds "most believable"; "there are human souls and animal souls"). Zimmerman's argument runs the multiplication problem in the dualist's favour. Organisms and brains are "vague things, not unlike rivers or clouds"; "I find it hard to believe that I am a vague object, for which there are many equally good candidates". If consciousness is irreducible, "It would be quaint to suppose that the laws of nature governing this new aspect of the world are careful to select objects of sizes and shapes that interest us". So "the materialist who admits that consciousness is something special ... should not expect any standard sort of materialism to be true". **This is the argument that moves from Tenet 1's irreducibility to the rejection of animalism.** Its own author rates it low: "If this were the most that could be said for dualism, one should probably simply settle for what George Graham calls “Strong Ontic Ignorance”" (2017). In 2005 he says such arguments support "nothing stronger than" ontic ignorance. *Cost:* Hershenov 2023 (verified, raw abstract): "parallel problems plague Zimmerman’s emergent dualism, there are too many souls produced where we want just one". **Map guard:** [[manyism]] already says that Zimmerman's use of the too-many-minds problem "does not make it one the Map can borrow". Report the argument; do not adopt it. *Hasker himself*: no direct Hasker reply to Olson's thinking-animal argument was found. **Do not attribute one.**
4. **Pronoun revisionism and derivative thinking.** These are available in principle: "I" in the animal's mouth would refer to the subject, or the animal would think only through its coupled subject. But Olson 2021 notes that no dualist wants the body to think as well: "That would give us two thinkers for each human being, one material and one immaterial." Derivative thinking via *parthood* (Swinburne, Baker) is unavailable to the Map, whose subject is not a part of the organism. Derivative thinking via *pairing* would need the pairing law the Map does not have (P-SC2).
5. **The argument from vagueness and fission.** *Chisholm and Swinburne* (verified via Zimmerman 2005, raw): "Stipulations about whether a person survives a certain borderline adventure are bootless if the person is in fact an immaterial substance whose identity over time is an all-or-nothing affair." Zimmerman's verdict is the same low ceiling (ontic ignorance). Zimmerman 2008 (raw) also reports Olson saying that Chisholm's version is "the most impressive sort of argument for dualism of which he knows", to which Zimmerman adds "I am inclined to agree — though I am looking for a better one".

## What Dualism Does and Does Not Refute: Tenet by Tenet

| Commitment | Tension with animalism? | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Tenet 1, Dualism (bare irreducibility) | **None** | The tenet's definition includes "the view that physical systems have irreducible phenomenal properties". A human animal bearing such properties satisfies it. Zimmerman 2005: property dualism allows that "persons are material objects with a nonphysical, mental ‘aspect’". Olson 2015 locates the incompatibility at "a sort of substance dualism", the claim that no organism thinks. Hylomorphic animalists even include an immaterial form. |

| Tenet 3, Bidirectional Interaction | None at the tenet level | An animal's irreducible mental properties could be causally efficacious (interactionist property dualism). The agency note requires "a persisting subject that exercises causal powers in its own right". An organism is a persisting substance, so the tenets' text does not exclude it. Whether any agent-causalist identifies the agent with the organism was not checked (gap). |
| Tenet 4, No Many Worlds | None directly | Animalism is neutral on Everett. The indexical objection presupposes a determinate subject (Posit One, P-I2), and an organism is determinate only up to the vagueness Zimmerman presses. | P-I3 (boundaries not readable off physical structure) | **Direct** | Animalism reads the boundaries off organisms. Dicephalus, the craniopagus case ([[subject-fusion]]) and split-brain cases ([[split-brain-consciousness]]) are where the two views would come apart. Animalists contest each case. |

## Binding Guards for Any Writer

1. **Stances.** Olson, Snowdon, DeGrazia, Blatti, van Inwagen and Merricks are animalists (SEP 2026 lists all six among animalism's proponents or advocates). Never enlist them for dualism. Olson's "Question 1" and his remote-control objection are objections to dualism. Lowe, Swinburne, Hasker and Zimmerman are dualists. Zimmerman is an emergent dualist, not a Cartesian.
2. **Coiners.** Snowdon named animalism (1991: 109). The thinking-animal argument was developed by Snowdon, Carter, McDowell and Ayers and "sharpened and popularized" by Olson. Olson named "new animalism" (2015) and doubts it. Madden and Noonan defend versions of it. "Strong Ontic Ignorance" is George Graham's phrase, quoted by Zimmerman.
3. **Report, don't borrow.** Zimmerman's too-many-candidates argument for dualism is reported, and the Map does not use it ([[manyism]] already says so). The Map's single-subject case rests on unity as primitive. Do not rewrite the fold so that the multiplication problem appears to support the Map.

The individuation page's own Relation to Site Perspective already concedes the property-dualist route ("Bare irreducibility leaves a property dualist free to individuate subjects by their physical bearers"). Fold A points to it rather than restating it.
5. **Quote status.** Every quotation in the folds was grep-verified in raw text: Olson 2021, PDF from the journal site; Olson 2008, the Abstracta issue PDF; Hershenov 2023, the TheoLogica PDF. ## Gaps in Research

- Olson 2001 ("A Compound of Two Substances"), Olson 2007 and Snowdon 2014 were not read directly. In particular, it was not confirmed whether Olson anywhere states in his own words that animalism is compatible with property dualism. The compatibility claim rests on Tenet 1's text, Zimmerman 2005, Olson 2015's "dualism of mind and life" framing, and the hylomorphic animalists.
- Zimmerman 2010 (*Aristotelian Society Supplementary Volume* 84(1)), the formal statement of his candidates argument, was not obtained; the 11-12 note has its Crossref metadata.
- No source was found that engages animalism from an interactionist quantum-interface position like the Map's. The connection between the pairing law and Hershenov's objection is the Map's own reconstruction.
- Whether any agent-causal theorist (e.g. O'Connor) identifies the agent with the organism was not checked. This bears on the Tenet 3 row.
- McKee (2025, "Dualism leads to Many Minds", *Synthese*) is a title-only lead from the 11-12 note. If it turns the too-many-minds problem against dualism, it would strengthen the Hershenov row.
