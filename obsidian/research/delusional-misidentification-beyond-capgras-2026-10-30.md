## Executive Summary

The Map's interest in this family is one lesson about recognition, and that lesson belongs in the void that makes the recognition claim. The per-syndrome clinical record is thin (case reports; Förstl et al. 1991 found 18 Frégoli, 11 intermetamorphosis and 17 reduplicative-paramnesia cases among 260 misidentifications). And topics has two slots, one already spoken for.

**Finding 1. The family does not share Capgras's Factor 1.** The affective-route account of Capgras (absent autonomic response to familiar faces) does not generalise. Breen, Caine, Coltheart, Hendy and Roberts (2000), with two mirrored-self cases, one reverse intermetamorphosis and one reduplicative paramnesia, report that their four cases "suggest that this particular deficit is not a critical factor in the development of other forms of DM". Coltheart, Menzies and Sutton (2010, Table 1, pp. 267–268) assign each delusion a different neuropsychological impairment: autonomic over-response for Frégoli (a hypothesis, not a finding: "no empirical studies of autonomic responsivity in these two delusions have been reported", p. 266), and two different impairments for mirrored-self misidentification, either "loss of knowledge about how mirrors work" or impaired face processing. Arnold Pick, who named reduplicative paramnesia, considered a familiarity account of it and rejected it in 1903: "The patients I have reported had 'sense of familiarity' as well as Volkelt's 'certainty of memory'", yet "they cannot identify one situation from the other" (p. 267).

**Finding 2. No recognition/felt-familiarity double dissociation is established within the family.** The tempting pairing, Capgras as too little familiarity and Frégoli as too much (Ramachandran & Blakeslee 1998, via carriers), fails on three counts. Frégoli's autonomic responses are essentially unmeasured ("Almost no detailed work of this type has been done with Fregoli cases", Langdon, Connaughton & Coltheart 2014, p. 628). Capgras and Frégoli co-occur ("Any co-occurrence of Fregoli and Capgras is inconsistent with Ramachandran and Blakeslee's hypothesis", p. 627; one clear case and three suggestive). And lesion network mapping finds "a similar pattern of connectivity across cases of hyper and hypo familiarity" (Darby et al. 2017). The cleanest dissociation in the neighbourhood is non-delusional. Vuilleumier, Mohr, Valenza, Wetzel and Landis (2003) describe "abnormal 'hyperfamiliarity' for unknown faces" that "was not accompanied by delusions", and title it "a double dissociation with prosopagnosia". A further complication is that "familiarity" names at least three different measures in this literature: autonomic response (skin conductance), task or fMRI familiarity (Darby's retrosplenial region), and reported feeling. Ellis and Lewis (2001) report that covert recognition "can be fractionated into autonomic and behavioural/cognitive types", and Garry Young (2009) argues that experiential states reported alike as lacking familiarity "are quite distinct".

**Finding 3. On the presented/judged axis, insight loss is the family norm and sits on the judged side; what is presented varies by syndrome.** In Frégoli the stranger's appearance is presented accurately (patients "are aware of the mismatch", Langdon et al. 2014, p. 619), and identity is attributed against it. Reduplicative paramnesia presents familiarity and certainty of memory and fails at identification across occasions (Pick). The mirror-agnosia route to mirrored-self misidentification is a knowledge deficit, not a presented anomaly. Pick's 1903 patient supplies an early partial-insight case: her "better power of judgment ... leads her to doubt the reality of these double events" (p. 267). Details at [[#insight-axis|The Presented/Judged Axis]].

**Originators verified**: Pick named reduplicative paramnesia ("I have chosen the term 'reduplicative paramnesia.'", 1903, p. 261, page image); Benson, Gardner and Meadows (1976) revived the term for a post-traumatic neurological syndrome ("here termed reduplicative paramnesia") and did not coin it. Courbon and Fail (1927) named the Frégoli syndrome after the actor and mimic Leopoldo Fregoli (via Langdon et al. 2014; original unread). Courbon and Tusques (1932) described intermetamorphosis (confirmed by Tusques's own bibliography). Christodoulou (1978) named the syndrome of subjective doubles. ## Per-Syndrome Findings {#per-syndrome}

Langdon, Connaughton and Coltheart (2014, Table 1, p. 622) give the most useful classification. It sorts person misidentifications by whether the real person is familiar (including oneself) or a stranger, and by what is mistakenly believed about physical and psychological identity. Their footnote 4 disclaims completeness. The syndromes below follow their rows, with reduplicative paramnesia and non-delusional hyperfamiliarity added.

### Capgras (baseline; the Map already covers it)
- **Phenomenology**: a person emotionally close is acknowledged as physically identical or very similar, but believed psychologically different: an impostor (Langdon et al. 2014, Table 1 row 1).
- **Factor 1**: disconnection of face recognition from the autonomic system (2010, Table 1); experienced on most accounts, not on the revisionist one ([[capgras-delusion-and-the-affective-recognition-channel#is-factor-1-experienced|Capgras §Is Factor 1 Experienced?]]).
- **Not re-examined here.**

### Frégoli {#fregoli}
- **Phenomenology**: a stranger, or a recent or casual acquaintance, is believed to be a known person (personally familiar or famous) in disguise. Langdon et al. (2014, p. 627) state the core as "the misidentification of a real person as a different known person appearing in the physical guise of the real person". The appearance is processed: patients "are aware of the mismatch between the perceived form of the stranger and the known person's usual appearance" (p. 619), which is why they refer to disguises and plastic surgery. Courbon and Fail's patient believed the actresses Sarah Bernhardt and Robine pursued her in the forms of people she met (Langdon et al. 2014, p. 616, quoting the Ellis, Whitley & Luauté 1994 translation; *carrier only, do not quote*).
- **Two-factor accounts** (Langdon et al. 2014, §4):
  - *Ellis and Young (1990)*: the cognitive system over-excites particular person identity nodes (PINs), so a known person is identified as present without a matching face. Their abstract: "Frégoli syndrome and intermetamorphosis are explained as defects at different stages of an information-processing chain."
  - *Ramachandran and Blakeslee (1998)*, carrier only: hyperactive temporo-limbic connections, excess affective arousal to unfamiliar faces. This is the hypo/hyper mirror of Capgras.
  - *Hirstein (2005)*, carrier only: mind-reading mismatch between internal and external representations of persons.
- **Evidence status**: 38 clear cases out of 72 surveyed. Face processing was assessed in nine, "with six of these nine showing some impairment" (p. 627). Seven one-to-one cases fit Ellis and Young better than Ramachandran and Blakeslee. One clear concurrent Capgras–Frégoli case and three suggestive ones count against Ramachandran and Blakeslee. Conclusion: "Only Ellis and Young's hypothesis was generally consistent with our findings" (p. 628). The authors call for skin-conductance testing across modalities. No autonomic data exist in the sources read (2010, p. 266; Langdon 2014, p. 628).
- **Presented/judged**: appearance is presented as a stranger's. The identity attribution runs against it. Whether any felt familiarity is presented is **unmeasured**, and the surviving account locates the error in person-identity processing. So the Map's scope rule ("Factor 1 insofar as it is experienced") currently gives it nothing to claim in Frégoli.
- **Bearing on the Map**: identity can be attributed against presented appearance. That is a separate failure from Capgras's, and Frégoli is not the felt-familiarity mirror of Capgras on the record.

### Hyperfamiliarity for faces (non-delusional neighbour) {#hyperfamiliarity}
- **Vuilleumier, Mohr, Valenza, Wetzel & Landis (2003)**, abstract only. The author list is confirmed by PubMed and the UNIGE institutional record; Crossref lists only Vuilleumier. The patient is a 21-year-old woman with a left lateral temporo-occipital infarct. She "was severely impaired in discerning that unknown people seen in everyday life were unfamiliar, although she had no difficulty recognizing familiar people". Her "sense of personally knowing faces was severely impaired when unknown faces evoked weak signals of familiarity based on spurious cues". "Her difficulty was not accompanied by delusions". The title calls it "a double dissociation with prosopagnosia".
- The 2010 paper lists this case as Frégoli's no-delusion counterpart ("Patient experiences faces of strangers as highly familiar", Table 1).
- **Presented/judged**: a familiarity feeling presented without identity attribution, and no disguise hypothesis adopted. Whether she *judged* the spurious familiarity to be spurious cannot be told from the abstract (full text Cloudflare-blocked).
- **Bearing**: the clearest raw-verified case of a reported familiarity anomaly without delusion, as the previous note found. Its double dissociation is with prosopagnosia, not Capgras.

### Intermetamorphosis (and reverse intermetamorphosis) {#intermetamorphosis}
- **Phenomenology**: a stranger or known person is believed to have transformed into a different known person, both physically and psychologically (Langdon et al. 2014, Table 1 row 6). Their footnote 16: "Sometimes this transformation is witnessed." Row 5, the generic variant without transformation, "likely involves more basic face-processing difficulties" (their gloss).
- **Reverse intermetamorphosis**: oneself transforming into another known person (row 3; Breen et al. 2000, case RZ; abstract only).
- **Two-factor**: Ellis and Young (1990) place it at a different stage of the processing chain from Frégoli (abstract). No Factor-1 measurement found.
- **Evidence status**: very thin (11 of 260 in Förstl et al. 1991).
- **Presented/judged**: if the transformation is witnessed, the anomaly reaches presented appearance, unlike Frégoli. That makes intermetamorphosis the one family member where the misidentification may sit in what is seen. The evidence is too thin to lean on.

### Subjective doubles {#subjective-doubles}
- **Phenomenology**: a stranger is believed to be a physical duplicate of oneself (Langdon et al. 2014, Table 1 row 7). Christodoulou (1978, *Am J Psychiatry*) named the syndrome; no abstract exists and the paper was not read. Langdon et al. note that Christodoulou described it as Frégoli-like in its "doubling-up" of persons (p. 621; paraphrase only). Christodoulou (1978, *J Nerv Ment Dis*) followed 20 patients across all four "syndrome of doubles" variants (abstract).
- **Evidence status**: case reports (e.g. Quarenta et al. 2022, abstract). No Factor-1 proposal found.
- **Caution**: do not use subjective doubles as a clinical illustration of fission or branching for Tenet 4. The patient believes in a numerically distinct duplicate, which is a belief about the world and not evidence about indexical identity.

### Reduplicative paramnesia {#reduplicative-paramnesia}
- **Pick (1903)**, *Brain* 26(2), 242–267, section III "On Reduplicative Paramnesia" at pp. 260–267, read from page images. Pick had published an account of "a new form of paramnesia" in a paralytic (*Jahrbücher für Psychiatrie und Neurologie*, vol. xx; unread). The 1903 paper adds a 67-year-old woman with senile dementia who held there were two clinics, the second one storey higher (paraphrase; that page was read in OCR only). Pick names the symptom (p. 261): "I have chosen the term 'reduplicative paramnesia.'" It is a series of events that the patient's memory divides into "manifold occurrences" (p. 261), impressed on him "as repetitions thereof".
- **Pick's revision on familiarity** (p. 267), the key originator-level datum. He had formerly held that the phenomenon "originated mainly from a disturbance of the 'sense of familiarity'". After the new case he came "to the opinion that the affair is somewhat different": his patients had the sense of familiarity and Volkelt's certainty of memory, and the failure was that they could not identify one situation with the other.
- **Pick on judgement** (pp. 266–267): the paralytic repeated the story of two clinics and two Professors Pick "monotonously and without judgment"; the woman's "better power of judgment ... leads her to doubt the reality of these double events".
- **Benson, Gardner & Meadows (1976)**, abstract: three head-trauma patients "persistently relocated the hospital at another geographical site, even in the face of compelling counter-evidence". The abstract points to right-hemisphere and frontal structures.
- Förstl et al. (1991): place misidentification is "frequently associated with neurological diseases, predominantly of the right hemisphere". Politis & Loane (2012) find a consensus that right and bifrontal lesions are common factors.
- **Co-occurrence**: Hudson and Grace (2000) describe one patient with Frégoli-type misidentification of her husband and reduplication of her home, after a right anterior fusiform lesion (abstract).
- **Presented/judged**: familiarity presented (on Pick's reading); identification across occasions fails. Insight varies with judgement: Pick's woman doubts, while Benson's patients hold out against counter-evidence. This is the family's clearest analogue of the clinical-insight article's "felt real, judged unreal" cell, though only partially and only in one 1903 case.
- **Bearing**: the coiner's own verdict is that reduplication is not a familiarity failure, which runs against extending the Capgras channel story to places.

### Mirrored-self misidentification {#mirrored-self}
- **Phenomenology**: the person in the mirror is believed to be a stranger (Connors et al. 2012; 2010 paper p. 262).
- **Two routes**: Breen, Caine & Coltheart (2001), abstract. Of two patients, "one patient, FE, had significant face processing deficits while the other patient, TH, had relatively intact face processing", and "further testing with TH revealed striking deficits in his ability to interpret reflected space". They propose "a combination of cognitive deficits ... including perceptual, affective and reasoning impairments". **Affect is in their list; do not write that mirrored-self has no affective component.** The 2010 Table 1 (pp. 267–268) gives the same two Factor 1s: "Mirror agnosia (loss of knowledge about how mirrors work)", with "Mirrors are treated as windows", and impaired face processing. Their no-delusion counterparts are other mirror-agnosia cases (Binkofski et al. 1999) and "Many cases of prosopagnosia".
- **Dementia gradient**: Ajuriaguerra, Strejilevitch and Tissot (1963), as translated by Connors and Coltheart (2011, abstract). In 30 dementia patients, the most severe could not recognise their reflection and showed the delusion; moderate cases showed mirror agnosia; mild cases could not designate body parts in the mirror.
- **Hypnotic analogue**: Barnier et al. (2011) found that subjects "had no difficulty generating sensible reasons to explain the stranger's presence", and some held the belief across all challenges. Connors et al. (2012): in hypnosis "the Factor 1 alone suggestion was just as effective in creating the delusion as the combined Factor 1 and Factor 2 suggestion", because "the hypnotic context, itself known to disrupt belief evaluation, can act as Factor 2".
- **Feinberg's line**: Roane, Feinberg & Liberta (2019, abstract) find mirror misidentification "associated with right hemisphere impairment" and class it among the "neuropathologies of the self". Feinberg & Roane (2017) report self-referential content attributed to the "double" in some misidentification and confabulation cases.
- **Presented/judged**: the reflection is presented. On the mirror-agnosia route the anomaly is a knowledge deficit about mirrors, with nothing anomalous presented. On the face-processing route the presented face fails to match the stored self-face (2010 Table 1). Insight is typically absent.
- **Self-recognition and Cotard**: no source was found tying mirrored-self misidentification to Cotard. That link is the reviewer's suggestion. The two share only that the self is the target, and Cotard has 4 words of headroom, so leave it. The self-recognition bearing is better routed to [[immunity-to-error-through-misidentification]]: mirror-based self-ascription is the textbook identification-dependent case, so the delusion is consistent with IEM rather than a counterexample. This is a **lead only**: the Shoemaker/Wittgenstein mirror example was not verified in this run.

## Double Dissociation Verdict {#double-dissociation}

Graded by the tiers of [[logic-of-dissociation-inference#three-tiers|the logic of dissociation inference]]:

| Pairing | What is dissociated | Tier earned | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capgras / prosopagnosia | overt identification vs autonomic covert response | 1 (cross-literature) | The classic pairing (Ellis & Young 1990). The open P3 at todo ~L2032 grades the void's L118 and Capgras L37 claims. Not re-litigated here. |
| Capgras / Frégoli as hypo- vs hyper-familiarity | autonomic familiarity, in opposite directions | **none on the record** | No autonomic data for Frégoli (2010 p. 266; Langdon 2014 p. 628). Co-occurrence contradicts the hyper/hypo model (Langdon 2014 p. 627). Shared lesion connectivity across hyper and hypo (Darby 2017). |
| Hyperfamiliarity / prosopagnosia | familiarity sense vs identification | 1 (authors' own framing; cross-patient) | Vuilleumier et al. (2003) title. Non-delusional. |
| Capgras / reduplicative paramnesia | familiarity loss vs identification failure with familiarity intact | 1 at most | Pick (1903) for RP, from clinical description only. Cross-literature and a century apart. |
| Mirrored-self FE / TH | face processing vs reflected-space knowledge | 1 (two patients, one study) | Breen et al. (2001). Both are delusional, so the delusion has more than one Factor-1 route. |

**Verdict on the research question**: the family shows **differential vulnerability** (tier 1). Identity can fail while appearance is processed (Frégoli). Identification across occasions can fail while familiarity holds (reduplicative paramnesia). Reflected-space knowledge can fail while face processing holds (mirrored-self, TH). The family does **not** show a double dissociation between recognition and felt familiarity, and the hypo/hyper symmetry is the specific claim the record undercuts.

**The word "familiarity"** carries at least three measures:
- **Autonomic response** (SCR). This is Capgras's Factor 1, measured.
- **Task or fMRI familiarity.** Darby et al. (2017) found all 17 misidentification lesions connected to "the left retrosplenial cortex, the region most activated in functional magnetic resonance imaging studies of familiarity". Darby's data therefore **support** a familiarity-associated region across Capgras, Frégoli and reduplicative paramnesia: do not cite Darby against a familiarity component.
- **Reported feeling**: Vuilleumier's patient; Pick's "sense of familiarity".

Ellis and Lewis (2001) fractionate covert recognition into autonomic and behavioural/cognitive types. Breen, Caine and Coltheart (2000, *Cognitive Neuropsychology*) put two pathways downstream of face recognition, one semantic/biographical and one affective. Garry Young (2009) argues that states reported alike as lacking familiarity differ experientially. **Any Map sentence that says "familiarity" should say which of the three it means.**

## The Presented/Judged Axis {#insight-axis}

This applies the register distinction of [[clinical-insight-presented-and-judged-unreality]]: insight is a judgement, and the anomalous datum, where experienced, is presented.

| Syndrome | What is presented (on the evidence read) | What is judged | Insight | Map's scope-rule territory |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Capgras | identification intact; felt familiarity absent on experiential readings, nothing on the revisionist reading | impostor | usually absent | Factor 1 insofar as experienced (conditional) |
| Frégoli | stranger's appearance, accurately ("aware of the mismatch") | known person in disguise | absent | none until felt familiarity is measured |
| Hyperfamiliarity (non-delusional) | a sense of personally knowing unknown faces | familiarity misjudged; no disguise hypothesis | no delusion; whether the feeling was judged spurious is unknown | yes (reported feeling) |
| Intermetamorphosis | possibly a witnessed transformation of appearance | transformation into another known person | absent | possibly (thin) |
| Subjective doubles | a stranger perceived as physically identical to oneself (patient's belief) | a double exists | absent | unknown |
| Reduplicative paramnesia | sense of familiarity and certainty of memory (Pick) | two places or two events | variable: Pick's 1903 patient doubts, Benson's patients persist | yes (Pick's familiarity); the failure is identification |
| Mirrored-self (mirror-agnosia route) | the reflection; nothing anomalous | a stranger behind a window | absent | none (knowledge deficit) |
| Mirrored-self (face-processing route) | a face that fails to match the stored self-face | a stranger | absent | possibly |

The family confirms the clinical-insight article's scope rule. Insight loss, the Factor-2 side, is uniform and outside the Map's claims. What varies is whether there is a presented anomaly at all, and where there is, what it is: appearance, familiarity, or nothing.

- **Mirrored-self misidentification**: no coiner established. Ajuriaguerra, Strejilevitch and Tissot (1963) studied it in dementia "before they became widely known in the English literature" (Connors & Coltheart 2011, abstract). The term is used by Breen et al. (2000, 2001).

> ### Beyond Capgras: The Misidentification Family
>
> The other delusional misidentifications show recognition binding more than two components. In Frégoli delusion a stranger is taken for a known person in disguise, though the patient stays "aware of the mismatch" in appearance (Langdon, Connaughton & Coltheart, 2014): identity is attributed against the appearance presented. In reduplicative paramnesia a familiar place is held to have a duplicate. Arnold Pick, who named it, first traced it to a disturbance of the "sense of familiarity" and then revised the view: his patients had that sense, yet they "cannot identify one situation from the other" (Pick, 1903, p. 267). In mirrored-self misidentification the person in the mirror is a stranger; of two patients, one had face-processing deficits and the other, with face processing relatively intact, was markedly impaired at interpreting reflected space (Breen, Caine & Coltheart, 2001).
>
> The affective-familiarity account of Capgras has little support elsewhere in the family. Four cases of other forms suggest that loss of the normal affective response "is not a critical factor" in them (Breen et al., 2000), and Frégoli patients' autonomic responses have scarcely been measured (Langdon et al., 2014), so pairing Capgras and Frégoli as too little and too much familiarity is [[logic-of-dissociation-inference|not a double dissociation]] on the record. What the family does show is differential failure: each syndrome loses one contribution while another is spared. Each patient's explanation (a disguise, a second hospital, a stranger behind the glass) answers the anomaly without locating it, as the Capgras patient's impostor does.

## Guards for the Fold Writer {#guards}

- **Quote only the four ledger fragments**, in the passage as drafted. If a sentence must change, change the paraphrase, not the quotation.
- **Pick named the condition (1903, p. 261).** Benson, Gardner and Meadows (1976) revived the term. Do not credit Benson et al. with coining it, and do not move Pick's revision into Benson's mouth.
- **Frégoli is not "hyperfamiliarity" or "the mirror image of Capgras" as a finding.** That symmetry is Ramachandran and Blakeslee's hypothesis, untested autonomically and contradicted by co-occurrence. The fold says "not a double dissociation on the record". Keep "on the record".

## Major Positions {#positions}

**Relation to tenets**: none of these bears on Dualism. Each locates the anomaly in information processing that physicalist and dualist readings can both model. None makes consciousness causally idle: the delusional beliefs are asserted, defended and acted on.

## Key Debates {#debates}

- **One mechanism or several?** Förstl et al. (1991) and Darby et al. (2017) point to overlap and a shared network. Breen et al. (2000, 2001) and the 2010 Table 1 point to syndrome-specific Factor 1s. The two can both hold: a shared familiarity-associated region plus different local deficits. Ongoing.
- **What generates Frégoli content?** Hyperarousal versus person-identity hyperexcitation versus mind-reading. Langdon et al. (2014) favour the second on case evidence. Ongoing, and blocked on the absence of SCR and face-processing data.
- **What is "familiarity"?** Autonomic, task-defined, or felt (Ellis & Lewis 2001; Garry Young 2009; Darby et al. 2017). This is the debate the Map most needs to keep straight.
