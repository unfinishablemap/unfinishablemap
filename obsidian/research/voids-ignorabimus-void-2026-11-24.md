## Executive Summary

In 1872 the physiologist Emil du Bois-Reymond told the Congress of German Naturalists and Physicians that science faces two permanent limits, the nature of matter and force and the origin of sensation from brain motion, and closed with the word *Ignorabimus*. In 1880 he expanded the list to seven "difficulties" and graded them: three transcendent, three not, one conditional. The grading is the interesting part. It was done by an explicit criterion, it separated the origin of life (declared tractable) from the origin of sensation (declared not), and after roughly 150 years the sorting has arguably held up. Auguste Comte's better-known declaration that the composition of the heavenly bodies is beyond knowledge failed within a generation, and the fetched text shows why: his criterion was sound and his application of it was wrong. Together the two cases suggest that the error in a limit-verdict tends to sit in the classification step and not in the principle, which is the claim [[closure-types-void]] already makes on other evidence. The material strengthens an existing page more than it defines a new void.

#### du Bois-Reymond, "The Limits of our Knowledge of Nature" (1872; English translation 1874)
- **URL**: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Popular_Science_Monthly/Volume_5/May_1874/The_Limits_of_our_Knowledge_of_Nature
- **Type**: Primary text (address at the Forty-fifth Congress of German Naturalists and Physicians, Leipzig; translation in *Popular Science Monthly* vol. 5, May 1874)
- **Key points**:
  - The criterion is "astronomical knowledge": complete knowledge of the positions and motions of a system's parts, the kind Laplace's imagined Mind would have. Where such knowledge would leave nothing further to ask, the question is merely unsolved.
  - Applied to the brain, astronomical knowledge would leave consciousness untouched. The atoms would be seen to move, "but it is a dumb show".
  - The two limits are stated as permanent, and the space between them is claimed without reservation for science.
  - He raises, and declines to pursue, the possibility that the two limits are one.
  - He argues from conservation of energy that mental phenomena have no place in the causal order.
- **Quotes** (all grepped in the fetched page text):
  - "What conceivable connection subsists between definite movements of definite atoms in my brain, on the one hand, and on the other hand such (for me) primordial, indefinable, undeniable facts as these"
  - "Our knowledge of Nature is thus shut up between two limits, the one forevermore determining our incapacity to comprehend matter and force, the other determining our inability to understand mental facts from their material conditions. Between these limits the man of science is lord and master; he dismembers and builds up, and no one durst say wherein his knowledge and his power are circumscribed. Beyond these limits he cannot now, nor can he ever, go."
  - "Finally, the question arises whether the two limits of our knowledge of Nature are not perhaps identical, i. e., whether, supposing we understood the nature of matter and force, we should not also understand how the substance that underlies them could, under certain conditions, feel, desire, and think. Certainly this is the simplest hypothesis, and, according to well-known principles of scientific research, until it is disproved it must be preferred to that other hypothesis, which, as we have said, makes the universe doubly incomprehensible. But such is the nature of things that we cannot attain clearness of view with regard to this point, and it were idle to dwell upon it."
  - "the mechanical cause passes completely into the mechanical effect. Hence the mental phenomena, which in the brain appear in company with material phenomena, are, so far as our understanding is concerned, void of sufficient basis. They lie beyond the law of causality"
  - "With regard to the enigma of the physical world the investigator of Nature has long been wont to utter his "Ignoramus" with manly resignation."
  - "he must resign himself once for all to the far more difficult confession— "Ignorabimus!""
- **Reading caution**: the closing *Ignorabimus* sentence is attached, in this translation, to "the enigma what matter and force are". The consciousness limit is declared permanent earlier in the address ("forevermore", "nor can he ever"), so the word covers both, but the final sentence names only matter and force. An article should not quote the last sentence as if it were about consciousness.
- **Tenet alignment**: supports the Dualism tenet's negative premise (no intelligible passage from motion to sensation) while rejecting its positive conclusion. The conservation passage is an early statement of the causal-closure argument that the Bidirectional Interaction tenet has to answer. The "simplest hypothesis" passage is a parsimony argument for what is now called Russellian monism, made and then set aside by the same author.

#### Comte, *The Positive Philosophy* (Martineau's condensed translation), Book II, ch. 1
- **URL**: https://archive.org/download/cu31924020343855/cu31924020343855_djvu.txt (OCR full text of the Cornell copy, vol. 1; source record https://archive.org/details/cu31924020343855)
- **Type**: Primary text in a free, condensed English translation. The wording is Harriet Martineau's, not a literal rendering of the *Cours de philosophie positive*.
- **Key points**:
  - The declaration in this text concerns the planets in the sentence usually quoted, and "the heavenly bodies" for temperature and internal constitution. The popular version, that we will never know what stars are made of, compresses this.
  - Comte states a general criterion first, and the criterion is correct by later lights: whatever sight can deliver may be hoped for, "whether we at present see the method or not".
  - He then misapplies it, sorting chemistry and temperature onto the non-visual side. Spectroscopy is knowledge obtained by sight.
- **Quotes** (grepped after whitespace collapse; the OCR prints "mineralogical" as "liineralogical", so the span is cut before that word):
  - "We see how we may determine their forms, their distances, their bulk, and their motions, but we can never know anything of their chemical or"
  - "Whatever knowledge is obtainable by means of the sense of Sight, we may hope to attain with regard to the stars, whether we at present see the method or not ; and whatever knowledge requires the aid of other senses, we must at once exclude from our expectations, in spite of any appearances to the contrary."
  - "As to questions about which we are uncertain whether they finally depend on Sight or not, — we must patiently wait, for an ascertainment of their character, before we can settle whether they are applicable to the stars or not."
  - "but I regard this order of facts as for ever excluded from our recognition. We can never learn their internal constitution"
- **Tenet alignment**: a clean case for Occam's Razor Has Limits read in reverse. The confident verdict rested on an unstated assumption about what a sense could carry, and nothing in the state of knowledge in the 1830s flagged the assumption as an assumption.

- **URL**: http://aleph0.clarku.edu/~djoyce/hilbert/problems.html

## The Void

### Nature of the Limit

The candidate void is second-order. It is the unavailability, from inside inquiry, of the verdict that a given open question is permanently open. Du Bois-Reymond's Latin pair names the two states exactly: *ignoramus* is the Unexplored and *ignorabimus* is the Unexplorable, and the dispute he started was over whether anyone is ever entitled to move a question from the first to the second.

This is the same structure [[closure-types-void]] describes as its "second-order void" and [[meta-epistemology-of-limits]] describes as the difficulty-versus-impossibility problem. The research did not find a new limit. It found the two best historical specimens of the existing one.

### Evidence for the Limit

What the two cases add is evidence about where limit-verdicts go wrong.

**Comte: right criterion, wrong sorting.** The fetched text shows a careful procedure. Comte states a criterion (what sight can reach), allows that methods not yet imagined may count, and even prescribes patience for doubtful cases. He then places chemical composition and temperature on the wrong side. The failure was a classification error made under a correct rule, and nothing available to him marked the classification as the weak step.

**du Bois-Reymond: a graded verdict that has mostly held.** He also used a criterion (would Laplacean knowledge of positions and motions leave anything to ask?) and applied it seven times. The assessment that follows is this note's, not a sourced claim:

- His three "not transcendent" calls were life, apparent design, and thought and language. Design had a mechanism when he spoke. The origin of life remains unsolved but is universally treated as the kind of problem he said it was, a question "only of the arrangement of atoms and molecules". Nobody now proposes it as a permanent limit.
- His three "transcendent" calls were the existence of matter and force, the origin of motion, and the origin of sensation. All three are still open, and two of them (riddles 1 and 5) are the subjects of the Map's [[intrinsic-nature-void]] and of the [[hard-problem-of-consciousness]].
- Free will he graded conditionally, and the condition he named, declaring the feeling of freedom a delusion, is the move a large part of the later literature made.

A record of seven calls from one person is anecdote. It still cuts against the simple induction that confident limit-claims fail. The Comte case is the one always cited. The du Bois-Reymond case, with a mixed and explicit scorecard, is rarely set beside it.

**The asymmetry remains.** [[meta-epistemology-of-limits]] already states that a dissolved limit proves it was apparent while no persistence proves permanence. 150 years of persistence for riddles 1, 2 and 5 is persistence and nothing more. What the case shows is narrower: a sorter using an explicit criterion placed the tractable and the intractable where they have so far stayed, including one pair (life versus sensation) that David Friedrich Strauss, as the 1880 address reports him, would have found easier to accept sorted the other way.

**Hilbert's point about what counts as knowing.** Hilbert's "no ignorabimus" allows "the proof of the impossibility of its solution" as a settlement. On that reading the opposite of *ignorabimus* is not an answer to every question. It is a verdict on every question. The live issue for the voids project is then whether verdicts of impossibility are obtainable outside mathematics, where no proof procedure exists. Du Bois-Reymond offered an argument, not a proof, and the voids section offers neither proofs nor a substitute for them.

### Phenomenology

Both primary texts show how a limit-declaration feels to the one making it, and the two descriptions differ.

Du Bois-Reymond distinguishes two confessions by their emotional cost. *Ignoramus* is uttered "with manly resignation" and sustained by the expectation of later success. *Ignorabimus* is "the far more difficult confession". He also reports, with evident surprise, that he took the point about sensation to be a commonplace and was "almost ashamed to offer so stale a draught". The declaration felt obvious to its author and scandalous to its audience.

Comte's text has no such strain. The exclusion is stated flatly ("for ever excluded from our recognition") and justified by a harmony between what we need to know and what we can know. The confident tone and the wrong verdict went together, and the anguished tone went with the verdict that held. Two cases license no rule, but they are a caution against reading felt certainty as a guide in either direction.

The reception is a third datum. The verdict was read at once as a political act: the "Black Band" charge treats a claim about the limits of mechanics as an alliance with the Church. Limit-claims are heard as taking sides, and the hearing is largely independent of the argument. The voids section, which makes limit-claims in the service of a dualist framework, should expect the same reading and should say plainly where its claims do and do not depend on the framework.

## Approaches to the Edge

### Direct Methods (if any)

Proof, where a formal system exists. This is Hilbert's route and it is closed outside mathematics and logic.

### Indirect Methods

- **State the criterion and apply it more than once.** The difference between an auditable verdict and a bare one is that du Bois-Reymond's seven gradings can be checked against each other. A criterion that sorts life and sensation differently has made a commitment that later evidence can embarrass.
- **Locate the classification step.** Comte's example suggests asking, of any limit-claim, what was assumed about the reach of the available channel. His assumption was about light. The corresponding assumption in the hard-problem case is about what structural and dynamical description can carry.
- **Record the dissolutionist alternative.** There were three responses, not two: solve it (Hilbert), resign (du Bois-Reymond), deny the riddle (Mach, the Vienna Circle, *Tractatus* 6.5). A limit-claim that has not addressed the third is incomplete.
- **Downgrade the modality.** Du Bois-Reymond's own retreat from *Ignorabimus* to *Dubitemus* in 1880 is a model: he kept the gradings and softened the watchword.

### What AI Might See

Du Bois-Reymond's argument is explicitly independent of cognitive capacity. Laplace's Mind, "exalted as it would be high above our own", gains nothing on either limit, and Laplace and Leibniz "were no wiser than we". That makes his claim different in kind from McGinn's, which ties the limit to human cognitive constitution, and it is the point on which Pecere resists Tye's assimilation of the two. For the Map the difference matters when asking what a non-human mind could contribute. On McGinn's version a differently built mind might cross. On du Bois-Reymond's version no mind working from positions and motions crosses, however capable, and an AI system given complete physical description would be exactly his Laplacean Mind watching a "dumb show". The claim is testable only in the weak sense that such a system either does or does not produce an account others can follow.

## Connection to Tenets

### Most Relevant Tenet

**Occam's Razor Has Limits.** Both cases turn on parsimony reasoning that could not see its own gap. Comte's harmony between needs and knowledge is a simplicity assumption about what the world must make available. Du Bois-Reymond explicitly invokes parsimony for the identity of his two limits ("the simplest hypothesis ... must be preferred") and then declines to rely on it, on the ground that clarity on the point is unattainable. That is a nineteenth-century scientist declining to let simplicity settle a question where the relevant knowledge is missing, which is the tenet's own position.

### Implications

- **Dualism**: the address supplies the negative premise in a form that owes nothing to the Map's framework and comes from a committed mechanist. It does not supply the conclusion. Per Pecere, du Bois-Reymond rejected substance dualism and intrinsic-consciousness views alike. An article must not present him as an ally beyond the gap itself.
- **Bidirectional Interaction**: a tension to state, not hide. The conservation-of-energy passage says mental phenomena "lie beyond the law of causality". The author who gives the Map its cleanest historical statement of the gap gives the causal-closure objection in the same paragraph. His grading of free will then follows: no difficulty for those who call the feeling a delusion, transcendent for everyone else, and dualism "entangled in the same net".
- **Minimal Quantum Interaction**: his closure premise is the Laplacean one, complete determination by positions and motions. The premise is where the Map's tenet locates its opening, and the argument as he states it has no purchase on an indeterministic physics. That is an observation about the argument's premise and not evidence for the tenet.
- **No Many Worlds**: no connection found.
- **For the voids framework**: the section classifies pages as Unexplored, Unexplorable or Occluded. Each such label is a small *ignoramus* or *ignorabimus*. The Comte case says the label is most likely to be wrong at the step where a channel's reach was assumed, and the du Bois-Reymond case says an explicit criterion applied across several questions is what makes a label checkable later.

- The German originals were not consulted. All du Bois-Reymond quotations are from the anonymous *Popular Science Monthly* translations, which render *transzendent* variously as "transcendent" and "transcendental".
- Hearnshaw (2010) on how and when spectroscopy overturned Comte was not retrieved, so this note makes no dated claim about the overturning beyond the general one.
- Whether any systematic survey exists of declared scientific limits and their fates (beyond the two famous cases) was not established. Two cases, one failed and one largely holding, are illustrations and cannot support a base rate.
- Possibly unanswerable: whether du Bois-Reymond's sorting held because his criterion tracks something real or because the questions he called transcendent are simply the hardest. Persistence does not distinguish the two.
