## Executive Summary

When someone close dies, the survivor usually believes it at once. What comes slowly is everything the belief ought to bring with it: the end of expecting the person, of turning to tell them something, of sensing them in the house. Between a third and four fifths of bereaved people in the surveyed studies report some sensed or perceived presence of the dead while knowing the person has died. Phenomenologists (Fuchs, Ratcliffe) describe this as living in two realities at once; a neuroscience model (O'Connor and Seeley) describes it as a learned prediction of the other's persistence that only experience can retrain. That is the first face, a lag. The second face is a lapse. Most grief fades while the death and the person's importance stay exactly as they were. Marušić argues the fading cannot be justified by the griever's reasons, and Moller argues that it leaves the recovered person unable to register how much was lost. If both faces are real, the full weight of a death is hard to hold at any time: early, the fact has not sunk in, and later, the feeling that measured it has gone. That conjunction is this note's synthesis and no source states it.

Every datum here is predicted by a physicalist account of attachment learning and hedonic adaptation. The void does not discriminate between dualism and its rivals, and the article must say so.

### Moller (2007), "Love and Death"

- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.5840/jphil2007104621 (read from a copy of the journal pages at https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5f80be5a21d29366e84a124e/t/606103c23f6ac2697e67601c/1616970692826/love.pdf; the text carries the journal's running heads and page numbers 301–316)
- **Type**: Paper, *Journal of Philosophy* 104(6): 301–316. **Read**: full text.
- **Key points**:
  - Starts from the empirical resilience literature (Bonanno and colleagues, Zisook, Futterman) and from Gilbert's immune neglect. His empirical premise concerns "half or more of bereaved spouses" (p. 302) [raw]; that sentence runs on past a footnote block in the extraction and was not captured whole, so only the phrase is quoted and his full claim is not reconstructed here.
  - Distinguishes believing a fact from having taken it in: "the difference between the superficial discrimination of some fact and what we sometimes describe by saying that someone has really “absorbed” or “taken in”" what has happened (p. 311) [raw].
  - Argues that resilience costs the bereaved an accurate picture of their loss (quotes below).
  - Considers and rejects the reply that our level of resilience is a sensible mean between the "Super-resilient", who feel nothing, and those who never recover.
  - He does **not** say resilience is bad on balance. His stated aim is that "Our embrace of the Adaptive Theory should be an uneasy one at best" (p. 313) [raw].
- **Tenet alignment**: none claimed. The argument is about value and self-knowledge. It is neutral on the metaphysics of mind, and its psychology is evolutionary and adaptive throughout.
- **Quotes**:
  - "Our “emotional immune system,” while promoting our interests by allowing us to continue functioning in the face of trauma, also renders us unable to take in and register fully the significance of our losses." (p. 310) [raw]
  - "Resilience thus seems to deprive us of our ability to care about those we love to their full measure after they are gone, and so deprives us of insight into our own condition." (p. 311) [raw]
  - "In a sense, resilience is a form of benign—or at least adaptive—blindness." (p. 312) [raw]
  - "failing to recognize how great a good we have irretrievably lost means that we are to some extent deluded about our own condition" (p. 312) [raw]
  - Proust, as quoted by Moller at p. 312 from *Within a Budding Grove* (Moncrieff et al. translation, Modern Library, p. 340 per Moller's note 29): what is feared beyond the loss is "not to feel it as a pain at all—to remain indifferent" [raw-secondary; Proust not read].

### Marušić (2018), "Do Reasons Expire? An Essay on Grief"

- **URL**: http://hdl.handle.net/2027/spo.3521354.0018.025 (read from the Wayback copy of the PhilArchive PDF, https://web.archive.org/web/20221018103029/https://philarchive.org/archive/MARDRE-2)
- **Type**: Paper, *Philosophers' Imprint* 18(25), December 2018, 21 pages. Open access (CC BY-NC-ND 3.0). **Read**: full text. No DOI was found in Crossref or OpenAlex; the handle is the journal's identifier.
- **Key points**:
  - States a puzzle about the rationality of grief's fading: grief responds to a reason, the reason does not change, yet it does not seem wrong to grieve less.
  - The puzzle is sharpest in anticipation. A person in grief who foresees its end sees that end as a failure.
  - The usual explanations (one must get on with life) cite what he calls the wrong kind of reason: they explain why grief fades without justifying it.
  - Examines four responses and concludes that none works. He holds that the puzzle cannot be solved and that this has a principled explanation.
  - Reads Moller as holding that resilience is "a form of blindness to the significance of loss" [raw, his footnote 6]. He credits Moller (2007) and Nussbaum (2001, ch. 1) as influences and says the puzzle as he states it is new.
- **Tenet alignment**: none claimed. The essay is about reasons for emotion and takes no position on mind and body.
- **Quotes**:
  - "My grief has passed, almost entirely. But my mother’s death has not been undone." [raw]
  - "I will argue that the puzzle eludes a solution, but that there are principled reasons for why that is so." [raw]
  - "I was surprised that only a few weeks after my mother’s death, I could lead my life more or less exactly as I did before her death" [raw]
  - "When we grieve, the thought that we will stop grieving strikes us as the thought that we will no longer care" [raw; the sentence continues after a dash and is cut here]
  - "we anticipate the end of grief as a failure to adequately respond to our reasons" [raw]
  - "the diminution of grief does not seem to be primarily a response to a change in the significance or value of the loss but is, rather, occasioned by the needs of the griever" [raw]
  - Freud, as quoted by Marušić from "Mourning and Melancholia" (1917/1999, 243–4 per Marušić): "We rely on its being overcome after a certain lapse of time." [raw-secondary]
- **Page numbers**: not given. The PDF is two-column and the text extraction interleaves columns, so page positions are unreliable. Each string above was verified whole, but a writer who wants page numbers must open the PDF.
- **Also read**: Marušić's later essay for a general readership, "The loss remains, so why does intense grief usually fade?" (*Psyche*, https://psyche.co/ideas/the-loss-remains-so-why-does-intense-grief-usually-fade), which gives the same autobiographical opening: "I was convinced, perhaps naively, that my life would always be infused with pain over her death." [raw] Its standfirst says grief has a "double vision" that takes in both the bereaved and the dead and that this helps explain moving on. That later position was not studied here and may soften the 2018 conclusion. See Gaps.

### Fuchs (2018), "Presence in absence. The ambiguous phenomenology of grief"

- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11097-017-9506-2 (read from the author-hosted PDF of the published pages, https://www.klinikum.uni-heidelberg.de/fileadmin/zpm/psychatrie/fuchs/Phenomenology_of_Grief.pdf)
- **Type**: Paper, *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* 17(1): 43–63 (published online 11 April 2017). **Read**: full text.
- **Key points**:
  - The core structure of grief is "a conflict between a presentifying and a ‘de-presentifying’ intention" (abstract, p. 43) [raw].
  - Four aspects: disrupted shared bodily life, loss of a shared world, two strands of time that drift apart, and an "as-if presence" of the dead that leads to what the abstract calls "a cognitive-affective conflict between two experienced realities" [raw].
  - Compares bereavement to amputation and sensed presence to a phantom limb, with a stated difference: the phantom is sharply located and the dead person's presence is vague.
  - Grief resolves by gradual readjustment, through identification with the dead person and through recollection and symbolisation. He criticises Freud's detachment model for missing how the relation is re-established.
  - Methodological caveat he states himself: grief is heterogeneous and culturally variable, and the analysis is not normative.
- **Tenet alignment**: none, and the author should not be enlisted. Fuchs writes from an embodied and intercorporeal phenomenology and treats the self-other overlap as bodily. This paper makes no claim about survival or about mind apart from body.
- **Quotes**:
  - "The event cannot be grasped; it has already seized the person before she can realize what it actually means." (p. 45) [raw]
  - "the other is lost as a real person, while he is still present in my inmost self-experience" (p. 49) [raw; the sentence ends with a quoted phrase, "cannot be gone", whose quotation marks are garbled in the extraction]
  - "he cannot accept or even realize the pastness of the past" (p. 50) [raw]
  - "the mourner oscillates between the intruding impression of the deceased’s presence and the reality of his irrevocable absence" (p. 54) [raw]
  - "a conflict which neither the amputees nor the bereaved are able to resolve" (p. 54) [raw]
  - Freud, as quoted by Fuchs (p. 55) from "Mourning and Melancholia" (Freud 1917, 244f. per Fuchs): "Reality-testing has shown that the loved object no longer exists, and it proceeds to demand that all libido shall be withdrawn from its attachment to that object." and "in the meantime the existence of the object is psychically prolonged" [both raw-secondary; Freud not read].
- **Caution on Fuchs's use of Rees**: his footnote 12 reads "Rees (1971), in a study of 300 bereaved men and women, found a sense of presence of the dead spouse in 39%, and illusions or hallucinations in 14%" [raw]. Rees's own abstract gives 293 people (227 widows, 66 widowers), and Kamp et al.'s table gives 14.0% as the *visual* figure alone, with auditory 13.3% and tactile 2.7% beside it. Do not copy Fuchs's sentence.
- **Page numbers**: from running heads in the extraction, as for Moller.

### Ratcliffe (2021), "Sensed presence without sensory qualities: a phenomenological study of bereavement hallucinations"

- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11097-020-09666-2 (read from the White Rose repository copy of the published article)
- **Type**: Paper, *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* 20(4): 601–616 (published online 24 March 2020). **Read**: full text.
- **Key points**:
  - "the most common form of experience is a non-specific sense or feeling of presence" [raw], which raises the question of how someone can seem present with no sensory content.
  - His answer: at least some such experiences consist in "a dynamic and non-localized experience of significant and salient possibilities" [raw] that amounts to a sense of currently relating to a particular person.
  - Uses C. S. Lewis to mark what memory cannot supply, namely the other's resistance to one's own expectations.
- **Tenet alignment**: none. Ratcliffe's question is what the experience consists of. He does not ask whether anything is there, and the paper should not be cited for or against survival.
- **Quotes**:
  - Lewis, as quoted by Ratcliffe from *A Grief Observed* (Lewis 1966, pp. 18–19 per Ratcliffe): "What pitiable cant to say ‘She will live forever in my memory!’ Live? That is exactly what she won’t do" [raw-secondary; Lewis not read].

### Kamp et al. (2020), "Sensory and Quasi-Sensory Experiences of the Deceased in Bereavement: An Interdisciplinary and Integrative Review"

- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1093/schbul/sbaa113 (PMID 33099644; PMCID PMC7707065)
- **Type**: Review, *Schizophrenia Bulletin* 46(6): 1367–1381. Nine authors including Ratcliffe. **Read**: full text (Europe PMC XML).
- **Design**: narrative interdisciplinary review. It reports no pooled estimate.
- **Result and grade**: "Prevalence estimates of having at least one of these experiences range from 47% to 82% across several studies." [raw] The authors call the findings on correlates "tentative".
- **What the authors conclude**: these experiences are common, heterogeneous, and unlikely in general to indicate psychopathology. Research on everyday reality testing "suggests preserved reality testing in SED, in the sense that the experiencer knows the deceased has died" [raw]. The attachment "searching" theory fits the early months, but "the theory does not account for SED occurring later" [raw], sometimes years on.
- **Limitations they state**: no agreed definition, self-selected samples recruited by advertisement, and "there is currently no validated measure of SED" [raw].
- **Tenet alignment**: neutral. The review surveys psychological, cultural and spiritual framings without endorsing a survival reading.

### Rees (1971), "The Hallucinations of Widowhood"

- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.4.5778.37 (PMID 5096884; PMCID PMC1799198)
- **Type**: Paper, *BMJ* 4(5778): 37–41. **Read**: abstract only (the PMC scan could not be fetched as text). Modality figures come from Kamp et al.'s Table 3.
- **Sample**: "227 widows and 66 widowers" [raw-abs], 293 people, interviewed in a defined area of mid-Wales; 80.7% of all widowed residents and 94.2% of those fit for interview.
- **Design**: cross-sectional interview survey by a general practitioner. No comparison group.
- **Result**: "Almost half the people interviewed had hallucinations or illusions of the dead spouse." [raw-abs] By modality, per Kamp et al.'s table: sense of presence 39.2%, visual 14.0%, auditory 13.3%, auditory-verbal 11.6%, tactile 2.7% [raw-secondary]. The experiences often lasted years and were commonest in the first ten. They were more frequent after long and happy marriages and were not related to social isolation or known depressive illness.
- **Authors' conclusion**: "These hallucinations are considered to be normal and helpful accompaniments of widowhood." [raw-abs] Also: "It was unusual for the hallucinations to have been disclosed, even to close friends or relatives." [raw-abs]

### O'Connor & Seeley (2022), "Grieving as a form of learning: Insights from neuroscience applied to grief and loss"

- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2021.08.019 (PMID 34520954; PMCID PMC8858332)
- **Type**: Theory and review paper, *Current Opinion in Psychology* 43: 317–322. **Read**: full text (author manuscript in PMC).
- **Who coined what**: the "Gone-But-Also-Everlasting" model is O'Connor and Seeley's own name for their proposal, introduced here ("We propose the Gone-But-Also-Everlasting theory").
- **Claim and grade**: a proposal, worded as hypothesis throughout. "We hypothesize that the neural architecture of the bond supports the belief (or semantic knowledge) that the other persists, despite any sensory evidence to the contrary." [raw] After a death, "prediction error is experienced until the updated semantic knowledge can be learned" [raw], so "grieving can be considered a form of learning, requiring time and feedback" [raw]. The abstract says the conflict between the two knowledge streams is "perhaps explaining the duration of grieving".
- **Evidence base**: prairie-vole pair-bond neuroscience (partner-approach neurons in the nucleus accumbens), human neuroimaging of self-other overlap, attachment theory. The step from voles to a human belief in the other's "everlasting" persistence is the authors' inference.
- **Tenet alignment**: this is the physicalist account of the first face, stated by its authors. It predicts the lag between knowing and expecting directly.

### O'Connor et al. (2008), "Craving love? Enduring grief activates brain's reward center"

- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2008.04.256 (PMID 18559294; PMCID PMC2553561)
- **Type**: Paper, *NeuroImage* 42(2): 969–972. **Read**: full text (author manuscript, eScholarship).
- **Sample**: "Bereaved women (11 CG, 12 NCG)" [raw], 23 in all, who had lost a mother or sister to breast cancer within five years; mean age 43.70.
- **Design**: event-related fMRI with photographs of the deceased against a stranger, paired with grief-related or neutral words.
- **Result**: both groups showed pain-related activity; "only those with CG showed reward-related activity in the nucleus accumbens (NA)" [raw]. The cluster was "positively correlated with self-reported yearning, but not with time since death" [raw].
- **Authors' conclusion**: the study "supports the hypothesis that attachment activates reward pathways"; continued reward activity "may interfere" with adapting.
- **Grade**: one small all-female study with a between-group contrast. A single result. It bears on complicated grief, not on ordinary bereavement.

### Maciejewski, Zhang, Block & Prigerson (2007), "An Empirical Examination of the Stage Theory of Grief"

- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.297.7.716 (PMID 17312291)
- **Type**: Paper, *JAMA* 297(7): 716–723. **Read**: abstract only.
- **Sample and design**: longitudinal cohort (Yale Bereavement Study), "233 bereaved individuals living in Connecticut" [raw-abs], deaths from natural causes, five rater-administered items from 1 to 24 months after the loss.
- **Result**: "Counter to stage theory, disbelief was not the initial, dominant grief indicator. Acceptance was the most frequently endorsed item and yearning was the dominant negative grief indicator from 1 to 24 months postloss." [raw-abs] Once rescaled, "disbelief decreased from an initial high at 1 month postloss" [raw-abs].
- **Why it matters here**: it limits the first face. On a single self-report item most bereaved people endorse acceptance from the first month. Whatever lags, explicit assent to the fact is not it.
- **Caution**: Crossref lists a published correction ("Incorrect Example", *JAMA* 297(20): 2200) and an authors' reply to correspondence (*JAMA* 297(24): 2692). Neither was read.

### Bonanno et al. (2002), "Resilience to loss and chronic grief: A prospective study from preloss to 18-months postloss"

- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.83.5.1150 (PMID 12416919)
- **Type**: Paper, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 83(5): 1150–1164. Eight authors. **Read**: abstract only.
- **Sample and design**: "prospective data on 205 individuals several years prior to the death of their spouse and at 6- and 18-months postloss" [raw-abs].
- **Result**: five patterns (common grief, chronic grief, chronic depression, improvement during bereavement, resilience). "Common grief was relatively infrequent, and the resilient pattern most frequent." [raw-abs] The abstract gives no percentages, and none is given here.
- **Who coined what**: the resilience-trajectory framing in bereavement research is Bonanno's. Moller cites this paper and Bonanno et al. (2005) for his empirical premise.

### Infurna & Luthar (2016), "Resilience to Major Life Stressors Is Not as Common as Thought"

- **URL**: https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615621271 (PMID 26993272; PMCID PMC4800830)
- **Type**: Paper, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 11(2): 175–194. **Read**: full text (author manuscript in PMC).
- **Sample and design**: re-analysis of the German Socio-Economic Panel, "28 annual waves (1984 – 2011)" [raw], using growth mixture models of yearly *life satisfaction* around spousal loss, divorce and unemployment. The outcome is life satisfaction. Grief was not measured.
- **Result for spousal loss**: with the earlier studies' specifications the resilient class was largest, "with 75% of the population likely to belong to this sub-group" [raw]. With slope variance estimated, "only 27% of the sample was likely to belong to a resilient trajectory" [raw]. With a multi-phase model and class-specific variances, the solution came out "with 36% and 64% of participants in the Resilient and Recovery sub-groups" [raw].
- **Authors' conclusion**: rates of resilience depend heavily on model specification; when two assumptions were relaxed "we found that a resilience class was least common" [raw-abs]. They warn against "any categorical statements" about such rates.
- **Why it matters here**: Moller's and Marušić's empirical premise is contested. Note what the re-analysis leaves standing: in its own models the larger group is a *recovery* class that dips and returns over years. Fading still occurs. What is disputed is how many people never dip.

## The Void

### Nature of the Limit

Two limits, at opposite ends of mourning.

**The lag.** The belief that the person has died arrives quickly and is usually sincere (Maciejewski et al.: acceptance is the most endorsed item from month one). What trails is the body of expectation that the belief should rewrite: habits built around the person, the sense of their being nearby, the impulse to tell them something. Fuchs describes the mourner as living in two realities; O'Connor and Seeley describe two streams of knowledge in conflict. For a time the survivor cannot bring what they know and what they expect into one view. This face is temporary for most people and so is a weak void: a limit on how fast a mind can take in a fact.

**The lapse.** Later the conflict settles, and with it goes the response that tracked the loss. Marušić's point is that nothing about the reason has changed: the person is as dead and as loved as before. Moller's point is that the recovered survivor has lost the means of registering what is gone, while retaining every relevant belief. If grief is a way of perceiving value, which [[emotional-epistemology-void]] treats as undecidable from inside, then recovery closes the only window through which the loss was seen at its size.

**The conjunction** (this note's synthesis, not any source's): the period when the feeling is at full strength is the period when the fact has not fully landed, and the period when the fact has landed is the period when the feeling has faded. Whether there is an interval in between where both are held is an empirical question none of these sources addresses. The article may raise the question. It may not assert that no such interval exists.

### Evidence for the Limit

- Sensed or perceived presence of the dead is common among the bereaved (Rees: almost half of 293; Olson et al.: 61% of 46 widows; Grimby: half of 50 felt presence; reviews: 30–60% and 47–82%), and it coexists with knowing the person is dead (Kamp et al. on preserved reality testing).
- These experiences were mostly undisclosed (Rees; Olson et al.: 54% had told no one), which suggests the first face goes largely unreported.
- Grief typically diminishes while its object does not change (Bonanno et al. 2002; conceded even by Infurna and Luthar's recovery class).
- First-person philosophical testimony that the fading is surprising and looks, from inside grief, like a failure (Marušić).

### Evidence against, or limiting, the void

- **Explicit disbelief is not the dominant early state.** Maciejewski et al. found acceptance most endorsed throughout. The lag is in expectation and habit; explicit belief is not what trails. An article that says the bereaved "cannot believe" the death overstates.
- **Sensed presence may not be a failure to comprehend at all.** Rees calls the experiences "normal and helpful". Ratcliffe reads them as a way of still relating to the person. The continuing-bonds literature treats an ongoing relation as healthy. On those readings the presence is a new form of relation that the survivor fully understands.
- **The resilience premise is contested.** Infurna and Luthar show the "most people are resilient" figure depends on modelling choices, and their outcome was life satisfaction.
- **The lapse may be accurate and not blind.** Moller himself considers the reply that fading is a sensible mean. Marušić's later "double vision" position (unread in detail) may hold that diminished grief can be a fitting response. Fuchs holds that grief resolves by re-integration: the dead person is incorporated and represented, and the relation is "re-established on a new level". If that is right, the weight of the loss is carried forward in another form.
- **Heterogeneity.** Fuchs warns that grief varies by person and culture. All the prevalence studies are of widowed spouses, mostly elderly, in Wales, the United States and Sweden.

### Phenomenology

Fuchs gives the fullest description: shock in which "The event cannot be grasped"; a present that feels unreal beside a past that is still going on; and oscillation between the impression of presence and "the reality of his irrevocable absence". Ratcliffe adds that the commonest form of presence has no sensory content: the survivor simply has a sense of relating to someone. Lewis, through Ratcliffe, marks what imagination cannot restore, which is the other's capacity to surprise. On the second face Marušić reports surprise at how soon ordinary life resumed, and a recoil, while grieving, from the thought of not grieving.

## Approaches to the Edge

### Direct Methods (if any)

Diary and experience-sampling studies could track expectation (reaching for the phone, laying a place, turning to speak) separately from explicit belief and from felt grief across the first two years. No such study was found. The search here was not designed to find one, so this is an untested gap in this note. The literature may well contain one.

### Indirect Methods

- Comparison with phantom limbs (Fuchs; Ratcliffe 2019, unread), where a body schema outlasts the limb as an expectation outlasts the person.
- Comparison with [[alief-and-acting-against-belief]]: behaviour and expectation that run against a sincerely held belief.
- The anticipation test Marušić proposes: ask how the end of grief looks from inside it.
- Moller's thought experiment of the Super-resilient, who feel nothing at a spouse's death, used to isolate what resilience costs.

### What AI Might See

A system whose model of the world updates in one step on being told of a death would show no lag. Whether it would then register the loss at all is the question Moller's argument raises: on his view registering a loss requires the response, and holding the belief is not enough. This is speculation and no source addresses artificial minds. It connects to the claim in [[emotional-epistemology-void]] that affect may disclose what propositions do not, with the same caveat that the claim cannot be validated from inside.

## Connection to Tenets

### Most Relevant Tenet

None strongly. The nearest is **Occam's Razor Has Limits**, in the modest sense that "she knows he is dead" turns out to hide at least three separable things (assent, expectation, valuation) that a simpler description would merge.

**Dualism** and **Bidirectional Interaction** are not supported by anything here. **No Many Worlds** has a thematic link only: the loss is of this particular person, and no duplicate would repair it, which is [[beloved-void]]'s point and an indexical one.

### Implications

- **Strongest rival reading, and it is sufficient.** A physicalist account predicts both faces. The lag is slow retraining of a learned prediction that the attachment figure persists (O'Connor and Seeley). The lapse is hedonic adaptation, which Moller himself calls the "emotional immune system" and treats as an evolved mechanism. Nothing in the data calls for a non-physical mind. **The void does not discriminate between the Map's dualism and physicalism, and the article must say so in its tenet section.**
- **Sensed presence is not evidence of survival and must not be offered as such.** Rees, Olson, Grimby, Castelnovo and Kamp report experiences; none claims the dead are present. Kamp et al. say the experiencer "knows the deceased has died".
- **Authors who would not accept a dualist reading, or who take no such position:** O'Connor and Seeley (the model is neural learning); Fuchs (embodied, intercorporeal phenomenology); Ratcliffe (brackets the metaphysical question); Moller and Marušić (arguments about value and reasons, with an adaptive psychology assumed); Bonanno, Infurna, Luthar, Maciejewski and Prigerson (empirical psychology and psychiatry). None should be cited as supporting any tenet.
- Under the suspended cumulative-evidence step ([[positions/voids-as-evidence|voids-as-evidence]], P-V3), this is a catalogued limit and nothing more.

## Gaps and cautions

**Not verified, or verified only through a carrier**

- Rees (1971), Olson et al. (1985), Grimby (1993), Castelnovo et al. (2015), Maciejewski et al. (2007) and Bonanno et al. (2002) were read at **abstract only**. Rees's modality percentages come from Kamp et al.'s table.
- Freud, Proust and C. S. Lewis are quoted **only through carriers** (Fuchs and Marušić; Moller; Ratcliffe). The page references attached to them are the carriers'. A writer who wants to quote any of the three must fetch the primary.
- "Grief work" is attributed to Freud by Fuchs. The term's coinage and its English popularisation were not checked.
- "Continuing bonds" is attributed here to Klass, Silverman and Nickman from general knowledge and the Crossref reissue record. The 1996 date and the claim that they coined it are **unverified**.
- The "searching" account is attributed to Bowlby and Parkes on Kamp et al.'s authority. Parkes (1970) was not read.
- "Emotional immune system" is Moller's phrase for what Gilbert and colleagues' immune-neglect work (1998, cited by Moller) describes. Who first used "psychological immune system" was not checked. Cite Gilbert through [[miswanting]].
- Moller's and Fuchs's page numbers were assigned from running heads in extracted text. Marušić's are not given at all.
- Moller's sentence beginning "research indicates that not only do half or more of bereaved spouses" was captured only up to a footnote block. Do not complete it from inference.
- Marušić's later position (the 2022 book and the *Psyche* essay's "double vision") was not studied. The article should describe the **2018** conclusion as the 2018 conclusion.
- The DSM-5 sentence on pangs of grief appears in Marušić's essay as a quotation. It was not checked against DSM-5 and is not quoted here.

**Single-study or small-sample results**

- O'Connor et al. (2008): 23 women, one study, and about complicated grief. Do not generalise to ordinary bereavement or write "the brain's reward centre keeps expecting the dead".
- Olson et al. (1985): 46 widows in two nursing homes. Grimby (1993): 50 people.
- Rees (1971) is one practitioner's survey with no comparison group, from 1971 mid-Wales.
- The O'Connor and Seeley model is a hypothesis resting substantially on prairie-vole work. Call it a proposal.

**What a later writer must not overstate**

- Do not write that the bereaved "cannot believe" or "do not really know" the death. Acceptance is the most endorsed item from the first month (Maciejewski et al.), and reality testing is preserved during sensed presence (Kamp et al.).
- Do not call sensed presence a "hallucination" without noting that the commonest form has no sensory content (Ratcliffe) and that the field's own term is "sensory and quasi-sensory experiences of the deceased".
- Do not give a single prevalence figure. Give the ranges (30–60%; 47–82%) with the reason they differ, or Rees's own figures with his sample.
- Do not copy Fuchs's "study of 300" or his "illusions or hallucinations in 14%".
- Do not write "most bereaved people are resilient" as settled. Bonanno et al. (2002) found the resilient pattern most frequent; Infurna and Luthar (2016) found that result model-dependent on a different outcome measure. Do not give a percentage for Bonanno et al. (2002): the abstract has none.
- Do not say Moller thinks resilience is bad or that we should grieve longer. He says the benefits are real and the embrace should be uneasy.
- Do not say Marušić solves the puzzle, and do not say he thinks grief should last. He says the puzzle eludes solution.
- Do not assert that the full weight of a loss can *never* be held. That is this note's conjecture. No source claims it, and Fuchs's re-integration account points the other way.
- "No study has tracked expectation separately from belief in bereavement" is **not licensed**. This note ran no search for such a study. If the article wants the claim, a writer must first search (suggested terms: `bereavement experience sampling expectation`, `grief diary "forgot" deceased`, `"momentary forgetting" bereavement`) with a positive control such as `bereavement experience sampling` returning known ecological-momentary-assessment grief studies.
- Do not present any of this as evidence for dualism or for survival of death.

**Possibly unanswerable**

- Whether grief discloses value or merely feels as if it does. Moller's second face depends on the first option, and [[emotional-epistemology-void]] holds the question open.
