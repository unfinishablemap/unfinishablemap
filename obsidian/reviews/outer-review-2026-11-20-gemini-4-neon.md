# Referee Report: "The Plenitude Void"

This manuscript is not suitable for publication in a top-tier philosophy journal (*Mind*, *Synthese*, or *Philosophical Studies*) and warrants **rejection**. Its central thesis—that determining whether conscious experience is as rich as it seems constitutes a structurally self-concealing "void" driven by a "check-and-find loop," a confabulation default, theory-dependence, and uncalibratable external measures—rests on an empirical and philosophical bibliography whose attested sources terminate prior to 2020. Audited against peer-reviewed psychophysics, cognitive neuroscience, and philosophy of mind from 2020–2025, both poles of the manuscript's supposed standoff lose their load-bearing premises, its ensemble-coding and "subjective inflation" mechanism is mathematically and empirically undercut, its calibration skepticism is directly contested by non-circular calibration frameworks, and its handling of illusionist, control-theoretic, computational, and representational-format rivals relies on anachronistic bracketing and question-begging fiat.

## Empirical Vulnerabilities: Challenged and Unreplicated Foundations

The manuscript constructs its epistemic impasse out of a stylized symmetry between classic pre-2020 paradigms: George Sperling's (1960) partial-report paradigm and Zohar Bronfman et al.'s (2014) color-diversity paradigm on the "overflow" side, pitted against Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris's (1999) inattentional blindness and change-blindness studies on the "grand-illusion" side. Even after incorporating Edward Ward, Adam Bear, and Brian Scholl (2016) to cut the inferential bridge from ensemble statistics to individual-item awareness, the manuscript still assumes that four foundational empirical premises remain standing: that Sperling subjects genuinely report rich all-item phenomenology, that Bronfman-style color diversity proceeds "cost-free" outside focal attention, that inattentional blindness demonstrates radical unattended sparsity, and that every introspective check manufactures the missing detail. While none of the manuscript's cited studies appear to have been formally retracted in the literature consulted here, peer-reviewed empirical research from 2020–2025 has directly challenged, mathematically undermined, or failed to replicate key generalizations of all four premises.

First, the manuscript treats the phenomenology of Sperling's (1960) partial-report paradigm—the alleged subjective impression of seeing every letter in the array—as an uncontested starting point generating the richness dilemma. Florian Cova, Maxence Gaillard, and François Kammerer (2021) directly tested this empirical premise by reproducing both Sperling's (1960) and Landman et al.'s (2003) paradigms while systematically eliciting participants' subjective reports. Their results fail to support the overflow thesis: many participants did not report the rich all-item phenomenology attributed to them by local theorists, prompting the authors to characterize the received claim that overflow subjects report seeing all presented stimuli as an "urban myth". Compounding the erosion of post-Sperling iconic-overflow support, Hakwan Lau (2022) documents a basic scoring error in the widely cited Sligte et al. (2010) high-resolution iconic memory study—failure to normalize for 25% chance accuracy on a four-alternative forced-choice identification task—which, once corrected, drops putative high-resolution gray-scale capacity to 58.4%, nearly indistinguishable from ordinary working memory capacity (55.0%). If the putative datum of rich all-item iconic phenomenology fails to emerge reliably under systematic probing, the manuscript constructs an epistemic void around an overstated explanandum.   

Second, the manuscript relies on Bronfman et al. (2014) as its empirical overflow benchmark and uses only Ward, Bear, and Scholl (2016) to narrow the item-awareness inference. Yet Brylee Hawkins et al. (2022) tested Bronfman et al.'s dual-task paradigm in peripheral vision on a large projection screen and failed to replicate the "cost-free" representation effect outside central vision. While Hawkins et al. replicated Bronfman et al. in central vision on a standard monitor, peripheral color-diversity accuracy fell significantly from 74.4% in cued regions to 66.3% in uncued regions (Wilcoxon W = 208.00, p < 0.001; n = 21), and primary letter-recall accuracy also suffered when the secondary diversity probe targeted uncued regions (t(20) = 2.4, p = 0.03, Cohen's d = 0.53). Crucially, uncued performance stayed above 50% chance (66.3%), showing partial attention-dependent degradation rather than either cost-free overflow or total blindness. Even more damaging to the manuscript's self-concealment thesis, observers did *not* suffer an undetected illusion of peripheral vividness: they rated uncued colors as significantly less vivid than cued colors (1.99 vs. 2.18 on a 1–4 scale; t(20) = 3.11, p = 0.01, d = 0.68), assigned far lower confidence to correct uncued trials than to correct cued trials (1.83 vs. 3.01; t(20) = 7.95, p < 0.001, d = 1.74; n = 21), and retained high metacognitive efficiency tracking their own peripheral decline.   

Third, the sparsity pole suffers an equally serious revision. The manuscript cites Simons and Chabris (1999) as an anchor for attention-gated sparsity. In the largest inattentional blindness investigation to date (analyzing n = 5,296 subjects in Experiment 3 alone, adding stimulus-absent catch trials and forced-choice feature questions), Makaela Nartker, Chaz Firestone, Howard Egeth, and Ian Phillips (2025) demonstrated that classic retroactive yes/no inattentional-blindness reports exhibit a substantial conservative response bias—participants prefer to deny noticing unexpected stimuli (c = 0.31, 95% CI [0.22, 0.41] on the traditional awareness question in Experiment 3; c = 0.45 in super-subject pooling; and c = 0.67, 95% CI [0.49, 0.84] for color). Although Experiment 3 sensitivity did not reliably exceed zero for shape (d' = 0.15, 95% CI [−0.20, 0.50]; 52.82% correct) or location (d'_2afc = −0.17, 95% CI [−0.44, 0.10]; 46.60% correct), non-noticers retained strong above-chance sensitivity for color (d' = 0.78, 95% CI [0.41, 1.16]; 64.02% correct), with overall super-subject d' ranging between 0.07 and 0.51 across analyses—and a companion *Journal of Vision* conference report on dynamic displays found above-chance shape discrimination as well (d' = 0.23, 95% CI [0.13, 0.33]). While Nartker et al. carefully note that residual sensitivity is *consistent with* partial awareness rather than settling the overflow dispute outright, their companion taxonomy (Nartker et al., 2023) disaggregates "failing to see" into six distinct perceptual and post-perceptual error mechanisms—from acuity limits and crowding to conservative thresholds and inattentional amnesia. Deploying Simons and Chabris (1999) as an undifferentiated sparsity pillar is no longer viable in a specialist journal.   

Fourth, the manuscript's signature structural mechanism—the "check-and-find loop," which asserts that *"every introspective probe for sparsity generates the detail it was meant to measure"*—fails under gaze-contingent empirical testing. Using gaze-contingent virtual reality in active, real-world audiovisual environments, Michael Cohen, Thomas Botch, and Caroline Robertson (2020) found that almost a third (~33%) of free-viewing observers (n = 160) failed to notice when more than 95% of their visual field (everything outside a 10° radius around fixation) was completely desaturated. Decisively, in Experiment 2 (n = 20), even when participants were *explicitly instructed* to distribute attention widely and actively search their periphery for color loss during gaze-contingent viewing, their 50% detection threshold still capped out at roughly 37.5° of visual angle (t = 5.22, p < 0.001 against a 45° ceiling)—leaving more than two-thirds of the everyday visual field undetected despite deliberate checking (with mean detection latency of 4.67 ± 0.36 s when detected). Combined with foveated early-visual metamer bounds quantified by William Broderick, Gizem Rufo, Jonathan Winawer, and Eero Simoncelli (2023, *eLife* RP90554) and intact peripheral metacognition in Hawkins et al. (2022), these findings contradict the claim that probing peripheral vision universally manufactures rich detail or seals sparsity off from detection.   

## The Subjective Inflation Misfire and Bibliographic Obsolescence

In its ensemble-coding section—which frames a fourth position asking *"not how much detail is present but in what format"* (citing Cohen, Dennett, and Kanwisher 2016)—the manuscript imports "subjective inflation" via Dobromir Rahnev et al. (2011) and J. D. Knotts et al. (2019) to explain why peripheral vision feels rich despite low-fidelity encoding. That reliance mishandles a contested, actively revised empirical hypothesis as settled cognitive architecture.   

First, the foundational computational model of subjective inflation introduced by Rahnev et al. (2011)—which explains liberal peripheral/unattended detection criteria via a fixed absolute decision criterion across attentional states—has been mathematically undermined. Jennifer Laura Lee, Rachel Denison, and Wei Ji Ma (2023) demonstrated that Rahnev et al.'s behavioral results are mathematically unidentifiable: because near-threshold visual detection tasks lack controlled external noise, a determinate mapping from stimulus space to decision-variable space, and incentives to adjust criteria, an infinite family of *flexible*, uncertainty-sensitive Bayesian decision rules accounts for the exact same data. When noise-controlled paradigms properly test criterion flexibility (e.g., Denison et al., 2018), observers adjust criteria flexibly according to attention-dependent uncertainty; Lee et al. conclude that the fixed-criterion model of subjective inflation is *"poorly tenable"*.   

Second, the manuscript's characterization of subjective inflation as an "anti-correlated confidence signal" where felt vividness moves inversely with attention is directly contradicted by task-dependent reversals in the empirical literature. Matteo Toscani, Pascal Mamassian, and Matteo Valsecchi (2021) compared central and peripheral visual confidence at matched objective orientation-discrimination performance (n = 15, gaze-monitored) and observed systematic peripheral *underconfidence*—the exact opposite of peripheral inflation—leading them to attribute peripheral richness to constructive filling-in and centre-to-periphery filling-out rather than metacognitive overconfidence. Follow-up work by Nino Sharvashidze, Matteo Toscani, and Matteo Valsecchi showed that switching the task from local orientation discrimination to global scene-gist categorization flips observers from peripheral underconfidence to peripheral overconfidence (mean point of equal confidence = −0.915, 95% CI [−1.578, −0.253], t(11) = −3.04, p = 0.011). Peripheral confidence bias is therefore task- and feature-specific rather than a uniform self-concealing signal. Even Brian Odegaard's own laboratory titled a July 2025 *Journal of Vision* conference abstract *"The theory of subjective inflation in peripheral vision: accept, reject, or revise?"*, while J. D. Knotts, Matthias Michel, and Brian Odegaard (2020) explicitly conceded against G. Abid (2019) that post-perceptual biases can also resist trial-by-trial feedback in some contexts and that visibility and confidence ratings are *not* a perfect, linear index of phenomenology.   

Third, the manuscript conflates visibility, felt vividness, and metacognitive confidence into a single undifferentiated sub-personal signal. Exploratory neuroimaging analyses by Matan Mazor, Nadine Dijkstra, and Stephen Fleming (2022) report dissociable neural correlates for subjective visibility versus decision confidence, while a Stage 2 Registered Report by Matan Mazor, Rani Moran, and Stephen Fleming (2021) documents systematic metacognitive asymmetries between presence and absence judgments (compatible with default reasoning or unequal variance across signal-absent and signal-present distributions). Treating subjective inflation as a single established mechanism collapses distinctions that contemporary metacognition research has rigorously pried apart.

## Inferential Failures: Calibration Skepticism, Informal Unfalsifiability, and the Anti-Parsimony Fiat

Even if the manuscript's empirical bibliography were overhauled, three load-bearing logical and methodological inferences fail under scrutiny.

First, the manuscript attempts to neutralize third-person neuroscience and psychophysics through a sweeping epistemological veto: *"The void is not that no external method could work; it is that external methods inherit the problem at the point of calibration."*. That inference helps itself to global calibration skepticism while ignoring the central 2020s philosophy-of-science responses that directly challenge it. Matthias Michel (2023 [online 2021]) evaluates the leading skeptical objections to calibrating consciousness detection procedures (including Irvine's arbitrariness argument and Schwitzgebel's reliability worries), distinguishes *concordance calibration* from *model calibration*, and argues that signal-detection methods and converging empirical regularities allow non-circular calibration. Tellingly, even philosophers who argue that Michel's own calibration defense falls short reject the manuscript's global pessimism: Andy McKilliam (2025, *Noûs*) develops **iterative natural-kind reasoning**—modeled on how thermometry bootstrapped objective temperature instruments out of fallible sensations of hot and cold—showing how consciousness science can start from fallible introspective reports and iteratively leverage empirical regularities without presupposing or pre-weighting any theory of consciousness. In a companion *Ergo* (2025) paper, McKilliam explicitly applies iterative inference to the best explanation to demonstrate how physiological and neural profiles can detect introspective errors in mental imagery—the very domain into which this manuscript extends its check-and-find loop.   

Moreover, third-person empirical paradigms in the 2020s have already placed concrete differential constraints on local/overflow versus global-access accounts without collapsing into a check-and-find circle. Matthias Michel and Adrien Doerig (2022) used **postdictive effects**—in which successive stimuli combine to shape what is subsequently perceived—to mount an empirical challenge directly against local theories of consciousness. Likewise, the pre-registered, multimodal Cogitate Consortium adversarial collaboration (Ferrante et al., 2025, *Nature*, n = 256 across fMRI, MEG, and iEEG) directly juxtaposed Integrated Information Theory (IIT) and Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (GNWT), identifying content information in visual, ventrotemporal, and inferior frontal cortex alongside sustained duration-tracking posterior responses, while challenging IIT via the absence of sustained posterior synchronization and challenging GNWT via the general absence of offset ignition and limited prefrontal representation of certain conscious dimensions. Although Anil Seth and Tim Bayne (2022) and Asger Kirkeby-Hinrup et al. (2025) rightly emphasize persisting methodological obstacles in no-report paradigms and theory comparison, those obstacles are tractable empirical design problems—not an a priori calibration barrier sealing off an unexplorable void.   

Second, the manuscript's reply to Popperian unfalsifiability is informal and disconnected from formal consciousness science. Conceding that the unfalsifiability objection *"has force,"* the text leans on an informal split between "evidential" and "definitional" unfalsifiability alongside an asymmetry observation (grand-illusion theories make testable predictions, whereas overflow posits non-reported content). Yet the manuscript completely omits Johannes Kleiner and Erik Hoel's (2021) formal proof of the **falsification dilemma** for theories of consciousness (alongside Adrien Doerig, Aaron Schurger, and Michael Herzog's 2021 hard criteria). Kleiner and Hoel formally show that if a theory's empirical prediction function and behavioral/report inference function are *strictly dependent*, the theory becomes unfalsifiable; whereas if they are *independent* (as non-access phenomenal overflow requires) and a universal substitution exists, then either every inference operation is wrong or any minimally informative theory is *already falsified*. An informal distinction between "evidential" and "definitional" unfalsifiability neither engages nor escapes this formal dilemma.   

Third, the manuscript commits an **internal inferential inconsistency** when confronting the explanatory economy of deflationary models. Addressing Knotts et al.'s (2019) parsimony argument—that a single mechanism accounts for apparent richness without postulating an extra, inaccessible category of phenomenal overflow—the manuscript concedes the economy yet denies it any epistemic weight: *"The economy is real, and by Occam's Razor Has Limits a real economy is not by itself evidence."*. This is immunization by fiat. No serious Bayesian or abductive epistemologist treats parsimony as *conclusive* proof; rather, explanatory economy serves as defeasible comparative evidence when two hypotheses account for the same observations and the ontologically heavier hypothesis yields no compensating predictive gain. Crucially, the manuscript's *own* asymmetry-in-falsifiability paragraph concedes that sparsity/grand-illusion accounts generate concrete empirical predictions whereas overflow posits non-reporting content. Conceding both superior testability *and* genuine explanatory economy to the rival hypothesis, while stripping both of comparative evidential force via an unargued anti-parsimony maxim, reduces the argument to special pleading.   

## Convenient Bracketing and Untested Competing Frameworks

A publishable analytic philosophy paper cannot neutralize live, well-developed competitors through outdated citations or one-sentence dismissals. The manuscript's framing brackets or ignores three major 2020s research programmes that directly address—and dissolve—its supposed void.

First, its treatment of **illusionism and eliminativism** is anachronistic and question-begging. Representing eliminativism through Churchland (1981) and answering illusionism via an unpaginated paraphrase of Michael Tye (2009) claiming that *"appearances presuppose consciousness"* bypasses the entire contemporary defense of introspective illusionism. François Kammerer (2021, *Synthese*; 2022b, *Philosophical Studies*; 2025, *Philosophical Studies*) develops a detailed cognitive-introspective illusionism in which "appearances" and "seemings" are generated by flawed, cognitively impenetrable introspective representations shaped by a naive theory of mind—requiring no phenomenal consciousness to appear—and demonstrates that Moorean appeals to the obviousness of phenomenality (or the claim that appearances presuppose phenomenality) beg the question against illusionism. Even more directly on point, Kammerer (2022a, *Erkenntnis*, "How rich is the illusion of consciousness?") explicitly analyzes the **rich-versus-sparse** distinction *within* illusionism, contrasting "rich illusions" (cognitively impenetrable activation of positively erroneous introspective representations) with "sparse illusions" (incomplete representations leading to erroneous judgments). Dismissing illusionism with a single unpaginated 2009 paraphrase leaves untested a framework that models the manuscript's exact explanandum without positing any epistemic void.   

Second, the manuscript leaves **Attention Schema Theory (AST)** wholly untested. Michael Graziano, Arvid Guterstam, Branden Bio, and Andrew Wilterson (2020) and Graziano (2020, 2022, *PNAS*) advance a mechanistic control-theoretic framework—explicitly synthesizing AST with global workspace, higher-order, and illusionist accounts—in which the brain constructs a continuously updated internal model of attention ("the attention schema") to predict and control attentional allocation. Because an efficient control model strips out neural mechanics, the attention schema systematically caricatures attention as a ghostly, nonphysical mental essence or vividness ("experienceness"). As attention shifts from item to item and location to location, the continuously updated schema naturally attributes vivid awareness to whichever item is currently attended. AST thus derives the manuscript's "check-and-find loop" directly from standard cybernetic control theory without invoking non-reductive mystery or structural cognitive closure.   

Third, the manuscript omits **formal physicalist models of richness and ineffability** alongside the **2020s philosophy of perceptual format**. Even though the manuscript acknowledges that Ned Block is a physicalist, it never confronts Xu Ji, Eric Elmoznino, George Deane, Yoshua Bengio, and colleagues' (2024, *Neuroscience of Consciousness*) formal physicalist account of *both* richness and ineffability. Formalizing richness via the Shannon entropy and conditional Kolmogorov complexity of high-dimensional neural trajectories, and ineffability via lossy compression into discrete working-memory attractors and low-dimensional linguistic reports, Ji et al. prove mathematically that the information content of neural trajectories strictly exceeds that of the terminal attractor states into which they collapse. Because information loss is itself a function of neural states that cognitive processing can approximately compute, a purely physical system can register its own compression loss and thereby produce a mechanistically justified report of ineffable richness and Sperling-style overflow. Concurrently, while the manuscript restricts its "format" fourth position solely to Cohen, Dennett, and Kanwisher's (2016) summary-statistics view, it omits the defining 2020s philosophical literature on perceptual format and overflow: Jake Quilty-Dunn's (2020, *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*; 2020, *Noûs*) perceptual-pluralism challenge to the assumption that Sperling's iconic memory is purely iconic rather than partly discursive; Christopher Masciari's (2021) integration of latent working memory, feature binding, scene statistics, and the refrigerator-light illusion; Hakwan Lau, Matthias Michel, Joseph LeDoux, and Stephen Fleming's (2022) mnemonic account of subjective experience; and Ned Block's own monograph *The Border Between Seeing and Thinking* (2023), which updates his iconic-format framework well beyond the 1995 and 2007 articles to which this manuscript remains confined.   

## Dispositive Weaknesses Justifying Rejection

1. **Reliance on empirically challenged and unreplicated foundational studies across both poles of the richness debate.** The manuscript builds an epistemic impasse from Sperling (1960), Simons and Chabris (1999), and Bronfman et al. (2014) while omitting 2020–2025 empirical work that undermines every one of those anchors: systematic reproductions show that Sperling/Landman participants' subjective reports do not support the overflow thesis (**Cova, F., Gaillard, M., & Kammerer, F., 2021**, "Is the phenomenological overflow argument really supported by subjective reports?" *Mind & Language*, 36(3), 422–450, DOI: `10.1111/mila.12291`; see also **Lau, H., 2022**, *In Consciousness We Trust*, Oxford University Press, DOI: `10.1093/oso/9780198856771.001.0001`); Bronfman et al.'s "cost-free" color-diversity effect fails to replicate in peripheral vision while observers accurately report reduced vividness and confidence (**Hawkins, B., Evans, D., Preston, A., Westmoreland, K., Mims, C. E., Lolo, K., Rosario, N., & Odegaard, B., 2022**, "Color diversity judgments in peripheral vision: Evidence against 'cost-free' representations," *PLOS ONE*, DOI: `10.1371/journal.pone.0279686`); and large-scale signal-detection testing (n = 5,296) exposes conservative reporting bias (c = 0.31–0.67) and residual color sensitivity (d' = 0.78) in classic inattentional blindness (**Nartker, M., Firestone, C., Egeth, H., & Phillips, I., 2025**, "Sensitivity to visual features in inattentional blindness," *eLife*, 13, RP100337, DOI: `10.7554/eLife.100337`; **Nartker et al., 2023**, *i-Perception*, 14(5), 1–6, DOI: `10.1177/20416695231198762`).
2. **Uncritical reliance on "subjective inflation" despite mathematical unidentifiability, empirical reversal, and neural dissociation.** The manuscript imports Rahnev et al. (2011) and Knotts et al. (2019) as a settled "anti-correlated confidence signal" explaining peripheral vividness, omitting mathematical proof that Rahnev et al.'s fixed-criterion model is unidentifiable and "poorly tenable" against flexible Bayesian decision rules (**Lee, J. L., Denison, R., & Ma, W. J., 2023**, "Challenging the fixed-criterion model of perceptual decision-making," *Neuroscience of Consciousness*, 2023(1), niad010, DOI: `10.1093/nc/niad010`), empirical evidence of peripheral *underconfidence* at matched orientation performance (**Toscani, M., Mamassian, P., & Valsecchi, M., 2021**, "Underconfidence in peripheral vision," *Journal of Vision*, 21(6):2, 1–14, DOI: `10.1167/jov.21.6.2`), explicit concessions regarding intra- versus post-perceptual ambiguity (**Knotts, J. D., Michel, M., & Odegaard, B., 2020**, "Defending subjective inflation: an inference to the best explanation," *Neuroscience of Consciousness*, 2020(1), niaa025, DOI: `10.1093/nc/niaa025`), and neuroimaging and registered-report evidence separating visibility, confidence, and presence/absence metacognition (**Mazor, M., Moran, R., & Fleming, S. M., 2021**, *Neuroscience of Consciousness*, 2021(1), niab005, DOI: `10.1093/nc/niab005`; **Mazor, M., Dijkstra, N., & Fleming, S. M., 2022**, *Journal of Neuroscience*, 42(12), 2562–2569, DOI: `10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1220-21.2022`).
3. **Empirical contradiction of the "check-and-find loop" as a general self-concealing mechanism.** The manuscript's central claim that *"every introspective probe for sparsity generates the detail it was meant to measure"* is directly contradicted by gaze-contingent real-world viewing experiments showing that even when observers *actively distribute attention* to search for peripheral color loss, they still miss complete desaturation beyond ~37.5°—across more than two-thirds of the visual field (**Cohen, M. A., Botch, T. L., & Robertson, C. E., 2020**, "The limits of color awareness during active, real-world vision," *PNAS*, 117(24), 13821–13827, DOI: `10.1073/pnas.1922294117`), corroborated by intact peripheral metacognitive awareness of degraded vividness (**Hawkins et al., 2022**) and foveated metamer bounds (**Broderick, W. F., Rufo, G., Winawer, J., & Simoncelli, E. P., 2023**, *eLife*, 12, RP90554, DOI: `10.7554/eLife.90554.1`).
4. **Unargued calibration skepticism and informal unfalsifiability defense disconnected from 2020s methodology of consciousness science.** The manuscript blocks third-person adjudication via blanket calibration skepticism (*"external methods inherit the problem at the point of calibration"*) and retreats to an informal "evidential vs. definitional unfalsifiability" distinction, omitting non-circular calibration theory (**Michel, M., 2023 [online 2021]**, "Calibration in consciousness science," *Erkenntnis*, 88(2), 829–850, DOI: `10.1007/s10670-021-00383-z`), iterative natural-kind bootstrapping for detecting introspective error (**McKilliam, A., 2025**, "Natural kind reasoning in consciousness science: An alternative to theory testing," *Noûs*, 59(3), 634–651, DOI: `10.1111/nous.12526`), postdictive constraints on local overflow theories (**Michel, M., & Doerig, A., 2022**, "A new empirical challenge for local theories of consciousness," *Mind & Language*, 37(5), 840–855, DOI: `10.1111/mila.12319`), formal falsification proofs (**Kleiner, J., & Hoel, E., 2021**, "Falsification and consciousness," *Neuroscience of Consciousness*, 2021(1), niab001, DOI: `10.1093/nc/niab001`; **Doerig, A., Schurger, A., & Herzog, M. H., 2021**, *Cognitive Neuroscience*, 12, 41–62, DOI: `10.1080/17588928.2020.1772214`), and multimodal adversarial testing of posterior versus prefrontal theories (**Cogitate Consortium / Ferrante, O., et al., 2025**, *Nature*, 642(8066), 133–142, DOI: `10.1038/s41586-025-08888-1`).
5. **Convenient bracketing of contemporary illusionism and complete omission of Attention Schema Theory.** By anchoring eliminativism in Churchland (1981) and brushing off illusionism via an unpaginated paraphrase of Tye (2009) (*"appearances presuppose consciousness"*), the manuscript ignores contemporary introspective illusionism that exposes that premise as question-begging and explicitly distinguishes *rich* versus *sparse* illusions (**Kammerer, F., 2022**, "How can you be so sure? Illusionism and the obviousness of phenomenal consciousness," *Philosophical Studies*, 179(9), 2845–2867, DOI: `10.1007/s11098-022-01804-7`; **Kammerer, F., 2022**, "How rich is the illusion of consciousness?" *Erkenntnis*, 87(2), 499–515, DOI: `10.1007/s10670-019-00204-4`), while leaving untested Attention Schema Theory's control-theoretic derivation of the check-and-find impression (**Graziano, M. S. A., 2022**, "A conceptual framework for consciousness," *PNAS*, 119(18), e2116933119, DOI: `10.1073/pnas.2116933119`).
6. **Omission of formal physicalist models of ineffable richness and contemporary representational-format debates, paired with a question-begging anti-parsimony fiat.** The manuscript strips explanatory economy of all evidential weight by assertion (*"a real economy is not by itself evidence"*) despite conceding superior empirical testability to sparsity models, and leaves untested formal physicalist information-theoretic proofs that mechanistically derive both Sperling overflow and justified reports of ineffable richness (**Ji, X., Elmoznino, E., Deane, G., Constant, A., Dumas, G., Lajoie, G., Simon, J., & Bengio, Y., 2024**, "Sources of richness and ineffability for phenomenally conscious states," *Neuroscience of Consciousness*, 2024(1), niae001, DOI: `10.1093/nc/niae001`) alongside the 2020s philosophical literature on iconic versus discursive formats, latent working memory, and mnemonic experience (**Quilty-Dunn, J., 2020**, "Is iconic memory iconic?" *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*, 101(3), 660–682, DOI: `10.1111/phpr.12625`; **Masciari, C. F., 2021**, *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 28(11–12), 125–147, DOI: `10.53765/20512201.28.11.125`; **Lau, H., Michel, M., LeDoux, J. E., & Fleming, S. M., 2022**, *Nature Reviews Psychology*, 1(8), 479–488, DOI: `10.1038/s44159-022-00068-6`; **Block, N., 2023**, *The Border Between Seeing and Thinking*, Oxford University Press).

**Verdict:** In its current state, "The Plenitude Void" is unfit for academic submission to any peer-reviewed philosophy journal and must be rejected outright until its pre-2020 empirical premises, outdated citation apparatus, question-begging defensive bracketing, and unengaged methodological inferences are rebuilt from the ground up against the 2020–2025 literature.
