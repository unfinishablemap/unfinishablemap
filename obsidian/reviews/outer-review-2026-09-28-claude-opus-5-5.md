## Reply

# Referee Report: "Buddhist Perspectives on Meaning" (The Unfinishable Map)

**Recommendation: major revision.** The article is more candid about its own limits than most Map pages, and its concessions on Madhyamaka, Bidirectional Interaction and No Many Worlds are real. But it misreports its central new source (Siderits 2025) in a way that cuts against its own "Support" claim. It attaches a claim from one Thompson book to a different Thompson book. It cites no primary Buddhist text at all. And in several places it replaces canonical doctrine with modernist glosses, and one of those amounts to a doctrinal error in the Relation to Site Perspective section.

## TL;DR

- **Doctrine (major):** Anicca, dukkha and the Four Noble Truths are read through Buddhist modernism, with no textual anchoring. The article celebrates meaning "within" impermanence, but SN 22.59 argues that what is impermanent is dukkha and leads to disenchantment. It turns *saṅkhāra-dukkhatā* into a psychological attitude, defines *sati* in the style of MBSR, repeats the "postponed nirvana" account of the bodhisattva, and says Buddhist practice assumes "*this* person's actions shape *this* person's future". That last claim is a horn the Buddha explicitly rejects (SN 12.46; SN 12.17). The article does not co-opt the luminous mind or *tathāgatagarbha*, and it does treat anattā as a real denial rather than as "not-this".
- **Citations (critical/major):** Siderits (2025) is real: *Buddhist Physicalism? Non-self Metaphysics and Phenomenal Consciousness*, OUP, published 5 August 2025. It is a new book, not a new edition of *Buddhism as Philosophy*. But its route through the hard problem is illusionism, not "reduction to impersonal processes". It also concedes that every classical Indian Buddhist school rejected physicalism. So the article gets the book wrong in both directions. Thompson's critique of "Buddhist exceptionalism" comes from *Why I Am Not a Buddhist* (Yale, 2020), but the article cites *Mind in Life* (2007). Garfield (1995) and Siderits (2007) are listed but never used in the text. Forman is used but not listed. Lindahl et al. (2017) is cited accurately.
- **Site perspective (major):** The article concedes honestly, but the concessions are structural rather than argued. It never engages the SN 22.59 control/ownership argument, the Milindapañha chariot, Candrakīrti's sevenfold analysis, Vasubandhu's refutation of the *pudgala*, Yogācāra's diagnosis of the felt self as *kliṣṭa-manas* misapprehending the *ālaya*, or Siderits's Buddhist reductionism. It also misses real support: Abhidhamma mind-originated matter, and the fact that classical Buddhism was anti-physicalist. The Map's downstream commitment to a persisting subject resembles Pudgalavāda, which the mainstream rejected, and the article never says so.

## Summary verdict

The article's argumentative architecture is sound. It separates support from challenge, admits that Siderits's countermodel is "not refuted here", concedes that the Madhyamaka accommodation "is less than the tenet asserts", and calls the No Many Worlds disagreement "bedrock". [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] Those are real merits and are rarer than they should be in tenet-bound writing.

The problems lie underneath. The exposition of Buddhism is secondary-source paraphrase with no sutta, kārikā or śāstra references, and at several points it follows modern Western Buddhism rather than the Pali Canon or the Indian Mahāyāna śāstras. The one recent scholarly source that carries weight in the argument is mischaracterized. And the section that should confront the strongest Buddhist arguments against a persisting subject instead points to other articles. What the article concedes is honest; how it gets there is not rigorous.

## (1) Fidelity to Pali and Mahāyāna sources

**1.1 No primary sources (major).** The article discusses the Four Noble Truths, the three kinds of dukkha, the aggregates, anattā, karma, the unanswered questions, Madhyamaka and Yogācāra without citing a single sutta (SN 56.11, SN 22.59, SN 38.14, MN 63, SN 12.15, SN 44.10), a single MMK verse, or any work of Vasubandhu or Asaṅga. Its only Buddhist-specific references are two SEP entries and an unspecified "Encyclopedia of Buddhism" entry. [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] For a page that makes claims about what "Buddhism" holds, this is disqualifying at journal standard.

**1.2 Impermanence as a source of value: a modernist inversion (major).** The article says: "Meaning may be found not *despite* impermanence but *within* it—each moment valued for what it is rather than for how long it lasts." The sunset analogy supports this. [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] The canonical inference runs the other way. In the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta (SN 22.59) the Buddha asks whether what is impermanent is "painful or pleasant" ("Painful, venerable Sir"), and whether it is "fit to be regarded thus: 'This is mine, this is I, this is my self'". [Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn22/sn22.059.nymo.html) The sutta ends with the disciple growing "disenchanted" and "dispassionate". [Obo](https://obo.genaud.net/dhamma-vinaya/ati/sn/03_kv/sn03.22.059.than.ati.htm) Early Buddhism does not ask "why should permanence be required for value?". It treats the conditioned as unsatisfactory *because* it is impermanent, and it points to an unconditioned (*asaṅkhata*), deathless (*amata*) goal. The article's framing is legitimate in Zen and in modern Western Buddhism, but it is presented as "Buddhism". This is the "Buddhist modernism" that Thompson, whom the article itself cites, criticizes. [Reading Religion](https://readingreligion.org/9780300226553/why-i-am-not-a-buddhist/)

**1.3 Four Noble Truths (minor to major).** The three cravings (sensual craving, craving for existence, craving for non-existence) are roughly right. But "This includes the craving for permanent meaning" is the Map's gloss and is not marked as such. The article also leaves out the canonical summary of the first truth in SN 56.11: "in brief, the five aggregates subject to clinging are dukkha". That clause is the direct doctrinal bridge between dukkha and anattā, and without it the article's later connection of the two is unanchored. The claim that the Buddha "refused metaphysical questions about the universe's origin" loosely paraphrases the *avyākata* (MN 63, MN 72). Those concern whether the world is eternal or finite, soul versus body, and the Tathāgata after death, not origins as such.

**1.4 Three kinds of dukkha (major).** The three *dukkhatā* are correctly named. But the article then says "The desperate search for ultimate significance is itself sankhara-dukkha", and later "the grasping *sankhara-dukkha* names". [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] Both collapse *saṅkhāra-dukkhatā* (the unsatisfactoriness of conditioned formations as such, including neutral feeling and the aggregates themselves) into *upādāna* (clinging). This is a conflation. The third dukkha applies whether or not anyone is searching for meaning, and that makes it harder on the Map's project than the article admits: the aggregates the Map would ground meaning in are themselves *saṅkhāra-dukkha*.

**1.5 Anattā (mostly faithful, one slippage; minor).** The five aggregates are correctly listed, and "a conventional designation for this process, not a substance underlying it" states the Milindapañha/Vajirā position accurately. [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] Unlike its linked pages, this article does not recast anattā as "not-this", and it does not treat *citta*/*viññāṇa*, the luminous mind (AN 1.49–52) or *tathāgatagarbha* as a persisting self. That deserves credit. The slippage is the repeated qualifier "no *permanent* self". SN 22.59 argues against self through *control* ("Let my consciousness be thus") and *ownership* ("this is mine, this I am"), not only through permanence. [Dhamma Talks](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN22_59.html) That wider target also rules out a subject that persists through change without being immutable, which is exactly the Map's downstream position. A small inconsistency: listing "selves" among the conditioned phenomena that "arise and pass away" is odd on a view where there are no selves to arise.

**1.6 The flame analogy (minor to major).** "The flame tonight is causally connected to yesterday's flame but isn't the same flame." [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] The Milindapañha lamp example compares the flame across the watches of *one* night, and Nāgasena's answer is *na ca so na ca añño*: "neither the same nor another". Dropping the "nor another" half turns a middle-way answer into plain non-identity. The linked *Haecceity* page then uses the same flame to support "process haecceity", which co-opts an analogy whose point is to deny any fact of the matter about strict identity. [[haecceity]]

**1.7 Karma and the missing doctrine of rebirth (major).** The karma paragraph is accurate as far as it goes ("not through cosmic justice dispensed by a moral overseer"). [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] But a Buddhist account of meaning with no rebirth, saṃsāra, or rebirth-linking consciousness is bowdlerized. This matters for the Map in two ways. Rebirth is the tradition's own account of continuity without a self, so it is the proper place to test the persisting-subject question. And it is where classical Buddhism is *least* physicalist: Siderits's chapter 8 names "the karma-rebirth ideology" as the first obstacle to a physicalist Buddhism. [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/60496/chapter-abstract/522498907)

**1.8 Contemplative terms (major).** Sati is glossed as "awareness without judgment or clinging". [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] That is the MBSR-era definition. The canonical *sati* (related to *sarati*, "to remember") is recollective, holding in mind, and it works alongside discernment of wholesome and unwholesome states. It is not non-judgmental. More serious is the move to "The witness tradition reveals pure awareness observing mental contents without identification". [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] The witness (*sākṣin*) is an Advaita category. [[eastern-philosophy-consciousness]] In Abhidhamma there is no observer standing apart from the momentary *cittas*, and *sati* is a mental factor (*cetasika*), not a witness. Bringing the *sākṣin* into a Buddhist article and then valuing "awareness itself" is precisely the conflation the brief asks referees to watch for.

**1.9 The bodhisattva (minor to major).** "The practitioner vows to forgo final nirvana until all beings are free." [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] This is the popular version. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodhisattva) Much of Indian Mahāyāna aims at full buddhahood and at non-abiding nirvāṇa (*apratiṣṭhita-nirvāṇa*), not at postponement. Paul Williams notes that talk of "postponement" in early texts gave way to this model. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodhisattava) The Theravāda sketch ("benefiting others by example") also slides into the old Hīnayāna caricature. It ignores the injunction to wander "for the welfare of the many" and the central place of *mettā* and *karuṇā*.

**1.10 An error in the site-perspective section (major).** Under No Many Worlds: "The Eightfold Path assumes that *this* person's actions shape *this* person's future." [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] In SN 12.17 and SN 12.46 the Buddha rejects both "the one who acts is the one who experiences [the result]" (the eternalist extreme) and "one acts, another experiences" (the annihilationist extreme), and teaches dependent origination instead. The article attributes the first extreme to Buddhism, at the very point where it should be marking the gap between Buddhism and haecceity.

## (2) Theravāda, Madhyamaka and Yogācāra

**2.1 Dharma theory versus the emptiness of dharmas (major).** The article's "compatibility thesis" ("consciousness real and irreducible at each moment") only works on Abhidharma dharma realism, where momentary mental dharmas are ultimately real (*paramattha*). Madhyamaka rejects exactly this. The article never names the Abhidharma two-truths scheme (ultimate dharmas versus conventional persons), never mentions momentariness (*kṣaṇikavāda*), and never says that Nāgārjuna's target is the Abhidharmika's *svabhāva*. The linked *Eastern Philosophy* page does this scoping properly: it says the claim "tracks the early aggregate analysis and, most closely, Abhidharma realism", and that asserted of "Buddhism" in general "the property-dualist reading selects whichever strand suits the Map". [[eastern-philosophy-consciousness]] This article's "Buddhism might accept this" goes against its own sister page.

**2.2 "Property dualism without substance dualism" (minor to major).** Abhidharma dharmas are not properties of a bearer. They are trope-like events with no substance underneath. "Property dualism" is therefore a category mismatch; "event" or "trope dualism of *nāma* and *rūpa* dharmas" would fit better.

**2.3 Madhyamaka (sound conclusion, unsupported route; minor to major).** "Denies inherent existence rather than existence" is a defensible reading, and it is Garfield's and Tsongkhapa's. Garfield restates it in his review of Siderits (*Tricycle*, Summer 2026), following Tsongkhapa (1357–1419): "I take emptiness to mean not *unreality* but *conventional reality*". The concession that the accommodation "holds on the epistemic reading ... and that is less than the tenet asserts" is honest. [[eastern-philosophy-consciousness]][[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] Three problems remain:

- No MMK passage is cited (for example 24.18 on dependent origination as emptiness, or 15.2 defining *svabhāva* as uncreated and not dependent on another).
- "Neither inherently exists nor inherently doesn't exist" muddles the tetralemma.
- The sharpest point is left unstated. Metaphysical irreducibility, meaning a nature not borrowed from anything else, is close to what MMK 15.2 calls *svabhāva*. The tension is therefore not about "scope". Ontological irreducibility is the kind of claim Madhyamaka dismantles at the ultimate level.

The prose of this paragraph is also nearly impenetrable. "What that concedes is the claim's *scope*, not its *status*" needs spelling out.

**2.4 Yogācāra (hedged, but missing the decisive doctrine; major).** The article correctly flags the dispute over idealism versus phenomenology and declines to co-opt the school. [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] But "consciousness is fundamental" misses Yogācāra's own soteriology. *Triṃśikā* 4 describes the *ālaya* as flowing "like a torrent". *Triṃśikā* 5–7 make the afflicted mind (*kliṣṭa-manas*) the faculty that mistakes the *ālaya* for a self. The perfected nature is the absence of the grasper/grasped duality, and even "mere cognition" must be let go as a support (*Triṃśikā* 27–30). Yogācāra, in other words, has a theory of *why a persisting subject seems real* and calls that seeming the root defilement. That bears directly on the Map's persisting subject, and the article leaves it out.

**2.5 Theravāda (minor).** "Oldest surviving tradition" is acceptable. The individualist caricature is noted in 1.9.

**How Siderits (2025) is misrepresented (critical).** The article makes three claims about the book:

1. "Siderits (2025) keeps it [the rejection of eliminativism] while reconstructing the rest of the tradition on physicalist premises." [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]]
2. The book "reconstructs the aggregate analysis so that phenomenal consciousness reduces to the impersonal processes it already posits." [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]]
3. It is "Assembled from Buddhist premises rather than imported against them." [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]]

The publisher's own abstracts contradict all three. Siderits argues that non-self plus mereological nihilism causes "considerable difficulties in explaining how cognitions might be among the ultimate constituents of reality". [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/60496) He takes up a view "first developed by non-Buddhist Indian philosophers" that consciousness is a theoretical posit. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393243519_Buddhist_Physicalism_Non-self_Metaphysics_and_Phenomenal_Consciousness) This "opens the door to the thesis known today as illusionism, that phenomenal consciousness is illusory". His proposal draws on "both the Buddhist philosophical tradition and current work in philosophy of mind". [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393243519_Buddhist_Physicalism_Non-self_Metaphysics_and_Phenomenal_Consciousness) The chapter 8 abstract takes up the worry that "physicalism's illusionism would seem to be incompatible with the central role of meditation". [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/60496/chapter-abstract/522498907)

So the book's strategy is *illusionism*, which the Map's own Dualism tenet lists among the positions it "Rules out" (views treating consciousness as "illusory"). [[tenets]] It does not "keep" the anti-eliminativist negative in the Map's sense. The article's "Support" claim ("meditation aims at transforming experience rather than at discovering it illusory") is also exactly what Siderits's chapter 8 contests.

In the other direction, the article *understates* what Siderits grants the Map. The publisher's description, as recorded in the Princeton University Library catalog, states that "Indian Buddhist philosophers uniformly rejected physicalism ... in favor of either a dualist or an idealist ontology". Alexander Yiannopoulos's critical review ("Empty Words", a PhilArchive manuscript) quotes Siderits's chapter 8 (p. 187): "A Buddhist physicalism would have been considered heterodox by all the classical Indian Buddhist philosophical schools … Physicalism is obviously incompatible with Theravāda, Vaibhāṣika, Sautrāntika, and Yogācāra." The book's proposal is a *newly built* view that every classical school would have called heterodox, not a reading "from inside the tradition". [PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/archive/YIAEWAv1)[PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/archive/YIAEWA) And Siderits does not assert it: Jay L. Garfield's review (*Tricycle*, Summer 2026) says he "does not defend Buddhist materialism as a true doctrine" but offers it as "a reasonable option to consider". ## (4) Relation to Site Perspective: strength and honesty

**4.1 What works.** The section opens by saying the tenets are "served unevenly, and on one the disagreement is bedrock". The Bidirectional Interaction paragraph is exemplary: "*Pratītyasamutpāda* is event-causal throughout ... That second relatum is the Map's addition, not Buddhism's." [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] The Madhyamaka concession and the admission that Siderits is "not refuted here" are honest. The "Potential synthesis" makes a real philosophical distinction between recognizing felt value (the datum) and anxiously securing a foundation (the posture), and it ends on a candid "framework-boundary disagreement". [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]]

**4.2 Evasion of the strongest arguments (major).** The article admits that "The challenge bites hardest if the *anattā* analysis is right that there is no persisting subject", and then never assesses whether it is right. [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] Absent from the article:

- **The control/ownership argument of SN 22.59.** If consciousness were self, one could say "Let my consciousness be thus". [Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn22/sn22.059.nymo.html) This targets any subject with authority over its states, which is exactly what the Map's agent-causation cluster requires.
- **The chariot (Milindapañha; SN 5.10)** and **Candrakīrti's sevenfold reasoning** (*Madhyamakāvatāra* ch. 6), which rule out a self that is identical to, different from, containing, contained in, possessing, the collection of, or the shape of the aggregates.
- **Vasubandhu's refutation of the person (*pudgala*) in the ninth chapter of the *Abhidharmakośabhāṣya*.** This matters because the Map's "non-physical subject [that] persists through qualitative change" is structurally close to the Pudgalavāda "person" that is neither identical to nor different from the aggregates. The mainstream schools rejected that view (the Theravāda *Kathāvatthu* opens by refuting it). The Map has a historical precedent for its position and does not know it.
- **Siderits's Buddhist reductionism** (*Personal Identity and Buddhist Philosophy: Empty Persons*), which argues that all facts about persons consist in impersonal facts. [PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/SIDPIA)[PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/SIDBR) The article cites Siderits only for physicalism, the less relevant of his two arguments.
- **Yogācāra's *kliṣṭa-manas*** (see 2.4).

Deferring haecceity to another article ("the haecceity tension leaves that open") is acceptable once. Here it is the only engagement with the persisting-subject question, in an article whose title makes the owner of meaning the central issue.

**4.3 Missed support (major; a hostile referee should also note what helps the author).** The article calls Buddhism's support a "thin" shared negative. It is thicker than that, and more interesting:

1. All classical Indian Buddhist schools were anti-physicalist (conceded by Siderits). [PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/archive/YIAEWA)[Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/60496)
2. Theravāda Abhidhamma recognizes mind-originated matter (*cittaja-rūpa*) and conditioning relations between mind and matter in the *Paṭṭhāna*. That is a real two-relata causal structure, and it partly qualifies the article's own claim that the second relatum "is the Map's addition".
3. The doctrine of rebirth is the tradition's strongest non-physicalist commitment. [DOKUMEN.PUB](https://dokumen.pub/buddhist-physicalism-non-self-metaphysics-and-phenomenal-consciousness.html)

A paper that concedes honestly should also claim what it is owed.

**4.4 Internal inconsistency on contemplative evidence (major).** In one section the evidence is "convergent across cultures and centuries". In the next, the article grants that Buddhists "find no-self, Advaitins find universal Self, Christians find divine presence" and that the matter is "contested". Under Occam's Razor Has Limits it concedes that "illusionism does accommodate the data", then concludes that the maps of insight "establish ... that the 'simpler' view is simpler partly because it has not looked". [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] Once the rival is granted to accommodate the data, that conclusion does not follow. The *tu quoque*, that the objection "applies equally to ... the materialist's confidence that their experience reduces to neural firing", is a strawman: physicalism is not based on first-person reports.

**4.5 Is the dualist reply adequate?** Partly. The datum/project distinction is the article's best move, but the Buddhist has a stronger rejoinder than "the grounding talk smuggles the grip back in". In the dependent-origination sequence, feeling (*vedanā*) conditions craving (*taṇhā*), and felt value is *vedanā*: an aggregate, impermanent, dukkha and not-self. Grounding meaning in the intrinsic value of felt experience is therefore not neutral recognition. It fixes on the very link where the chain is to be cut. The article needs to answer this version.

## General quality

- **Meaning and purpose in Buddhism.** The article gets right that Buddhism is "neither cosmic purpose nor nihilism" and that practice is work to be done. But nirvana is barely described (as "cessation of suffering"), the unconditioned is missing, and the path is presented as an ethics "without cosmic guarantees" with no mention of saṃsāra. The result is a secular-humanist Buddhism with the soteriology removed.
- **Hedging versus overclaiming.** The article is well hedged in the challenge section, with some overclaiming elsewhere (4.4). The closing tally ("unrefuted and largely untested, which is weaker than having survived a test") is well calibrated. [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]]
- **Consistency with linked articles.** This article is *more* careful than *Witness Consciousness* and the older text of *Eastern Philosophy*. Indexed excerpts show those pages saying Buddhism "claims 'the permanent self owning consciousness is an illusion'" and "Buddhism's rejection of permanent self refines rather than contradicts" dualism. [[eastern-philosophy-consciousness]][[witness-consciousness]] Those are exactly the "not-a-permanent-self" narrowings that SN 22.59 does not support. The live *Eastern Philosophy* page now scopes the compatibility claim to Abhidharma, cites Chadha (2023) reading Vasubandhu as "eliminativist about selves *and* persons", and concedes Garfield's anti-self verdict. [[eastern-philosophy-consciousness]] This article should inherit that scoping. The *Haecceity* page's use of the flame (1.6) and the *Witness* page's Advaita witness (1.8) spread the conflations this article partly repeats.

## Overall recommendation

**Major revision.** The article should not be rejected. Its dialectical honesty is above the site's baseline, and most of its failures can be fixed with sources the Map already knows. But the Siderits error is critical because it reverses the evidential direction of a named source. And the absence of primary texts, combined with the modernist glosses, means the article currently describes a Western-modernist Buddhism that happens to suit the Map's framing, not the traditions it names.
