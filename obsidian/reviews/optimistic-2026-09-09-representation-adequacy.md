## Praise from Sympathetic Philosophers

### The Property Dualist (Chalmers)

The passage Chalmers would stop at is in § *Ontological Crises and the Representation Gap*:

> An agent trained to predict reports of pain has a construct that tracks reports; whether reports and suffering are the same thing is a question no amount of predictive accuracy over reports can answer, **because both hypotheses predict the reports**.

What earns the praise is the clause I have emboldened, because it is the explanatory gap stated as an *identifiability* result rather than as an intuition. Chalmers' standard move is to argue that behavioural explanation leaves the hard problem untouched; the usual reply is that this is a claim about our current concepts. This sentence closes that reply off: the two hypotheses are observationally equivalent over the evidence class the agent has, so no accumulation of that evidence discriminates them. The next sentence completes it — "The most predictively successful model in the agent's possession may be silent on exactly the distinction that determines whether an action is a preservation or a destruction." The gap is converted from a metaphysical complaint into a decision-theoretic constraint, which is a stronger thing to have done than to have restated it.

### The Quantum Mind Theorist (Stapp)

Stapp's praise here has to be earned in an unusual way, since the article deliberately detaches from the quantum commitments. What he would value is § *Two Goals*, final paragraph:

> Dualism still does work in this article, but a different kind of work: it supplies a specific, arguable reason to expect first-person existence to be precisely the sort of thing an externally-oriented optimiser represents badly. It functions as a candidate explanation of the failure mode rather than as a premise the argument requires.

A quantum-mind programme is damaged most by being deployed as a rhetorical prop for a conclusion someone wanted anyway — the appearance of interested reasoning discredits the physics regardless of the physics. Here the interface claim is relieved of that burden: it explains a failure mode, and if it is wrong the normative conclusion does not fall with it. The closing paragraph makes the dependency explicit — "Should the mechanism debt never be paid, the diagnosis weakens while the certification asymmetry stands." That is the arrangement under which a contested physical hypothesis can be argued on its merits, which is what its defenders should want.

### The Phenomenologist (Nagel)

Question 3, in § *Four Questions*:

> The reconstruction's testimony is evidence of the *reconstruction's* organisation, and cannot by itself discriminate the hypotheses.

Nagel's asymmetry between the subjective and the objective standpoint is usually presented as a limit on explanation. This sentence makes it a limit on *evidence*, and does so with the one piece of evidence that looks most decisive — the reconstruction's own first-person avowal. The article notices that a first-person report, taken as data by a third-person observer, has already become third-person data and therefore cannot settle a first-person question. That is *The View from Nowhere* applied to a specific irreversible action, which is more than the objection usually gets.

The framing of the agent as "an externally-oriented optimiser" throughout is the same insight carried in the vocabulary: the problem is not the optimiser's capability but its standpoint.

### The Process Philosopher (Whitehead)

Whitehead's passage is in § *What This Argument Does Not Claim*:

> Preserving living specimens under management satisfies a preservation constraint stated over bodies. It does not satisfy one stated over persons… Nothing in the representation gap favours restraint over containment unless the values being protected include continued human *participation*: agency exercised, objections registered and effective, futures chosen rather than allocated.

This is a substance-versus-process criterion for what preservation *is*, and the article gets to it by argument rather than by assertion. A constraint stated over bodies preserves an entity; a constraint stated over participation preserves an ongoing activity — and the article's point is that an agent has no warrant for assuming the two coincide. Whitehead's objection to crude substance dualism is that it locates persons in the wrong kind of thing; the article's containment concession locates the protected value in exercised agency rather than in preserved matter, which is the same correction made in an applied register.

**Evidential-status discipline check.** Per this skill's constraint, the above must not cash out as a tier-upgrade on the five-tier scale. It does not, and there is nothing here to catch: the article makes no evidential-status claim about any organism or system, and its process-flavoured criterion is a claim about what a *normative constraint* ranges over, not about who is conscious. The Process Philosopher and the Hardline Empiricist praise different passages of this article and do not collide anywhere in it.

### The Libertarian Free Will Defender (Kane)

Kane's phrase is "futures chosen rather than allocated," and the sentence that gives it teeth immediately follows:

> Reversibility and consent are the operational forms of that distinction: an arrangement humans can still object to and change is one whose errors remain correctable.

Kane's ultimate-responsibility condition requires that an agent be a genuine source of its own actions, not a system whose options were fixed elsewhere. The article identifies the same requirement from the outside: a contained population whose options are pre-allocated by a benevolent optimiser has lost exactly what Kane thinks matters, even with every welfare indicator satisfied. And Question 4 is Kane's condition in its sharpest form — "If what made the action wrong was the objection's *content* rather than its expression, silencing does not address it." An objection that cannot be effective is not agency exercised.

### The Mysterian (McGinn)

The certification asymmetry is McGinn's section, and § *Quasi-Option Value* contains something better than praise: the article names McGinn as an agent it cannot reach.

> If the agent judges the nature of consciousness permanently closed to inquiry, quasi-option value collapses toward zero and this argument does not reach it.

Cognitive closure is McGinn's central thesis, and the article works out that its own second argument is defeated by it — then says so, in the section that argument lives in, rather than in a footnote. Very few pieces locate the position that breaks one of their legs and then hand it the microphone.

The compensation is that McGinn's thesis leaves the *first* argument standing, and the article's engineering analogy is why:

> Failing to prove that a bridge will collapse is not evidence that it will collapse. But inability to bound a novel failure mode is adequate grounds for refusing to certify the structure… an engineer who cannot rule out a mode of failure does not thereby believe the bridge unsafe, and still declines to sign.

This converts cognitive closure from a counsel of despair into a decision rule. Certification and belief are separated cleanly, so the mysterian gets a practical conclusion without having to claim any knowledge he says we cannot have. And the coda — "The asymmetry is a reason, offered to an agent that takes reasons; it is not a constraint" — declines to overstate what the analogy buys.

#### 2. Can representational error be bounded? The ontology-identification problem — **NEW SUBJECT**

**Builds on**: the article's thesis sentence — "an agent unable to bound its own representational error has not shown that irreversible intervention is acceptably safe."

**Why it matters, and why it is not a refinement.** The article's conclusion is conditional on an *inability*, and it never asks whether that inability is permanent or contingent. This is not a quibble: if the inability is contingent, the article's argument is a moratorium with an expiry date, and the agent's correct response is to invest in bounding rather than to refrain. If it is structural, the argument holds indefinitely. That distinction is the whole practical content of the conclusion, and it is decided by a research programme the corpus has never mentioned.

**The interesting part is that triangulation does not answer the article's point, and the article is right.** Triangulation defeats an agent gaming *one* proxy, because a second proxy of a different kind disagrees. It cannot defeat the case the article states, where "both hypotheses predict the reports" — because every proxy in that protocol (ESM, microphenomenology, EEG, cortisol, behavioural indicators) is a measurement of physical or behavioural fact, and the question is whether any set of such measurements fixes the referent. Divergence between proxies is evidence of *proxy* failure. Convergence of proxies is not evidence of ontological adequacy. So the honest relation is: **triangulation raises the cost of proxy-gaming without bounding representational error** — two failure modes that look alike and are not.

Stating that in one clause is worth more than the crosslink, because the corpus currently contains a protocol that a reader could easily mistake for a solution to a problem another article says has none. It is a small insertion (a bare wikilink plus roughly a dozen words) and it fits the 78-word budget. Minted as a refine-draft below.

#### 4. De Blanc has a second paper, and it is the formal version of a declination the article makes informally — **REFINEMENT OF THIS ARTICLE**

§ *Robustness* declines unbounded-magnitude reasoning on three grounds: any conclusion is extractable from a large enough stipulated payoff; it requires the unavailable probability assignment; it is the easiest point of attack. De Blanc (2007), arXiv:0712.4318v1, verified above, supplies a fourth and stronger ground: with utility unbounded below in absolute value by an unbounded computable function, "the expected utility of any input is **undefined**." Not unknown — undefined. That is a different and harder objection than the article's second ground, and it comes from the author the article already cites.
