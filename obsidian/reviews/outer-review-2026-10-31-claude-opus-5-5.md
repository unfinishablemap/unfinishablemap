## Reply

# Referee Report: “Emotion Regulation and the Timing of Control” (The Unfinishable Map)

**Verdict: accept with major revisions.** The article describes its core citations accurately. Its deflationary conclusion is honest: where a conscious selector would sit is “fit, not evidence”. [topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control/) The problems are in what it leaves out. It skips the emotion-regulation-choice and valuation/reinforcement-learning literature, which directly models the stages where it places the selector. It also skips the field-validity, cultural and cost evidence that would complicate its “earlier is easier” discussion. And several neighbouring Map pages still argue things this article has abandoned.

## TL;DR

- **Citations hold up well.** Every quotation I could check against a primary source matched: Sheppes & Gross 2011, Webb, Miles & Sheeran 2012, Sheppes & Meiran 2008, Gross 2015, Koole & Rothermund 2011, Torre & Lieberman 2018, Bonanno & Burton 2013 and Webb, Schweiger Gallo et al. 2012. One quotation (Duckworth, Gendler & Gross 2016, “as a rule, earlier intervention is best”) could not be confirmed verbatim. One paraphrase (Gyurak et al.’s “proceeds without awareness”) may drop a hedge.
- **The treatment of “earlier is easier” is fair in direction but thin.** The article correctly says the generic timing hypothesis has not survived. It omits the cultural moderators of suppression’s costs (Butler, Lee & Gross 2007), the gap between lab and everyday life (Wilms et al. 2020), reappraisal’s own costs, and how the Webb meta-analysis mixes different outcome types.
- **The weakest point is that the selector is placed in the science’s gaps.** It sits exactly at the identification and selection stages, which Sheppes’s choice paradigm, Etkin, Büchel & Gross’s (2015) model-based/model-free framework and Doré, Weber & Ochsner’s (2017) neural-prediction work already model mechanistically. On the site, “Trilemma of Selection” still counts “depletion that requires recovery” as evidence for a selecting consciousness, [topics/trilemma-of-selection](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/trilemma-of-selection/) which contradicts this article and the Map’s own handling of the ego-depletion replications.

## 2. Fairness of the “Earlier Is Easier” Treatment

**Credit where due.** This is not a cherry-pick in the dualist’s favour. The article goes *against* the simple story. It reports Sheppes & Gross’s retreat from the generic timing hypothesis, the Webb result that pooled effectiveness does not follow stage order, the Paul et al. evidence that a family label does not fix timing, and the depletion caveat on Sheppes & Meiran 2008. [topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control/) Flagging that the 2008 “cost” inherits the ego-depletion record is better than most secondary literature does.

**Where it falls short:**

1. **Conflated outcomes.** “Pooled effectiveness does not follow stage order” relies on Webb et al. pooling experiential, behavioural and physiological outcomes. [google](https://scholar.google.com/scholar_lookup) Response modulation’s small positive d+ (0.16) is partly suppression changing the *expression* it targets. Gross 1998b’s foundational finding is that suppression does little for felt experience and raises sympathetic arousal. [nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3058745)[johnnietfeld](http://www.johnnietfeld.com/uploads/2/2/6/0/22606800/gross_2015.pdf) The article cites 1998b without reporting this. A reader leaves thinking response-focused strategies are “small but positive” without learning what they are positive *on*.
2. **Heterogeneity and the attentional-deployment null.** The article notes the distraction/concentration split, to its credit. But the family null (0.00) is an average of opposite-signed tactics, so “attentional deployment had no effect” says little about timing at all. The article should say so outright rather than use the null as evidence against stage order.
3. **Cultural moderators are missing.** Butler, Lee & Gross (2007) found that suppression’s “deleterious effects were reduced when individuals with more Asian values suppressed”, mediated by the suppressors’ responsiveness. [semanticscholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Emotion-regulation-and-culture:-are-the-social-of-Butler-Lee/1c222f5a1464eb8dc7c2cd1281968033ca511f18) Any claim about the relative cost of response-focused regulation needs this qualifier.
4. **Ecological validity is missing.** Wilms et al. (2020, *Frontiers in Psychology*) concluded that “participants regulated their negative emotions in largely different ways in the laboratory as compared with everyday life”. [frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00877/xml) Field evidence on intensity-driven choice is mixed: in one study, intensity was unrelated to distraction use. [springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42761-021-00087-1) The timing findings the article leans on come from short film or picture paradigms with instructed strategies and instructed onset times.
5. **Reappraisal’s costs are under-reported.** Late reappraisal’s cost is reported, but not the wider “reappraisal reconsidered” literature (Ford & Troy 2019). [springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42761-022-00166-x)[brettqford](https://www.brettqford.com/publications) Nor does the article report Ortner, Ste Marie & Corno (2016, *PLoS ONE* 11(12):e0167253), who found that “higher intensity stimuli were associated with greater cognitive costs of reappraisal, for unpleasant, but not pleasant pictures”, and that “greater habitual reappraisal predicted lower cognitive costs of reappraisal”.
6. **Automatic regulation without the usual costs is missing.** Mauss, Cook & Gross (2007, *J Exp Soc Psychol* 43(5):698–711) found that “priming emotion control reduces negative emotion experience without maladaptive cardiovascular responding”. This bears directly on the effort and timing story and on where the Map wants to place conscious control.

**Verdict on section 2:** balanced in direction, incomplete in scope. It is not cherry-picked toward the tenets, but it is cherry-picked toward lab paradigms from the Gross/Sheppes school.

## 3. Missed or Under-Weighted Counterarguments

**What the article does well:** it states repeatedly that the placement is “fit, not evidence”. It says “every such stage is modelled without an experiential term”. It cites the Map’s own warning that “The fit discriminates nothing”. And it concedes that Occam’s Razor “supplies no reason to posit one where those models already suffice”. [topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control/) That candour is rare and should be kept.

**What it brackets too conveniently:**

1. **The gaps are being filled, and the article places the selector in them anyway.** The selector goes at goal-setting, identification, strategy selection and pre-episode policy, [topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control/) which are the stages least modelled in the *process-model* literature the article reads. But they are exactly what the choice paradigm (Sheppes 2011/2014/2020), valuation/reinforcement-learning accounts (Etkin et al. 2015, where arbitration between model-based and model-free control can work by the reliability of each system’s predictions) and neural prediction of regulation decisions (Doré et al. 2017) address. [researchgate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/283286862_The_neural_bases_of_emotion_regulation) Disclaiming evidential weight does not answer the structural worry: the location was picked because it is where the rival account is least developed. That is the god-of-the-gaps pattern, even if the article is candid about it. The article should name the pattern and commit to a test that could shrink the slot.
2. **Treating “no experiential term” as if it meant “no consciousness” is a straw man.** Global workspace and higher-order accounts give *conscious access* a functional causal role. An explicit goal is held in the workspace, broadcast and kept up by top-down control. Those theories therefore explain why explicit and implicit regulation differ *without* dualism. The article’s asymmetry argument (“an automatic route does not show the deliberate route contributes nothing”) [topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control/) is logically sound, but physicalists accept it too. It does not separate dualism from functionalism, and the article does not say so.
3. **Active inference and predictive processing are missing.** Regulation as policy selection under expected free energy is a ready mechanistic home for “moments of policy”. The Map’s own `consciousness-selecting-neural-patterns` page cites active inference’s “policy selection” (Sandved-Smith et al. 2021), [concepts/consciousness-selecting-neural-patterns](https://unfinishablemap.org/concepts/consciousness-selecting-neural-patterns/) yet this article does not engage it.
4. **Self-report and post-hoc confabulation.** Strategy “selection” in field studies and many lab studies is known through self-report. The article cites the Map’s P-A4 (efficacy cannot be verified from the first person) [topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control/) but does not apply it to the *data*. If people’s reports of which strategy they chose are partly reconstructed after the fact (the Nisbett-and-Wilson worry, choice-blindness-type results), the selection stage is evidentially weaker than it looks.
5. **Causal closure and the Stapp example.** The article down-weights Schwartz–Stapp–Beauregard well: it says the data have a physical route (Buhle et al. 2014) and that the example adds no independent evidence stream. But its tenet section claims Minimal Quantum Interaction’s minimality is “fixed by the empirical record”, [topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control/) without engaging the decoherence-timescale objection or the closure argument. Reappraisal works over seconds through prefrontal-to-limbic pathways, and the article never explains why a Zeno-type mechanism would be needed at that timescale.
6. **Timing data underdetermine the metaphysics.** The article says this, which is a credit. It should also say the converse plainly: no result in this literature *could* count for the selector as framed. That makes the “location” a description, not a hypothesis. If the Map wants a hypothesis, its own `attention-and-the-consciousness-interface` page offers one: felt effort should scale with the entropy of the option set at threshold. [topics/attention-and-the-consciousness-interface](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/attention-and-the-consciousness-interface/) That prediction could be tested in a regulation-choice paradigm.
7. **Implementation intentions point both ways.** The article treats pre-episode plans as the selector’s best-evidenced point. But their theorised mechanism is *strategic automaticity*, which moves control from in-the-moment deliberation to cue-triggered automatic responses. On that reading, the stage with “the clearest evidence” is evidence that conscious control works by making itself unnecessary at the moment of regulation.

**Honesty assessment:** high on stated conclusions, middling on search scope. The article is honest about what its evidence shows. It is less honest by omission about how much mechanistic modelling already covers the stages it reserves.
