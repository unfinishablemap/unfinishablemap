The 24 October review corrected the two clearest source-fidelity problems: the interpretation of Sheppes and Meiran’s depletion result and the description of Ochsner et al. Its two most important empirical conclusions nevertheless exceed the evidence:

1. **“Pooled effectiveness does not follow stage order”** is not established by Webb, Miles and Sheeran. Their meta-analysis compared outcomes associated with strategy categories; it did not experimentally manipulate when the same strategy began. It shows that nominally earlier strategy families are not uniformly more effective, not that temporal order itself is unrelated to effectiveness. [topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control/)

Newer work also makes the article’s preferred examples—emotional-conflict adaptation and affect labelling—considerably less secure than the article acknowledges. ## 2. Citation-by-citation audit

### 1. Bonanno and Burton (2013)

The article accurately reports their rejection of uniformly good and bad strategies. It underuses the paper, however: Bonanno and Burton’s framework distinguishes context sensitivity, regulatory repertoire and responsiveness to feedback. These are directly relevant to the proposed selector, especially to deciding whether to maintain, switch or stop regulation.

The citation is valid but no longer sufficient as the article’s principal authority on flexibility. A 2024 two-culture ecological study found context sensitivity and repertoire associated with lower momentary distress, while feedback responsiveness produced more conditional results. More recent work warns that mere strategy variability is not flexibility and may predict greater distress when the context has not changed. [Nature+2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44220-024-00215-3)

**Verdict:** accurate, important, but empirically outdated when used alone.

### 2. Braunstein, Gross and Ochsner (2017)

The two-axis representation is accurate: explicitness of the regulatory goal and automaticity or control of the change process are separable dimensions. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5647798/)

The article then draws too much from it. An implicit or nonconscious **regulatory goal** is not equivalent to an episode containing no conscious contribution at any stage. A participant may consciously perceive the stimulus, consciously feel the emotion, consciously perform a task, or experience conflict without representing the higher-order intention “I am regulating my emotion”. Likewise, an automatic implementation process may execute a consciously adopted policy.

**Verdict:** source accurately represented; philosophical inference invalid.

### 3. Buhle et al. (2014)

The description of reappraisal as cognitive control altering semantic representations, with associated modulation of amygdala responses, is accurate. It supplies a legitimate physically implemented rival to the Stapp interpretation. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23765157/)

The article uses this citation properly: as evidence that the neuroimaging record does not discriminate between a conventional neural account and the Map’s proposed additional selector.

**Verdict:** accurate, relevant and appropriately calibrated.

### 4. Duckworth, Gendler and Gross (2016)

The “earlier intervention is best” formulation is accurately attributed. The article also correctly warns that Duckworth et al. are discussing self-control more generally rather than experimentally resolving the timing of emotion-regulation strategies.

The unresolved “asymmetry” is therefore not really an empirical contradiction. Duckworth et al. offer a policy heuristic: arranging situations before temptation intensifies will often be cheaper than resisting an established impulse. Sheppes and Gross offer a strategy-specific account of what happens once an emotional episode and a particular regulatory tactic are specified. Both could be true.

**Verdict:** quotation accurate; juxtaposition is illuminating but should not be presented as an unresolved empirical contest between equivalent claims.

### 5. Goldin et al. (2008)

The article accurately reports the sample—17 women—the early prefrontal response associated with reappraisal and the later response associated with suppression. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17888411/)

The article appropriately notes the small, single-site sample. More fundamentally, the result compares strategies with different instructions and targets. It does not show that moving the same strategy earlier caused the difference, nor that early neural engagement is the cause of superior regulation.

**Verdict:** accurate local description; limited evidential role.

### 6–7. Gross (1998a, 1998b)

The five strategy families and the antecedent-focused/response-focused distinction are accurately described. The page is particularly good in warning that a family’s nominal target does not uniquely determine when an actual instance begins to operate. [topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control/)

The danger is that the rest of the article intermittently returns to treating the families as positions in an empirical temporal sequence. The process model classifies strategies by the point in emotion generation that they initially target; it is not a stopwatch ordering every implementation of every tactic.

**Verdict:** accurate account, but not consistently respected in the later meta-analytic argument.

### 8. Gross (2015)

Identification, selection and implementation are accurately attributed to the extended process model. The article’s distinction between the emotion-generation clock and the regulation-decision clock is one of its strongest conceptual contributions. [topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control/)

However, the contemporary presentation of the model normally includes **monitoring** as a fourth stage or as the iterative process governing the first three: maintain the present strategy, switch strategies, or stop regulating. Monitoring was already explicitly discussed in the literature before 2020 and is central to current presentations of the model. [PubMed Central (PMC)+2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7137720/)

Omitting monitoring matters philosophically. It leaves unexamined one of the most plausible locations for conscious evaluation and gives the selector another place to which it can later be relocated.

**Verdict:** historically defensible but incomplete and no longer current.

### 9. Gross and Feldman Barrett (2011)

The continuum between models that sharply distinguish emotion generation from regulation and constructionist models that do not is accurately presented. The article correctly notes that neither co-author simply concedes the other’s theoretical position. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21479078/)

This is not a minor caveat. If generation and regulation are aspects of one continuously updating construction process, the article’s central spatial metaphor—finding where control “enters”—may be misleading. There may be no independently generated emotion into which regulation subsequently intervenes.

**Verdict:** accurate, but the challenge should restructure more of the article rather than occupy one short section.

### 10. Gross and John (2003)

The article correctly treats the study as evidence about individual differences and habitual strategy use, not as a test of the process model’s temporal ordering.

**Verdict:** accurate and properly scoped.

### 11. Gyurak, Gross and Etkin (2011)

The article accurately represents the authors’ dual-process framework and their treatment of emotional-conflict adaptation as an uninstructed form of regulation.

The empirical status of that paradigm has since weakened. A 2024 secondary analysis, systematic review and meta-analysis of 94 emotional-conflict studies could not fully reproduce the resolution or adaptation effects in its own data; only 28% of the reviewed studies reported the relevant analyses, and the pooled effects were small and sensitive to contingency learning. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38546549/)

Even a robust conflict-adaptation effect would establish behavioural adjustment without an explicit instruction to regulate. It would not by itself establish absence of awareness, conscious conflict processing or any phenomenal contribution.

**Verdict:** historically accurate; currently overstated and dependent on a contested paradigm.

### 12. Kappas (2011)

The claim that unregulated emotion is not a realistic practical category is accurately attributed. This is a theoretical position, not an empirical result.

**Verdict:** accurate and clearly presented as theoretical.

### 13. Koole and Rothermund (2011)

The broad definition of implicit regulation is accurately reproduced. But using that definition as evidence risks circularity: once “implicit emotion regulation” is defined as a process not needing conscious supervision or explicit intentions, examples classified under the definition cannot independently prove that consciousness is absent.

“Aims to change an emotional response” also raises an attribution problem. Is the “aim” represented by the person, implemented by a learned control system, or assigned by the theorist because the process has a regulatory function? Those alternatives have different implications for a selector.

**Verdict:** quotation accurate; evidential use confuses a theoretical definition with an experimental demonstration.

### 14. Morawetz et al. (2017)

The common activation of left ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, anterior insula and supplementary motor area across strategies is accurately reported. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27894828/)

This supports shared neural-control components. It neither tests temporal ordering nor establishes that experience is absent from those processes. The article mostly respects the first limitation but occasionally uses the general fact that neural models omit an experiential variable as though this were evidence of experiential dispensability.

**Verdict:** accurate; metaphysical relevance limited.

### 15. Ochsner et al. (2002)

Following the 24 October correction, the account of the experiment is accurate: reappraisal was associated with prefrontal changes, reduced negative affect and modulation of amygdala and orbitofrontal responses. [PubMed+1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12495527/)

The article clearly distinguishes Ochsner’s interpretation from Schwartz, Stapp and Beauregard’s later quantum interpretation. That is good source discipline.

**Verdict:** accurate after correction.

### 16. Paul et al. (2013)

The statement that expressive suppression affected responding as early as distraction is accurate. The authors’ preventive-use interpretation is now appropriately described as a suggestion rather than a demonstrated mechanism. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23747981/)

This is genuine evidence against identifying a strategy family with a fixed clock-time onset. It does not show that early and late uses are equally effective, effortful or durable.

**Verdict:** accurate and appropriately qualified.

### 17. Schwartz, Stapp and Beauregard (2005)

The article accurately identifies Ochsner’s reappraisal experiment as a worked example in their quantum-Zeno proposal and correctly labels the proposal a posit rather than an inference forced by the data. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1569494/)

The section remains disproportionately long relative to its empirical significance. A speculative reinterpretation of an otherwise conventionally explained neuroimaging result should not occupy nearly as much space as the missing contemporary literatures on choice, monitoring and flexibility.

**Verdict:** source-accurate and commendably disclaimed, but over-prominent.

### 18. Seneca, *De Ira*

The textual attribution and three-movement sequence appear accurate. The article explicitly states that Seneca was a corporealist, that the movements do not map directly onto Gross’s stages, and that the parallel is not evidence.

**Verdict:** legitimate historical analogy; no empirical weight claimed.

### 19. Sheppes and Gross (2011)

The generic and process-specific timing hypotheses are accurately represented. The article also correctly notes that this literature frequently operationalises timing through **emotional intensity**, not merely elapsed seconds. [people.socsci.tau.ac.il](https://people.socsci.tau.ac.il/mu/galsheppes/files/2016/10/Sheppes-G.-Gross-J.J.-2011.-Is-Timing-Everything-Temporal-Considerations-in-Emotion-Regulation.pdf)

That point should be made more forcefully. Emotion intensity, elapsed time, regulatory preparation, process-model target and latency of an observed effect are not interchangeable. The page currently acknowledges this locally but combines them again in its overall verdict.

**Verdict:** accurate citation; conceptual distinctions insufficiently propagated.

### 20. Sheppes and Meiran (2007)

The findings that late distraction remained effective and late reappraisal became less effective are accurately described. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17933748/)

The evidential scope is narrow: a particular sadness-film paradigm, particular instructions and particular definitions of early and late initiation. “Point of no return” should remain a strategy- and paradigm-specific hypothesis, not a general property of emotions.

**Verdict:** accurate but easy to overgeneralise.

### 21. Sheppes and Meiran (2008)

The article now accurately identifies the claimed cognitive cost as worse subsequent Stroop performance relative to late distraction, with small groups, a one-tailed test and no unregulated control condition. The page properly connects this with the replication problems surrounding the resource interpretation of ego depletion. [PubMed+1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19102598/)

The study can still contribute evidence that late reappraisal differs from late distraction. It cannot securely establish that late reappraisal consumes a unitary self-control resource.

**Verdict:** accurate after revision; appropriately downgraded in the main argument.

### 22. Thiruchselvam et al. (2011)

The immediate EEG result—distraction altered the late positive potential earlier than reappraisal—is accurately reported. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21354262/)

The article omits an important longer-horizon result: on later passive re-exposure, images previously processed through distraction elicited a larger LPP than images previously reappraised. Immediate speed and durable regulation therefore came apart. That directly supports the article’s general contextualism but complicates any simple advantage assigned to earlier or faster intervention.

**Verdict:** immediate result accurate; consequential follow-up result omitted.

### 23. Torre and Lieberman (2018)

The review accurately presented affect labelling as a candidate form of implicit regulation at the time. It is not an adequate current summary.

Later experiments found that naming an emotion before reappraisal or acceptance could make those strategies less effective. A 2026 two-study replication with 226 participants reproduced the reappraisal impairment and found no delayed benefit; another 2026 study found no sustained advantage of labelling over matching. [PubMed+2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36043172/)

This does not show that affect labelling never regulates. It shows that the literature is heterogeneous and that labelling should not be presented as an uncomplicated example of successful consciousness-free regulation.

**Verdict:** historically accurate, currently seriously incomplete.

### 24. Webb, Miles and Sheeran (2012)

The effect sizes are accurately reported: attentional deployment approximately zero overall, response modulation small, cognitive change small-to-medium, with distraction positive and concentration negative. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22582737/)

The article’s inference is the main problem. The meta-analysis coded strategies and pooled their effects on experiential, behavioural and physiological outcomes. It did not manipulate early versus late initiation within strategies. Consequently:

- It undermines a crude inference from strategy-family label to effectiveness.
- It demonstrates large within-family heterogeneity.
- It does **not** show that effectiveness “does not follow temporal order” in a causal sense.
- It cannot decide whether initiating a given tactic earlier would have improved that tactic’s outcome.

**Verdict:** numerical claims accurate; central inferential use overstated.

### 25. Webb, Schweiger Gallo et al. (2012)

The reported emotion-specific implementation-intention estimates appear source-faithful. They support the effectiveness of pre-episode planning relative to the comparison conditions used in that literature.

A much larger 2024 synthesis of 642 tests reported conventional effects varying from about 0.27 to 0.66 across outcome categories, but its robust Bayesian model-averaged estimate was approximately $d=0.15$, with strong evidence of publication bias and heterogeneity. That broader synthesis is not a direct replacement for the earlier emotion-specific estimates, but it requires the article to temper its language about the “clearest evidence”. [DOI+1](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563)

Most importantly, evidence that an if–then plan works does not demonstrate that the plan was formed by a phenomenally conscious process, still less by a nonphysical selector. It identifies an effective intervention point.

**Verdict:** old estimates probably accurate; interpretation and current calibration need revision.

## 3. The missing post-2020 literature

### Regulation choice

The largest omission is Matthews et al.’s 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of regulation choice. It identified 18 determinants across 219 studies, grouped into affective, cognitive, motivational, individual and social-cultural categories. Decisions about whether and how to regulate were associated with properties of the person, the emotion, the immediate situation and the broader social context. [PubMed+1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34165040/)

This evidence is directly relevant to the proposed selector. It suggests that “choice of strategy” is not a single experimentally isolated act but an outcome influenced by:

- emotional intensity and valence;
- perceived effort and available affordances;
- salient short- and long-term goals;
- habitual or default strategies;
- individual differences;
- interpersonal and cultural context.

That distributed determination does not disprove conscious selection. It does weaken the impression that the literature has identified an otherwise unexplained unitary choice point waiting for a nonphysical occupant. It also exposes a basic limitation of most timing studies: the experimenter supplies the regulatory goal and strategy, so those studies investigate **implementation of an instructed policy**, not endogenous identification or selection.

The article should also note that the familiar intensity pattern—distraction for high intensity, reappraisal for lower intensity—is neither universal nor context-free. More recent work has found sensitivity to emotion type, perceived demand, tactic plausibility and real-world task structure. [PubMed+3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39818915/)

### Flexibility

The Bonanno and Burton slogan is supported only in a qualified form by current evidence.

The strongest recent ecological study measured context sensitivity, repertoire and feedback responsiveness in US and Chinese samples. Context sensitivity and use of a repertoire were associated with lower distress, but the value of switching depended on whether the initial strategy was ineffective and on the outcome considered. Maintaining an effective strategy was often adaptive. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44220-024-00215-3)

Subsequent work sharpens the warning:

- switching strategies when the context remains stable can predict greater distress;
- variability should not be used as a proxy for flexibility;
- associations between context sensitivity and well-being may be nonlinear;
- more flexibility is therefore not automatically better. [PubMed+1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41910550/)

This strengthens the article’s contextualist theme but changes the relevant locus of control. The key process is not merely choosing a strategy before an episode. It includes monitoring whether the context or strategy effectiveness has changed and deciding whether to maintain, adjust, switch or stop. The page’s omission of monitoring therefore leaves its account of both flexibility and conscious selection incomplete.

### Implicit regulation

Recent evidence requires a more conservative taxonomy.

The term “implicit regulation” is used for heterogeneous phenomena, including absence of an explicit regulation goal, absence of instructions, automatic execution of a learned policy, conflict adaptation, extinction, affect labelling and incidental effects of cognitive tasks. A 2026 neural meta-analysis distinguished explicit and implicit paradigms principally through the explicitness of their regulatory goals; it did not establish that all conscious experience was absent from the implicit conditions. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42824971/)

The two examples relied upon by the article are now especially problematic:

- **Emotional-conflict adaptation:** effects are small, inconsistently reproduced and potentially entangled with contingency learning. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38546549/)
- **Affect labelling:** newer experiments show null effects, context-dependent effects, and impairment of subsequent reappraisal or acceptance. [PubMed+2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42311801/)

The strongest justified conclusion is:

> Some emotional change occurs without a consciously represented intention to regulate, and sometimes without reported awareness that regulation is occurring.

That supports the non-necessity of an explicit conscious regulatory intention for **some episodes**. It does not show that those episodes contain no conscious perception, feeling, conflict, attention or task engagement, and it does not show that conscious regulation never makes a causal contribution in other episodes.

### Contemporary process models

The article says that the extended process model contains identification, selection and implementation. That is an incomplete present-day account. Current summaries include monitoring, comprising decisions to maintain, switch or stop the regulation attempt. [PubMed Central (PMC)+2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7137720/)

A 2024 systematic review of 139 recent articles also identified ten models in contemporary use and warned that emotion-regulation research frequently conflates strategies with goals. Gross’s process model remains dominant, but it is not the only live framework. [PubMed Central (PMC)+1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11479914/)

That matters because the article sometimes treats the Gross framework as though its absence of an experiential term were an empirical finding. It is instead a property of one family of functional models and their chosen explanatory vocabulary.

## 4. Is “earlier is easier” represented fairly?

### The headline verdict is fair

The article is right that no universal rule licenses the ranking:

> situation selection → situation modification → attention → reappraisal → response modulation

from best or easiest to worst or hardest.

Strategy family, actual onset and effectiveness can come apart. Preventive suppression can act early; late distraction can remain immediately effective; distraction and concentration differ despite belonging to the same family; and strategy effectiveness depends on intensity, goals, context and outcome measure. [PubMed+2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23747981/)

### The supporting argument is not fully fair

The page conflates at least five different temporal variables:

1. **Nominal process-model target:** where the strategy first bears on emotion generation.
2. **Clock-time initiation:** when instructions or implementation begin.
3. **Emotional intensity at initiation:** how developed the response has become.
4. **Preparation mode:** whether regulation is proactive or reactive.
5. **Latency and horizon of the outcome:** immediate subjective change, neural change, memory, rebound or later re-exposure.

Webb et al. address differences among strategy categories, not variable 2. Sheppes and Gross often use intensity as the theoretically relevant temporal dimension. Goldin and Thiruchselvam measure latencies of neural effects. Duckworth discusses precommitment and situation design. Those findings should not be collapsed into one proposition about “earliness”.

Post-2020 timing research also prevents the article from swinging too far in the opposite direction. A 2022 EEG study found proactive and reactive reappraisal reduced reported negativity to a similar degree, but reactive reappraisal required more effort and proactive regulation began earlier. This supports a modest “earlier preparation can be cheaper” claim even though it does not support a universal ranking of strategy families. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36049633/)

The fairest summary would be:

> **Proactive preparation often reduces the effort required to implement reappraisal and may improve some immediate outcomes, but nominally earlier strategy families are not uniformly easier or more effective. Outcomes depend on tactic, emotional intensity, goal, context, individual capacities, measurement domain and time horizon.**

That is both more current and more discriminating than either “earlier is best” or “effectiveness does not follow temporal order”.

## 5. Missed counterarguments and tenet-protective bracketing

### 5.1 The cited experiments mostly do not test the proposed selector

The page relocates conscious contribution to the regulatory goal, the decision to regulate, strategy selection and pre-episode planning. But in most cited experiments, participants are instructed:

- that they should regulate;
- which strategy to use;
- when to begin;
- and often what regulatory outcome to seek.

The experiments therefore bypass identification and selection. They primarily test implementation of an externally supplied policy. The fact that implementation can be neurally modelled without a nonphysical variable says little about whether endogenous goal formation or strategy choice involves consciousness; conversely, it provides no evidence that those untested stages do involve a nonphysical selector.

The page should say explicitly: **its proposed selector is located mainly in stages that the central timing experiments experimentally remove.**

### 5.2 The selector can be relocated indefinitely

When implementation becomes automatic, the selector moves to strategy choice. When strategy choice is predicted by contextual variables, it can move to goal formation. When goal formation is modelled, it can move to monitoring, policy formation, endorsement or identity.

This is the same absorptive structure the self-control article commendably recognises: because the Map can reinterpret scaffolding as the past work of a selector, scaffolding cannot count as either a challenge or evidence for that selector. [concepts/self-control-as-skill-and-scaffolding](https://unfinishablemap.org/concepts/self-control-as-skill-and-scaffolding/)

The timing article needs a **relocation bound**:

- At what point would a fully predictive physical model count against the selector?
- What behavioural or neural finding could not be reclassified as the selector having acted earlier?
- What would distinguish conscious selection from context-sensitive valuation implemented physically?
- Does the selector predict any deviation from the physical model, or merely accompany one of its stages?

Without answers, “where a selector would sit” becomes compatible with every possible empirical result.

### 5.3 A conscious cause need not be a nonphysical cause

The page sometimes contrasts an “experiential term” with a physical valuation architecture. That is not the only live alternative. A physicalist may hold that conscious access, global availability, self-representation or metacognitive evaluation is causally functional while being physically realised.

Thus, even evidence that conscious awareness affects regulation would not discriminate interactionist dualism from physicalist theories of conscious control. The relevant contrast is not:

> conscious selector versus physical controller

but at least:

> no functionally important conscious access; physically realised conscious access; and nonphysically realised conscious selection.

The article presently moves too quickly from asking about a conscious contribution to discussing the possible location of a nonphysical selector.

### 5.4 Absence from a model is not evidence of causal absence

“Every such stage is modelled without an experiential term” is accurate as a statement about the chosen formal descriptions. It does not follow that experience is causally dispensable in the actual system.

A model may omit conscious experience because:

- it treats the person as a black-box decision system;
- subjective reports are included only as outcomes;
- “valuation” subsumes both conscious and nonconscious processes;
- the experiment externally supplies the goal;
- or the model was not designed to adjudicate theories of consciousness.

The article correctly calls the physical model’s fit nondiscriminating. It should apply the same caution before using model omission to characterise consciousness as unnecessary.

### 5.5 “Implicit” and “conscious” are not single binary variables

At least six distinctions are needed:

- awareness of the eliciting stimulus;
- phenomenal awareness of the emotion;
- awareness of conflict or regulatory difficulty;
- explicit representation of a regulation goal;
- awareness that a strategy is changing emotion;
- reportable knowledge of the mechanism.

A paradigm can lack the fourth and fifth while preserving the first three. Calling the entire process “without a conscious contribution” erases precisely the distinctions needed for the Map’s philosophical question.

### 5.6 Constructionism is bracketed rather than answered

The constructionist challenge threatens the framing of the article. If emotion generation and regulation are inseparable, there is no clean baseline emotional process followed by intervention. The question becomes how goals, predictions, bodily signals and social concepts continuously shape an evolving state.

The article acknowledges this and then resumes the entry-point picture. That is tenet-protective bracketing unless the page explains why the appraisal-oriented process model, rather than the constructionist alternative, is the appropriate framework for locating the proposed selector.

### 5.7 Immediate regulation is not the only relevant outcome

The page focuses predominantly on immediate emotional attenuation and neural latency. The omitted re-exposure result from Thiruchselvam et al. illustrates why that is insufficient: distraction may act faster while reappraisal produces more durable change.

A selector interested in rational agency might optimise:

- immediate relief;
- accurate memory;
- learning;
- later emotional response;
- social expression;
- task performance;
- or long-term well-being.

Different goals can rationally favour different strategies and timings. This is central to regulation choice, not a peripheral complication.

### 5.8 Implementation intentions do not establish conscious authorship

If–then plans show that policies formed in advance can guide later behaviour. They do not establish:

- that plan formation was phenomenally conscious;
- that consciousness, rather than language, rehearsal or executive processing, caused the plan’s efficacy;
- or that a nonphysical selector authored the plan.

The page does ultimately say the result is only a fit. Calling policy moments the “clearest evidence”, however, can still be read as implying evidence for the selector rather than evidence that policy formation is an effective control point.

### 5.9 The Stapp example risks borrowing empirical authority

The Stapp section is carefully qualified, but its length and specificity create an association between the quantum proposal and well-established reappraisal findings. Ochsner’s experiment does not detect quantum-Zeno control, anomalous causal influence or any deviation from a neural-control account.

The best treatment would be a visually and rhetorically separate box titled something like **“Map-specific speculative application”**, ending with an explicit statement that the experiment supplies no evidence for the proposed mechanism.

## 6. Tenet neutrality

The article is more neutral than many advocacy-oriented treatments would be. Its strongest sentences are:

- the selector’s placement is “fit, not evidence”;
- a physical valuation architecture predicts the same record;
- emotion regulation gives Bidirectional Interaction a possible location rather than support;
- Dualism neither gains nor loses from the timing evidence;
- Occam’s-razor limits supply no positive reason to add a selector where ordinary models suffice. [topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/emotion-regulation-and-the-timing-of-control/)

Those are genuine constraints on the Map’s interpretation, not cosmetic caveats.

The neutrality is nevertheless incomplete because the article preserves the selector through **upstream relocation** without imposing a stopping condition. The empirical evidence can remove conscious control from implementation, strategy execution and perhaps strategy choice while the selector survives at policy, goal endorsement or some earlier act. The article admits that the fit cannot discriminate, but it does not register the resulting loss of falsifiability.

My overall judgement is therefore:

> **The page does not dishonestly present emotion regulation as evidence for dualism. It does, however, protect the selector from pressure by relocating it into stages that the cited experiments do not test.**

That is softer than evidential overclaiming but remains a form of tenet-protective architecture.

## 7. Integration with neighbouring articles

### Mental Effort

The timing page now correctly treats the 2008 “cost” result as an ego-depletion measure with significant limitations. The Mental Effort page still says cognitive reappraisal is “costly mainly when begun late”. That wording is stronger than the evidence now accepted by the timing article. The robust non-depletion result is that late reappraisal was **less effective** in the cited paradigm; the claim of greater general cognitive cost depends on the fragile Stroop measure. [concepts/mental-effort](https://unfinishablemap.org/concepts/mental-effort/)

The changelog itself identifies this propagation problem. [workflow/changelog](https://unfinishablemap.org/workflow/changelog/)

### Self-Control as Skill and Scaffolding

This is the best-integrated neighbouring page. Its “moments of policy” framework aligns naturally with implementation intentions, situation selection and delegated control. It also states with admirable clarity that a physical planner fits the same evidence and that the relocation “discriminates nothing”. [concepts/self-control-as-skill-and-scaffolding](https://unfinishablemap.org/concepts/self-control-as-skill-and-scaffolding/)

Two updates are needed:

- The implementation-intention effect should reflect the 2024 publication-bias analysis.
- The self-control page should acknowledge that policy formation itself is influenced by distributed affective, cognitive, motivational and social determinants, rather than presenting it as a phenomenologically transparent unitary choice.

### Emotion and Dualism

The relationship is more strained. The timing article says the evidence supplies no support for dualism and that implicit regulation neither refutes nor supports conscious causation. Emotion and Dualism more confidently claims that phenomenal character does “distinctive work” and treats emotional intentionality and motivational force as aspects of a single conscious act. [topics/emotion-and-dualism](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/emotion-and-dualism/)

Those claims may be part of the Map’s philosophical case, but they should not be allowed to inherit empirical support from the timing page. A cross-link should explicitly state:

> Emotion-regulation experiments currently do not distinguish causal phenomenal emotion from physically realised valuation and control.

The changelog also flags the Emotion and Dualism claim about Damasio treating consciousness as causally idle as needing a fidelity check. That remains a separate unresolved integration issue. [workflow/changelog](https://unfinishablemap.org/workflow/changelog/)

### Alief and Acting Against Belief

The imported claim that implicit regulation can proceed “without awareness” needs the same qualification as the timing article. It should state which awareness is absent: awareness of the regulatory intention, awareness that regulation is occurring, or awareness of the emotion itself.

### Skill Delegation

Skill Delegation’s broader suggestion that delegation reveals the genuine causal power of consciousness is stronger than the timing page permits. Delegation shows that explicit instruction, practice or policy can produce later automatic performance. It does not identify phenomenal consciousness as the indispensable causal ingredient in learning.

The pages should converge on the more cautious Self-Control formulation: delegation is compatible with the Map’s selector but equally compatible with physically implemented learning and control.

### Clinical Neuroplasticity

This page provides the best model for calibration. It already states that psychotherapy, placebo and reappraisal have physically described top-down routes and that the data do not adjudicate between metaphysical interpretations. The timing article correctly avoids counting Ochsner’s study as an independent evidential stream.

The integration should become reciprocal: Clinical Neuroplasticity should identify the timing page as the place where reappraisal and the Stapp reinterpretation are audited, while retaining its present neutral conclusion.

### Incomplete propagation

The changelog records pending cross-reviews for the control-theoretic will, clinical neuroplasticity, the numbing void, skill delegation and valence-as-a-natural-kind. This confirms that the present integration is intentionally incomplete rather than fully audited. [workflow/changelog](https://unfinishablemap.org/workflow/changelog/)

# Concrete potential improvements

1. **Emotion Regulation and the Timing of Control — replace the lede’s consciousness claim.**  
   Replace “Regulation also needs no conscious contribution at any stage” with:
   > Some regulatory changes occur without an explicit conscious intention to regulate and sometimes without awareness that regulation is occurring. These findings establish that explicit conscious supervision is not necessary for every regulatory episode; they do not establish the absence of all conscious processing within those episodes.
2. **Emotion Regulation and the Timing of Control — correct the Webb inference.**  
   Replace “Pooled effectiveness does not follow stage order” with:
   > Strategy-family effectiveness does not reproduce a simple early-to-late ranking, although the available meta-analysis did not manipulate intervention timing and therefore does not directly test whether earlier initiation of a given strategy is more effective.
3. **Add a “Five meanings of timing” table.**  
   Distinguish nominal process target, clock-time onset, emotional intensity, proactive versus reactive preparation, and latency or durability of effect. Assign every cited study to the variable it actually manipulates or measures.
4. **Update the extended process model from three stages to four.**  
   Add monitoring—maintain, switch or stop—and explain whether the proposed selector is supposed to contribute there. Treat monitoring as a potential relocation point subject to the same “fit, not evidence” restriction.
5. **Add Matthews et al. (2021) as the central regulation-choice review.**  
   Summarise its 219 studies and 18 determinants. Explain that most timing experiments externally supply the goal and strategy and therefore do not test endogenous identification or selection.
6. **Replace the single Bonanno quotation with a current flexibility subsection.**  
   Add the 2024 cross-cultural ecological results and recent warnings that variability is not flexibility. Distinguish context sensitivity, repertoire and feedback responsiveness.
7. **Rewrite the implicit-regulation section around operational definitions.**  
   Include a table separating implicit goal, automatic process, uninstructed task, lack of regulatory awareness, lack of stimulus awareness and absence of phenomenal experience. Do not allow evidence for one category to support all the others.
8. **Update the emotional-conflict example.**  
   Report the 2024 reliability review, the small and inconsistent effects, sparse reporting of the relevant analyses and the possible role of contingency learning.
9. **Update the affect-labelling discussion.**  
   Present Torre and Lieberman as an influential 2018 interpretation, followed by the newer null, context-dependent and regulation-impairing findings. Remove affect labelling as an uncomplicated demonstration of implicit regulation.
10. **Add proactive-versus-reactive regulation studies.**  
   Use the 2022 EEG evidence to preserve a modest version of “earlier preparation can reduce effort”, while rejecting a universal strategy-family hierarchy.
11. **Add outcome horizons.**  
   Separate immediate subjective attenuation, neural latency, physiological expression, memory, subsequent re-exposure and long-term well-being. Include Thiruchselvam et al.’s re-exposure result.
12. **Recalibrate the implementation-intention paragraph.**  
   Retain the old emotion-specific estimates but add the 2024 642-test synthesis, its smaller bias-adjusted estimate and substantial heterogeneity. Rename policy moments an “effective intervention point”, not the “clearest evidence” for conscious selection.
13. **Add an explicit physicalist-conscious rival.**  
   State that conscious access might be causally effective while physically realised. Distinguish evidence for conscious functional involvement from evidence for nonphysical interaction.
14. **Impose a selector relocation bound.**  
   Specify at least one result that would lower confidence in the selector rather than move it to an earlier stage. Candidate tests include prospectively predicting endogenous regulation choices from measured physical and contextual variables, perturbing putative conscious access while preserving implementation, and comparing instructed with genuinely self-generated regulation.
15. **Move the Stapp discussion into a boxed Map-specific application.**  
   Begin and end the box by saying that Ochsner’s findings contain no distinctive evidence for quantum-Zeno control.
16. **Mental Effort — change “costly mainly when begun late”.**  
   Use “less effective in one late-reappraisal paradigm, with a disputed subsequent-task measure suggesting additional cost”. This would align the neighbour with the audit already incorporated into the timing page.
17. **Self-Control as Skill and Scaffolding — update implementation intentions and choice.**  
   Add the publication-bias-adjusted planning estimate and the distributed determinants of regulation choice. Preserve its strong “fit discriminates nothing” calibration.
18. **Emotion and Dualism — prevent evidential inheritance.**  
   Add an explicit sentence that timing, reappraisal and implicit-regulation evidence currently do not discriminate dualism from physically implemented conscious or nonconscious control. Complete the queued fidelity check on the Damasio claim.
19. **Alief and Acting Against Belief — disambiguate “without awareness”.**  
   Identify the particular awareness measure in every imported claim.
20. **Skill Delegation — weaken “reveals conscious causal power”.**  
   Replace it with “is compatible with a causal role for conscious calibration” and add physically implemented learning as the principal rival.
21. **Clinical Neuroplasticity — add a reciprocal link.**  
   Point readers to the timing article for the Ochsner/Stapp case while retaining the conclusion that the data do not adjudicate between metaphysical interpretations.
22. **Complete the queued cross-reviews before presenting the cluster as integrated.**  
   Prioritise the control-theoretic will, skill delegation, numbing void and valence pages because each offers another possible relocation point for the selector.
23. **Separate source fidelity from claim validity in the workflow.**  
   A quotation matching its source and a DOI resolving correctly establish textual fidelity, not that the article’s inference follows. Each claim ledger should separately record: source accuracy, construct match, causal relevance, external validity, replication status and currentness.
24. **Prohibit conclusion-fixing empirical guards.**  
   Guards may enforce honest representation of the Map’s tenets, but should not require conclusions such as “no conscious contribution at any stage” before the evidence audit. Competing formulations should be evaluated before a binding sentence is selected.
25. **Introduce a literature-freshness gate.**  
   For empirical articles, require at least one systematic search covering post-2020 reviews and a targeted search covering the preceding two years. A bibliography ending eight years before publication should trigger manual review even when all listed references are genuine.
26. **Create a site-wide construct dictionary.**  
   Standardise the meanings of *implicit*, *automatic*, *unconscious*, *unaware*, *effortful*, *controlled*, *access-conscious* and *phenomenally conscious*. Require articles to name the operational measure rather than moving freely among these terms.
27. **Flag externally supplied control in study summaries.**  
   Every experiment should state whether the goal, strategy, timing and target were chosen by the participant or imposed by the experimenter. This would immediately reveal which studies can bear on identification and selection.
28. **Add consistency propagation to the review pipeline.**  
   When a deep review weakens a claim—such as late reappraisal’s “cost”—automatically search linked pages for stronger inherited formulations and block completion until they are revised or explicitly justified.
29. **Require a strongest-rival paragraph for every selector claim.**  
   Here the rival is a context-sensitive physical valuation-and-monitoring architecture that can include physically realised conscious access. The article should state what, beyond compatibility, favours the Map’s selector over that rival.
30. **Version the empirical verdict separately from the tenet interpretation.**  
   Maintain one section stating what the affective-science literature supports and another stating how the Map conditionally interprets it. This would make later empirical updates less likely to be constrained by an already chosen dualist framing.
