## 1. Introduction and Scope of the Audit

The text posits that the phenomenology of remembering—specifically characterized by the "double presence" of the rememberer and the past protagonist, accompanied by an irreducible "pastness quale"—constitutes a temporally extended "substantial self". By attempting to synthesize Husserlian temporality, Tulving's episodic memory taxonomy, and interactive dualism, the manuscript argues that reductive physicalist accounts inherently fail to capture the subjective givenness of temporal selfhood. Furthermore, it asserts that the constructive nature of memory is not merely an automatic neurocomputational process, but rather a specialized biological locus where a substantial conscious agent exerts bidirectional, top-down causal influence over physical brain states.   

As per the standard procedures for peer review at top-tier philosophy of mind and cognitive science journals, this audit assesses the structural integrity, empirical validity, and logical coherence of the manuscript's core arguments. In accordance with strict currency standards, the project-internal changelog updated on 2026-07-27 was consulted prior to this review to ensure the evaluation targets the most recent iteration of the author's claims.   

This review focuses strictly on four critical dimensions: the survival of the manuscript's empirical claims regarding episodic memory and temporal experience against the 2020–2025 scientific literature; the identification of any foundational results that have failed replication or been retracted; the illegitimate deployment of metaphysical commitments to bracket empirical challenges; and the severe lack of rigorous testing against competing contemporary frameworks, including constructive-memory accounts, narrative-identity theory, and Parfitian reductionism.

## 2. The Empirical Attrition of Episodic Memory Claims

A philosophical framework that stakes its metaphysical conclusions on the architecture of human cognition must survive rigorous contact with the contemporary empirical consensus. The manuscript tethers its defense of dualism to highly specific mechanisms of episodic memory. In both the cognitive and neurobiological domains, the text relies on outmoded or highly contested empirical dogmas, ignoring recent paradigm shifts that dismantle its foundational premises.

### 2.1 The Collapse of the Autonoetic Axiom

The manuscript asserts that "autonoetic consciousness"—defined as the self-knowing awareness enabling mental time travel, as originally proposed by Endel Tulving—is the strict, defining phenomenological feature of episodic memory. The text argues that this "double presence," wherein the subject simultaneously occupies the position of the present rememberer and the past protagonist, is irreducible to any functional or computational description. The author leverages this phenomenological uniqueness as a primary exhibit for a non-physicalist model of the mind, arguing that if one strips away the felt sense of re-experiencing, the resulting mental state is merely semantic knowledge, not genuine remembering.   

This orthodox view of autonoesis as strictly constitutive of episodic memory has faced severe, potentially fatal, attrition in the contemporary literature, a development the manuscript acknowledges only in passing before hastily dismissing it. The text briefly cites De Brigard's deflationary view but fails to grapple with the actual mechanistic and computational arguments advanced against the construct. De Brigard (2024) systematically demonstrates that the construct of autonoetic consciousness lacks both empirical validity and necessity for episodic memory. Contemporary functional taxonomies of memory increasingly model episodic retrieval without positing a unified, irreducible "feeling of re-experiencing".   

By clinging to the necessity of autonoesis, the manuscript falls into the trap of conflating a contingent retrieval output with the constitutive definition of the cognitive kind. The functional/computational characterization of episodic memory, which the manuscript attempts to reject as "eliminativist," has proven far more robust in explaining within- and between-individual variability. For example, when patients with schizophrenia experience profound deficits in episodic memory alongside altered perceptual experiences (hallucinations), their memory impairments and retrieval failures are heavily modulated by cognitive load, anxiety, and multisensory integration, completely independent of the pure phenomenological "quale" of pastness. Furthermore, computational models of gaze behavior and scene construction during retrospective causal judgment demonstrate that the "feeling" of mental simulation can be dissociated into discrete, unconscious sub-personal probabilistic weights.   

If autonoetic consciousness is a contingent, epiphenomenal, or computationally reducible feature of retrieval—as the 2024 literature strongly suggests—the manuscript's primary load-bearing pillar for dualism collapses. The "double presence" cannot serve as the irreducible signature of temporal selfhood if episodic memory functions comprehensively without it in human cognitive networks.   

### 2.2 The Reconsolidation Window and the Crisis of Replication

To rescue consciousness from the threat of epiphenomenalism, the manuscript endorses a principle of "Bidirectional Interaction". The text posits that the constructive, error-prone nature of episodic memory is an adaptive feature that opens a specific biological interface: the "reconsolidation window". During this window, when a reactivated memory trace supposedly destabilizes, the physical system is asserted to be underdetermined, providing a site where the non-physical conscious self can bias the reconstruction to sustain its narrative and agency.   

This is an egregious over-extrapolation built upon a highly unstable empirical foundation. The manuscript leans heavily on the clinical and neurobiological concept of memory reconsolidation—the process by which an accessed memory becomes labile and requires de novo protein synthesis to be restabilized. However, the human memory reconsolidation literature, particularly regarding the pharmacological blockade of emotional memory traces (e.g., using propranolol to decouple negative affect from episodic recall), has been engulfed in a massive replication crisis throughout the 2020s.

While early studies suggested that administration of a beta-adrenergic receptor antagonist could effectively "erase" or decouple the affective component of a traumatic memory during the reconsolidation window, recent comprehensive meta-analyses and rigorous replication attempts have repeatedly failed to find consistent evidence for this effect. In multi-site replication efforts led by prominent memory researchers, the disruption of human memory reconsolidation via noradrenergic blockade has shown a persistent, systemic failure to reduce conditioned fear responding or alter the underlying memory trace. The phenomenon is highly sensitive to boundary conditions—such as trace dominance, memory age, and instructional sets—that frequently prevent the memory from destabilizing at all.   

The manuscript completely ignores this replication crisis. It treats the "reconsolidation window" as a reliable, established biological mechanism of neural underdetermination perfectly suited for dualist intervention. Anchoring a radical metaphysical claim—that a substantial non-physical self exerts downward causal influence over matter—to a behavioral neuroscience paradigm that is currently failing to replicate in healthy human populations represents a profound methodological failure.

## 3. Illegitimate Metaphysical Bracketing

A rigorous philosophical argument must allow its metaphysical postulations to be challenged by the empirical and logical consequences of its own premises. The manuscript under review routinely violates this principle. Whenever cognitive science, clinical data, or logical inference corners the manuscript's non-reductive claims, the text introduces an ad hoc metaphysical commitment specifically designed to bracket the claim from further challenge.

### 3.1 Haecceity as an Ad Hoc Shield against the Thinness of "For-Me-Ness"

The text argues that episodic memory constitutes the self across time, grounding the immediate, synchronic sense of ownership in the "minimal self"—the pre-reflective "for-me-ness" of experience. However, the author is forced to acknowledge the standard philosophical objection: the basic synchronic subjectivity of the minimal self is metaphysically too thin to ground numerical, diachronic identity across decades of profound neurobiological and psychological change.   

Rather than resolving this tension through a rigorous analysis of psychological continuity, biological persistence, or structural network topology, the manuscript introduces the medieval metaphysical concept of "haecceity" (the non-qualitative property of "this-ness") as an auxiliary commitment. It asserts that there is a "deep further fact" about the conscious subject that bridges the gap between synchronic mineness and diachronic survival, entirely independent of the physical continuity of the brain.   

This is not a philosophical solution; it is a blatant evasion. By defining haecceity as a "deep further fact" that guarantees numerical identity regardless of the severe constructive errors, fragmentations, and confabulations inherent in the episodic memory system, the manuscript insulates the self from the actual data of cognitive science. When clinical phenomenology shows that the minimal self can be severely disrupted in schizophrenia (e.g., Sass and Parnas's ipseity disturbance models) or fractionated in depersonalization/derealization disorders where mineness and temporal presence operate independently, the text simply retreats to the unobservable, unfalsifiable haecceity to preserve the "substantial self."   

This bracketing is particularly problematic in the manuscript's rejection of the Many-Worlds Interpretation (MWI) of quantum mechanics. The text argues that MWI is incompatible with memory-constituted selfhood because MWI provides only epistemic branch-local determinacy, whereas the substantial self requires an objective indexical fact ("I have *this* past and no other"). The manuscript utilizes a "No-MWI" position to reject the ontological extravagance of branching universes and argue for objective reduction modulated by consciousness. But the necessity of this objective indexical fact is entirely dependent on the prior assumption of haecceity. The argument is viciously circular: the text asserts a deeply controversial metaphysical property (haecceity) to justify a specific quantum interpretation (No-MWI), and then uses that same quantum interpretation to argue for the causal efficacy of the substantial self in the physical world.   

### 3.2 The Relocation Fallacy and Epistemic Feelings

A similar illicit bracketing occurs in the manuscript's treatment of the "pastness quale." The text attempts to counter the metacognitive/epistemic-feeling readings advanced by researchers such as Perrin, Michaelian, and Sant’Anna (2020). These researchers argue that the "feeling of pastness" is an epistemic feeling—a downstream metacognitive output produced by monitoring retrieval features such as processing fluency, sensory vividness, and spatiotemporal context.   

The manuscript dismisses this robust framework by arguing that relocating the felt-pastness to a metacognitive tier does not remove it from conscious experience, thereby preserving its irreducibility as a phenomenal datum. The author claims that because a feeling exists, dualism is still required to explain it. This response structurally misapprehends the force of the epistemic feeling account and engages in a relocation fallacy.   

Contemporary research in affective metacognition posits that the "feeling of remembering" (or mnemicity) is not a primitive experiential property, but rather a "cognitive gadget"—a distinctively human, culturally learned ability. Through cultural learning, human agents are trained to discriminate metacognitive feelings from perceptual or emotional feelings, interpreting them as indications that a mental simulation is a memory of the past rather than an imagination of the future.   

If the feeling of pastness is a learned cognitive gadget generated by sub-personal reality-monitoring algorithms, it is entirely susceptible to functional reduction. The manuscript's assertion that the feeling "remains an irreducible datum" begs the question. By bracketing the feeling of pastness as inherently "qualitative" and magically immune to functional explanation, the text refuses to engage with the possibility that what it identifies as a primitive quale is actually a computationally synthesized heuristic. The failure to engage with the cultural-evolutionary and mechanistic components of the epistemic feeling literature renders the manuscript's defense of dualism fundamentally hollow, retreating to an argument from incredulity rather than addressing the computational models directly.   

## 4. Untested Competing Frameworks

The mandate of a peer-reviewed philosophical article is to expose its thesis to the strongest possible counterarguments. The manuscript frequently raises competing frameworks—such as radical simulationism, narrative identity theory, and Parfitian reductionism—only to subject them to superficial, strawman critiques before discarding them without rigorous testing.

### 4.1 Radical Simulationism and the Computational Resolution of the Discrimination Problem

The manuscript acknowledges the Simulation Theory of Memory (STM), accepting the cognitive neuroscience consensus (the Constructive Episodic Simulation Hypothesis) that remembering the past and imagining the future rely on a shared core network, primarily recruiting the hippocampus and the default mode network. It agrees that memory is highly constructive and that traces are flexibly recombined rather than retrieved as static archival files.   

However, the manuscript rejects the post-causal, "radical simulationist" philosophical conclusions of STM championed by Michaelian (2016, 2022). Michaelian argues that remembering is simply imagining the past, requiring no retained causal connection to the original experience. The manuscript argues that a pure simulationist view fails the "discrimination problem"—the question of how a subject tells a memory from a free imagination—because physical systems cannot generate the irreducible "feeling of remembering" on their own.   

The text proposes a "split verdict," adopting the simulationist mechanism but insisting that consciousness must provide the missing discriminatory feeling via a non-physical, bidirectional contribution. This is an entirely unearned theoretical leap. The contemporary STM literature offers robust, entirely physicalist solutions to the discrimination problem. Sub-personal source-monitoring frameworks rely on predictive coding, heuristic evaluations of sensory detail, and contextual spatiotemporal binding to accurately discriminate memory from imagination without requiring any non-physical "conscious contribution."   

By positing that the discrimination problem requires a dualist intervention, the manuscript ignores the computational density of source monitoring. The text claims that because subjects report a "robust and distinct felt difference," this constrains eliminative reduction. But computational models of scene viewing and retrospective causal judgment routinely generate high-confidence source tags that seamlessly align with these phenomenological reports. The manuscript fails to test its thesis against the leading computational models of predictive coding in memory networks, preferring instead to assert a gap in the physicalist account that modern cognitive science has already largely closed.   

### 4.2 Narrative Identity Theory Stripped of its Complexity

The manuscript heavily relies on the concept of the "narrative self," citing philosophers such as Marya Schechtman and Paul Ricoeur to argue that personal identity is a story woven from memory, intention, and cultural context. The text claims that autonoetic consciousness—mental time travel—is the indispensable phenomenological mechanism for this narrative selfhood.   

This interpretation grossly distorts the contemporary application of narrative identity theory. Schechtman's account of narrative identity explicitly focuses on psychological unity and the characterization of the person, inherently disavowing the necessity of metaphysical or numerical identity. As analyzed by Pugh (2020) and others in the context of neurotechnology and identity crises, the narrative view accommodates highly dynamic, even radical changes to the psychological architecture without requiring the rigid persistence of a "substantial self". Continuity of narrative is compatible with radical change as long as it is integrated into a coherent self-understanding.   

More damningly, the manuscript's claim that episodic mental time travel is the strict requirement for narrative identity is empirically false. Recent research on amnesia and personal identity thoroughly undermines this premise. Craver (2020, 2023) and Boyle & Brown (2024) have demonstrated that the functions related to self-knowledge and personal identity reconstruction are highly distributed across various memory systems. Patients with profound episodic amnesia can sustain deeply coherent personal narratives through "collective functions" (sharing and co-constructing experiences within social groups) and "preservative functions" (the semantic retention of past character traits, values, and life structures).   

These functions operate coextensively and seamlessly integrate to sustain narrative selfhood even in the total absence of the autonoetic "double presence." By defining the narrative self as utterly dependent on the irreducibility of episodic mental time travel, the manuscript leaves the vast literature on semantic self-knowledge and socially scaffolded identity completely untested. The text sets up a false dichotomy: either a dualist substantial self exists to bind episodic memory, or the self is a fractured illusion. It ignores the well-documented empirical middle ground of semantic, socially distributed psychological continuity.

### 4.3 The Evasion of Parfitian and Buddhist Reductionism

Perhaps the most glaring omission in the manuscript's argumentative structure is its failure to genuinely engage with Derek Parfit's reductionist account of personal identity, as well as its historical antecedents in Buddhist mereological reductionism. The text mentions Parfit primarily in the context of dismissing his denial of the "deep further fact". Parfit famously argues that numerical identity over time is not what matters for survival or moral responsibility; what matters is psychological connectedness and continuity—the overlapping chains of beliefs, desires, intentions, and memories (X today is strongly connected to Y in the past).   

In a Parfitian ontology, there is no underlying "substantial self" that owns these experiences. The experiences, bound by psychological continuity and causal links, are all that exist. The manuscript counters this by asserting that the self seeks its past "as lived experience" and that the "I" must be a determinate subject for the indexical objection to Many-Worlds quantum mechanics to hold.   

This is not a refutation of Parfit; it is a refusal to accept the terms of the debate. If Parfitian reductionism is true, the manuscript's entire formulation of the "indexical objection" is meaningless, as the "I" is merely a linguistic and cognitive convention for grouping causal psychological elements. In contemporary Parfitian readings of Buddhist philosophy (e.g., Siderits, Finnigan, Vasubandhu), the mereological reduction of the person explicitly eliminates the necessity of the "substantial self" that the manuscript claims is required for moral responsibility and diachronic agency.   

The Buddhist Abhidharma analysis demonstrates that an ontology of momentary psycho-physical elements can perfectly account for the accumulation of karmic debt (moral responsibility) without a persistent entity traversing time. The manuscript fails to test its concept of "haecceity" against Parfit's famous "Complex View" thought experiments (e.g., fission, teletransportation). It simply assumes that the "felt continuity of first-person subjectivity" guarantees metaphysical persistence. But Parfit's core insight is precisely that phenomenological continuity is a causal property of the brain, not evidence of a persistent entity. By utilizing an undefined, bracketed "substantial self" to override the Parfitian reduction without addressing the intricate mechanics of psychological connectedness, the manuscript engages in philosophical dogmatism rather than rigorous conceptual analysis.   

## 5. Identified Weaknesses Justifying Rejection

The manuscript "Phenomenology of Memory and the Self" represents a structurally flawed attempt to force contemporary cognitive neuroscience into an outdated dualist ontology. The methodology relies on empirical obsolescence, epistemological circularity, and superficial engagement with counterarguments. To achieve publication status in a tier-one journal, a manuscript must systematically disarm the strongest versions of its opponents' claims, an objective this text systematically evades.

The following specific weaknesses render the manuscript unready for academic submission:

1. **Failure to Account for the Deflation of Autonoetic Consciousness:** The manuscript treats the "double presence" as an irreducible and strictly constitutive feature of episodic memory, failing to address current literature demonstrating that autonoesis lacks construct validity and is a non-necessary, contingent feature of retrieval that can be functionally modeled.

2. **Reliance on Falsified Memory Reconsolidation Paradigms:** The text uses the memory reconsolidation window as the underdetermined physical interface for non-physical causal interaction, entirely ignoring the profound replication crisis and widespread empirical failures of human memory reconsolidation paradigms (e.g., propranolol blockade) in the contemporary literature.

- *Omitted/Mishandled Source:* Elsey, J., & Kindt, M. (2020-2024 synthesis, reflecting massive failures to replicate human reconsolidation blockade). See also: *American Journal of Psychiatry* reviews (2024) highlighting the systemic inconsistencies of pharmacological reconsolidation therapy.
3. **Misapprehension of the Epistemic Feeling / Cognitive Gadget Literature:** The manuscript erroneously claims that the "feeling of pastness" survives metacognitive relocation as an irreducible quale, failing to engage with the mechanistic argument that mnemicity is a computationally tractable, culturally acquired cognitive gadget used for reality monitoring.

- *Omitted/Mishandled Source:* Perrin, D., Michaelian, K., & Sant'Anna, A. (2020). "The phenomenology of episodic memory is an epistemic feeling." *Frontiers in Psychology*, 11, 1531.
4. **False Necessity of Episodic Memory for Narrative Identity:** The manuscript makes the rigid empirical claim that autonoetic mental time travel is strictly necessary to construct narrative selfhood, ignoring robust clinical evidence that amnesic patients successfully sustain personal identity through semantic, collective, and preservative cognitive functions.

- *Omitted/Mishandled Source:* Boyle, A., & Brown, S. (2024). Discussed in relation to Craver's robust models of collective and preservative memory functions in personal identity reconstruction (ESPP 2025 abstractions).
5. **Evasion of Parfitian Reductionism via Ad Hoc Bracketing:** The manuscript dismisses Parfitian reductionism not by refuting the "Complex View" of psychological connectedness, but by illegitimately inserting the metaphysical concept of "haecceity" to guarantee an objective indexical fact, thereby begging the question against reductionist ontology and ignoring Buddhist mereological analogs.

- *Omitted/Mishandled Source:* Pugh, J. (2020). "Clarifying the Normative Significance of 'Personality Changes' Following Deep Brain Stimulation." *Science and Engineering Ethics*, 26, 1655-1680. (Applying Schechtman and Parfitian continuity rigorously without requiring substantial metaphysical entities).

**Verdict:** Due to critical empirical attrition, the reliance on untestable ad hoc metaphysical brackets, and a systemic failure to engage rigorously with the contemporary cognitive science and philosophy of memory literature, this manuscript requires immediate rejection.
