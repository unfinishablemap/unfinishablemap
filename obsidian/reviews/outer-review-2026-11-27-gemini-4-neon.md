# Referee Report on The Interested Party Void

This manuscript merits **outright rejection** at any top-tier philosophy journal (*Mind*, *Synthese*, *Philosophical Studies*). Arguing that consciousness cannot neutrally investigate its own nature because it holds existential stakes in every answer, *The Interested Party Void* attempts to construct an insurmountable meta-epistemic barrier out of empirical social psychology, analytic epistemology, and metaphilosophical skepticism. Audited against contemporary scholarship, however, the submission fails basic academic standards across every dimension of review: its entire external bibliography terminates in the year 2000, its flagship empirical pillar relies on social-psychological findings that suffered major pre-registered replication failures in the 2020s, its dialectical framing selectively softens undercutting defeat to insulate its parent site's prior dualist commitments, and its core argument ignores contemporary debiasing, adversarial-collaboration, social-epistemic, and debunking-epistemology literatures that directly undermine its central inferences.

A check of the site's live changelog through 27 November 2026 shows no recent edits or entries pertaining to *The Interested Party Void*. Because direct retrieval of the live target URL was inaccessible, this audit evaluates the manuscript's verbatim indexed passages, verified external reference list, and argumentative structure alongside the parent site's published philosophical tenets. ## Audit of Empirical Accuracy and Cited Results

The manuscript builds its claim of irremediable "motivational corruption" on four empirical and epistemological pillars: terror management theory (TMT), motivated reasoning, beneficial or positive illusions, and epistemic circularity. While none of the manuscript's seven pre-2001 external sources has been formally retracted in the record reviewed here, its empirical pillars have either failed large-scale pre-registered replication, been fundamentally reinterpreted by 2020s cognitive science, or been substantively misread by the author.

### Terror Management Theory and the Replication Collapse

The manuscript asserts without qualification that "Terror management theory, developed by Greenberg, Solomon, and Pyszczynski, demonstrates that mortality salience—awareness of death—triggers worldview defence," adding that "Over 300 studies confirm that reminders of mortality bias judgment toward beliefs offering symbolic or literal immortality," operating below conscious awareness through "death-thought accessibility" (DTA) via proximal and distal defenses. Its sole reference for this empirical foundation is a 1997 book chapter by Greenberg, Solomon, and Pyszczynski. Treating pre-replication-crisis TMT as settled scientific demonstration in 2026 is indefensible.

The empirical breakdown behind these figures spans multiple independent replication teams and meta-analytic frameworks. In **Many Labs 4**, Klein et al. (2022, *Collabra: Psychology*, 8(1), 35271, doi:10.1525/collabra.35271) conducted a pre-registered direct replication of Greenberg et al.'s (1994, Study 1) classic mortality-salience worldview-defense experiment across 21 laboratories (2,281 participants recruited; 17 labs and 1,578 participants after pre-registered exclusions), comparing independent ("In House") protocols against protocols developed with the direct guidance of TMT's original authors ("Author Advised"). They found little evidence that mortality salience increased worldview defense across every primary exclusion specification—yielding Hedges' *g* = 0.07, 95% CI [−0.03, 0.17], *p* = .187 for Exclusion Set 1, *g* = 0.09, 95% CI [−0.03, 0.21], *p* = .135 for Exclusion Set 2, and *g* = 0.09, 95% CI [−0.04, 0.22] for Exclusion Set 3—while the unexcluded 21-lab pooled sample (*N* = 2,220 in the initial dataset) yielded a negligible *g* = 0.03, and original author involvement failed to salvage the effect. Although TMT proponents Chatard, Hirschberger, and Pyszczynski (2020 preprint) argued that restricting analysis to labs meeting the 40-per-cell guideline produced a weak Author-Advised replication (*g* = 0.13, *p* = .045 under one specific exclusion rule), **Hoogeveen, Berkhout, Gronau, Wagenmakers, and Haaf (2023)** (*Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science*, 6(3), doi:10.1177/25152459231182318) revisited both Klein et al.'s and Chatard et al.'s exclusion schemes through a full-scale Bayesian hierarchical multiverse analysis. Across the majority of models and priors, Bayesian model comparison yielded evidence *against* a mortality-salience effect, with the team's multiverse modeling bounding plausible overall effect sizes between *d* = 0.03 and *d* = 0.18—too small for typical single-lab TMT experiments to detect reliably.

Independent high-powered replications reinforce this null picture. **Schindler, Reinhardt, and Reinhard (2021)** (*Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 93, 104087, doi:10.1016/j.jesp.2020.104087) tested worldview defense across three studies—including a registered experiment of *N* = 1,356 (total *N* = 1,763)—obtaining non-significant mortality-salience effects, Bayesian evidence favoring the null, and a non-significant internal meta-analysis (*g* = 0.09, *p* = .058). **Sætrevik and Sjåstad (2022)** (*Meta-Psychology*, 6, MP.2020.2628, doi:10.15626/MP.2020.2628) failed to replicate both traditional national-patriotism defense and novel mechanistic measures across Norwegian lab (*N* = 101) and US online (*N* = 784) samples. **Treger, Benau, and Timko (2023)** (*PLoS ONE*, 18(5), e0285267, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0285267) likewise reported five failed replications across five unique samples, with internal meta-analyses indistinguishable from zero.

The manuscript's appeal to "Over 300 studies confirm" misreads pre-crisis publication volume as evidential robustness. **Schindler, Hilgard, Fritsche, Burke, and Pfattheicher (2023)** (*Personality and Social Psychology Review*, 27(2), 195–225, doi:10.1177/10888683221107267) meta-analyzed the mortality salience × social-norm salience literature (64 primary effect sizes; 92 broad) and showed that an unadjusted g = 0.34, 95% CI [0.26, 0.41], shrank sharply under bias adjustments: while three-parameter selection models (3PSM, g = 0.23, 95% CI [0.11, 0.35]) and p-uniform (g = 0.28, 95% CI [0.14, 0.41]) retained attenuated estimates, primary bias corrections spanned g = −0.36 to 0.15, narrow-scope adjusted estimates were uniformly non-significant (g = −0.38 to 0.04), and lab-study PET-PEESE corrections collapsed to null (SPET g = −0.39, 95% CI [−1.01, 0.22], p = .168; SPEESE g = −0.01, 95% CI [−0.37, 0.35], p = .947). Tellingly, although the half p-curve showed right skew, the full p-curve was markedly left-skewed—diagnostic of p-hacking and researcher degrees of freedom—with estimated average power of just **5%–10%**. Even the more favorable multitool systematic review by **Chen, Benjamin, Guo, Lai, and Heine (2025)** (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 129(1), 20–41, doi:10.1037/pspa0000438)—which found evidential value for nonzero, highly heterogeneous effects on *p*-curve, selection models, and WAAP-WLS—concluded that different bias-correction tools yield conflicting verdicts, most TMT studies are severely underpowered (earlier *p*-curve power estimates hovering around 25%–26%), many individual findings are likely spurious, and future replications should plan for *r* = .18 rather than the classic *r* = .35 estimate summarized from pre-crisis reviews. A critical methodological commentary (*Replication Index*) further highlighted that the lower bound of Chen et al.'s unconditional *z*-curve Expected Discovery Rate sits at 5%, indistinguishable from chance false positives. TMT's own founders acknowledged in Chatard et al. (2020) that pre-crisis TMT studies routinely dropped unsupportive experiments and reflected publication bias. Meanwhile, 2025–2026 empirical tests of TMT's specific proposed mechanisms show that thinking about death evokes existential anxiety in only 35% of respondents (29% report no fear at all) and fails to increase compensatory belief in scientific worldviews (*β* = 0.07, 95% CI [−0.05, 0.20], *p* = .260), while even where mortality salience passes a modest DTA manipulation check (effect size 0.15), DTA exhibits null or only weak correlations (*r* = −0.13) with health-valuation judgments.

### Motivated Reasoning: Cherry-Picking Kunda and Ignoring the 2020s Bayesian Turn

The manuscript's second empirical pillar invokes **Kunda (1990)** to claim that "people chronically engage in self-serving reasoning while remaining unaware of doing so" and that "The distortion hides in locations outside conscious awareness—introspection finds no trace of it". This commits two fatal scholarly errors: it distorts its own cited authority, and it misses the 2020s methodological overhaul of motivated-reasoning research.

First, Kunda (1990) does *not* support a thesis of pervasive, uncheckable motivational corruption. An entire half of Kunda's landmark review documents **accuracy-driven reasoning**, demonstrating that when error stakes are high or reasoners expect to justify their conclusions to critical peers—precisely the institutional structure of peer-reviewed philosophy and science—accuracy goals prompt deeper processing, delay premature hypothesis freezing, reduce overconfidence, and weaken cognitive biases. Equally central to Kunda's framework is the **evidential reality constraint**: "People do not seem to be at liberty to conclude whatever they want to conclude merely because they want to," because motivated reasoners must construct justifications capable of persuading a dispassionate observer and remain constrained by existing evidence. Quoting Kunda's "illusion of objectivity" while suppressing both her accuracy-goal evidence and her evidential constraint misrepresents the source.

Second, 2020s cognitive science has heavily undermined causal inferences drawn from traditional motivated-reasoning paradigms. **Tappin, Pennycook, and Rand (2020a)** (*Current Opinion in Behavioral Sciences*, 34, 81–87, doi:10.1016/j.cobeha.2020.01.003) demonstrated why paradigmatic politically motivated-reasoning study designs routinely fail to support causal inference. When **Tappin, Pennycook, and Rand (2020b)** (*Cognition*, 204, 104375, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2020.104375) explicitly benchmarked partisan belief updating against Bayes' rule conditioned on participants' prior beliefs, higher analytic thinking (Cognitive Reflection Test scores) did *not* amplify identity-protective bias; rather, higher analytic reasoning correlated with significantly smaller mean absolute deviations from the unbiased Bayesian posterior on the primary difference measure (τ = −.20, Z = −6.27, p < .001; weaker at τ = −.06, Z = −1.72 on the log-ratio measure), with analytic thinkers updating more closely to the Bayesian benchmark on *both* favorable and unfavorable signals. While Tappin et al. (2020b) carefully cautioned that deeply entrenched cultural issues might still elicit stronger bias than their factual political items, follow-up work (**Tappin, Pennycook, & Rand, 2021**, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 150, 1095–1114, doi:10.1037/xge0000974; **Pennycook & Rand, 2021**, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 25(5), 388–402, doi:10.1016/j.tics.2021.02.007) and recent Annual Review of Psychology syntheses confirm that the once-influential claim that cognitive sophistication exacerbates biased reasoning has proven difficult to replicate.

Moreover, large-scale experimental decompositions show that directional motivation accounts for a far smaller share of belief error than the manuscript assumes and is measurably responsive to incentives. Across four survey experiments (N = 3,485) and a Twitter field experiment (N = 5,379), Pennycook, Epstein, Mosleh, et al. (2021) (Nature, 592(7855), 590–595, doi:10.1038/s41586-021-03344-2) estimated that 51.2% (95% CI [38.4%, 62.0%]) of false-headline sharing stemmed from inattention and 33.1% (95% CI [25.1%, 42.4%]) from confusion, leaving just 15.8% attributable to directional preference; subtly shifting attention to accuracy doubled or more than doubled sharing discernment (2.0× in Study 3; 2.4× in Study 4), though subsequent reviews note partisan moderation and questions about long-term durability. Even where social motivations demonstrably skew veracity judgments, Rathje, Roozenbeek, Van Bavel, and van der Linden (2023) (Nature Human Behaviour, 7(6), 892–903, doi:10.1038/s41562-023-01540-w) showed that accuracy incentives reduced partisan bias in headline judgments by roughly 30% (d = 0.23–0.50 across comparisons), with scalable non-financial accuracy interventions also producing reliable gains (n = 983 in Study 4). Philosophically, Carter and McKenna (2020) (Canadian Journal of Philosophy, 50(6), 702–718, doi:10.1017/can.2020.16) further demonstrate that motivated reasoning does not generate an undifferentiated skeptical abyss; its epistemic import stratifies across distinct levels of basing, knowledge, and intellectual humility.

### Positive Illusions and the Misappropriation of Smilansky

A striking empirical omission in the manuscript is its complete neglect of the psychological literature on positive illusions (Taylor & Brown, 1988, 1994) and comparative self-enhancement—precisely the empirical body testing whether minds systematically inflate their own significance and control. Instead, the author leans on a single 2000 philosophical monograph—Saul Smilansky's Free Will and Illusion—and misapplies it in a glaring non-sequitur.

In "The Void Cuts Both Ways," the manuscript writes: "Materialists face the same charge from the opposite direction. Smilansky argues in Free Will and Illusion that some beliefs about consciousness may be 'beneficial illusions' that consciousness cannot afford to see through: 'We could not live adequately with a complete awareness of the truth about human [freedom]'". This citation misfires on every substantive count. First, Smilansky's thesis concerns free will (reconciling insights from compatibilism and hard determinism under what he calls "Fundamental Dualism"), not phenomenal consciousness or mind-body dualism. Second, Smilansky's "Illusionism" contends that hard determinism is partly true (libertarian ultimate origination does not exist), yet false belief in libertarian agency functions as a pragmatically necessary illusion. That argument posits an adaptive illusion favoring anti-reductive, agency-inflating beliefs, directly undermining the manuscript's claim that materialists face a symmetrical bias "from the opposite direction"—and compounding the error by sliding directly from Smilansky's free-will illusionism into philosophy-of-mind eliminativism and consciousness illusionism.

Had the manuscript engaged actual 2020s research on positive illusions and self-enhancement, the evidence would have cut still further against its blanket claims. Zell, Strickhouser, Sedikides, and Alicke (2020) (Psychological Bulletin, 146(2), 118–149, doi:10.1037/bul0000218) meta-analyzed 124 articles (291 independent samples; >950,000 participants) on the Better-Than-Average Effect (BTAE, standardized effect size dz = 0.78, 95% CI [0.71, 0.84], correlating only modestly with self-esteem at r = .34 and life satisfaction at r = .33). Critically, Zell et al. found that BTAE is significantly larger for vague personality traits than for measurable, checkable abilities, while the broader comparative-evaluation literature shows that trait ambiguity inflates the effect, concrete feedback and individuated comparison targets shrink it, and difficult or rare tasks reverse it into a worse-than-average bias—exposing a substantial non-motivational cognitive anchoring component rather than omnipotent ego protection. Nor is self-enhancement universal: individuals with low self-esteem reliably exhibit self-verification, seeking confirmation of pessimistic self-views rather than flattering illusions. Philosophically, Wilson and VanderWeele (2024) (Philosophia, 52(3), 757–778, doi:10.1007/s11406-024-00758-w) demonstrate that positive-illusion accounts routinely conflate instrumental and epistemic rationality, ignoring cases where optimistic beliefs are epistemically grounded (resourced or agentive optimism) rather than illusory. Meanwhile, Guenther et al. (2024) (Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin) show that self-enhancement tracks felt personal authenticity (r(173) = .45, p < .001; daily b = .38, p < .001), which—like TMT if real—would asymmetrically favor ego-elevating ontologies rather than physicalist eliminativism.

### Epistemic Circularity and the Calibratability of Consciousness Science

The manuscript's final pillar cites Alston (1986) to assert that epistemic circularity "means there is no non-circular way to evaluate the reliability of our basic cognitive faculties—perception, introspection, reason, memory—without using those same faculties. Consciousness cannot step outside itself to check whether its self-reports are accurate. No technical fix awaits this limitation, because it is a structural feature of all epistemic self-evaluation rather than a practical obstacle".

This move commits both an interpretive inversion and a domain-scope fallacy while ignoring contemporary philosophy of consciousness science. First, Alston (1986, 1989, 1993) explicitly argued against deriving skeptical paralysis from epistemic circularity: he contended that epistemic circularity does not preclude reliabilist justification for beliefs formed via basic sources and defended the practical rationality of relying on firmly established doxastic practices. Second, Alstonian circularity is entirely domain-general, applying equally to visual perception, memory, and deductive inference in astrophysics or molecular biology. Importing a universal epistemological constraint to diagnose a special "interested party" barrier in consciousness research proves far too much: if the inability to "step outside" our basic cognitive faculties creates an "unexplorable" void, then all empirical science inhabits the same void.

Equally fatal, declaring that "No technical fix awaits" introspective unreliability ignores a thriving 2020s literature directly addressing calibration in consciousness science. Michel (2023 [online-first 2021]) (Erkenntnis, 88(2), 829–850, doi:10.1007/s10670-021-00383-z) confronts the recurring skeptical claim that consciousness detection procedures cannot be calibrated, applying Signal Detection Theory (separating sensitivity from response criteria in paradigms like post-decision wagering) to show how measurement procedures can be iteratively assessed and corrected. Extending and critiquing Michel's framework, McKilliam (2025) (Ergo: An Open Access Journal of Philosophy, 12(11), 267–288, doi:10.3998/ergo.7304) articulates the natural-kind method for detecting introspective errors in consciousness science, demonstrating how convergent theoretical and empirical regularities expose systematic first-person measurement failures. In parallel, Fleming (2024) (Annual Review of Psychology, 75, 241–268, doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-022423-032425) integrates computational and neural evidence on metacognition and confidence, showing that confidence judgments are inferential models empirically dissociable from first-order performance via formal signal-detection metrics (meta-d', M-ratio) and responsive to targeted feedback calibration. Contemporary philosophy of quantitative psychology likewise shows that iterative measurement circularity—dating back to Fechnerian psychophysics—operates constructively through intersubjective agreement rather than terminating inquiry.

## Ideological Framing and the Shielding of Prior Commitments

The manuscript is embedded within an explicitly committed non-reductive philosophical program anchored by five unproven "chosen starting points": Dualism (irreducibility of consciousness, ruling out reductive physicalism, eliminative materialism, epiphenomenalism, and illusionism), Minimal Quantum Interaction (non-physical post-decoherence outcome selection), Bidirectional Interaction (interactionist dualism with persisting subjects), No Many Worlds, and Occam's Razor Has Limits (rejecting parsimony as a truth-tracking heuristic). Across its central dialectical turns, the paper deploys four framing strategies that bracket or soften counterevidence to shield these prior tenets from epistemic defeat.

First, the manuscript relies on an empirically unsupported symmetry framing ("The Void Cuts Both Ways"), proclaiming that the void "does not favour one side of the mind-body debate. It contaminates all positions equally". While acknowledging the familiar accusation that dualism is wish-fulfillment serving a desire for consciousness to be fundamental and significant, the text tries to manufacture a symmetrical bias against materialism, eliminativism, and illusionism by speculating that "accepting them brings the comfort of intellectual conformity or the relief of dissolving hard problems". The evidential double standard is glaring: against dualism stand deep existential and self-enhancing drives, whereas against physicalism the author offers uncited pop-psychology ("intellectual conformity," "relief") propped up only by the misapplied Smilansky quotation. More decisively, contemporary experimental cognitive science indicates that intuitive human mind-body psychology is strongly asymmetric in favor of dualism. Berent (2023) (Open Mind: Discoveries in Cognitive Science, 7, 564–587, doi:10.1162/opmi_a_00094; see also Berent, 2024, Neuroscience of Consciousness, niae016, doi:10.1093/nc/niae016) and Berent, Theodore, and Valencia (2022) (PNAS, 119, e2211628119) report experimental evidence that intuitive dualism—perceiving minds as ethereal and separable from bodies—arises as a pervasive implicit bias tied to Theory of Mind (attenuated in autism spectrum disorder, yet persisting on implicit association tests even among educated adults who explicitly reject dualism). While Berent acknowledges that psychological mechanisms alone cannot settle ontology and that linguistic learning also contributes to dualist concepts, her experiments show that tacit dualist biases actively generate the exact "problem intuitions" (philosophical zombies and Mary's Room) that the parent site deploys as cumulative support for its Dualism tenet. Asserting equal contamination across both camps functions directly to obscure an empirically documented psychological asymmetry cutting against dualism.

Second, the manuscript executes a textbook motte-and-bailey retreat under falsifiability pressure. Across its main argument, the paper advances dramatic, structural claims—the "motivational corruption of inquiry itself," an "occluded" void "with elements of the unexplorable," and "no technical fix awaits". Yet when confronting a Popperian unfalsifiability objection ("if every position can be re-described as motivated, the void claim risks unfalsifiability"), the text abruptly retreats to a trivial internalist motte: "the void is not the claim 'every belief is wrong,' but the weaker claim 'no investigator can verify the absence of motivated distortion in self-relevant inquiry.' That claim is consistent with some beliefs being correct—it simply denies that consciousness can certify which ones from the inside". No contemporary epistemologist or cognitive scientist maintains that an isolated mind can certify from the inside the complete absence of unconscious distortion. That platitude of ordinary fallibilism cannot underwrite the paper's dramatic thesis that consciousness cannot neutrally or reliably investigate its own nature, nor justify classifying the domain as structurally "unexplorable".

Third, in "Relation to Site Perspective," the manuscript neutralizes a direct self-targeted defeater via costless rhetorical inoculation ("epistemic humility") paired with a one-way epistemic ratchet against physicalist parsimony. The author concedes that the site's Dualism commitment "is precisely the kind of conclusion that the interested party void predicts consciousness would favour" and that Bidirectional Interaction "faces the void most directly". Under contemporary explanationist epistemology of genealogical defeat—formalized by Korman and Locke (2023) (Philosophy and Phenomenological Research, 106(1), 176–195, doi:10.1111/phpr.12848)—recognizing that an unproven starting commitment matches a truth-insensitive motivational predictor constitutes a paradigmatic undercutting defeater calling for suspension of belief rather than serene retention. Instead, the manuscript waives the epistemic cost outright: "The Map interprets this not as grounds for abandoning dualism but as grounds for holding it with epistemic humility". Immediately after insulating Dualism from defeat, the text pivots to Tenet 5 (Occam's Razor Has Limits) to turn the void offensively against physicalism, suggesting that rivals' appeals to "parsimony, may themselves be motivated rather than neutral". Weaponizing generalized higher-order motivational suspicion against opponents' theoretical virtues while keeping one's own unproven tenets intact under the banner of "humility" violates basic norms of philosophical argumentation—precisely the abuse analyzed by Königs (2025) (Synthese, 205(93), 1–26) in defending principled bracketing of higher-order evidence in scholarly philosophical dispute.

Fourth, the manuscript obscures an internal contradiction between its introspective skepticism and its parent framework's reliance on first-person phenomenal evidence. The parent site's cumulative case for Dualism leans heavily on conceivability arguments, Mary's Room, intrinsic qualia, and first-person subjective character. Yet The Interested Party Void insists that distortion "hides in locations outside conscious awareness—introspection finds no trace of it" and that "Consciousness cannot step outside itself to check whether its self-reports are accurate". If first-person introspective self-reports and phenomenal intuitions are motivationally opaque and unverifiable, then intuition-driven anti-physicalist arguments suffer far greater epistemic erosion than third-person cognitive-neuroscientific models anchored in intersubjective experimental data.

## Untested Counterarguments and Omitted Contemporary Frameworks

Beyond its empirical failures and protective framing, the manuscript rests on an unexamined Cartesian individualism: it models consciousness inquiry as an unaided, single-mind introspective exercise ("Consciousness cannot step outside itself..."; "investigating the self while being the self..."). Because it treats the epistemic agent as a solitary subject attempting to scrub their own psyche clean, it overlooks three major 2020s frameworks that directly challenge its conclusion of structural incorrigibility.

### Empirical Debiasing and Adversarial Collaboration in Consciousness Science

The manuscript's blanket verdict that "No technical fix awaits this limitation, because it is a structural feature of all epistemic self-evaluation rather than a practical obstacle" is contradicted by empirical evidence at both the cognitive and institutional levels.

At the individual cognitive level, alongside the accuracy-prompt and accuracy-incentive evidence cited above (Pennycook et al., 2021; Rathje et al., 2023), 2020s dual-process reasoning research shows that even rapid, automatic ("System 1") intuitive biases respond to targeted training. Boissin, Caparos, Voudouri, and De Neys (2022) (Judgment and Decision Making, 17(4), 646–690, doi:10.1017/S1930297500008895; extending Boissin, Caparos, Raoelison, & De Neys, 2021, Cognition, 211, 104645, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2021.104645) used the two-response paradigm under strict 3-second time limits and concurrent cognitive load to demonstrate that brief, single-shot bias explanations boost both deliberative and initial intuitive logical accuracy, with gains persisting over at least two months. Empirical debiasing is undeniably partial rather than universal—between 12% and 44% of reasoners show no improvement across repeated sessions, moderated by open-minded thinking dispositions and prior conflict detection, and classroom educational interventions show modest average effects with uncertain far transfer. Nevertheless, durable, measurable mitigation refutes the manuscript's binary claim that cognitive bias admits of no practical remediation.

At the institutional level, the manuscript's opening legal maxim—nemo iudex in causa sua ("no one should judge their own case")—actually undercuts its own pessimistic inference. Legal and scientific institutions do not declare truth-seeking impossible because participants have stakes in the outcome; they build adversarial institutional architectures with pre-registered protocols, blinded third-party analysis, and mutually agreed diagnostic criteria. Clark, Costello, Mitchell, and Tetlock (2022) (Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition, 11(1), 1–18, doi:10.1037/mac0000004) codify how adversarial collaboration harnesses competing researcher motivations by obligating rivals to co-design discriminating tests and commit to theoretical predictions before data collection, sharply constraining post-hoc motivated rationalization.

Most conspicuously for a 2026 submission in philosophy of mind, the manuscript omits the landmark real-world deployment of adversarial collaboration in the very domain it deems unexplorable: Cogitate Consortium, Ferrante, Gorska-Klimowska, et al. (2025) (Nature, 642(8066), 133–142, doi:10.1038/s41586-025-08888-1). Uniting proponents of Integrated Information Theory (IIT) and Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (GNWT), the consortium evaluated pre-registered, differentially weighted predictions across N = 256 participants using three neuroimaging modalities (fMRI n = 120, MEG n = 102, iEEG n = 34), explicitly separating theory proponents from data acquisition and analysis—which were performed by theory-neutral teams specifically "to minimize bias and post-hoc interpretation". The results posed substantial, publicly audited empirical challenges to core predictions of both IIT and GNWT. Even though proponents subsequently contested auxiliary physiological interpretations—such as whether task-irrelevant stimulus offset triggers conscious ignition or whether prefrontal mixed selectivity limits mesoscopic decoding—COGITATE directly undercuts the thesis that theoretical or existential stakes render consciousness science incapable of structured, bias-constrained empirical adjudication.

### Social Epistemology of Science and Mandevillian Cognition

Equally fatal is the manuscript's unargued fallacy of composition: inferring from the premise that individual conscious investigators possess motivational stakes to the conclusion that collective inquiry into consciousness cannot achieve reliable epistemic traction. Contemporary social epistemology of science directly challenges the individualistic premise that objective inquiry requires motivation-free individual minds.

Peters (2021) (The British Journal for the Philosophy of Science, 72(4), 1061–1081, doi:10.1093/bjps/axy079) deploys agent-based modeling and the concept of Mandevillian cognition to argue that individual cognitive vices—including confirmation bias, non-epistemic values, and partisan commitment—can produce collective epistemic benefits across a competitive, epistemically diverse scientific community. Motivated advocates scrutinize rival hypotheses and auxiliary assumptions far more vigorously than indifferent bystanders, strengthening the division of cognitive labor; assuming that individual bias necessarily ruins collective reliability betrays, in Peters's words, "a narrow, individualistic account of science". Parallel work in anti-individualist social epistemology (Greco, 2022, Synthese, 200(5), 421, doi:10.1007/s11229-022-03893-7) and the science-and-values demarcation literature (\*Philosophy of Science, 2024, doi:10.1017/psa.2024.20) reinforces that contextual stakes are not inherently epistemically illegitimate so long as communal procedures preserve epistemic integrity and stakeholder accountability.

Remarkably, the manuscript inadvertently concedes the core social-epistemic principle in a single unexamined aside, noting that "A Buddhist tradition motivated to dissolve the self and a Western tradition motivated to preserve it might, where they agree, point toward something beyond either motivation". The author never confronts the consequence of that admission: if opposing motivational vectors across distinct investigative communities can triangulate toward truth-tracking convergence, then interested-party bias is a tractable problem of social-epistemic design, not an inescapable "unexplorable void."

### The Unresolved Self-Defeat of Global Debunking Arguments

Finally, the manuscript fails to resolve the dialectical self-defeat inherent in empirically premised global debunking arguments. In "The Suspicion Cascade," the author notes a superficial psychological regress ("If my investigation of consciousness is motivated, then perhaps my belief that the investigation is motivated is also motivated—a pre-emptive move to inoculate [oneself]"), and assumes that retreating to the weak claim ("no investigator can verify the absence of motivated distortion in self-relevant inquiry") avoids self-refutation.

It does not. Contemporary debunking epistemology—exemplified by Bagwell (2023) (Synthese, 201(1), Article 25, doi:10.1007/s11229-022-04021-1; see also Bagwell, 2021, Ergo, 8, 496–522, and Moon, 2023, in Machuca, ed., Evolutionary Debunking Arguments)—demonstrates that an empirically premised global debunking argument is epistemically self-defeating whenever its skeptical conclusion undercuts the warrant for the empirical or philosophical premises needed to establish it. The manuscript falls squarely into this trap. Its premises—claims about terror management, subconscious motivated reasoning, introspective opacity, and suspicion cascades—are themselves empirical and philosophical claims about human consciousness and cognition produced by conscious human researchers who hold acute existential and reputational stakes in theories of the mind (vividly illustrated by the defensive disputes surrounding TMT's own replication failures). Retreating from rebutting defeat ("every belief is wrong") to undercutting defeat ("no investigator can verify the absence of motivated distortion in self-relevant inquiry") offers zero shelter, because standard genealogical debunking arguments always operate as undercutting defeaters rather than rebutting defeaters (Korman & Locke, 2023, Philosophy and Phenomenological Research, 106(1), 176–195; Korman & Locke, 2020, Oxford Studies in Metaethics, 15, 309–332). If the manuscript's undercutting principle applies globally to self-relevant inquiry into the mind, it immediately undercuts the author's warrant to cite Greenberg et al. (1997), Kunda (1990), or introspective phenomenology as premises for the void—nor can the author escape via Hoffman-style scientific anti-realism (cf. Hoffman & Singh, 2024), because the parent framework is explicitly metaphysically realist about dualism and mental causation. Conversely, if empirical psychology can reliably uncover subconscious cognitive distortions despite being conducted by conscious, interested human beings, then the manuscript's premises refute its own conclusion. Compounding this dilemma, the manuscript slides illicitly from non-inferential laboratory biases to the wholesale defeat of inferential philosophical and scientific arguments without ever showing that structured inferential evaluation is epistemically severed from truth.
