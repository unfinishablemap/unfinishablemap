## Audit basis

I reviewed the article as currently rendered. It identifies **29 July 2026** as its last modification date. I also consulted the live changelog, which was updated on **6 August 2026**; the assessment below therefore concerns the current post-revision text rather than an older indexed version. ## Overall judgment

The article’s modest thesis—that several selected African traditions contain sophisticated, non-reductive accounts of persons, agency, life and relation—is defensible. Its stronger thesis—that these traditions independently converge on **phenomenal consciousness as irreducible**, and especially on something close to the Map’s **interactionist substance dualism**, is not established.

Its recurring error is **category substitution**:

- relational or moral **personhood** becomes relational consciousness;
- *Ori* as destiny or personal divinity becomes phenomenal awareness;
- vital force becomes proto-consciousness;
- belief in ancestors becomes evidence for consciousness surviving death;
- distinctions among aspects of a person become a mind–body interface;
- anti-substantialist or process ontologies become modes through which a preselected soul operates.

The page explicitly denies that there is one African philosophy, acknowledges objections from Hountondji and Wiredu, distinguishes Tempels from later *-ntu* taxonomies, notices Ubuntu’s anti-substantialist pressure, and says cross-cultural convergence is not independent evidence for dualism. Those are genuine strengths. But the exposition repeatedly steps across its own safeguards: immediately after rejecting a single African view, it claims a “widespread pattern” across Bantu, Akan, Yoruba, Igbo and Zulu traditions without supplying tradition-specific evidence. Wiredu’s methodological warning was precisely that responsible generalisation requires prior, ethnically specific philosophical analysis. [[african-philosophy-of-consciousness]]

The article should therefore not presently be relied on as a survey of African philosophies of consciousness. It is better described as a **Map-oriented comparative reconstruction**, insufficiently separated from neutral exposition.

## 1. Akan dualism

### What the article gets right

The Akan material is the article’s strongest candidate for genuine interactionist dualism. Gyekye does understand Akan thought as recognising both bodily and spiritual dimensions and as allowing causal influence between them. The article is therefore justified in treating Akan philosophy as relevant to the interaction problem and as resistant to straightforward reductive physicalism. [Gyekye, *An Essay on African Philosophical Thought*](https://static1.1.sqspcdn.com/static/f/1011404/27275314/1475595180567/Gyekye.pdf)

### Central attribution error: Gyekye does not endorse the reported tripartism

The article says that Gyekye “identifies a tripartite structure” consisting of *okra*, *sunsum* and *honam*, after which it treats *sunsum* as an ontologically intermediate interface between soul and body. That is not Gyekye’s conclusion.

Gyekye acknowledges that some anthropological presentations can create the impression of three components. He then argues that *okra* and *sunsum* are functionally distinguishable but not two separate spiritual substances: *sunsum* is the active or personality-bearing aspect of the spiritual principle. His express conclusion is that the Akan conception is **dualistic rather than tripartite**—a spiritual unity in relation to the body. [[african-philosophy-of-consciousness]]

That matters for three further claims:

1. **The “interface layer” is unsupported.** Gyekye does not place *sunsum* between *okra* and body as a third ontological grade. He attributes psychological and bodily interaction to the spiritual complex, including *sunsum*, but that is not a mediator mechanism comparable to a physical interface.
2. **Consciousness is not simply assigned to the *okra*.** Gyekye’s account gives *sunsum* the functions of thinking, desiring, feeling and personality. The article’s claim that consciousness is “grounded in the *okra*” suppresses precisely the internal complexity of Gyekye’s analysis.
3. **The three-stage interaction model is the Map’s construction.** The suggestion that Akan thought provides a more graduated version of Cartesian interactionism depends on treating *okra* and *sunsum* as ontologically separate after Gyekye has declined to do so.

There is contemporary support for a revisionary three-aspect interpretation, but it must be represented as a dispute. A 2025 University of Ghana thesis explicitly records that Gyekye regards *okra* and *sunsum* as metaphysically identical, then argues against him that they are slightly metaphysically different. That would be a useful source for a genuinely contested tripartite interpretation; it does not justify attributing the interpretation directly to Gyekye. [University of Ghana thesis (2025)](https://ugspace.ug.edu.gh/items/8dfbee07-ce96-4611-b9fb-bb2a589c3e11)

### Serious dissent omitted

The article gives Wiredu one brief paragraph but does not allow his quasi-physical analysis to alter its main classification. Nor does it engage recent alternatives. A 2023 specialist volume includes Tartaglia’s argument that the Akan scheme is better aligned with contemporary idealism, that Gyekye’s dualism was not meant to be ontologically fundamental, and that the tradition should not be assimilated to panpsychism. That is a substantial competitor to both the Map’s substance-dualist reading and its later appeal to African vitalism as panpsychist convergence. [Tartaglia, "Gyekye and Contemporary Idealism" (2023)](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-031-36163-0_2)

**Verdict on Akan:** the relevance to interactionism is real, but the article’s distinctive tripartite-interface account rests on a misattribution.

## 2. Yoruba *Ori*

### What the cited source supports

Abimbola’s work is genuine and highly relevant to *Ori*, pre-birth destiny, personal divinity, and the narratives surrounding Ajala, the maker or keeper of heads. Abimbola also describes a person consulting Ifá to discover the wishes of their *Ori*, which regulates the course of their life. The article is therefore justified in connecting *Ori* with destiny, identity and pre-birth choice. [Abimbola, *Ifá: An Exposition of Ifá Literary Corpus*](https://thinkyorubafirst.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Ifa-An-exposition-of-Ifa-literary-corpus.pdf)

### The Ajala narrative is misstated

The article says that each person’s *Ori* kneels before Ajala and selects a destiny. In Abimbola’s narrative, prospective persons travel to Ajala and choose or receive heads. Two travellers select defective heads; Afuwape says that **he** has come to choose a head, and Ajala eventually helps choose a suitable one for him. *Ori* is the object chosen or bestowed, not an already existing inner consciousness that kneels and chooses itself. [[african-philosophy-of-consciousness]]

This is not a minor narrative slip. It changes the metaphysics. The article turns a story about a person’s acquisition of destiny into a story about a conscious nonphysical entity exercising pre-embodied agency.

### Abimbola does not support the “consciousness in a vessel” claims

The article attributes three much stronger propositions to Abimbola:

- *Ori* is “inner consciousness”;
- the body is a vessel inhabited by *Ori*;
- Abimbola documents awareness as dependent on a nonphysical presence.

Those propositions are not established by the cited book. Its relevant chapter is expressly about *Ori* and the choice of destiny. Full-text searches do not locate “consciousness,” “awareness,” or “vessel,” and the page offers no textual passage from which its phenomenal-consciousness interpretation is derived. [Abimbola, *Ifá: An Exposition of Ifá Literary Corpus*](https://thinkyorubafirst.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Ifa-An-exposition-of-Ifa-literary-corpus.pdf)

Absence of those English words would not by itself prove that no consciousness interpretation is possible. It does show that the article cannot present the interpretation as something Abimbola “documents” without a much fuller argument connecting *Ori* to Yoruba terms for cognition, mind, perception and experience.

### Holistic and materialist competitors are omitted

A modern Yoruba-inflected account by Temitope Adefarakan treats *Ori* within an **embodied integration** of body, mind, spirit and soul. It explicitly presents Yoruba connectedness as antithetical to Cartesian separation of mind from body. That is almost the opposite of the article’s body-as-vessel reconstruction. [Adefarakan, *Sharing Breath*](https://read.aupress.ca/read/sharing-breath/section/c53bd62e-abaf-4bd3-859d-20cf02db467a)

The 2023 specialist volume also includes a chapter devoted to a central-state materialist interpretation of the Yoruba concept of person and its critique. Whatever the ultimate verdict on that interpretation, its existence demonstrates that the relevant scholarly debate is not “Yoruba anti-materialism versus Western materialism.” It concerns how *Ori*, *emi*, body, brain and other person-components should be related, and whether Western categories illuminate or distort them. [*Conversations on African Philosophy of Mind, Consciousness and Artificial Intelligence* (Springer, 2023)](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-031-36163-0)

**Verdict on Yoruba:** the destiny and personal-divinity material is substantially grounded; the claims about phenomenal awareness, bodily vessels and straightforward anti-materialism are imposed rather than demonstrated.

## 3. Tempels, Bantu vital force and panpsychism

### What the article gets right

The page correctly identifies vital force as central to Tempels’s reconstruction. It also correctly warns about his colonial and paternalistic framing and correctly separates Tempels’s account from the later *muntu/kintu/hantu/kuntu* taxonomy associated with Kagame and Jahn. Those are useful corrections to a common secondary-literature conflation. [[african-philosophy-of-consciousness]]

### Vital force is recoded as consciousness

Tempels does not say that all entities possess quantities of something analogous to phenomenal consciousness, with human consciousness as its highest concentration. His basic proposal is more radical and less psychological: force is not an attribute added to being; in his reconstruction, force is what being is. He distinguishes divine, human, animal, vegetable and mineral forces and treats human beings as supreme among created forces. He does not thereby classify mineral or vegetable forces as subjects of experience. [Tempels, *Bantu Philosophy* (1959)](https://www.cyrho.com/data/Placide%20Tempels%20Bantu%20Philosophy%201959.pdf)

The article consequently slides among three very different claims:

1. everything is dynamically constituted as force;
2. everything has life-like or mind-like properties;
3. everything has or contributes phenomenal consciousness.

Tempels gives some support to the first. The second requires interpretation. The third is not supplied.

### Tempels cautions against the very dualist translation the article performs

Tempels explicitly warns that searching for direct equivalents of “soul,” “mind,” “will” and similar categories would already assume that the peoples he discusses divide human beings into soul and body in the European manner. He regards that as a false methodological start. The Map nevertheless uses his force ontology as support for a soul-like consciousness acting on physical outcomes. [Tempels, *Bantu Philosophy* (1959)](https://www.cyrho.com/data/Placide%20Tempels%20Bantu%20Philosophy%201959.pdf)

This point is reinforced by the Map’s own neighbouring article on the interaction problem. That article classifies Tempelsian force ontology—and an integrated Yoruba person—as **monist avoidance**: positions in which no gap arises between inert matter and immaterial mind, and which are therefore unavailable to the Map’s substance dualism. The current article instead says those same materials align African philosophy with the Map’s dualist and interactionist starting point. Both classifications cannot stand without a careful account of competing interpretations. [[interaction-problem-across-traditions]]

### The combination-problem claim fails

A hierarchy of vital force does not solve the panpsychist combination problem. A hierarchy can rank entities without explaining:

- whether lower-level entities are conscious subjects at all;
- whether human consciousness is composed from them;
- how multiple subjects would constitute one unified subject;
- why greater vital rank generates phenomenal unity.

If Tempels’s forces are not micro-subjects, the combination problem does not arise—but then his system is not the panpsychism the article invokes. If they are micro-subjects, rank alone supplies no combination mechanism. The article attempts to obtain both the panpsychist convergence and the solution to panpsychism’s chief difficulty from a source that clearly provides neither.

### Critical literature is treated ceremonially

Hountondji is cited for the objection to a monolithic collective “Bantu philosophy,” but the article reproduces the structure of the criticised method: it extracts a unified metaphysics from Tempels and then extends it to a transcontinental pattern. Contemporary scholarship continues to reassess Tempels rather than treating his reconstruction as transparent ethnographic documentation; the 2024 collection *Beyond Bantu Philosophy* is an obvious omission. [Hountondji, *African Philosophy: Myth and Reality*](https://books.google.com/books/about/African_Philosophy_Second_Edition.html)

**Verdict on Bantu vital force:** adequately introduced as a contested force ontology, but inaccurately converted into a consciousness hierarchy, panpsychist convergence and solution to the combination problem.

## 4. Ubuntu and relational personhood

### The defensible claim

Ubuntu traditions and theories plausibly support important claims about relational identity, moral personhood, interdependence, dignity, solidarity and the process through which a human life becomes fully humane. Some philosophers also construct a deeper process ontology from Ubuntu. The article is therefore right that the material is not exhausted by atomistic individualism. [Metz, African Human Rights Law Journal](https://www.ahrlj.up.ac.za/metz-t)

### The central equivocation: personhood is not phenomenal consciousness

The article moves from the proposition that a person becomes or realises personhood through others to the proposition that **consciousness itself is constituted by interpersonal relations**. That inference is not supplied.

At least four questions must be kept separate:

1. What makes an organism a human being?
2. What gives someone moral status or dignity?
3. What enables someone to achieve mature or virtuous personhood?
4. What makes there be something phenomenally experienced at all?

An Ubuntu theory could answer the second and third relationally while holding that an infant, isolated individual or morally deficient adult is nevertheless phenomenally conscious. The proverb therefore does not, by itself, reframe the hard problem or show that consciousness exists between persons rather than in individual subjects.

### Metz is real but is used beyond his intended scope

The article candidly acknowledges that Metz interprets Ubuntu primarily as ethics. Metz’s work develops communal relations—identity and solidarity—as the basis of right action, moral personhood, dignity and human rights. It does not argue that phenomenal experience is ontologically constituted by social relations. The article then selects a stronger consciousness-ontological reading because the Map finds it “philosophically productive,” but supplies no author who makes that precise step. [[african-philosophy-of-consciousness]]

Ramose can support a genuinely ontological section, but his account is commonly analysed in terms of process, event and “be-ing becoming.” That could challenge substance metaphysics rather than provide a relational operating condition for an otherwise self-subsisting soul. It certainly does not license importing the Akan *okra* into Ubuntu to preserve the Map’s preferred substance. [[african-philosophy-of-consciousness]]

### The Akan–Ubuntu hybrid is a Map invention

The page resolves Ubuntu’s anti-substantialist pressure by suggesting that an Akan *okra* remains ontologically distinct while community shapes how it develops. No source is offered for this synthesis, and the move combines distinct traditions precisely at the point where one threatens the site’s tenet.

Comparative synthesis is legitimate, but it must be labelled:

> **Map proposal:** combine an Akan-like enduring spiritual principle with an Ubuntu-like account of relational development.

It cannot be presented as an interpretation of Ubuntu itself.

### Dissent is missing

The literature includes disagreements about whether Ubuntu is primarily an ethic, an account of dignity, a virtue theory, a process ontology, a political ideal, an obsolete communitarian ideology or a framework whose scholarly development has only begun. Matolino and Kwindingwi’s “end of Ubuntu” argument, Metz’s reply, later extensions of the scepticism, Ramose’s ontology and Molefe’s recent dignity-and-moral-perfectionism treatment represent materially different positions. The article presently converts that contested field into one metaphysical lesson about consciousness. [Matolino and Kwindingwi, "The end of ubuntu" (2013)](https://philpapers.org/rec/MATTEO-15)

**Verdict on Ubuntu:** the relational account of selfhood and moral personhood is defensible; the constitution of phenomenal consciousness, hard-problem reframing and compatibility with a separate soul remain unsupported Map extrapolations.

## 5. Citation integrity and currency

### Existence

The six bibliography entries—Gyekye, Tempels, Wiredu, Abimbola, Metz and Hountondji—are all genuine works and are broadly bibliographically recognisable. There is no obvious fabricated source in the reference list. [Gyekye, *An Essay on African Philosophical Thought*](https://static1.1.sqspcdn.com/static/f/1011404/27275314/1475595180567/Gyekye.pdf)

### Fidelity

Bibliographic existence masks serious **claim-to-source failures**:

- Gyekye is made to endorse the tripartism he explicitly rejects.
- Abimbola is made to document phenomenal consciousness and a body-as-vessel doctrine that his cited discussion does not provide.
- Tempels’s force ontology is rewritten as a hierarchy of consciousness.
- Metz’s moral theory is used as a springboard for an uncited consciousness ontology.
- Wiredu and Hountondji appear as brief qualifications, but their objections are not allowed to constrain the article’s subsequent generalisations.

This is therefore not a fabricated-bibliography problem. It is a **source-framing and inferential attribution problem**.

### Currency

For a page revised in 2026, the literature is markedly stale. Its newest external bibliography item is Metz from 2007. It omits, among other things:

- the 2023 volume devoted specifically to African philosophy of mind, consciousness and artificial intelligence, containing competing idealist, materialist, quasi-physicalist and proto-panpsychist analyses;
- the 2024 reassessment of Tempels;
- Molefe’s recent systematic Ubuntu ethics work;
- the 2025 Akan thesis directly addressing personhood, consciousness and the contested three-aspect interpretation. [[african-philosophy-of-consciousness]]

The six sources are also presented as a bare final bibliography, without page references or claim-level attribution. That makes it difficult for a reader to tell where exposition stops and Map reconstruction begins.

## 6. Missed counterarguments and tenet-protective framing

### Holism may oppose rather than support substance dualism

The article notices that Ubuntu’s constitutive relationality sits uneasily with substance dualism, but then preserves the substance and demotes relation to a developmental or operational condition. Likewise, the integrated Yoruba and force-ontological Bantu alternatives may dissolve the assumed mind–matter divide rather than supply additional versions of it. The article does not defeat these alternatives; it translates them into the Map’s vocabulary.

### Traditional belief does not establish metaphysical truth

The ancestral-consciousness section argues that traditions recognising ancestors never assumed that consciousness was biological. That is historically or anthropologically relevant. It does not show that post-mortem consciousness exists, that consciousness is nonphysical, or that the relevant beliefs arose from phenomenological insight rather than religious, ritual, social or mnemonic functions.

The article briefly says its point survives even if the survival claims are rejected. But once survival is bracketed, the remaining conclusion is only that some traditions possess non-materialist concepts—not that their concepts identify the nature of consciousness correctly. [[african-philosophy-of-consciousness]]

### The convergence is manufactured at too high a level of abstraction

*Okra*, *sunsum*, *Ori*, vital force, Ubuntu personhood and Cartesian mind are allowed to “converge” only after their differences have been abstracted away. The resulting common proposition—roughly, that something not straightforwardly reducible to inert matter affects embodied life—is so broad that idealism, process monism, emergentism, religious anthropology, hylomorphism and various physicalisms could also satisfy it.

The neighbouring convergence article is more disciplined: it calls the West African lineage a candidate rather than an established independent line, admits the translation problem and explicitly says that the Map’s framework is an interpretive lens rather than an additional confirmation. Those cautions should govern this article’s exposition, not merely appear elsewhere on the site. [[cross-cultural-convergence-on-mental-causation]]

### The quantum comparison is source-free

Nothing in the cited Akan, Yoruba, Bantu or Ubuntu sources identifies quantum indeterminacy, outcome selection or anything structurally equivalent. At most, the Map can say that its own proposed mechanism is intended to solve a causal problem that some readings of these traditions also face. Saying that their interaction puzzles “map onto” quantum indeterminacy risks lending historical traditions an apparent mechanistic precision they do not claim. [[african-philosophy-of-consciousness]]

### The African/Western contrast is overdrawn

The article contrasts relational African philosophies with an isolated Western subject, communal oral thought with solitary phenomenology, and longstanding African non-materialism with a West that supposedly had to rediscover the hard problem after centuries of materialism. This suppresses both African disagreement and Western traditions of interactionism, idealism, phenomenology, hylomorphism, personalism and social constitution.

The slogan contrasting “what is it like for me?” with “what is it like for us?” may be a useful Map heuristic. It is not presented with evidence that it is a traditional African methodological formula, and it should not be written as though it summarises an entire oral philosophical tradition. Wiredu’s insistence that African philosophy includes both traditional and modern components also makes the article’s general oral-transmission framing inadequate. [[african-philosophy-of-consciousness]]

## 7. Integration with surrounding articles

Integration is extensive at the level of links but unstable at the level of doctrine.

**The interaction-problem article contradicts the target article.** It classifies Tempelsian force ontology and integrated Yoruba personhood as monist avoidances unavailable to the Map. The target classifies them as independent resonance with dualism and bidirectional interaction. The site needs to present those as competing scholarly readings and decide what evidential role, if any, each can play. [[interaction-problem-across-traditions]]

**The non-Western hard-problem article amplifies the weakest claims.** It says Ubuntu locates consciousness in relations and treats *Ori-inu* as a hidden source of knowing, but its reference list contains no direct African specialist source. This creates a circular structure: the survey article supplies an unsupported interpretation, and the neighbouring article repeats it as cross-cultural evidence. [[the-hard-problem-in-non-western-philosophy]]

**The mental-causation article is more epistemically cautious but still source-dependent on the target.** It correctly acknowledges that the West African line is not yet established as independent and that conceptual translation is a serious problem. Yet it also asserts, via the target article, that Ubuntu maintains irreducibility and genuine agency. Ubuntu ethics does not by itself establish either proposition. [[cross-cultural-convergence-on-mental-causation]]

**The intersubjectivity link is conceptually relevant but not evidentially sufficient.** That article develops a generic account of communication, phenomenological realism and intersubjective corroboration. It does not supply the missing exegesis showing that Ubuntu makes phenomenal consciousness relationally constituted. Linking to a Map concept page cannot substitute for direct Ubuntu scholarship. [[intersubjectivity]]

Overall, the cluster currently risks **source laundering by internal repetition**: a speculative interpretation begins on one page, is cited by several neighbouring pages, and then appears to be a well-integrated cross-cultural result.

## Final assessment

The article has a worthwhile organising aim and several responsible caveats, but its central comparative conclusions are not yet reliable. Its major problem is not hostility or disrespect toward African traditions; it is **assimilation by philosophical translation**. Distinct theories of destiny, force, personhood, spirituality and social relation are repeatedly redescribed as theories of phenomenal consciousness, and then filtered for compatibility with the Map’s prior commitments.

The appropriate editorial response is not merely to add more citations. The article needs a structural separation between:

1. what particular texts and scholars say;
2. competing interpretations within African philosophy;
3. cross-traditional philosophical comparison;
4. the Map’s own speculative appropriation.

## Concrete potential improvements

### Target article and neighbouring articles

1. **Retitle or split the target article.** “Selected African Conceptions of Personhood, Mind and Vitality” would fit the present evidence better, or create separate Akan, Yoruba, Tempels/Bantu and Ubuntu articles followed by a clearly synthetic comparison.
2. **Correct the Akan section.** State that Gyekye distinguishes *okra*, *sunsum* and body functionally but concludes that *okra* and *sunsum* form a spiritual unity, yielding dualism rather than three-substance tripartism.
3. **Delete or reclassify the *sunsum*-as-interface claim.** Present it as a Map reconstruction or as a contested proposal supported by a scholar who explicitly defends ontological tripartism, not as Gyekye’s position.
4. **Give the Akan disagreement its own subsection.** Compare Gyekye’s interactionism, Wiredu’s quasi-physicalism, Tartaglia’s idealist reconstruction and Lamptey’s revisionary three-aspect model.
5. **Correct the Ajala narrative.** Make clear that prospective persons choose or receive heads; do not say that an already existing *Ori* kneels and chooses itself.
6. **Remove the attribution of “conscious experience,” “awareness” and “body as vessel” to Abimbola** unless exact textual evidence is supplied.
7. **Expand the Yoruba ontology.** Explain how *Ori* relates to other Yoruba person-components and include embodied, holistic, materialist and dualist interpretations rather than treating *Ori* as a transparent synonym for nonphysical consciousness.
8. **Rewrite the Tempels section around force ontology rather than consciousness ontology.** Distinguish vital rank from sentience and human supremacy among created forces from “highest concentration of consciousness.”
9. **Remove the claim that African vital-force hierarchies solve the panpsychist combination problem.** At most, explain why some force ontologies avoid the problem by not positing micro-subjects.
10. **Separate Tempels, Kagame and Jahn into independently sourced subsections.** The present historical distinction is useful but needs direct references for each reconstruction.
11. **Rebuild the Ubuntu section around explicit alternatives:** ethical personhood, dignity, virtue or moral achievement, relational identity, political Ubuntu, and Ramose-style process ontology.
12. **Do not import the Akan *okra* into Ubuntu exposition.** Place that hybrid only in a boxed or labelled “Map synthesis” section.
13. **Remove or radically qualify “most African traditions” claims about ancestors.** Replace them with named traditions, defined corpora and sources, and distinguish belief in continuing social membership from a philosophical demonstration of persisting phenomenal consciousness.
14. **Label “what is it like for us?” as a Map-generated heuristic**, not as the question asked by “the African oral tradition.”
15. **Revise `interaction-problem-across-traditions`.** Resolve its classification of Yoruba and Tempelsian views as monist avoidances against the target article’s classification of them as dualist support.
16. **Revise `the-hard-problem-in-non-western-philosophy`.** Add direct African specialist sources for its Ubuntu and *Ori* claims or downgrade them to proposed comparisons.
17. **Revise `cross-cultural-convergence-on-mental-causation`.** Remove the unsupported claim that Ubuntu itself maintains mind–brain non-reducibility, and preserve its better caution that West African independence and conceptual translation remain unsettled.
18. **Revise `intersubjectivity`.** Add a properly sourced section distinguishing socially shaped experience from socially constituted phenomenal existence, or cease using it as evidential support for the stronger Ubuntu reading.
19. **Revise the site’s panpsychism and combination-problem articles.** Explicitly distinguish vitalism, animism, force ontology, cosmopsychism and subject-level panpsychism rather than treating them as interchangeable.

### Site methodology

1. **Introduce a claim-to-source ledger.** Every substantive sentence should be tagged internally as direct source report, close paraphrase, scholarly reconstruction, disputed interpretation, Map inference or speculation.
2. **Require page-level citations for primary-source attributions.** A six-item bibliography is not adequate when the article assigns highly specific doctrines to Gyekye, Abimbola and Tempels.
3. **Maintain a translation ledger for key terms.** Record the original-language term, literal range, ritual or social use, competing philosophical translations and why “mind,” “soul,” “consciousness,” “person” or “force” was selected.
4. **Add scope labels to every cultural generalisation.** Specify people, language, period, textual or oral corpus, region and interpreting scholar; prohibit movement from “one author’s reconstruction” to “African traditions” without additional evidence.
5. **Adopt a minimum-dissent rule.** Each tradition-specific section should include at least one serious internal disagreement and one methodological challenge to the dominant interpretation.
6. **Create an exposition–appropriation firewall.** Complete the neutral account and its disputes before asking how the material relates to the Map’s tenets.
7. **Prohibit cross-tradition repairs unless explicitly marked as original synthesis.** An Akan soul cannot be used to repair Ubuntu anti-substantialism while the result is still described as an African traditional position.
8. **Apply an inference test to religious and ancestral claims.** Distinguish “this community has a concept of survival” from “survival is metaphysically possible” and from “survival actually occurs.”
