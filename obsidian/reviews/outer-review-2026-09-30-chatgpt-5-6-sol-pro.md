## Reply

# External audit: “Galilean Exclusion”

## Overall judgement

**Major revision is warranted.** The article contains a valuable and defensible core insight:

> The success of mathematical physics without phenomenal vocabulary does not, by itself, show that phenomenal reality is reducible to physical structure.

That is a strong **undercutting argument** against one route from physics’ success to physicalism. The article’s problem is that it repeatedly upgrades this modest conclusion into three much stronger claims:

1. Galileo made an essentially methodological choice rather than advancing a metaphysical thesis.
2. That choice permanently fixed what scientific explanation can address.
3. The hard problem therefore became “structurally inevitable.”

None of those stronger conclusions follows from the genealogy alone. The first is historically misleading unless heavily qualified; the second mistakes one form of physical explanation for science as an indefinitely revisable practice; and the third depends on an antecedent anti-functionalist, anti-representationalist conception of phenomenal character. In its present form, the article sometimes presents that contested conception as a result of the Galilean history rather than one of the premises needed to make the history philosophically consequential.

The source list contains no fabricated works, and the September revision substantially improved the treatment of ontic structural realism. But several citations are being asked to support conclusions broader than their sources warrant.

## 1. The Galileo reading

### What the article gets right

The article correctly identifies a passage in *The Assayer* in which Galileo distinguishes qualities inseparable from the conception of a corporeal body—figure, size, position, motion or rest, and number—from colours, tastes, smells, sounds and heat as sensed. It is also correct that the familiar terminology of “primary” and “secondary” qualities was systematised later, especially by Boyle and Locke.

The tickling example is accurately represented. Galileo argues that ticklishness is in the sentient being, not in the touching hand as an additional external quality. He similarly proposes that particles and motions in external bodies produce tastes, smells, sounds and warmth in sensitive organs. Modern scholarship therefore describes his position as locating secondary qualities inside sentient bodies as effects of mechanical causes. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qualities-prim-sec/)

The article’s later “relocation” language is therefore preferable to language of simple elimination. Galileo leaves a phenomenon in the sensitive body; he denies that it inheres in the insentient object that causes it.

### Where it goes wrong

The sentence that Galileo “did not *discover* that secondary qualities are unreal” is unobjectionable as an epistemological reminder: Galileo did not empirically prove a uniquely correct metaphysics of qualities. But the article then says he “*decided* to exclude them from physics,” making the passage sound like a self-consciously neutral restriction of method.

That is not Galileo’s own stated position. In *The Assayer*, he treats figure, motion and number as essential or “primary” affections of bodies and says that the other qualities would be annihilated if sentient animals were removed. His argument is explicitly about which attributes genuinely belong to external bodies, not only about which variables it is useful for physicists to model. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qualities-prim-sec/)

The historically safer distinction is:

- **Galileo’s claim:** external bodies really possess mathematically characterisable affections; sensible qualities as experienced do not inhere in those bodies.
- **The article’s retrospective diagnosis:** Galileo’s evidence did not justify treating the methodological success of quantification as a complete metaphysics of reality.

The second may be a good criticism of the first. It should not be substituted for it.

### Exclusion versus relocation and explanation

The opening claim that modern science “removed subjective experience from its domain of inquiry” is also too strong. Galileo did not merely throw sensation away. He offered a mechanical programme for explaining how external particles and motions produce sensory effects in sentient bodies. The relevant SEP article describes this as an antecedent of later neuropsychological explanation. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qualities-prim-sec/)

The article should therefore say that Galileo excluded sensible qualities from the **fundamental properties attributed to external physical bodies**, or from the **explanatory primitives of mathematical physics**. He did not exclude sensory phenomena from every possible natural-scientific inquiry.

This correction would sharpen rather than weaken the philosophical issue. The genuine question becomes:

> Can a causal explanation of why a sensitive body undergoes an experience also explain the phenomenal character of that experience?

That is the explanatory-gap question. It is not settled simply by observing that Galileo relocated sensory qualities.

### “Foundational” and “made modern science possible”

The claim that this one decision “made modern science possible” is too monocausal and historically unargued. Recent Galileo scholarship describes the primary/secondary-quality passage as something that has attracted considerable retrospective attention while being relatively incidental to Galileo’s own wider programme. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2026/entries/galileo/)

The article is entitled to follow the Husserl–Whitehead genealogy, but it must label that genealogy as an interpretive reconstruction. “A founding move in one influential philosophical reconstruction of modern science” would be substantially safer than “the foundational methodological move.”

## 2. Citation-by-citation audit

### Galilei, *The Assayer* (1623/1957), translated by Stillman Drake

**Existence and bibliographic identification:** correct.

**Supports:** the contrast between mathematically characterisable bodily affections and sensible qualities; the tickling example; the dependence of tastes, colours and similar qualities on a sensitive body; a mechanistic causal account of sensory production.

**Does not support:** the representation of Galileo as making only a neutral methodological convenience or consciously withholding metaphysical judgement.

There is also a translation issue worth surfacing. More recent translations use “sensitive body” for *corpo sensitivo*, whereas the Drake tradition can encourage more mentalistic formulations such as qualities residing in “consciousness.” The present article mostly uses the safer sentient-body formulation, but it should cite the relevant passage or section and mention the translation choice. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qualities-prim-sec/)

**Verdict:** genuine and central source; interpretation needs major qualification.

### Husserl, *The Crisis of European Sciences and Transcendental Phenomenology* (1936/1970), translated by David Carr

**Existence and edition:** correct; the Carr translation was published by Northwestern University Press in 1970. [Northwestern University Press](https://nupress.northwestern.edu/9780810167988/crisis-of-european-sciences-and-transcendental-phenomenology/)

**Supports strongly:** the article’s statement that Husserl diagnosed a substitution of the mathematically constructed or “substructed” world for the experienced life-world. Sections §9h–i are precisely the relevant locus. Husserl says that the mathematically idealised world came to be treated as the real world while the experienceable life-world was demoted. [Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews+1](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/science-and-the-life-world-essays-on-husserl-s-crisis-of-european-sciences/)

**Does not support by itself:** interactionist dualism, the impossibility of naturalising consciousness, or the claim that all future science must remain incapable of explaining phenomenal character. Husserl’s argument concerns objectivism, forgotten experiential grounding, and the meaning of scientific idealisation. It is not a straightforward argument from phenomenology to the Map’s ontology.

The article should replace the bare book reference with **§9h–i**, and identify this as “Husserl’s philosophical reconstruction of Galileo,” not simply as established history.

**Verdict:** faithful core citation; metaphysical implications inflated.

### Whitehead, *The Concept of Nature* (1920)

**Existence and attribution:** correct.

**Supports strongly:** the “bifurcation of nature” diagnosis. Whitehead attacks the division between the nature encountered in awareness—greenness, warmth, birdsong, hardness—and a conjectured causal nature of molecules and electrons. He rejects the picture of one nature as dream and the other as reality, insisting that there is one nature and that scientific understanding concerns relations within it. [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/18835/pg18835-images.html)

The article’s gloss—scientifically respectable structure versus dismissed experienced quality—is directionally accurate but somewhat reductive. Whitehead’s immediate target is a theory of perception dividing apparent nature from causal nature, not simply a sociological rule about what scientists respect.

**Verdict:** substantially faithful; add Chapter II and present Whitehead’s one-nature alternative.

### Whitehead, *Science and the Modern World* (1925)

**Existence and bibliographic identification:** correct.

**Supports:** Whitehead’s later development of an ontology in which colours and other qualities are ingredients in natural processes rather than unreal mental additions; it also develops his criticisms of misplaced concreteness and simple location. [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/68611/68611-h/68611-h.htm)

**Current problem:** no distinct claim in the article is pinned to a chapter or passage in this book. It currently functions as a general prestige citation for “developing it further.”

**Verdict:** relevant but underused and unpinned. Either attach it to a specific proposition and page/chapter or remove it from this short concept article.

### Thompson, *Mind in Life* (2007)

**Existence and bibliographic identification:** correct, though “Belknap Press of Harvard University Press” would be the more precise imprint description. [RCNi Company Limited+1](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/597746)

The book’s documented central programme is to integrate biology, phenomenology and embodied dynamicism, enriching the resources for addressing the explanatory gap rather than claiming to have closed it. [Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/mind-in-life-biology-phenomenology-and-the-sciences-of-mind/)

**Citation problem:** the article attaches Thompson 2007 directly to the claim that modern science inherited from Galileo the assumption that mathematical-physical description exhausts objective reality. I could verify Thompson’s broad phenomenological and anti-reductionist programme, but the article supplies no page or chapter supporting that precise historical assertion. As currently given, the citation is not adequately auditable.

*Mind in Life* is better used to support:

- the need to integrate phenomenological and biological descriptions;
- the irreducibility of first-person inquiry as an evidential practice;
- the enactive alternative to Cartesian mind–world separation.

The explicitly Galilean “blind spot” genealogy is much more securely attached to the 2024 coauthored book.

**Verdict:** real and relevant source, but weak support for the exact sentence to which it is attached.

### Frank, Gleiser and Thompson, *The Blind Spot* (2024)

**Existence, publisher and date:** correct; MIT Press published it on 5 March 2024. [MIT Press](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262048804/the-blind-spot/)

**Supports strongly:** the critique of mistaking abstract mathematical models for reality as such; the claim that science originates in lived experience; the rejection of a supposedly perspective-free “God’s-eye view”; and the call to restore experience to scientific self-understanding. [MIT Press+1](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262048804/the-blind-spot/)

But this source also presents a significant problem for the article’s sieve analogy. Thompson explicitly distinguishes legitimate scientific abstraction from the metaphysical hierarchy sometimes attached to it: there is nothing in mathematical science itself that requires treating human experience as ontologically inferior. In another interview, the authors stress that the problem is not scientific modelling but forgetting its experiential grounding and reifying the model; Adam Frank calls the offending doctrine a metaphysics “glued onto science,” not science itself. [Love & Philosophy+1](https://loveandphilosophy.com/beyond-dichotomy-podcast/evan-thompson-philosopher)

Thus *The Blind Spot* supports:

> Scientific abstraction can be illegitimately converted into an exclusionary metaphysics.

It does **not** straightforwardly support:

> Scientific method is essentially a sieve that must continue filtering out phenomenality.

Indeed, the book’s constructive programme presupposes that scientific culture and method can be reformed.

**Verdict:** strong source for the anti-objectivist diagnosis; contrary evidence against the article’s fixed-method formulation.

### Chalmers, *The Conscious Mind* (1996)

**Existence and attribution:** correct.

**Supports:** the structural-functional formulation of the hard problem. Chalmers argues that ordinary empirical explanations are structural or functional while phenomenal consciousness is not exhausted by structure or function. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2024/entries/consciousness-neuroscience/)

**Does not support:** the article’s historical claim that physical and phenomenal descriptions “are different in kind because Galileo made them so.” Chalmers’s argument is modal, conceptual and metaphysical; its force does not depend on a seventeenth-century historical decision. If anything, Chalmers supplies one of the independent premises the Galilean argument requires: that phenomenal character is not reducible to structure and function.

**Verdict:** relevant to the hard problem, but the article reverses the order of support. Chalmers’s anti-reductionist premise gives the Galilean genealogy its bite; the genealogy does not establish the Chalmers premise.

## 3. Does the sieve argument survive?

The sieve metaphor is rhetorically effective but logically ambiguous. Its strongest reconstruction is:

1. A Galilean-style science describes only structure, dynamics and function.
2. Phenomenal character is not identical to structure, dynamics or function.
3. Therefore, merely extending that style of description cannot explain phenomenal character completely.

That argument is valid. But premise 2 is the central disputed proposition in the philosophy of consciousness. Functionalists, representationalists, identity theorists, phenomenal-concept theorists and illusionists reject it in different ways. The Galileo history does not establish it.

### The form of the argument that survives

The article can securely conclude:

> The success of structural-functional explanation cannot, without an additional metaphysical argument, establish that phenomenal character is exhausted by structure and function.

This is a meaningful result. It blocks an inference from methodological success to ontological completeness. Husserl, Whitehead and *The Blind Spot* all provide resources for this narrower warning.

It also supports a conditional:

> If phenomenal character is non-structural and non-functional, then a methodology restricted to structural-functional explanation will leave it out.

That is true, but it is conditional on the very anti-reductionist premise the Map endorses.

### The target/evidence objection

The article’s response to report-based cognitive science says that reports are third-person data “about” experience rather than experience itself. That observation is true but insufficient.

Scientific data are ordinarily not identical to their targets. A seismograph trace is not an earthquake; a detector event is not an electron; a spectrum is not a star. The fact that evidence is not the phenomenon itself does not show that it cannot support explanations of the phenomenon.

Consciousness presents an unusual epistemic asymmetry because the subject has first-person access that the investigator lacks. The article is right to stress that. But it still needs to explain why indirect access is merely evidence in other sciences yet a constitutive barrier here.

One possible answer is that phenomenal explanation requires acquaintance rather than causal or structural understanding. But then the article must defend that standard of explanation. Otherwise, it risks demanding that an explanation of pain itself hurt, or that an account of red itself appear red. That confuses **explaining**, **representing**, and **instantiating** a phenomenon.

### The revisability objection

A physical sieve has a fixed mesh. Science does not. It changes its explanatory categories, evidential practices and conceptual ontology. Neurophenomenology, psychophysics, disciplined introspection, second-person methods and contemporary formal work connecting first- and third-person descriptions are attempts to alter the investigative apparatus rather than simply pass reports through an unchanged Galilean filter.

Varela’s neurophenomenological proposal explicitly requires rigorous first-person methods and “mutual constraints” between phenomenological and cognitive-scientific findings. Later work describes disciplined first-person accounts as integral to validating neuroscientific proposals, and current formal research continues to model passages among phenomenological, computational, behavioural and physiological descriptions. [Theotodman+2](https://www.theotodman.com/Abstracts/Abstract_24/Abstract_24399.htm)

These programmes may fail to close the metaphysical explanatory gap. But their existence defeats the simple historical inference:

> Science began by abstracting from X, therefore science as such cannot develop methods directed at X.

### The genetic-fallacy objection

The internal September review says it considered the objection that the history of physics’ omission of experience tells us nothing about whether experience is physical, and judged that the article had already conceded the point. But the current article still says that the hard problem is “structurally inevitable,” that physical and experiential descriptions differ “because Galileo made them so,” and that scientific explanation was “constructed by removing” its current target.

Those are not merely claims that physics’ silence fails to prove physicalism. They treat the genealogy as an explanation of the explanatory gap itself. The genetic-fallacy objection therefore remains live.

A more defensible claim would be:

> The Galilean genealogy helps explain why modern physical theories do not contain phenomenal predicates and why their empirical success cannot automatically settle the ontology of experience. It does not establish that no expanded natural science could explain experience.

### The article’s universal-hard-problem addition

The 10 September addition asks whether a similar residue survives every level transition. This is a useful cross-reference, but it does not strengthen the sieve argument. It potentially changes the subject:

- If the problem occurs at every emergence transition, it is not specifically generated by excluding consciousness.
- If phenomenal consciousness remains uniquely difficult, the article needs to explain what distinguishes the phenomenal remainder from ordinary higher-level novelty.
- If every higher-level property outruns lower-level structure, the argument threatens to become a general anti-reductionism rather than an argument about Galileo or consciousness.

Calling the universalist interpretation “well-motivated rather than forced” is calibration language, not an answer to these alternatives. The added cross-link records a tension without resolving it.

## 4. Are the counterarguments fairly represented?

### Functionalism: no

The article acknowledges that functionalism maps mental states to functional roles, but then effectively treats it as another way of collecting reports. This conflates two different levels:

- **Functionalism** is a metaphysical or constitutive thesis about what mental states are.
- **Report-based experimentation** is an epistemic method for gathering evidence about mental states.

A functionalist does not normally say: “We cannot reach experience, so let us study reports instead.” The functionalist says that possessing the relevant sufficiently rich causal-functional organisation is what having the experience consists in. Reports, introspective dispositions, memory, discrimination, attention, affective effects and behavioural control may all be parts of that organisation.

Absent- and inverted-qualia objections challenge this view, but sophisticated functionalists dispute whether the imagined systems really preserve the complete relevant functional organisation. The contemporary debate is not exhausted by the observation that reports are not qualia. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy+1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/functionalism/)

The article therefore begs the question when it says functionalism “accommodates” experience only inside the Galilean framework. To establish that, it must first show that functional identity leaves a phenomenal residue. That argument exists elsewhere on the site, but the present page cannot treat its conclusion as supplied by Galileo.

### Identity theory and phenomenal-concept strategies: largely absent

An identity theorist can agree that phenomenal and physical descriptions have very different cognitive roles while denying that they pick out different properties. Water and molecular descriptions are not interchangeable at the conceptual level, yet may refer to the same reality. A phenomenal-concept theorist similarly explains the epistemic gap through special concepts or modes of presentation rather than an ontological gap.

These positions may be wrong, but they directly answer the move from difference in description to difference in ontology. The article needs at least a paragraph explaining why its historical thesis is not neutral between:

- ontological dualism;
- one property under distinct concepts;
- psycho-physical identity discovered empirically;
- an epistemic or conceptual gap without an ontological gap.

### Representationalism and relational accounts of qualities: absent

The page frequently moves between “redness as experienced,” secondary qualities and an allegedly intrinsic phenomenal residue. But many theories of colour and perception understand colour as relational, dispositional, representational or response-dependent. Functional and representational theories of qualia are established positions, not merely attempts to ignore the phenomenon. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qualia/)

The article need not survey the entire colour literature, but it should not present only two options:

1. phenomenal qualities are intrinsic contents excluded by physics; or
2. they are abolished.

There are intermediate views on which experienced colour is real but relationally constituted and objectively investigable.

### Modern consciousness science: too uniform a picture

The article acknowledges reports, then dismisses them as third-person transformations. It does not adequately engage:

- disciplined phenomenological reports;
- mutual-constraint methodologies;
- no-report and minimal-report paradigms;
- psychophysical modelling of phenomenal similarity spaces;
- causal interventions that alter reported and unreported conscious contents;
- attempts to formalise mappings between phenomenal and neural structures.

None of these automatically solves the hard problem. Their relevance is narrower: they show that contemporary consciousness science is not adequately described as a single unchanged seventeenth-century method.

### Neurophenomenology: unfairly categorised

The claim that panpsychism, neurophenomenology and radical enactivism “gain resources” but risk losing the rigour made possible by Galilean exclusion is too indiscriminate.

- Panpsychism is principally a metaphysical position.
- Neurophenomenology is expressly a methodological programme for increasing rigour in first-person investigation.
- Radical enactivism is a family of theories concerning cognition and content.

Lumping all three together under “breaking from the framework” obscures their different aims. Varela’s programme was explicitly presented as a rigorous methodological remedy based on reciprocal constraint, not a retreat from science. [Theotodman+1](https://www.theotodman.com/Abstracts/Abstract_24/Abstract_24399.htm)

The site’s own methodology article calls neurophenomenology a “real advance,” making the Galilean page’s casual rigour warning an internal tension.

## 5. The ontic-structural-realist challenge

This is the article’s best-handled major objection after the September repair.

It now correctly distinguishes:

- OSR as a thesis about the fundamental ontology described by physics;
- the additional thesis that phenomenal character is also exhausted by relational structure;
- the composite position on which no non-structural residue exists in either matter or experience.

It also correctly concedes that the circularity charge—“physics omitted the residue and then declared itself complete”—does not touch a view which denies that there is any non-structural residue in the first place. The September review specifically repaired an earlier formulation that wrongly made ordinary OSR sound committed to abolishing consciousness or qualia.

Two qualifications should still be brought into the present article rather than left entirely to the linked page:

1. **The strongest global position is a composite.** Most OSR literature concerns physical ontology and is largely silent about consciousness. Phenomenal structuralism does the additional work.
2. **Newman’s problem is not a clean external refutation.** It was originally pressed against epistemic structuralism and therefore also bears on structuralist commitments used elsewhere by the Map. The site’s OSR article itself acknowledges that the argument yields an asymmetry in available replies rather than a decisive result.

The Galilean article should retain its current concession and add one sentence saying that structuralism constitutes a genuine rival interpretation rather than merely another materialism guilty of the original exclusion.

## 6. Tenet alignment and protective bracketing

### Dualism

The section initially says the history provides “context,” which would be properly calibrated. It then upgrades that context into “a recognition of structural limits built into the scientific method.”

That is precisely what remains disputed. The Galileo history shows at most that a successful physical methodology abstracted from phenomenal quality. It does not show that every possible scientific method is structurally unable to explain it, nor that the gap is ontological.

This is protective bracketing because the tenet is allowed to settle the interpretation of the history: once irreducibility is assumed, the methodological omission becomes evidence of a structural limit. A functionalist, identity theorist or phenomenal-concept theorist interprets the same history differently.

The site’s own tenets page says that dualism is a chosen commitment in an unsettled dispute, not a result that the site reports as won.

A properly aligned formulation would be:

> Under the Map’s dualist tenet, the Galilean genealogy explains why the success of physical science is not regarded as evidence against irreducible consciousness. The genealogy is compatible with dualism but does not independently establish it.

### Bidirectional interaction

This paragraph is better calibrated because it begins conditionally: **if** consciousness makes physical causal contributions, then a description excluding them will be causally incomplete.

But this is a framework-internal consequence, not evidence that physics actually omits causes. Rival positions will say that the causal work is already performed by the physical realisers of conscious states, or that phenomenal properties are identical with those realisers, or that irreducible phenomenal properties are causally inert.

The section should say explicitly:

> This is what follows from Tenet 3; the Galilean history supplies no independent evidence for downward causation.

Without that sentence, “physics incomplete” sounds like a result derived from the article rather than a restatement of the tenet.

### Limits of Occam’s razor

This is the most protectively framed subsection. It says materialist simplicity is purchased by “excluding the phenomenon” and declaring it reducible to what remains.

But functionalists, identity theorists and representationalists deny that they have excluded anything. Their claim is that the phenomenon has been identified or explained, not omitted. Illusionists dispute the article’s description of what the phenomenon is.

The omission charge works only after an independent case has established a non-structural residue. It cannot itself establish that residue.

A calibrated version would say:

> The genealogy blocks a particular parsimony argument: one cannot infer completeness merely from the success of a description constructed around quantifiable structure. Whether a materialist account actually omits phenomenal facts remains the substantive dispute.

### Conflict with the site’s own evidential discipline

The site’s evidential-status discipline says that a tenet may remove a defeater but must not upgrade the evidence, and that compatibility must not be presented as discriminating support.

The Galilean article needs a philosophical analogue of that rule:

> A genealogy may expose an unearned assumption in a rival view, but it must not upgrade compatibility with dualism into evidence that science has an intrinsic dualist boundary.

## 7. Final assessment

The article should not be discarded. Its strongest contribution is a clear diagnosis of a genuine inference error:

> “Physics explains its domain without intrinsic phenomenal predicates” does not entail “phenomenal reality is nothing over and above what physics describes.”

That point survives the audit.

What does not survive is the stronger claim that Galileo’s decision by itself made the hard problem inevitable or that contemporary scientific explanation remains definitionally confined by a seventeenth-century sieve. To reach that conclusion, the article must separately defend the non-structural, non-functional nature of phenomenality and answer theories that deny the alleged residue. Once those premises are exposed, “Galilean exclusion” becomes a useful historical and methodological lens rather than an independent argument for dualism.

# Concrete potential improvements

## Improvements to specific articles

1. **Rewrite the opening of “Galilean Exclusion.”** Replace “the foundational methodological move” with “a founding move in an influential Husserlian and Whiteheadian genealogy of modern science.” Replace “removed subjective experience from its domain of inquiry” with “excluded phenomenal qualities from the fundamental properties and explanatory primitives attributed to external physical bodies.”
2. **Separate Galileo’s position from the article’s diagnosis.** Add a short subsection titled “Galileo’s Metaphysical Claim and the Retrospective Methodological Critique.” State that Galileo himself treated primary affections as genuinely bodily and secondary qualities as dependent on sensitive bodies; the article’s “decision, not discovery” language is a criticism of his warrant, not a neutral paraphrase.
3. **Replace the sieve paragraph with an explicit conditional argument.** State its premises openly: Galilean explanation is structural-functional; phenomenal character is not exhausted by structure-function; therefore that explanatory form is incomplete. ## Improvements to site methodology

1. **Separate body_last_modified from metadata/provenance changes.** The 21 September date currently suggests substantive revision even though the changelog records zero body changes. Public pages should display both dates when they differ.
2. **Require claim-level citation verification, not merely work-level verification.** Confirming that a book exists and has the correct publisher is not enough; the review must record the exact proposition, page or section, and whether the source supports, qualifies or contradicts it.
3. **Create a citation-entailment ledger.** For each load-bearing citation record: claim, source passage, support level, limitations, and interpretive additions by the Map.
4. **Extend the evidential-status discipline to philosophical and historical claims.** Add explicit categories such as “documented textual claim,” “contested historiography,” “Map reconstruction,” and “framework-conditional implication.”
5. **Add a genealogy-to-metaphysics check.** Every historical argument should be tested for the invalid transitions “origin therefore validity,” “initial method therefore permanent limit,” and “past exclusion therefore present ontological residue.”
6. **Add a method–ontology separation check.** Reviews should ask separately what the investigative method records, what the theory claims the target is, what ontology is assumed, and whether conclusions have moved illicitly between those levels.
7. **Require a strongest-rival paragraph for tenet-adjacent concept pages.** A page that bears on Dualism should present the strongest identity-theoretic, functionalist, representationalist or illusionist interpretation in terms its proponents would recognise.
8. **Audit reciprocal links for circular corroboration.** Automated cross-linking should flag cases where Article A supports premise P by citing Article B while Article B supports P by citing Article A. Internal coherence must not be counted as independent evidence.
9. **Review cross-link prose substantively.** The workflow record notes that prose inserted into neighbouring pages can initially escape direct review. Any automated cross-link that adds argumentative content rather than a bare link should trigger a claim-level review of both pages.
10. **Do not exempt methodological or historical claims from calibration review.** The September deep review treated the article as making no evidential-status claims because its subject was history and method, yet “structurally inevitable” and “built into scientific method” are strong necessity claims requiring at least as much calibration as empirical claims.
11. **Introduce an “explanation standard” field for hard-problem articles.** Each article should state what it demands of an explanation: prediction, causal mechanism, identity, conceptual entailment, phenomenal acquaintance, structural mapping, or reproduction of experience. Many apparent disagreements currently arise because these standards shift unannounced.
12. **Require independent external support for every load-bearing cross-cluster thesis.** Site-authored synthesis can organise evidence, but a chain of Map pages should not be allowed to serve as the sole support for claims such as “the boundary admits no criterion” or “the same residue appears at every level transition.”
13. **Add a “what would weaken this interpretation?” subsection.** For this article, relevant developments would include a successful structural account of phenomenal similarity, a compelling functional identity theory, a naturalised first-person methodology, or a theory showing that the explanatory gap is conceptual rather than ontological.
14. **Prioritise human curation for historically load-bearing pages.** The current page is recorded as entirely AI-contributed with no human-curation date. A historian-of-science or early-modern-philosophy review would be particularly valuable before the Galileo genealogy continues propagating through linked articles.
