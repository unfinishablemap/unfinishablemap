## Reply

# Referee report: “Knowledge Argument”

**Recommendation: major revision.**

I reviewed the live article on 19 August 2026. Its page metadata still records 9 August 2026 as the last substantive revision. The 19 August changelog records a Tallis-reference formatting sweep and indicates that variants in the Knowledge Argument article were deliberately left unchanged; I found no later substantive correction relevant to this audit. The site root also states openly that the Map begins from five chosen tenets and that its argumentation is partisan. That is legitimate, but it makes especially important the separation of neutral exposition, framework-conditional inference, and independent evidence. [[knowledge-argument]]

## Overall assessment

The article is lucid, well structured, and more self-aware than many defences of the knowledge argument. Its discussion of Dennett acknowledges that the anti-physicalist response risks begging the question, and its final relation-to-the-Map section concedes both that the epistemic-to-metaphysical inference is contested and that interactionism is imported from the Map rather than established by Mary’s case. Those concessions are important. [[knowledge-argument]]

Nevertheless, the article is not yet reliable as a map of the contemporary debate. Its central formalisation suppresses the argument’s most contested bridge premise; acquaintance is appropriated as support for dualism instead of treated as a leading physicalist reply; the treatment of Jackson’s 2003 reversal is materially inaccurate; the phenomenal-concept discussion omits stronger responses already acknowledged elsewhere on the site; and several empirical claims substantially outrun their sources. Much of the post-2020 literature that bears directly on these issues is absent.

The resulting article is strongest as a **framework-conditional advocacy essay**: given the Map’s antecedent dualism, Mary illustrates one way of describing the epistemic gap. It is much weaker as an independent argument that physicalism is false, and weaker still as evidence for interactionist dualism.

## 1. The formal argument hides its load-bearing premise

The article formalises the case approximately as follows:

1. Mary knows all physical facts.
2. On seeing colour, she learns something.
3. What she learns was not included among the physical facts.
4. Therefore there are nonphysical facts.
5. Therefore physicalism is false.

It then says that the “force comes from premise 2.” That is incorrect. Under the ordinary, broad meaning of “learns something,” the inference from 2 to 3 is invalid. Someone can acquire an ability, acquaintance, a new mode of presentation, a new concept, a nonconceptual representation, or a capacity to token a state without discovering a previously unknown fact. The force therefore comes from an unstated bridge premise:

> Every relevant epistemic gain Mary makes must consist in learning a previously unknown true proposition.

That bridge is precisely what the ability, acquaintance, phenomenal-concept, representationalist, and old-fact/new-mode replies deny. Strengthening premise 2 to “Mary learns a new fact” would make the inference more plausible, but would build the disputed conclusion into the premise. [[knowledge-argument]]

The article should therefore distinguish at least five claims:

- Mary enters a new psychological or representational state.
- Mary acquires new know-how.
- Mary becomes acquainted with colour experience.
- Mary can entertain an old truth under a new concept or mode of presentation.
- Mary learns a truth that was not among the complete physical truths.

Only the fifth directly threatens physicalism, and it does not follow without further argument from the first four.

The formulation of premise 1 is also underspecified. “All physical facts” could mean all lower-level scientific descriptions, all truths entailed by an ideal physical theory, all broadly physical truths including higher-level psychological truths, or all propositions available without instantiating certain physical states. Different physicalists will accept different formulations. The Map’s own Materialism article recognises physicalism as a family of views, but the Knowledge Argument article generally addresses it as a single, homogeneous thesis. [[materialism]]

Even if the argument succeeded against reductive physicalism, it would not by itself select interactionist dualism. It would remain compatible with property dualism, epiphenomenalism, Russellian monism, neutral monism, panpsychist approaches, and some forms of idealism. The article ultimately admits this, while Alter’s recent book develops the argument toward Russellian monism rather than interactionism. The opening claim that Mary “directly supports dualism” should therefore be narrowed to something like “provides a disputed argument against physical completeness.” [[knowledge-argument]]

## 2. The standard replies are not adequately represented

### Ability hypotheses

The article names Lewis and Nemirow and offers two familiar objections: abilities are allegedly neither necessary nor sufficient for phenomenal knowledge. But the counterexamples are presented at assertion level. It does not reconstruct why an ability theorist would reject the proposed cases, distinguish pure ability accounts from hybrid accounts, or engage Nemirow’s later defence of the view. [[knowledge-argument]]

This matters because a contemporary physicalist need not claim that Mary gains **only one ability**. A mixed account can say that she acquires know-how, recognitional and imaginative capacities, a new representational state, thin acquaintance, and ordinary physical beliefs generated by those states. Veillet’s 2026 treatment explicitly allows physicalists and anti-physicalists to agree on several such forms of epistemic novelty. The ontological dispute then concerns their explanation, not whether Mary changes at all. (See [Veillet 2026, *Synthese* (verified)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-026-05527-8))

The article should consequently distinguish:

- the pure ability hypothesis;
- ability-plus-representation accounts;
- abilities as part of, rather than exhaustive of, Mary’s progress;
- the stronger claim that no combination of physical abilities and representations can explain revelation.

At present, it refutes only a simplified version of the first.

### Acquaintance hypotheses

This is the most serious omission. The article does mention acquaintance, but treats it as “the strongest grade of indexical knowledge” and places it on the dualist side. That effectively takes a major physicalist reply, adds the adjective “irreducible,” and presents the result as evidence for the Map. [[knowledge-argument]]

The acquaintance hypothesis says that Mary can become directly acquainted with an experience or quality without thereby learning a new proposition about a nonphysical property. Morris’s 2025 defence argues specifically that acquaintance can explain the revelatory character of Mary’s first experience while avoiding commitment to distinctively phenomenal facts. Alter likewise treats acquaintance and ability as separate families of reply rather than variants of the anti-physicalist conclusion. (See [Morris 2025, *Philosophia* (verified)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11406-025-00930-w))

The decisive question is not whether acquaintance is epistemically distinctive. Physicalists can concede that. The question is whether:

1. the object of acquaintance must be nonphysical;
2. the acquaintance relation must be nonphysical;
3. acquaintance entails propositional knowledge of a fact omitted from Mary’s physical knowledge.

The article presently argues for none of these.

Its treatment also conflates three notions:

- **de se or self-locating knowledge**, concerning who or where one is;
- **acquaintance**, a direct epistemic relation to an object, property, or experience;
- **phenomenal concepts**, first-personal ways of representing experiences.

Mary may acquire acquaintance without resolving a de se uncertainty, and she may gain a phenomenal concept without discovering a new object or property. The Map’s proposed hierarchy of “grades” of indexical knowledge therefore cannot simply be assumed in the Knowledge Argument article; it needs an independent defence.

### Phenomenal-concept strategies

The article correctly identifies Loar and Papineau as central proponents and raises a Chalmers-style dilemma. But its description is incomplete and partially conflates the phenomenal-concept argument with the modal zombie argument.

Chalmers’s direct treatment is “Phenomenal Concepts and the Explanatory Gap,” published in the 2007 anthology *Phenomenal Concepts and Phenomenal Knowledge*. The article instead cites his 2010 two-dimensional argument. More importantly, Chalmers presents the phenomenal-concept “master argument” as avoiding reliance on a metaphysical inference from conceivability to possibility. The article’s statement that Type-B physicalists respond by denying that conceivability tracks possibility therefore does not directly answer the phenomenal-concept challenge it has just described. (See [Chalmers, "Phenomenal Concepts and the Explanatory Gap"](https://consc.net/papers/pceg.html))

The omission is particularly noticeable because the Map’s dedicated Phenomenal Concept Strategy article already recognises Carruthers and Veillet as furnishing a strong physicalist reply. That page also notes that Ball’s eliminativist treatment of special phenomenal concepts may have collateral consequences for the knowledge argument itself: if the relevant “new way of knowing” is eliminated, one of Mary’s central premises may be weakened. Neither point is imported into the Knowledge Argument article. [[phenomenal-concepts-strategy]]

Post-2020 alternatives also receive no attention:

- de Sá Pereira develops a Type-B account in which Mary gains nonconceptual content that enriches her conceptual repertoire without establishing a new nonphysical property.
- Sassarini explains part of the intuition through opacity and Frege-puzzle structure in propositional-attitude reports.
- Veillet addresses the “significance challenge”: why Mary’s progress seems surprising and substantial even if physicalism is true. (See [Humanities & Social Sciences Communications (2020)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-020-00671-z))

These are not peripheral additions. Each directly targets the suppressed inference from new epistemic access to new ontology.

### Representationalism and the old-fact/new-mode response

Representationalism receives no adequate standalone treatment, even though it was central to Jackson’s own conversion. A representationalist may say that Mary’s first colour experience represents an already known physical property in a new sensory format. The novelty is genuine at the level of representation and psychological state, but does not require a newly discovered property.

The article needs to explain why this is inadequate rather than merely redescribing the case in anti-physicalist vocabulary. Without such an argument, “new phenomenal knowledge” remains ambiguous between a new fact and a new way of representing an old fact.

## 3. Citation and attribution problems

### Jackson’s 2003 reversal is misdescribed

The most consequential citation error concerns Jackson’s “Mind and Illusion.” The article says Jackson abandoned the knowledge argument because epiphenomenal qualia could not causally affect Mary’s beliefs about them, and then uses that supposed concern to motivate the Map’s bidirectional interactionism.

That is not the central argument of the cited paper. Jackson explicitly says his physicalist answer rests on representationalism and on diagnosing an illusion produced by confusing intensional properties of representations with properties instantiated by represented objects. He allows that Mary acquires new representational states and new abilities, while denying that she thereby discovers a new property. [[knowledge-argument]]

The epiphenomenalist self-knowledge problem is a real anti-epiphenomenalist argument, but it should not be attributed to Jackson’s 2003 explanation of why he changed his mind. The error matters because the article transforms a physicalist representationalist reply into apparent support for interactionist causation. That is both a citation error and a tenet-protective distortion.

### Alter’s book is miscited

The bibliography dates Alter’s *The Matter of Consciousness* to 2024. Oxford University Press gives a publication date of 2 March 2023, and the full title is *The Matter of Consciousness: From the Knowledge Argument to Russellian Monism*. The subtitle is substantively important because it makes clear that Alter’s positive destination is not straightforwardly the Map’s interactionist dualism. (See [Alter, *The Matter of Consciousness*, OUP (verified)](https://academic.oup.com/book/45669))

### The Chalmers citation is not the direct source

The article should cite Chalmers’s chapter on phenomenal concepts for the master dilemma rather than relying on the 2010 two-dimensional argument. The latter is related, but it is not a precise citation for the argument as presented. (See [Chalmers, "Phenomenal Concepts and the Explanatory Gap"](https://consc.net/papers/pceg.html))

### Body and bibliography are out of synchronisation

Papineau, Dennett, Nida-Rümelin, Levine, and Schwitzgebel are named or invoked in the prose but are not properly represented in the bibliography. Tallis appears in the references without a corresponding substantive use in the body. The article should distinguish “Works cited” from “Further reading” rather than maintaining a hybrid list whose evidential role is unclear. [[knowledge-argument]]

## 4. Empirical claims are overstated

### Sytsma and Machery do not directly test the Mary intuition

The article cites Sytsma and Machery to suggest that the intuition that Mary learns something is less uniform outside professional philosophy. Their study is relevant to differences between philosophical and folk concepts of subjective experience, but its experiments concern attributions involving humans, robots, colour perception, and pain. It is not a direct population study of the Mary case or of whether participants judge that Mary learns a new fact. The article’s use is therefore indirect and should be described as such. [[knowledge-argument]]

A more directly relevant empirical source is Berent’s 2024 study of responses to the Mary scenario. Its two relatively small studies found that judgments about transformative experience were affected by whether the change was represented as embodied or registered in the brain, supporting a psychological explanation involving intuitive dualism rather than straightforward evidence for ontological dualism. The samples are too small to settle the issue, but the work is much closer to the claim the article wants to make and should be discussed. (See [Berent 2024, *Neuroscience of Consciousness* (verified)](https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2024/1/niae016/7641203))

### Fox et al. cannot support the claimed introspective boundary

Fox et al. is a preliminary, cross-sectional study of experienced meditators’ reported tactile body maps, compared partly against population-average measures. The authors themselves note important limitations, including the absence of individual objective measures. It does not establish the broad proposition that introspection is unreliable only for causal processes while remaining reliable for subtle phenomenal content. (See [PLOS ONE (reviewer supplied an incomplete URL)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article))

The article’s boundary between unreliable causal introspection and reliable phenomenal introspection may be defensible, but it needs a systematic argument and a much wider literature. Neither Nisbett and Wilson nor Fox et al. establishes it.

### Clinical neuroplasticity is not discriminating evidence for dualism

The article says that clinical neuroplasticity provides empirical support for the causal efficacy of newly acquired phenomenal knowledge. The linked Clinical Neuroplasticity article is substantially more cautious: it calls the evidence suggestive, recognises standard Hebbian and materialist explanations, says that differences in learning route do not establish differences in ontological type, and concludes that the observations are compatible with rather than probative of the Map’s view. [[knowledge-argument]]

The stronger claim in the Knowledge Argument article does not follow. Physicalists normally agree that undergoing a new experience can change a physical brain and subsequently alter reports and behaviour. Evidence that experience causes learning therefore does not discriminate between:

- experience understood as a physical or functionally realised state; and
- a nonphysical experience exerting additional causal influence.

To constitute support for interactionist dualism, the evidence would need a prediction that differs between those models.

### Literature-state claims lack a review basis

Statements that the explanatory gap shows no sign of closing, or that the evidence supports a sharply delimited reliability of introspection, are broad assessments of an active literature. The article provides neither a systematic review nor transparent inclusion criteria for making them. They should be marked as the Map’s assessment rather than reported as established findings. [[knowledge-argument]]

## 5. Several passages protect dualism rather than argue for it

### Epistemic novelty is repeatedly redescribed as ontological novelty

The opening and formal sections speak as though Mary’s new epistemic condition is itself evidence of a nonphysical fact. Later, the article acknowledges that this inference is contested. The caveat arrives too late: by then, the contested bridge has already been used to organise the discussion and classify rival accounts as attempts to explain away an established result. [[knowledge-argument]]

A neutral exposition should state from the outset:

> Everyone agrees that Mary undergoes some epistemic or cognitive change. The dispute is whether that change requires a new fact, and if so whether the fact is nonphysical.

### Acquaintance is made dualist by stipulation

Describing Mary’s gain as acquaintance can be neutral. Describing it as an irreducible, highest-grade form of indexical knowledge already adopts the conclusion. The Map must argue that no physical relation, representational change, or self-locating update can constitute this acquaintance. Its bespoke indexical hierarchy does not supply that argument merely by classifying the case. [[knowledge-argument]]

### Jackson’s reversal is redirected into a defence of interactionism

The article takes what it represents as Jackson’s epiphenomenalist difficulty and says the Map can preserve the knowledge argument by allowing phenomenal knowledge to cause physical changes. This moves directly from a problem for epiphenomenalism to support for the Map’s preferred fifth-stage conclusion. Even were the attribution correct, showing that interactionism avoids one objection to epiphenomenalism would not establish that interactionism is true.

### Causal efficacy is not uniquely dualist

The claim that Mary’s new experience causes subsequent discrimination and report is not evidence against physicalism unless one first assumes the experience is nonphysical. Otherwise, the same causal sequence is predicted by ordinary physical learning. Here the Map’s ontology is being used to classify the cause and then the classified cause is offered as evidence for the ontology.

### Russellian and process approaches are treated as allies too quickly

The article invokes process and Russellian approaches while asserting that structural knowledge never entails experience. But the failure of structural entailment is itself a contested anti-physicalist premise. Moreover, Russellian monism is an alternative account of the intrinsic nature of physical reality, not direct support for a separate interacting mental substance or for quantum-level intervention. Alter’s trajectory from Mary to Russellian monism illustrates this underdetermination. [[knowledge-argument]]

### The cumulative case may double-count one intuition

The article combines Mary, the explanatory gap, qualia, inverted qualia, zombies, and indexical knowledge as mutually reinforcing evidence. Yet many of these arguments depend on overlapping premises: the apparent conceivability of experience without structure, the authority of first-person acquaintance, or the inability to derive phenomenal truths from physical descriptions. Correlated arguments do not automatically provide independent confirmation. The article needs a dependency analysis before claiming cumulative amplification. [[knowledge-argument]]

### “Minimal assumptions” understates the burden

The argument depends on more than Mary’s physical omniscience and post-release surprise. It also requires assumptions about:

- the propositional character of her gain;
- the individuation of facts;
- the relation between modes of presentation and properties;
- the completeness conditions on physical knowledge;
- the reliability and interpretation of the Mary intuition;
- the exclusion of physical acquaintance and nonconceptual representation.

Calling the conclusion modest obscures these bridge commitments.

## 6. Integration with neighbouring articles

The article has **high link density but low argumentative synchronisation**. It points readers toward relevant surrounding pages, yet repeatedly fails to inherit their qualifications and strongest objections.

The dedicated Phenomenal Concept Strategy article contains a more developed physicalist rejoinder and acknowledges Ball’s potentially damaging implications for the knowledge argument. The Knowledge Argument article imports neither. It instead presents Chalmers’s dilemma as though the dialectic largely stops there. [[phenomenal-concepts-strategy]]

The Clinical Neuroplasticity article grades its evidence as weak, compatible with several interpretations, and vulnerable to ordinary learning explanations. The Knowledge Argument article promotes the same material to empirical support without explaining why its evidential grade has changed. This is a concrete cross-article inconsistency. [[clinical-neuroplasticity-evidence-for-bidirectional-causation]]

The Illusionism article recognises a spectrum from strong eliminativist formulations to subtle representational and metacognitive accounts. The Knowledge Argument article instead says, in effect, that illusionists hold that Mary learns nothing substantive. A sophisticated illusionist can grant substantial physical, cognitive, representational, and self-model changes while denying that these reveal intrinsic nonphysical properties. The local treatment therefore caricatures a neighbouring article’s more nuanced taxonomy. [[illusionism]]

The Qualia article acknowledges that intrinsicness, ineffability, and privacy are contested commitments rather than neutral definitions. The Knowledge Argument article nevertheless uses “phenomenal fact” without clarifying whether this means merely a fact about experience or an intrinsically private, nonstructural fact. The latter reading presupposes much of the anti-physicalist conclusion. [[qualia]]

The Indexical Knowledge article offers useful distinctions, but its “three grades” are a Map-specific proposal rather than an established taxonomy. Both pages use that taxonomy to support irreducibility, producing a risk of circular cross-citation: the Knowledge Argument supports phenomenal indexicality, while phenomenal indexicality is then invoked to strengthen the Knowledge Argument. [[indexical-knowledge-and-identity]]

Against Epiphenomenalism is more careful than the Jackson paragraph in acknowledging that phenomenal-concept strategies may explain report causation physically, moving rather than simply resolving the dispute. That qualification should be imported into the Knowledge Argument article. [[epiphenomenalism-argument]]

Finally, the proposed aesthetic and cognitive extensions are asserted to strengthen the argument without demonstrating that they evade the same replies. Aesthetic revelation may be especially amenable to ability, acquaintance, and recognitional-capacity explanations; mathematical or conceptual understanding raises separate questions about understanding and representation. Calling these cases stronger without a comparative argument adds examples rather than independent support.

## Referee conclusion

The article currently overstates what the knowledge argument establishes. Its strongest defensible conclusion is:

> Mary’s case presents a durable challenge to accounts that identify complete third-person physical description with every form of epistemic access to experience.

That is important but substantially weaker than:

> Mary discovers a nonphysical fact, and her resulting behaviour supports interactionist dualism.

Moving from the first claim to the second requires independent arguments against ability, acquaintance, representational, phenomenal-concept, nonconceptual-content, and old-fact/new-mode accounts. Those arguments are presently missing or incomplete.

The Jackson misattribution should be corrected immediately. The absence of a genuine acquaintance section and of relevant 2023–2026 literature requires substantive revision rather than a reference refresh. The article’s final caveats show that its authors understand the underdetermination; the main task is to propagate those caveats into the formal argument, evidence sections, and opening conclusion.

2. **Knowledge Argument:** Define the target thesis. Separate reductive physicalism, Type-A physicalism, Type-B physicalism, representationalism, Russellian physicalism or monism, and functionalism rather than treating “physicalism” as one position.
