# Audit: “Mine-ness and the Ownership Void”

**Text audited:** live page marked “Last modified: 2026-11-19,” together with the site changelog and the principal neighbouring articles. The live page now reflects the 19 November text. [[mine-ness]]

## Executive verdict

**Major revision is needed.** The article successfully identifies a real family of dissociations involving agency, alienation, affect, bodily self-relation and explicit ownership judgements. It does **not**, however, establish its stronger thesis: that these cases isolate one global, separable phenomenal feature—interchangeably called *mine-ness* and *for-me-ness*—which can vanish while otherwise similar consciousness continues.

The article’s central conclusions should be separated as follows:

| Claim | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Ownership, agency, affect, bodily identification and reflective self-awareness can come apart | **Well supported** |
| Depersonalisation is compatible with degraded phenomenal mineness | **Plausible but underdetermined** |
| Depersonalisation demonstrates loss of one global mineness feature | **Not established** |
| Thought insertion shows ownership preserved while agency is lost | **A contested interpretation, not a clinical result** |
| Meditation and depersonalisation instantiate the same absence with different valence | **Overstated** |
| First-person inquiry cannot obtain a wholly ownerless point of view | **Defensible** |
| The mechanism of ownership is therefore structurally unknowable | **Does not follow** |
| The evidence lends support to dualism, quantum interaction or rejection of Many Worlds | **No; at most it is compatible with those positions** |

The article is strongest when it presents mine-ness as an investigable dimension and acknowledges Knappik’s localist challenge. It becomes weakest when it turns a contested phenomenological interpretation into an empirical discovery, and then turns a limitation of first-person access into an in-principle explanatory or metaphysical barrier.

## 1. The foundational problem: *for-me-ness* and *mineness* are conflated

The opening defines mine-ness as an experience’s presenting as belonging to a subject, immediately glosses that as its happening “for me,” calls it the phenomenological *for-me-ness*, and later treats its disappearance as compatible with continued consciousness. Yet the article also endorses the Zahavian claim that for-me-ness is constitutive of every conscious state. These are not merely two formulations of the same proposal: if a feature is constitutive of consciousness, a conscious state from which it has disappeared is precisely what requires argument. [[mine-ness]]

Marie Guillot’s distinction was designed to prevent this slide:

- **For-me-ness:** the experience is first-personally given or available to a subject.
- **Me-ness:** the subject is in some way aware of herself in undergoing it.
- **Mineness:** the experience presents as the subject’s own.

Guillot argues that these notions are not equivalent and, in particular, that for-me-ness does not conceptually entail me-ness or mineness. [discovery.ucl.ac.uk+1](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1485857/17/Guillot%202016%20%20I%20Me%20Mine%20-%20On%20a%20Confusion%20Concerning%20Subjective%20Character%20of%20experience%20VoR.pdf)

This distinction supplies the most obvious interpretation of the clinical cases: **mineness may be attenuated while for-me-ness remains**. A depersonalised subject can first-personally encounter an experience that feels alien or insufficiently “mine”; a thought-insertion patient can have privileged access to content while denying that it is hers. Neither case by itself establishes consciousness without any first-personal givenness.

The problem is not unknown to the project. The changelog explicitly records that the “for-me-ness/mineness vocabulary collision” was not repaired and was marked “NEEDS-HUMAN”; it also records that the stronger sentence that depersonalisation “proves” disappearance was merely changed to “shows.” [[changelog]] This is the article’s largest unresolved defect, not a terminological nicety. Almost every subsequent inference depends on which feature is allegedly degraded.

There is also an internal oscillation:

- Meditation is said to produce experience without “someone experiencing it.”
- DPD and meditation are said to share the “same structural absence.”
- Later, meditation is described as reducing mine-ness’s “grip rather than removing it altogether.”
- Depersonalisation is nevertheless said to show that mine-ness “can vanish.” [[mine-ness]]

Those propositions cannot all carry their ordinary meanings. The article needs separate variables for first-person availability, explicit appropriation, self-identification, agency, narrative identity, bodily ownership and the felt salience of self.

## 2. Depersonalisation: genuine dissociation, but no clean global-tag experiment

### What the evidence supports

The Billon quotation—DPD as involving “the lack of a phenomenal feature that marks my experiences as mine”—is **verbatim and correctly attributed**. Billon does indeed argue that a more or less extensive lack of mineness is the best explanation of depersonalisation reports. [philpapers.org](https://philpapers.org/archive/BILWII.pdf)

The Map is also right that such reports pose a serious challenge to simple equations of consciousness, agency, affect and ordinary self-familiarity. Patients can remain perceptually and cognitively engaged while experiencing thoughts, emotions, actions, memories or bodily sensations as alien, distant, mechanically produced or insufficiently connected to themselves. Billon catalogues several different symptom clusters rather than just one generic loss. [philpapers.org](https://philpapers.org/archive/BILWII.pdf)

### Where the article overstates the result

Billon explicitly presents his analysis as **abductive**: lack of mineness is proposed as the best unified explanation of reports, not read directly from an experimental measure. He also describes the relevant dimensions as graded rather than all-or-nothing. [philpapers.org+1](https://philpapers.org/archive/BILWII.pdf) The Map occasionally acknowledges this by labelling its account a “reconstruction,” but elsewhere reverts to stronger language such as “DPD shows that mine-ness can vanish.”

That stronger conclusion is not warranted for four reasons.

First, the explanandum is heterogeneous. Emotional numbing, reduced agency or control, bodily alienation, unreality, observer-like detachment, changes in memory and changes in temporal experience do not amount automatically to the disappearance of one global feature. Knappik’s central objection is precisely that the variability and independent occurrence of depersonalisation and derealisation favour local, type-specific self-relations over a single impaired mark common to all experience. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11097-022-09872-0)

Second, the article’s claim that phenomenal contents remain “largely fixed” is too strong. If pain ceases to feel fully suffered, music loses affective significance, memories cease to feel personally lived, actions feel automatic, and the body is encountered as distant, then the experiential content has changed in several dimensions. The evidence may show that sensory discrimination and some cognition continue; it does not provide a content-matched pair differing only by a mineness bit.

Third, the coherence of the report does not by itself prove the claimed architecture. The article reasons that patients can report experiences as alien only if bare experience and mine-ness are separable. But the rival can say that the patient retains minimal for-me-ness—the first-person access required for reporting—while losing affective familiarity, self-identification or a higher-order sense of ownership. Indeed, the Map’s dedicated depersonalisation page presents universalist, localist and Billonian readings and explicitly describes the result as an architecture claim with “no metaphysical significance yet.” [[depersonalisation]]

Fourth, Ciaunica and colleagues’ “cracked window” metaphor is accurately cited, but the Map’s identification of the window’s transparency specifically with a global mineness tag is its own extrapolation. The source concerns disturbed embodied and prereflective self-awareness more broadly. [University of Greenwich](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/36034/)

### Current state of the evidence

The omitted 2026 paper by Roberto Sá Pereira is directly on point. It argues that the phenomenal-for-me-ness thesis lacks evidential support independent of the phenomena it was introduced to interpret, and that depersonalisation, schizophrenia and related cases admit more precise accounts in terms of multisensory integration, predictive processing and self-modelling. It was available on 4 August 2026, before the article’s 19 November modification. [PubMed+1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42550361/)

This does not settle the dispute in Sá Pereira’s favour. It does mean that the Map’s claim to current coverage is incomplete. The article needs to answer the circularity challenge: **what observation would distinguish a missing phenomenal mineness feature from disrupted affective, interoceptive, egocentric or metacognitive processes that cause people to describe their experiences as alien?**

**Verdict on depersonalisation:** it is strong evidence that components of ordinary self-experience can dissociate. It is suggestive but non-decisive evidence for phenomenal mineness, and no evidence yet that for-me-ness disappears.

## 3. Thought insertion: the article’s reverse dissociation is not secure

The host article now says explicitly that preserved ownership plus lost agency is “the Gallagher-style reading.” That is an improvement over presenting it simply as the phenomenology. Gallagher did distinguish a sense of agency from a sense of ownership, and used thought insertion as a possible example of the distinction. [aec6905spring2013.wordpress.com+1](https://aec6905spring2013.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/01/gallagher-2000-philosophyselfcogsceince.pdf)

But three problems remain.

### 3.1 The purported patient quotation is not a quotation

The sentence:

> “this thought occurs in my mind but I did not produce it”

is not the patient’s wording in Gallagher 2000. Gallagher quotes a patient who says that thoughts were put into his mind and belonged to another person. The Map’s sentence is a schematic philosophical gloss, presented inside quotation marks after “The patient says.” [aec6905spring2013.wordpress.com](https://aec6905spring2013.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/01/gallagher-2000-philosophyselfcogsceince.pdf)

The changelog explicitly records that “The patient says: ...” was left in place “as mandated.” [[changelog]] That is a clear quotation-integrity failure. The sentence can remain as an unquoted paraphrase—“On Gallagher’s interpretation, the patient’s position can be represented as…”—but must not be presented as testimony.

### 3.2 The agency/ownership analysis is disputed at its foundation

Ratcliffe and Wilkinson argue that the standard distinction does not illuminate thought insertion. On their analysis, the alien element may be quasi-perceptually presented thought-content, rather than an ordinary episode of thinking that is experienced as owned but not authored. They also warn that the literature repeatedly relies on a small number of ambiguous reports. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5257266/)

The Map’s own dedicated thought-insertion article is better calibrated. It distinguishes several interpretations and calls “ownership preserved, agency lost” the most contested of them. [[thought-insertion]] The host article should not state a clean reverse dissociation when its linked specialist page says that the relevant ownership claim is exactly what is disputed.

### 3.3 Ownership judgement is not necessarily phenomenal mineness

A patient may acknowledge that a thought is located in her mental stream or accessible only to her, while denying that it is genuinely hers. That acknowledgement could be a judgement about location, access or retention rather than a preserved phenomenal quality of ownership. The article presently moves too quickly from “in my mind” to “mine in the relevant phenomenal sense.”

**Verdict on thought insertion:** it supports the need to distinguish alienness, agency, access, state-ownership, content-ownership and first-person availability. It does not currently supply a secure reverse dissociation between phenomenal ownership and agency.

## 4. Contemplative self-dissolution: overlap is real, identity is not established

The article’s treatment of meditation contains a useful insight: affective appraisal and context substantially influence whether altered self-experience is welcomed or terrifying. A 2026 preregistered survey compared 60 people reporting meditation-triggered DPDR-like states with 61 reporting other triggers. Cambridge Depersonalisation Scale scores did not significantly differ, while the groups differed in mystical experience, ego dissolution, mindfulness-related measures and emotional appraisal. [Nature+1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-51014-y)

This supports **phenomenological overlap** and the importance of context. It does not establish the article’s stronger conclusion that both groups have the “same structural absence” of mine-ness with a separate valence layered on top.

The study itself complicates that picture:

- Most respondents in both groups described mixed rather than simply positive or negative experiences.
- Negative valence was common even in the meditation-triggered group.
- Among participants above the clinical-scale threshold, the difference on the challenging-experiences measure disappeared.
- Only four participants had received an official DPDR diagnosis.
- The groups differed demographically and clinically, and more than 90% lived in Western countries.
- Meditation type and prior experience varied substantially. [Nature+2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-51014-y)

The study measured DPDR-like symptoms, ego dissolution and appraisal; it did not isolate Guillot-mineness or demonstrate complete absence of for-me-ness. Nor was the phenomenon confined to advanced practitioners: more than a third of the meditation group reported onset within their first 100 hours of practice. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-51014-y)

The use of Deane, Miller and Wilkinson is also too strong. The quoted description of a “temporally deep generative model” is accurate, and the paper offers a unified active-inference framework. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7673417/) But the authors stress both similarities **and important differences**, and describe the control dynamics of depersonalisation and meditative selflessness as radically, even diametrically, different. [PubMed Central (PMC)+1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7673417/) They also say that the Buddhist move does not deny the experiential differentiation of one person’s sensations from another’s. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7673417/)

The cross-traditional claim is still less secure. The article groups Theravāda no-self practice, Zen and Advaita witness practice as reports of experience continuing without an experiencer. But the Map’s own comparative article recognizes a substantive divergence:

- Advaita reports an enduring or foundational witness.
- Buddhist traditions generally deny such a witness.
- Other traditions frame the experience relationally or as union.
- The reports may describe different states or may be shaped by tradition-specific interpretation. [[comparative-phenomenology-of-meditative-traditions]]

Advaita therefore cannot straightforwardly serve as evidence for experience without a subject. In many Advaita presentations, precisely what remains is the true Self or witness-awareness.

**Verdict on contemplative evidence:** current evidence supports overlapping forms of self-alteration, differences in appraisal, and substantial heterogeneity. It does not yet identify one common missing ownership feature across traditions and clinical conditions.

## 6. Does the article meet the strongest deflationary and no-ownership accounts?

**No. It acknowledges some of them but does not engage them at the point where they threaten the inference.**

### Knappik

The article’s empirical caveat is unusually candid: its reply survives only if local, type-specific self-relations are themselves phenomenal, which Knappik does not grant. That is correct. But the later claim that plurality merely “multiplies” ownership voids ignores that concession. A plurality of functional or state-type-specific relations does not yield a plurality of irreducible phenomenal appropriation mechanisms. [[mine-ness]]

### Gallagher’s deflationary phenomenology

Gallagher argues that a phenomenological account need not posit an added ownership quality: ownership may be implicit or intrinsic to embodied experience, which is itself a form of deflationism. He also directly engages Bermúdez, Dainton and eliminativist objections. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5609435/)

This creates a dilemma for the article’s “tag” metaphor. If mine-ness is intrinsic to the mode of experience rather than an additional quality, describing DPD as removal of a separable tag mischaracterises the view. If it really is an independently variable tag, the article owes a contrastive account of its implementation and measurement.

### Metzinger and predictive/self-model accounts

The article accepts Metzinger’s transparency architecture but responds that it leaves open why there is anything it is like to instantiate the model. That is a legitimate hard-problem objection, but it is not an objection to Metzinger’s explanation of ownership phenomenology. The site’s dedicated self-model article explains the naturalist proposal more fully: inaccessible model construction causes the system to experience being a self rather than having a model. [[self-model-theory-of-subjectivity]]

The host article needs to distinguish two explanatory targets:

1. Why the system classifies or experiences states as self-related.
2. Why any such modelling is phenomenally conscious.

A rival may explain the first while remaining silent or revisionary about the second. Repeating the hard problem does not show that a special mineness feature explains either.

### Sá Pereira’s construction account

The omitted 2026 challenge is especially important because it argues that ownership phenomena can be explained by egocentric registration, predictive self-modelling and ordinary self-ascription without a primitive phenomenal-for-me property. [PubMed+1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42550361/) This should be a central adversarial section, not a bibliography addition.

### No-owner and conventionalist positions

The article tends to restate no-owner proposals as versions of the question “but to whom do the experiences belong?” That risks begging the question. A genuinely ownerless/process account denies that experiences first exist and are then appropriated by an additional subject. Its task is to explain first-person organisation, privacy and self-ascription without a metaphysical ownership relation; the Map must show why those explanatory resources are insufficient rather than insisting that an owner remains to be found.

The article also needs to separate:

- denial of a substantial self;
- denial of a special phenomenal ownership quality;
- denial that experiences stand in a metaphysical ownership relation;
- denial that consciousness has first-person organisation.

Those are different positions, and evidence against one may leave the others untouched.

## 7. Tenet-protective bracketing

The article deserves credit for saying that separability is “compatible with, not evidenced by” dualism. But the restraint is not maintained in the surrounding prose.

### Dualism

Immediately after the disclaimer, the article describes mine-ness as having “no internal structure, no constituent parts, and no functional decomposition.” Neither the clinical evidence nor Zahavi’s use of “irrelational” establishes that. “Irrelational” in this context rejects a reflective subject–object relation; it does not mean physically or computationally unstructured. The article then uses the unsupported structurelessness claim to make dualism appear especially fitting. [[mine-ness]]

### Bidirectional interaction and quantum influence

The article moves from phenomenal ownership to a non-physical subject “claiming” physical processes and then to quantum outcome-biasing. Nothing in DPD, thought insertion or meditation discriminates a quantum-interaction model from predictive, enactive, higher-order, representational or other accounts. The move is a tenet-conditioned speculation, not an implication of the evidence.

There is also a textual contradiction. Earlier, mine-ness is “not a relation between two pre-existing things”; later, “ownership is the relation” by which the non-physical subject claims physical processes. [[mine-ness]] The article must choose between an intrinsic structure of experience and a subject–process appropriation relation, or explain how those levels connect.

### No Many Worlds

The article concedes that branch-relative indexicality can recover local mine-ness, then treats the absence of felt “shadow versions” as suggestive against Many Worlds. That phenomenology is non-discriminating: a branch-relative observer is not expected to experience other branches as shadow streams. The section therefore expresses the tenet’s preferred metaphysics without deriving any evidential pressure from mine-ness.

### Limits of Occam’s razor

The generic point—that simplicity cannot compensate for leaving phenomenality unexplained—is legitimate within the Map. But it adds nothing specific to the ownership case until the article shows that the deflationary models have omitted an independently established feature rather than redescribed the same reports at a different level.

**Overall diagnosis:** the article does not explicitly claim that the evidence proves the tenets, but it repeatedly assigns the evidence tenet-friendly significance after rivals have been acknowledged only in caveats. This is a softer form of protective bracketing: the strongest objections are admitted, then prevented from altering the headline conclusion.

## 8. Is the “ownership void” genuine?

### A defensible narrow void

There is a real methodological limit here:

- Any report or introspective examination is first-personally available to the person making it.
- A subject cannot report from a literally zero-person perspective.
- Reports of total loss of all first-person givenness are therefore difficult, perhaps impossible, to validate from the first-person side alone.

This can be called a **first-person contrast limit**. It explains why reports can demonstrate alienation or attenuation more readily than total elimination.

### The overstated void

The article slides from that modest limit to three much stronger propositions:

1. The subject cannot introspect the construction of ownership.
2. The mechanism therefore cannot be empirically explained.
3. The ground of ownership is structurally inaccessible in principle.

Only the first follows plausibly. Perceptual construction, memory formation, language production and motor control are also mostly unavailable to introspection; this does not make their mechanisms unknowable. Third-person experiments, lesion evidence, perturbation, computational models and disciplined correlations between reports and processing can investigate mechanisms that subjects cannot directly witness.

Indeed, the article itself cites candidate mechanisms involving transparency, active inference, affect, interoception and self-modelling. Its evidence therefore supports **introspective opacity**, not the claim that investigation can reveal only absence and “never machinery.”

The article also merges four different issues:

- the impossibility of an experience that is for no one;
- non-objectifying prereflective awareness;
- lack of introspective access to subpersonal processes;
- metaphysical unknowability of why experience belongs to a subject.

These require separate arguments. Zahavi’s “irrelational” account may support the second. It does not establish the third or fourth.

A sharper dilemma exposes the overstatement:

- **If for-me-ness is constitutive of consciousness**, asking for the mechanism that first creates ownerless experience and then makes it “ours” may be a category mistake.
- **If mine-ness is a separable and removable feature**, its causes, correlates and interventions are in principle contrastively investigable.

The current article tries to obtain both advantages: separability when arguing from clinical variation, and constitutive inaccessibility when arguing for a void.

**Verdict:** the ownership void is genuine as a limitation on first-person contrast and direct introspective access. It is overstated as an in-principle limit on scientific, functional or metaphysical explanation.

## 9. Integration with neighbouring articles

### Self and self-consciousness

The neighbouring article says every experience has built-in for-me-ness and that the minimal self is the form experience takes, not an added feature. It later treats pathology as scalar disturbance rather than clean elimination. [[self-and-self-consciousness]]

That directly conflicts with the host page’s separable “tag” and its claim that for-me-ness can vanish. The clean resolution is to reserve *for-me-ness* for constitutive first-person availability and *mineness* for a potentially variable sense of ownership. At present the pages use one term for both.

### Depersonalisation

The dedicated page is better than the host article. It distinguishes rival interpretations, treats physicalist models as competitors, and limits the secure conclusion to an architecture-level dissociation with no settled metaphysical significance. [[depersonalisation]] The host should inherit that verdict rather than reverting to “DPD shows that mine-ness can vanish.”

### Thought insertion

The dedicated page explicitly says that the host article’s preserved-ownership interpretation is the most contested reading. [[thought-insertion]] This is a direct cross-page calibration failure.

### Comparative meditation

The neighbouring article records genuine disagreements about whether a witness remains, whether awareness consists of momentary events, and how theory-laden the reports are. [[comparative-phenomenology-of-meditative-traditions]] The host article flattens those distinctions into a single “experience without someone” category.

### Phenomenal consciousness

The phenomenal-consciousness page distinguishes hard and subtle illusionist positions and cautions that the regress from “it seems” to phenomenality simply assumes what illusionists deny. [[phenomenal-consciousness]] The mine-ness article should exhibit the same discipline: self-reports establish that subjects represent or describe alienation, while the specifically phenomenal architecture remains a further interpretive claim.

### Self-model theory

The self-model page presents transparency as a concrete explanation of why construction is not introspectively visible. [[self-model-theory-of-subjectivity]] The mine-ness article cites that explanation but does not expose it to a discriminating test; it simply says it cannot solve the hard problem.

### Consciousness and the ownership problem

The ownership article adopts Guillot’s taxonomy more explicitly than the mine-ness page and acknowledges disputed interpretations of thought insertion. [[consciousness-and-the-ownership-problem]] However, the two articles risk circular support: one treats ownership as a deep metaphysical problem and the other cites that internal treatment as reinforcement. Neither can serve as independent evidence for the other’s premises.

## Concrete potential improvements

1. **Rewrite the opening definition of concepts/mine-ness.** Reserve *for-me-ness* for first-personal givenness, *me-ness* for awareness of the experiencer, and *mineness* for presentation-as-mine. Keep the existing URL if necessary, but do not let URL stability prevent conceptual correction.
2. **Replace the global-tag thesis with an evidence ladder.** Distinguish:  
   **Tier 1:** components of self-experience dissociate;  
   **Tier 2:** a global phenomenal mineness feature is one explanation;

   The evidence presently supports Tier 1, is compatible with Tier 2, and does not establish Tier 3.
3. **Revise the depersonalisation section.** Replace “shows that mine-ness can vanish” with language stating that DPD reports are compatible with degraded mineness but underdetermine affective, interoceptive, agency-based, localist and global-feature explanations. Link explicitly to the witness-status divergence in topics/comparative-phenomenology-of-meditative-traditions.

Separate inability to occupy an ownerless perspective from inability to introspect implementation and from alleged in-principle explanatory closure.
10. **Delete or defend the unsupported structurelessness claim.** “Completely irrelational” does not entail no parts, no implementation or no functional decomposition. The dualism section should distinguish non-objectifying phenomenology from ontological simplicity.
11. **Downgrade the quantum and Many-Worlds sections.** Label them explicit framework-conditioned speculations. State that neither altered self-experience nor branch-local phenomenology currently discriminates the Map’s interaction model from physicalist, enactive, predictive or Many-Worlds accounts.
12. **Resolve the cross-page contradiction with self-and-self-consciousness.** That page should say that constitutive for-me-ness may persist while mineness or explicit ownership is attenuated; alternatively, the project must abandon the constitutive claim. It cannot retain both formulations without qualification.
13. **Strengthen consciousness-and-the-ownership-problem.** Give a non-question-begging formulation of the no-owner view: experiences may be first-personally organised without standing in an additional ownership relation to a substantial subject. Explain what evidence would rule that out.
14. **Make depersonalisation the canonical evidence hub.** Other pages should transclude or mechanically inherit its current evidence-tier verdict so that caveats cannot be maintained in the specialist article while stronger conclusions persist elsewhere.
15. **Introduce a site-wide construct registry.** Each recurrent term—*for-me-ness*, *mineness*, *ownership*, *agency*, *minimal self*, *witness*, *ego dissolution*—should have a canonical definition, allowed synonyms, prohibited conflations and links to rival definitions.

17. **Add an automated quotation-integrity rule.** Text introduced by “the patient says” must match a page-pinned primary source. Constructed examples and interpretive glosses must never be placed in quotation marks.
18. **Add cross-page contradiction tests.** Automated review should flag combinations such as “for-me-ness is constitutive” versus “for-me-ness vanishes,” “mine-ness is not a relation” versus “ownership is the relation,” and “most contested reading” versus “the patient says.”

20. **Override workflow constraints when correctness is at stake.** The changelog shows that URL concerns, scope limits and a mandate to retain wording prevented correction of a known conceptual collision and a constructed quotation.
