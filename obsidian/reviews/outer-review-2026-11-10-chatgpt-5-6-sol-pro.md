## Reply

# Independent audit: “Pain, Consciousness, and Causal Power”

## Overall verdict: major revision required

The article addresses a genuinely difficult problem and has improved since earlier versions. Its most defensible conclusion is that pain asymbolia exposes the inadequacy of identifying pain with nociceptive detection alone, and that it forces theories to distinguish sensory-discriminative, affective, motivational and self-regulatory components. The article also now concedes that asymbolia **constrains** accounts of pain without establishing that phenomenal awfulness itself causes behaviour.

That concession is not consistently respected. The opening still calls asymbolia “the single most discriminating empirical test case”; the causal section says consciousness determines post-reflex behaviour; and the tenet section says imagined pain changes behaviour “through phenomenal content alone.” Those stronger claims are not delivered by the evidence and sometimes directly contradict the article’s own caveats. [[pain-consciousness-and-causal-power]]

The most serious problems are:

1. **Pain is incorrectly defined as nociceptive signalling.**
2. **The Berthier cases are treated as more selective and clinically secure than they are.**
3. **Anticipation establishes causal effects of predictions, fear and representations, not specifically of phenomenal character.**
4. **Functionalism, higher-order theory, GNW and representationalism are presented in unnecessarily weak forms.**
5. **The IIT criticism is factually obsolete against IIT 4.0.**
6. **The Many-Worlds paragraph contains a clear factual error already identified by the Map’s own review system.**
7. **The article’s strongest caveats are locally stated and then silently overridden when the five tenets are discussed.**

## 1. The pain–suffering framework is built on a mistaken definition

The article says that pain is a sensory event consisting of nociceptive signals, while suffering is what consciousness does with those signals. That is contrary to current standard terminology. The International Association for the Study of Pain defines pain as an unpleasant sensory **and emotional** experience and explicitly distinguishes pain from nociception. Nociception is the neural encoding of noxious stimuli, and it can produce withdrawal and autonomic responses without implying pain. Pain can also occur without clear evidence of peripheral tissue damage, as in nociplastic pain. [IASP+1](https://www.iasp-pain.org/resources/terminology/)

This is not a harmless terminological choice. The article removes unpleasantness and emotional significance from “pain” by definition, calls the remaining signal pain, and then argues that consciousness must add the missing unpleasantness as suffering. The resulting explanatory gap is partly manufactured by the initial partition. A physicalist need not claim that pain is merely C-fibre traffic; the natural physicalist target is an integrated sensory-affective-regulatory state.

A distinction between pain and wider suffering remains valuable. A 2024 systematic review of 111 articles found that pain-related suffering is generally treated as closely related to but distinct from pain, and as a multidimensional response to threats to the person or self. But it also found continuing disagreement over the concept and eight interacting dimensions rather than the article’s relatively sharp signal-plus-conscious-overlay model. [PubMed Central (PMC)+1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11190900/)

The article’s statement that grief, dread and moral suffering would require “equally distinctive neural signatures” if physicalism were true is also unsupported. Physical realization does not require a one-to-one anatomical signature for every folk-psychological category. A state may be realised by distributed, context-sensitive and partially overlapping networks. The absence of a compact “grief circuit” would not favour nonphysical valence.

### “No analgesic touches any of these”

This is too absolute. It is reasonable to say that grief or guilt cannot ordinarily be treated merely by blocking peripheral nociception. But analgesic systems overlap with affective and stress systems. In one controlled study, hydromorphone altered physiological and subjective responses to social stress, while acetaminophen reduced some psychological components of the stress response. This does not show that analgesics cure grief, but it does refute the clean pharmacological separation asserted by the article. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29673620/)

### Meditation does not provide a clean pain-without-aversion case

Grant and Rainville’s study involved 13 experienced meditators and 13 controls. During mindfulness, meditators reported reduced pain intensity, and changes in unpleasantness generally paralleled changes in intensity. That is not evidence that an unchanged “raw nociceptive quality” remained while aversion alone disappeared. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19073756/)

The broader mindfulness literature is also mixed. An umbrella review found 21 meta-analyses covering 127 studies, with results substantially less uniform than headline claims about mindfulness analgesia suggest. The article should describe meditation as evidence for **partial and trainable modulation** of sensory, evaluative and reactive dimensions—not as an established pure dissociation. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37578535/)

## 2. The asymbolia evidence is real but much less selective than the article needs

The neurological phenomenon is real in the limited sense that patients with certain lesions can discriminate normally painful stimulation while displaying strikingly abnormal concern, withdrawal and protective learning. What is contested is whether this is properly understood as normal pain minus unpleasantness, a nociceptive sensation that is no longer pain, a wider threat-appraisal disorder, a failure of bodily ownership or “mineness,” or some combination.

### Berthier, Starkstein and Leiguarda

The bibliographic citation is correct, and the article accurately notes several central findings. Berthier and colleagues reported six patients who showed absent or inadequate withdrawal and emotional responses to painful stimuli. But the same patients also showed reduced responses to threatening gestures; five failed to react normally to verbal threats; and associated abnormalities included hemiparesis, cortical sensory loss, neglect and body-schema disturbance. Their lesions were extensive and heterogeneous, although all involved the insula. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3415199/)

The target article acknowledges the broader threat deficit and then says that “the core pattern remains a selective loss of affective-bodily concern.” That does not follow from the case series. The observations are at least as compatible with a broader disruption of threat significance, salience, self-modelled bodily concern or action preparation. Calling the loss “selective” converts an interpretive hypothesis into a clinical result.

The evidential base is also much thinner than “single most discriminating empirical test case” suggests. Feinstein and colleagues noted that the original insula-lesion result had not been replicated, that the Berthier lesions extended beyond the insula, and that a patient with extensive bilateral damage to the insula, anterior cingulate and amygdala retained—and sometimes displayed excessive—pain affect. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4734900/)

An inverse dissociation has also been reported: four epilepsy patients displayed pain-associated behaviour during posterior insulo-opercular seizures while denying pain sensation. Such cases support a distributed, multidirectional decomposition of pain processing, but they weaken any simple inference from behaviour–experience dissociation to a separable nonphysical phenomenal cause. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31738227/)

### Grahek

The article’s description of Grahek’s distinction between “feeling pain” and “being in pain” is broadly faithful to his project. His book expressly examines purported pain without painfulness and painfulness without pain. [MIT Press](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262072830/feeling-pain-and-being-in-pain/)

The problem is evidential status. Grahek’s classification is a philosophical interpretation of sparse and heterogeneous clinical reports, not a neutral clinical conclusion established by Berthier’s data. The article initially presents Grahek’s taxonomy as if it simply names what the patients retain and lose. It should instead say:

> On Grahek’s interpretation, the retained state is a sensory pain experience without the ordinary affective-motivational condition of being in pain.

That wording leaves open the competing possibility that what remains is not pain.

### Griffith and Kind

The article now says that Griffith and Kind argue that the residual state is not pain. That is correct but incomplete. Their challenge is not merely a terminological decision. They question whether the reports establish painful experience at all, whether the patients form a coherent neurological category and whether the standard composite interpretation commits a mereological mistake by treating a component of pain as itself pain. Their analysis of the threshold evidence also emphasises how little can safely be inferred from the old cases. [Cambridge University Press+2](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/pain-asymbolia-is-not-pain/14D42EF12870DC9C6BA506F2C5F5AC5F)

Thus the dispute does not leave an agreed clinical core of “intact felt hurtfulness minus concern.” The agreed core is narrower: discrimination of normally painful stimuli and severely abnormal pain-related avoidance or concern.

### Duval and Klein

The citation and publication details are current. But “Duval and Klein reply that it probably still is [pain]” overstates what their discussion establishes. Their abstract says that Griffith and Kind’s reasons are relatively weak and that the dispute cannot be resolved without first settling how the neurological condition should be taxonomised. That is a defence of continued uncertainty, not a positive clinical demonstration of pain without unpleasantness. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/pain-asymbolia-is-probably-still-pain/093BD46EB13CA8B9DE533B5EC82C816D)

### Klein’s imperative theory

Klein’s 2015 book is correctly listed in the bibliography but is not substantively engaged in the article. This is a major omission because it supplies one of the strongest replies to the article’s representationalism objection. Klein argues that pain represents in an imperative mode: it commands protection of an affected body part and thereby explains pain’s motivational force. [MIT Press](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262029704/what-the-body-commands/)

The article’s comparison between representing a red square and representing tissue damage assumes that both representations are merely descriptive. Imperative, evaluative and action-oriented representational theories explicitly reject that assumption. They can say that pain hurts or motivates because of the type of representational content or attitude involved, not because an additional nonrepresentational phenomenal property enters the causal chain. Pain asymbolia remains a problem for particular unitary imperative theories, but it does not defeat representationalism as a family.

### A current physicalist alternative is missing

Gerrans’s 2024 active-allostatic-inference account explains asymbolia through hierarchical self-modelling, allostatic regulation and the assignment of bodily states to a persisting self. It is designed to reconcile deaffectualisation and depersonalisation interpretations without invoking a nonphysical causal layer. Whether ultimately successful or not, it is exactly the kind of contemporary comparator the article needs to confront at full strength. [PubMed Central (PMC)+2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10860504/)

## 3. Asymbolia does not presently discriminate phenomenal causation

The article’s corrected caveat is right: damage to an affective-motivational system will jointly alter experience and behaviour whether the experience is causally efficacious, physically constituted by that system, or epiphenomenally correlated with it. The lesion does not independently vary phenomenal awfulness while holding its physical and functional realiser fixed.

This leaves several live explanations:

- a physical mechanism produces both felt unpleasantness and avoidance;
- unpleasantness is identical with or constituted by the relevant evaluative-regulatory role;
- the missing functional disposition explains why the residual sensation is no longer painful;
- a predictive-processing or active-inference valuation system produces both the experience and behavioural priority;
- phenomenal awfulness is nonphysical and adds independent causal influence.

The Berthier cases do not distinguish among these. The Map’s own co-optimization article states the problem more accurately: the lesions disrupt feeling and function together, and no behavioural measure separates constitutive physicalism from interactionism where both say behaviour follows felt awfulness. It identifies the relevant discriminator as a physical outcome difference conditioned on experience after accounting for the physical state—a test that has not been performed. [[co-optimization-reply-to-the-correlation-problem]]

Accordingly, asymbolia is **diagnostically revealing** but not currently **metaphysically discriminating**. It undermines “pain is nothing but nociceptive discrimination.” It does not establish “phenomenal unpleasantness is an additional nonphysical cause.”

## 4. The theory-by-theory comparisons need substantial rewriting

### Functionalism

The alleged dilemma is too easy. A functionalist need not identify pain with mere damage detection plus generic withdrawal. A sophisticated account can include sensory discrimination, negative evaluation, prioritisation, learning, guarding, autonomic response and action selection within the functional profile. The asymbolic patient may retain a nociceptive sensation while lacking the complete functional state that ordinarily counts as pain.

The article replies that informational content alone cannot produce suffering. But functionalism does not have to claim that **sensory information alone** does so. It can identify unpleasantness with the broader evaluative and motivational organization that is disrupted. The remaining explanatory-gap objection is general; asymbolia supplies no special refutation of that position.

### Higher-order theories

The article says higher-order theory must posit two separate monitoring streams, one sensory and one affective. That is not entailed. Higher-order theories require an appropriate higher-order representation of a first-order state; variants differ considerably over the nature and architecture of that representation. A single higher-order system can represent differentiated sensory and affective contents, or the first-order affective state itself may be degraded. Content differentiation does not imply two anatomically or computationally independent monitors. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness-higher/)

Calling the resulting account a “redescription” is also asymmetric. Saying that a phenomenal component has been nonphysically removed is equally a redescription unless the Map supplies an independently testable mechanism.

### Global neuronal workspace

GNW proposes that recurrent ignition amplifies and sustains a representation, making it globally available to specialised processors. It therefore has conceptual room for nociceptive localisation and affective valuation to be separately represented or differently broadcast. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32135090/)

The article’s objection—GNW does not explain why globally accessed affective information hurts—is a version of the general hard problem. It may be a legitimate objection to reductive consciousness theories, but asymbolia does not especially sharpen it or show a failed GNW prediction. The article should distinguish:

1. whether GNW can model the access and behavioural dissociation; and
2. whether GNW closes the phenomenal explanatory gap.

It appears capable of the first while the second remains philosophically disputed.

### Integrated Information Theory

This section is factually outdated. The article says that IIT treats consciousness through a single scalar Φ and therefore predicts only general, undifferentiated diminution. IIT 4.0 expressly distinguishes the **quantity** of consciousness from its structured **quality**. Specific experience is associated with a Φ-structure made up of distinctions and relations; the scalar quantity is derived from that structured object. [PLOS+1](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article)

IIT may face other serious objections, and it would still need to model the precise structural change corresponding to asymbolia. But the article cannot fairly claim that current IIT predicts only global dimming. This is a clear factual correction, not merely a matter of interpretation.

### Representationalism

The red-square comparison attacks only a thin descriptive representationalism. Evaluativist, imperative, interoceptive and predictive representational accounts explain why two represented contents have different motivational roles by differences in representational mode, precision, action policy, bodily ownership or valence—not merely by differences in their external referents. Klein and Gerrans should be treated as principal opponents rather than incidental references. [MIT Press+1](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262029704/what-the-body-commands/)

## 5. “Phenomenal character and causal power are inseparable” is not established

The hot-stove example relies heavily on experienced causal unity: the pain hurts and “therefore” one withdraws. But introspective presentation does not identify the causally effective property. A common physical process could produce the painful experience, the apparent causal relation and the behaviour. The article briefly concedes this epiphenomenalist interpretation and then immediately asserts that consciousness determines the subsequent response.

The extended-response argument is also underdetermined. Cooling a burn, guarding it and learning future avoidance may track perceived severity rather than objective tissue damage. But “perceived severity” can be implemented as a neural estimate used by planning and learning systems. That establishes the causal role of an internal evaluative representation, not a nonphysical phenomenal property over and above its realiser.

The proposed quality–action mappings are plausible but currently uncited:

- sharp pain → withdrawal and inspection;
- burning pain → cooling and guarding;
- aching pain → immobility and altered posture.

Even if robust, these mappings are confounded by injury type, afferent activity, tissue state, motor affordances and learned action policies. The phenomenal quality and behaviour may be parallel outputs of those variables. The claim that phenomenology provides a “more transparent” explanation than neural differences is an appeal to explanatory intuitiveness, not causal evidence.

## 6. Anticipated pain: a defensible premise followed by an invalid inference

### What the evidence supports

Anticipated pain can alter present mood, planning and behaviour before the anticipated painful event occurs. Neuroimaging has distinguished anticipation-related activity from activity during acute pain, and experimental instructions that increase anticipated pain can reduce movement and increase avoidance in chronic-pain patients. [PubMed+1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10373114/)

A defensible claim would therefore be:

> Representations and predictions of possible future pain can influence present behaviour without current injury-evoked nociceptive input.

That is important evidence for model-based cognition, prediction, fear learning and access-level mental causation.

### What the evidence does not support

The article instead says that no C-fibres are firing and that a phenomenal state drives avoidance. Neither follows.

First, the absence of new tissue damage does not establish the absence of all nociceptive or interoceptive activity. More importantly, no cited study isolates such activity as the relevant variable.

Second, conscious dread covaries with beliefs, episodic memory, attention, autonomic arousal, expected value, threat precision and motor policy. The experiments intervene on instructions, cues or expectations—not on phenomenal character while holding those other variables constant. They therefore cannot show that the **felt quality** of dread is the load-bearing cause.

Third, conditioned analgesic and hyperalgesic responses can be acquired from subliminal cues. This does not show that conscious anticipation never matters, but it demonstrates that conscious phenomenal awareness is not necessary for some expectation-linked pain modulation. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25979940/)

The article’s placebo discussion has the same limitation. Expectation-linked opioid release would establish that expectation, conditioning and prediction influence physiology. It would not establish that phenomenal expectation contributes something beyond the neural and functional expectation state. The Map’s neighbouring placebo article states this correctly: the evidence establishes access-level mental causation but not that phenomenal character is the causally decisive factor. [[empirical-phenomena-mental-causation]]

The target article acknowledges that an access-level model predicts the evidence equally well. But its tenet section later asserts that imagined pain acts through “phenomenal content alone.” That is a direct calibration regression, and the Map’s own tenet-alignment audit has already identified it. [[reviews/tenet-check-2026-11-03]]

A genuine discriminator would need to vary conscious dread while matching predictive content, learned cue value, attention, autonomic state and action policy. The Map’s own valence-selection page moves in this direction by proposing residual choice effects after fitting drift, decision bounds, vigour and salience, while also admitting that asymbolia is not currently a one-variable dissociation. [[valence-and-conscious-selection]]

## 7. The evolutionary argument binds fewer positions than the article implies

The evolutionary alignment problem is strongest against a **bare-correlation property epiphenomenalism** in which the relationship between adaptive behaviour and matching phenomenal valence is brute and causally idle. It is a real challenge for that view.

It does not have the same force against constitutive physicalism. On that position, painfulness is not an inert accompaniment to a selected functional state; it is the state, or is constituted by the state, that controls learning and avoidance. Natural selection therefore acts on the causally effective state without needing to “see” a separate phenomenal property. The remaining disagreement concerns whether the physical-functional state fully constitutes felt awfulness—not whether evolution could select the behaviour.

The article calls co-optimization a “non-causal rival,” but this conflates two different opponents:

- an epiphenomenalist co-optimizer, where experience remains causally inert;
- a constitutive physicalist, for whom felt awfulness is causally efficacious because it is constituted by the relevant physical-functional organization.

The Map’s dedicated co-optimization article correctly says that the usual trilemma has no straightforward purchase on the second position and also recognises that interactionism incurs its own outbound psychophysical-law debt. [[co-optimization-reply-to-the-correlation-problem]]

The neighbouring evolutionary article is better calibrated than the pain article: it describes the argument as suggestive rather than demonstrative, leaves the byproduct reply live and explicitly says that both physicalists and dualists can accept consciousness as causally efficacious. [[evolutionary-case-for-mental-causation]]

## 8. The phenomenal-concepts discussion turns a physicalist concession into a dualist conclusion

The article says that even if the phenomenal-concepts strategy succeeds, first-person acquaintance still escapes third-person physical description—and that this is the dualist’s central point.

That is too quick. The phenomenal-concepts strategy is specifically intended to allow an epistemic or conceptual gap while denying an ontological gap. Chalmers argues that especially “thick” acquaintance versions may merely relocate the explanatory problem, but that is an argument against particular versions of the strategy, not evidence that accepting a distinctive first-person mode of presentation already concedes dualism. [consc.net](https://consc.net/papers/pceg.html)

The article may argue that no physicalist phenomenal-concepts account succeeds. It cannot simply count the acknowledged epistemic difference as the dualist conclusion. That is a clear instance of tenet-protective bracketing: the rival’s defining distinction is stated and then effectively ignored.

## 9. The Many-Worlds paragraph is factually wrong

The article suggests that under Many-Worlds every psychophysical supervenience mapping might be realised somewhere. Everettian branching varies measurement outcomes or records under a common dynamics; it does not create branches governed by every possible psychophysical law. Everett’s branches are treated alike by the theory and obey the same dynamics. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13194-011-0023-9)

The Map’s own tenet-alignment review has already classified this as a factual error and also noted that the article invokes simplicity in the Map’s favour while warning elsewhere against one-sided uses of parsimony. [[reviews/tenet-check-2026-11-03]]

This paragraph should be removed rather than hedged. Rejection of Many-Worlds supplies no additional support for the pain argument in its present form.

## 10. Citation currency and hygiene

The principal citations exist, and the article has commendably added the 2024 Griffith–Kind and 2025/2026 Duval–Klein exchange. But source existence is not the same as proposition-level support.

The empirical core remains disproportionately dependent on:

- a heterogeneous six-patient series from 1988;
- older philosophical interpretations of those cases;
- small meditation studies from 2009–2012;
- textbook-style pain-pathway simplifications.

Material that should be incorporated includes the nonreplication and bilateral-lesion evidence, the inverse “symbolism for pain” cases, the 2024 suffering review, the 2024 active-inference treatment of asymbolia, the mindfulness umbrella review and IIT 4.0. [PubMed Central (PMC)+5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4734900/)

The changelog reveals a process problem. During the October refinement, numerous pre-existing bibliography entries—including Cassell, Chalmers, Cleeremans and Tallon-Baudry, Klein, Kragel and LaBar, Panksepp and others—were explicitly left uncited and treated as out of scope. A reference list should not function as a background reading dump: each item should support a proposition, be explicitly discussed, or be moved to a labelled further-reading section. [[workflow/changelog]]

## 11. Tenet-protective bracketing

The article frequently states a caveat at the point of local analysis and then restores the stronger tenet-supporting conclusion later:

- Asymbolia only constrains causal interpretations, but the opening calls it the most discriminating empirical test.
- Access-level accounts predict anticipation equally well, but the tenet section says phenomenal content acts alone.
- Noordhof and valence-prior accounts remain live, but physicalism is later described as declining to explain the central datum.
- The phenomenal-concepts strategy is allowed to explain an epistemic gap, but that gap is immediately reclassified as the dualist’s point.
- Co-optimization is recognised as a rival, but it is labelled “non-causal” without distinguishing constitutive physicalism from epiphenomenalism.
- The interactionist mechanism is admitted to be speculative, yet ordinary pain behaviour is repeatedly described as if it were already evidence for bidirectional interaction.

This is not merely rhetorical emphasis. It changes the evidential grade between sections without adding new evidence. The Map’s own November tenet check caught two of these regressions, but they remain in the live article. [[reviews/tenet-check-2026-11-03]]

## 12. Integration with neighbouring articles

Integration is uneven because several neighbouring pages now express a more defensible position than the target article:

- **Pain Asymbolia** records the lesion heterogeneity, broader threat deficit, lack of replication and preserved pain affect after bilateral limbic damage. Those qualifications should be imported into the main topic article rather than left behind a link. [[pain-asymbolia]]
- **Consciousness and Causal Powers** correctly says that asymbolia constrains a simple nociceptive identity claim but does not establish the Map’s reading, and it identifies predictive-processing valence priors as a same-prediction rival. [[consciousness-and-causal-powers]]
- **The Co-Optimization Reply** explicitly says the lesion changes feeling and function together and that the cases are not positive evidence for interactionism. [[co-optimization-reply-to-the-correlation-problem]]
- **The Evolutionary Case for Mental Causation** recognises that the evolutionary argument does not by itself decide between physicalist and dualist causal efficacy. [[evolutionary-case-for-mental-causation]]
- **Empirical Phenomena of Mental Causation** distinguishes access-level causation from the unestablished causal contribution of phenomenal character, although that article itself still contains one overstrong asymbolia sentence immediately before its better-calibrated section. [[empirical-phenomena-mental-causation]]
- **Emotion and Dualism** correctly says asymbolia binds the bare-correlation version while leaving the phenomenal-concept version standing, but continues to advertise emotional consciousness as the strongest empirical case for interactionism. That page and the target article require a shared evidential formulation. [[emotion-and-dualism]]

The target article should become the synthesis of these qualifications. At present it often reads as though it predates them.

## 13. Methodological assessment

The site’s review workflow is strong on change tracking, explicit calibration language and bibliographic existence checks. It is weaker on source entailment, theory reconstruction and reopening apparently “converged” prose after new comparator models become relevant.

The July deep review treated the article as six-times converged and expressly declined to re-litigate it because there was no textual delta. The June review reported no factual errors or internal contradictions. Yet the live page contains a basic pain–nociception error, an obsolete IIT characterisation, an incorrect Many-Worlds claim and an unresolved contradiction over anticipated pain. [[reviews/deep-review-2026-07-18-pain-consciousness-and-causal-power]]

That suggests three distinct review questions are being conflated:

1. Does the cited source exist and have the stated bibliographic metadata?
2. Does the source actually support the exact proposition?
3. Does that proposition, even if true, discriminate the Map’s interpretation from the strongest rival?

The first is being handled comparatively well. The second and third need to become explicit, independent stages.

## Bottom line

Pain is plausibly one of the most intuitively powerful pressures on epiphenomenalism, especially on a theory that treats the correlation between awfulness and avoidance as wholly brute. But the clinical and experimental evidence reviewed here does not establish interactionist phenomenal causation.

Pain asymbolia shows that nociceptive discrimination, affective concern, bodily ownership, threat appraisal and protective behaviour can come apart. Anticipated pain shows that predictive representations of future harm can influence present behaviour. Placebo effects show that expectations and learning can affect physiology. None yet isolates nonphysical phenomenal character as an additional cause.

The article would become substantially stronger by embracing that narrower conclusion: pain provides an unusually vivid **explanatory and correlation problem**, while the causal and metaphysical discriminator remains to be found.
