The **Basic Argument** is Galen Strawson's argument that "true" or ultimate moral responsibility is impossible, and that "it makes no difference whether determinism is true or false" (Strawson 1994: 5). You do what you do "because of the way you are". To be truly responsible for what you do, you must be truly responsible for the way you are, which would require being *causa sui*, the cause of oneself, "at least in certain crucial mental respects". Nothing can be *causa sui*. Strawson is the son of P. F. Strawson, whose "Freedom and Resentment" defends responsibility, and father and son reach opposite verdicts. **Constitutive luck**, in Thomas Nagel's phrase, is luck in "the kind of person you are". The Basic Argument generalizes it by regress: every attempt to reshape one's constitution is made by someone who already has one.

The Unfinishable Map's verdict holds only inside its own framework. The regress reaches the Map's non-physical agent as hard as a brain, because the agent did not choose the powers it chooses with: the [[#parity|parity problem]] (explained below). Strawson never discusses agent causation, souls or dualism, so this application is the Map's reading, though his reply to a self "independent" of character makes the point for any self. The Map's one reply, agent-causal origination, rejects the second premise rather than escaping the regress. That rejection is contested, and it ends at the primitive the [[rollback-argument-against-agent-causation|rollback argument]] presses ([[#meeting|where the two meet]], also below). So character is self-*shaped*, not self-created, and responsibility comes in degrees. The argument reaches ultimate desert, not causal efficacy: Strawson grants that people change the way they are, so the Bidirectional Interaction tenet is untouched.

## The Basic Argument {#the-argument}

Strawson named the argument in 1994, having first stated it in *Freedom and Belief* (1986). It "appears to prove that we cannot be truly or ultimately morally responsible for our actions" (1994: 5). His restatement isolates two premises:

1. "You do what you do, in any situation in which you find yourself, because of the way you are" (p. 12).
2. "To be truly morally responsible for what you do you must be truly responsible for the way you are – at least in certain crucial mental respects" (p. 13).

He takes (1) to be "incontrovertible" and names (2) as the premise "that must be resisted" if anything is (p. 13).

**The regress.** To be responsible for how you are, you must have chosen to be that way by principles of choice you already had, and chosen those by earlier ones. "True self-determination is impossible because it requires the actual completion of an infinite series of choices of principles of choice" (p. 7).

**The natural form.** Heredity and early experience make one as one is, and how one already is fixes how one tries to change and how far one succeeds. Random factors "for which one is ex hypothesi in no way responsible" add nothing (p. 7). That indifference to determinism makes this a **source** argument in the [[source-versus-leeway-incompatibilism|source-versus-leeway taxonomy]]: it never mentions alternatives, and it presses libertarian sourcehood as hard as compatibilist sourcehood.

**What Strawson concedes.** "The claim, then, is not that people cannot change the way they are." They can, "in certain respects" (p. 7). He denies only that self-change can make anyone "truly or ultimately" responsible. He fixes that standard with heaven and hell: true responsibility would make sense of the idea that "it could be just to punish some of us with (eternal) torment in hell and reward others with (eternal) bliss in heaven" (p. 9).

## Constitutive Luck {#constitutive-luck}

The expression "moral luck" is Bernard Williams's: "when I first introduced the expression moral luck, I expected to suggest an oxymoron" (Williams 1993, as quoted in Nelkin 2025). Nagel's "Moral Luck" replied to Williams's paper in their 1976 symposium. Nagel's own note calls Williams's paper the one "to which the original version of this essay was a reply".

Nagel describes "roughly four ways" in which the objects of moral assessment are subject to luck, and he names one of them. "The phenomenon of constitutive luck" is luck in "the kind of person you are", including "your inclinations, capacities, and temperament". Who first wrote "constitutive luck" is unsettled. The Stanford Encyclopedia says Nagel "influentially named" it (Caruso 2018, §2.4). The Internet Encyclopedia calls it an expression "he probably gets from Williams", and credits the labels resultant, circumstantial and causal luck to later writers (Latus).

Nagel's sentence on the vices already holds partial self-formation together with the luck that remains. Such a quality "may be the product of earlier choices", and "it may be amenable to change by current actions". "But it is largely a matter of constitutive bad fortune." Strawson's regress makes the luck total, since every reshaping of a constitution starts from one. Robert Hartman (2018), a defender of moral luck, argues that Strawson overgeneralizes from kinds of constitutive luck that plainly defeat responsibility to all kinds. Neither Nagel nor Williams is cited here for dualism.

## Strawson's Three Responses {#three-responses}

**Compatibilism** denies that responsibility requires responsibility for how one is. Strawson answers that compatibilist responsibility "famously fails to amount to any sort of true moral responsibility" by the heaven-and-hell standard (p. 17).

**Kane's libertarianism**, on Strawson's reading, accepts premise (2) and stops the regress at undetermined efforts of will that shape character. The target is Robert Kane's 1989 paper. If such efforts are partly indeterministic yet shaped "(as Kane grants) by my already existing character, why am I not merely lucky?" (p. 18). Genes and early influences shape "the general bent and strength of our capacity to make efforts of will" (p. 18). "Kane appears to accept step (2) of the Basic Argument" (p. 19). Answering Kane's 1996 book, Strawson (2000) wrote that Kane agrees ultimate responsibility requires a kind of self-creation, and that he does too. Kane's account is the Map's naturalistic rival ([[event-causal-libertarianism]]).

**The self picture** grants that no one is ultimately responsible for their "character or personality or motivational structure" (CPM). It "appeals to a certain picture of the self" (p. 20), a self S that "is imagined to be in some way independent of one's CPM" (p. 21). Strawson replies that "whatever S decides, it decides as it does because of the way it is", or else decides partly through indeterministic factors it cannot answer for (p. 21). "To be a source of true or ultimate responsibility, S must be responsible for being the way it is. But this is impossible, for the reasons given in the Basic Argument" (p. 21). He credits the picture to C. A. Campbell (n. 11). Note 8 extends the demand to Kant's "non-temporal noumenal nature", suggesting that Kant holds "radical or ultimate self-determination must take place even in the noumenal realm".

## Replies in the Literature {#replies}

**Agent-causal origination rejects premise (2).** Randolph Clarke's reply (2003: 170–76) is given here as the Stanford Encyclopedia's free-will entry summarizes it; those pages were not read. Agent-causal theorists can hold that aspects of how one is "fully explain an agent's choice without causally determining it", while "the agent himself causes the choice that is made". Then "it is not true that there is a sufficient 'ultimate' source of it external to the agent" (O'Connor and Franklin 2022, §3.1). The demand to be *causa sui* gives way to the demand that the agent originate; the agent does not become its own cause. Clarke offers the reply conditionally and is sceptical of libertarian accounts generally. Mark Istvan (2011) argues that such accounts "fail" to immunize the agent, as the skepticism entry reports (Caruso 2018, §2.3).

**Locus and degree.** Alfred Mele "argues that Strawson misconstrues the locus of freedom and responsibility", which belongs principally to actions and only "derivatively" to character (O'Connor and Franklin 2022, on Mele 1995: 221ff.). Mele (2006) and Timothy O'Connor (2009) suggest that freedom and responsibility "come in degrees and grow over time" as how one is becomes "increasingly shaped by one's own past choices". O'Connor co-wrote that entry, so it cannot confirm his view independently, and he argues that agent causation need not require substance dualism. Mele is agnostic about libertarianism.

**The luck pincer.** Neil Levy (2011) argues that every morally significant act is subject to constitutive luck, to present luck around the time of action, or to both. Levy criticizes libertarians and compatibilists alike.

**Sceptics split.** The skepticism entry says that agent-causal libertarianism "could, in theory, supply this sort of control". It reports that hard incompatibilists such as Derk Pereboom object instead that agent causation "cannot be reconciled with our best physical theories" (Caruso 2018, §2.2). A leading source sceptic thus does not rest his case against agent causation on the regress; his verdict on the Basic Argument itself was not checked.

## The Parity Problem: The Map's Reading {#parity}

This section is the Map's analysis. No source above makes it, and Strawson's text never mentions agent causation, souls, dualism or substance.

1. **The agent did not choose its nature.** The Map's agent-substance has a nature: its power to select, its sensitivity to reasons, its attentional capacities and the weights it brings to a torn choice. Whether the agent was created or is brute, it is not *causa sui*. The [[agent-causation|agent-causal]] literature does not claim otherwise. E. J. Lowe's free agents start causal chains through uncaused volitions. They are uncaused causes of their choices, not causes of themselves, and Strawson's demand concerns causes of themselves.
2. **The S reply applies word for word.** Whatever the agent decides, it decides as it does because of the way it is, or partly through factors it cannot answer for. Note 8 extends the demand to a non-temporal noumenal self. Dualism buys no exemption.
3. **Parity runs the other way too.** Kane's self-forming actions are not fixed by prior character either, so on the regress itself agent causation gains no structural advantage over Kane. Its claimed advantage is that the agent, not an undetermined effort, does the settling, a difference the Map's register calls "metaphysical (which thing is the causal source)" ([[positions/agency-and-will#^p-a2|P-A2]]).

Map pages have claimed more than this. [[moral-responsibility|Moral Responsibility]] grounds desert in origination: "people deserve praise and blame because they ultimately originated their actions". That is the claim under pressure. Where Map pages call character "self-created", this analysis supports only self-shaping.

## Where the Basic Argument Meets the Rollback {#meeting}

The [[rollback-argument-against-agent-causation|rollback argument]] is a distinct argument.

| | Rollback (van Inwagen 2000) | Basic Argument (Strawson 1986, 1994) |
|---|---|---|
| Kind of luck | Present luck | Constitutive luck |
| Question | Why did this exercise go to A rather than B? | Who made the maker? |
| Time-shape | Synchronic: one token choice | Diachronic: the agent's history |
| Needs probabilities? | Yes, inferred from replay frequencies | No; an a priori regress |
| Family | Leeway and luck | Source |

**Where they meet.** The agent-causal reply requires that how one is explains the choice "without causally determining it". If the agent's nature fixed the choice, constitutive luck would swallow it, which is Strawson's first horn. So the reply opens exactly the gap the rollback presses: given everything about the agent, what settled A rather than B? Strawson's reply to S already has two horns, and Levy's pincer makes it a dilemma. The Map grants that reasons make a choice intelligible without explaining why it went one way rather than the other ([[free-will]]; [[rollback-argument-against-agent-causation#where-replies-end|where every reply ends]]).

On the Map's reading, the two concessions are one, seen from two sides:

- Reasons do not explain why the settling went one way rather than another, so the agent's nature does not fix the choice. That is the reply's ground for denying that the choice is mere constitutive luck.
- For the same reason, nothing more basic explains why the settling went one way. That is the rollback's [[rollback-argument-against-agent-causation#bedrock|token primitive]].

The Map can say *who* originates. It has no further answer to why the originator's settling went this way, nor to why the originator is such as to settle at all. Both questions end at the bare exercise of the power. [[the-divided-will#luck-objection|The divided-will article]] treats the akratic form of present luck.

## What the Map Can Claim {#what-the-map-can-claim}

- **Origination, not authorship of powers.** The agent originates its exercises but did not author the powers it exercises. Origination without self-creation is the Map's commitment, not a result, and Istvan argues that it fails.
- **Self-shaping, not self-creation.** The agent's exercises reshape its dispositions, notably through [[attentional-economics|attention]], but the reshaping power is itself constitutive luck. We are not responsible for our initial characters, nor for the capacity we reshape them with. Responsibility grows by degrees, as Mele and O'Connor suggest.
- **Desert is where the Map is exposed.** Whether origination without self-creation suffices for desert is exactly what Strawson denies, and the tenets do not settle it. If the argument holds against the agent-causal subject, then "ultimate desert in retributive contexts", one residue item where the register holds that libertarian agency may do separating work ([[positions/agency-and-will#^p-a5|P-A5]]), does no such work.
- **No epiphenomenalism.** The argument targets desert, not efficacy: it asks who authored the powers, not whether their exercises do anything.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]].** The tenet is untouched, since Strawson grants that people change the way they are. The tenet's quantifier stays open: it does not say whether some or all choices are agent-caused. If only some are, character responsibility traces back through those choices, much as Kane's traces back through self-forming actions.

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]].** Dualism gains nothing, because note 8 extends the demand to a noumenal self. Nor is Strawson an ally: the collection reprinting the 1994 paper is titled *Real Materialism*, the Map records him championing panpsychist [[russellian-monism|Russellian monism]], and his "thin subject" view denies that subjects persist across gaps in consciousness ([[self-and-self-consciousness]]).

**[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]].** The persisting subject that this tenet's indexical objection relies on is a commitment, not a result. Persistence lets self-shaping accumulate across a life. It does not make the subject's original powers chosen.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]].** The interface says where a settling touches physics. It answers neither constitutive luck nor present luck.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]].** The Basic Argument is an a priori regress, not a parsimony judgement, so the tenet cannot answer it. The tenet constrains only the Map's own reasoning, so it brings no charge against compatibilist or event-causal rivals either.
