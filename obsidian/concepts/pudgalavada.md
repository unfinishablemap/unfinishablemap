Pudgalavāda ("Personalism") is the family of early Indian Buddhist schools—the Vātsīputrīyas, the Sāmmitīyas and three smaller offshoots—that held the person (*pudgala*) to be real rather than a mere designation over the five aggregates, while denying that it is either identical to those aggregates or different from them. Every other Abhidharma school treated the person as a conventional label for a causal series; the Personalists treated it as "true and ultimate" and yet "inexpressible" in its relation to what it depends on. That much every source, sympathetic and hostile, agrees on.

The Map's interest is calibration. Its commitments require a numerically determinate subject that persists through time ([[where-the-substance-commitment-enters]]), and two of its pages call the pudgala that subject's nearest Buddhist precedent. The parallel is structural only. The pudgala is real, irreducible to its constituents, and continues across lives; but it "cannot be found apart from" the aggregates (Priestley), and on Amber Carpenter's reconstruction it is "no further fact ... no extra entity" and no agent, whereas the Map's subject is non-physical and agent-causal. On those two features the nearer Indian precedent is the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika *ātman*, which the Map also declines because it rejects an unchanging core. The Map's subject sits between the two, and the classical refutations of the pudgala reach it unevenly ([which ones, explained below](#which-refutations-reach)). One caution governs what follows: no Pudgalavādin text survives in an Indian language, so the doctrine is known through Chinese translations reconstructed by Leonard Priestley and through its opponents, and each attribution here is to a named reconstruction ([which, explained below](#what-the-scholarship-disputes)).

## What the Personalists Held {#what-the-personalists-held}

Personalism was mainstream Indian Buddhism: on the seventh-century pilgrim Xuanzang's report the Sāmmitīya was "by far the largest of the Shrāvakayāna schools" (Priestley, IEP).

Its central claim, in Priestley's summary, is that the person is "'inexpressible,' that is, as indeterminate in its relation to the five aggregates, since it cannot be identified with the aggregates and cannot be found apart from them: the self and the aggregates are neither the same nor different." Other schools accepted the indeterminacy and concluded that persons are merely conceptual; the Personalists took it to "characterize a real self, a self that is 'true and ultimate,'" and added the person as a fifth category of existence, the "inexpressible", after past, present and future phenomena and Nirvana. Their standing analogy was fire and fuel: "the fire is not the same as the fuel, nor can it continue to burn without the fuel. Similarly, the person ... cannot exist without a body, feelings and the other aggregates." Their reason for refusing both horns survives in Vasubandhu's report: "if it were different from the skandhas, it would be eternal and thus unconditioned; but if it were identical to the skandhas, it would then be susceptible of annihilation" (Pruden, vol. 4, p. 1315).

Their scriptural anchor was the *Bhārasutta* (SN 22.22): "Bhārā have pañcakkhandhā, bhārahāro ca puggalo"—the five aggregates are the burden, the person the bearer. The orthodox reading takes the sutta's own gloss, "the venerable of such name and such clan", as a conventional designation; Priestley judges that reading strained. Their explanatory arguments were that memory of past lives needs "a real basis for identifying oneself ... with the person that one was in a previous life", and that "the operation of karma is incomprehensible if the person is nothing more than an assemblage of phenomena".

## What the Scholarship Disputes {#what-the-scholarship-disputes}

The evidence is, in Priestley's words, "quite limited, much of it surviving only in translation, and some of it from hostile sources". Three things are unsettled, and each bears on how close the precedent is.

**The person's ontological status shifted.** Priestley reports that the Personalists "seem to have regarded the self, at least initially, as conceptual, though 'true and ultimate.' A later source represents them as maintaining that it is neither conceptual nor substantial, and still later sources ascribe the view to them that the self is indeed substantial." Which stage one reads as the Pudgalavāda changes the parallel.

**Carpenter reads the doctrine as a thesis about individuation, not a substrate.** Her 2015 chapter takes the Personalist's core claim to be that "individuation of causal continua is presupposed, not projected. That some elements belong to each other, and not to any others, is really, ultimately true." She is explicit about what this does not add: "None of this, note, implies the existence of an immaterial substantial substratum for the aggregates." Her pudgala "is thus no substance, no 'underlying subject' of properties, nor an active agent of unity distinct from the unique unity it is", and her reconstructed Personalist says outright: "This is no further fact, venerable Sir, no extra entity". A footnote nonetheless leaves open whether the view is reductionist on Parfit's terms or "a variant of the 'further fact view'".

**Siderits reads it as non-reductive supervenience.** Christian Coseru reports that Siderits (2003) makes "a good case for taking the Pudgalavādins as proposing something analogous to the notion that persons non-reductively supervene on the aggregates"; this page relies on Coseru's report.

The Map follows Priestley and Carpenter as the floor: a real, ultimately individuated person that is inseparable from its aggregates and adds no substrate. The later reports of a substantial self come from hostile sources and are noted, not adopted.

## The Two Classical Refutations {#the-two-refutations}

**The *Kathāvatthu*.** The Theravāda *Points of Controversy* opens with the person: "Is 'the person' known in the sense of a real and ultimate fact?" The Personalist says yes. "Is the person known in the same way as a real and ultimate fact is known?" "Nay, that cannot truly be said." The Theravādin concludes that the first admission must then be withdrawn (Aung and Rhys Davids, I.1). The commentary glosses "in the same way" as "either as the factors of mind and body are known, by immediate consciousness, or under one of the twenty-four relation-categories", so the argument rests on a closed list of ways an ultimate can be known. The text then poses the rebirth trilemma: is the person that transmigrates the identical one, a different one, or both? The Personalist must answer none of these.

**Vasubandhu.** The ninth chapter of the *Abhidharmakośabhāṣya* opens: "There is neither direct perception nor inference of a soul independent of the skandhas. We know then that a real soul does not exist" (Pruden, vol. 4, p. 1314). Against the Vātsīputrīyas he poses a dilemma: "If it has a separate existence, like physical matter, then the pudgala exists as an entity. If it is only a collection, like milk, then it exists as a designation." The entity horn has costs: the pudgala "will be different from the skandhas ... (or it will be produced by causes and then it will not be eternal as you say), and you will have to name its causes; or it will be unconditioned: and this is a non-Buddhist opinion" (p. 1314). Gold's gloss is that a caused person "must change as they change". Against the Personalist claim that the pudgala is perceived, though through an inexpressible relation, Vasubandhu concludes that it "exists solely as a designation exactly like milk" (p. 1319).

The chapter's ownership argument matters most for the Map. The Vātsīputrīya asks, "In the absence of a soul, whose is the memory?", and explains that the genitive "designates its master", as Caitra is master of his cow. Vasubandhu answers: "What you call 'master' is then simply the cause, and what you call 'subject' is simply the result. ... Memory belongs to whatever causes memory" (pp. 1340–41). In the chapter's second half the target becomes the Vaiśeṣika *ātman*, a permanent non-physical self with independent power. Vasubandhu lays out the chain of action, "Memory causes a wish or a desire for action to surge up; from desire there proceeds imagination; from imagination there proceeds effort which gives rise to a vapor which sets in motion bodily action", and asks, "In this process, what activity do the Vaisesikas attribute to a 'soul'?" His own definition of an agent is deflationary: "amongst all its causes, that which is the principal cause of this action" (pp. 1351–52). Gold adds that for Vasubandhu "everything that is real or substantial (dravya) is causally efficient", and that the controller argument aims "only to indicate the Buddhist position's comparative simplicity and causal parsimony".

## How Far the Parallel Holds {#how-far-the-parallel-holds}

What follows is the Map's comparison, not a claim any source above makes. The Map's subject, set out in [[self-and-self-consciousness]] and [[haecceity]], is numerically determinate and non-physical, persists through qualitative change, exercises constrained causal influence on physical outcomes, and bears the indexical fact that this stream is mine.

On three counts the pudgala matches: it is real rather than nominal, irreducible to the aggregates other schools took to exhaust the person, and continuous through time (for the Personalists, across lives; the Map takes no position on rebirth), the diachronic job the Map's subject does. Carpenter's individuation reading matches more closely still, since "some causal streams are really individuated" is close to the particularity claim in [[haecceity]].

On the two counts that distinguish the Map's dualism from Abhidharma, it does not match. The pudgala is inseparable from its aggregates, including form (*rūpa*), and Carpenter disclaims an "immaterial substantial substratum". And the pudgala is no agent: "nor an active agent of unity distinct from the unique unity it is" closes the door the Map's [[agent-causation|agent-causal]] commitment walks through. Carpenter's 2017 essay frames the Map's need precisely, asking for something "at once an agent and a subject, as well as the synthesiser", and then argues that Buddhists meet the demands of responsibility without it.

The view that does posit a non-physical agent-subject is the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika *ātman* ([[indian-philosophy-of-mind]]), which is why the second half of Vasubandhu's chapter is aimed there. The Map declines that too, because the *ātman* is unchanging and the Map's subject is not ([[self-and-self-consciousness#Diachronic Identity|diachronic irreducibility]]). The honest placement is between: real and particular like the pudgala, non-physical and agentive like the *ātman*, unlike both in persisting through change. Carpenter (2015) notes that the Personalist "would acknowledge himself defeated" if the doctrine could not be distinguished from "commitment to an eternal soul". The Map takes as a horn what the Personalist took as a refutation, and so inherits the arguments aimed at the eternal soul as well.

## Which Refutations Reach the Map {#which-refutations-reach}

None of these is answered here; this section records reach and the reply the Map owes.

**Direct hits.** Vasubandhu's agent argument is a mental-causal-closure argument: the chain from memory through desire, imagination and effort to action is complete, so a self has nothing to do. The Map's reply must be that the chain is not sufficient at the point of selection, which is the [[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]] story; Vasubandhu's definition of the agent as "the principal cause" is one the Map can meet only if the subject's selection, physically minimal under Tenet 2, is what settles which open alternative becomes actual; whether that makes it the principal cause or one cause among many is unsettled. The entity-or-designation dilemma also reaches the Map, which takes the entity horn and accepts Vasubandhu's criterion that the real is the causally efficient. Two debts follow. The Map must "name its causes", and on the subject's origin it holds only a candidate, Hasker's [[emergent-dualism]], as a cousin rather than a commitment. And a caused subject "must change as they change"; the Map's answer, numerical identity through qualitative change on the organism analogy, is one Vasubandhu would meet by saying the organism is itself a designation. That exchange is where the disagreement reaches bedrock.

**Splits.** The ownership argument converts "whose?" into "what caused it?" and dissolves ownership understood as mastery. The Map does not need mastery: [[buddhist-perspectives-on-meaning]] already concedes that its subject claims constrained influence rather than sovereignty. What the argument leaves untouched is ownership as phenomenal for-me-ness, the sense treated in [[mine-ness]]. The Buddhist rejoinder there is ownerless reflexive awareness (*svasaṃvedana*) in the Dignāga tradition, which [[witness-consciousness]] touches under self-luminosity and this page does not assess. The *Kathāvatthu*'s opening also splits: its charge, that the person is an ultimate known in none of the standard ways, applies to a subject given as the for-me-ness of experience rather than observed as an object, and the Map's reply is a framework difference over what counts as knowing, not a refutation. Vasubandhu's argument from ineffable perception reaches any claim to introspective access, so Map pages should not say the subject is "found" in experience.

**Mostly misses.** The fire-and-fuel analysis targets the neither-same-nor-different relation, which the Map does not assert of subject and brain. The rebirth trilemma trapped the Personalist; the Map takes no position on rebirth, and for persistence within a life it answers "same", at the cost already recorded under the eternalism horn. Gold also reports a Personalist argument from the unpredictability of mental life, "something like an incredulous denial that we could be 'mere automatons'", which Vasubandhu met with "the possibility of change in a self-regulating system"; it is an ancestor of the Map's own argument from agency and of the compatibilist reply, a historical symmetry rather than evidence for either side.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The pudgala shares the Map's [[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]] tenet only in its negative claim, that the person is not reducible to its constituents. Abhidharma itself grants irreducible mental dharmas ([[buddhism-and-dualism]]), so the Personalist dispute never concerned whether mind is physical, only whether there is a real person over and above momentary events. The Map answers yes and adds what no Personalist did, that the person is non-physical. Madhyamaka would reject the pudgala and the Map's subject alike as empty; that challenge is met, so far as it is met, in [[buddhism-and-dualism#emptiness-challenge|the emptiness challenge]] and not re-run here.

[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] is where the ancient debate lands hardest. Vasubandhu's agent argument targets exactly the agent-causal reading the Map adopts, and the Map's only reply is its own claim that the physical chain is incomplete at the selection point. The Map counts this as a live rival argument it has not refuted.

The pudgala was invoked to secure a determinate subject across lives, close kin to the job the indexical objection under [[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]] asks of the persisting subject. The Map speculates that the Personalists were reaching for the same fact, but claims none of their authority, since they refused the "same subject" answer the Map gives.

Vasubandhu's closing appeal to "comparative simplicity and causal parsimony" is the ancient form of the argument [[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]] exists to answer. The Map's reply is the tenet itself: parsimony is unreliable while the causal facts at the selection point remain unsettled. That reply is a Map commitment, not a result the Buddhist debate supplies.
