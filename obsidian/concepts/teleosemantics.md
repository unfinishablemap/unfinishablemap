Teleosemantics—Ruth Millikan's coinage was *biosemantics*—is the leading *positive* naturalistic theory of mental content: the strongest attempt to explain how a physical state can be genuinely *about* the world using only resources a working scientist recognises. Where bare causal or informational theories fix content by covariation (state S means P because S reliably co-occurs with P), teleosemantics adds one ingredient that covariation lacks: **biological proper function fixed by selection history**. A state means P because the mechanisms that produce or consume it were *selected for* their role relative to P. That addition is meant to earn what a correlation can never earn on its own—the possibility of *misrepresentation*, of a state being *wrong*—and so to cross from mere correlation to genuine aboutness. This article gives that programme its strongest form across its four major proponents (Millikan, Neander, Dretske, Papineau) and its canonical objections *with* their best replies, before locating the Map's dissent: proper function delivers at most *selection-relative* correctness, which is not the rational, content-constituting normativity the Map's [dualism tenet requires](#relation-to-site-perspective). This page is the positive rival's home. The negative master argument against it—Hutto and Myin's [[the-naturalisation-failure-for-content|Hard Problem of Content]]—lives elsewhere and is engaged here only by reference.

## The Problem: From Covariation to Aboutness

Every naturalistic theory of content confronts one demand: explain how a neural state comes to *say* something that can be true or false. The natural sciences supply causal relations, statistical correlations, and evolutionary history. The trouble is that correlation is normatively inert. A tree ring correlates with rainfall without being *about* rainfall; a thermometer tracks temperature without representing it. What content adds to correlation is *correctness conditions*—a state can be tokened when its worldly condition fails to obtain, and then it *misrepresents*. Bare covariation cannot mark that difference, because whatever a state actually correlates with is, trivially, what it correlates with.

Teleosemantics' central move is to source the missing normativity from biology. Proponents adopt an **etiological** theory of function: a trait's function is not what it happens to do but what past tokens of its type were *selected for*. A heart's function is pumping blood—not making thumping sounds—because pumping is why hearts were favoured by selection. Transfer this to inner states: a representation's content is the condition its producing or consuming mechanism was selected to operate relative to. Because selection favoured performance under one specific condition, tokening the state *outside* that condition is genuine failure. The "ought" of representing-truly is glossed as the "ought" of functioning-as-selected. Whether that gloss succeeds is the pivot on which the whole programme—and the Map's dissent—turns.

## Millikan's Biosemantics: Proper Functions and the Consumer

Ruth Millikan founded the programme in *Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories* (MIT Press, 1984) and stated it most accessibly in the essay "Biosemantics" (collected in *White Queen Psychology and Other Essays for Alice*, MIT Press, 1993). Two ideas do the work.

First, **proper function** is etiological: an item's proper function is the effect that explains why its ancestors were reproduced. This makes function a natural, historically-grounded relation—no appeal to present dispositions, designers, or minds—yet a normative one, since an item can exist while failing to perform the function that explains its existence. That is malfunction, and malfunction is the biological template for misrepresentation.

Second, and distinctively, Millikan locates content on the **consumer** side. Every representation is a sign passed from a *producer* mechanism to one or more *consumer* mechanisms downstream. What the sign *means* is fixed not by what tends to produce it but by the world-condition onto which it must map for the consumer to carry out *its* proper functions in the way that historically succeeded—Millikan's term of art is performing them the "Normal" way, where "Normal" means the way that explains past success, not the statistically usual way. On this reading a bee-dance means *nectar in such-and-such direction* because that is the mapping the dance-reading consumer relies on to forage successfully; a frog's inner sign means whatever its snapping-and-digesting consumer was selected to have it map onto. Producers confer representational *status*; consumers confer *content*.

(The consumer-primacy reading is contested, including by Millikan herself, who stresses complementary producer and consumer roles; Schulte (2025) argues there are "two Millikans"—an explicit consumer theory in the early work and an implicit hybrid theory in the later. The Map treats the consumer-side emphasis as the canonical statement while noting the scholarship is live.)

## Neander's Informational Teleosemantics: The Producer Side

Karen Neander's *A Mark of the Mental: In Defense of Informational Teleosemantics* (MIT Press, 2017) develops the rival, **producer-side** account for sensory-perceptual representation. Here content is fixed by what the sensory system was selected to be *caused by*—the distal condition it has the function to *detect*—rather than by what a downstream consumer does with the result.

Neander's sophistication is to weave together three elements she argues are complementary rather than competing: biological **function**, **causal-information** relations, and **second-order similarity** (the inner state changes in ways that are structurally analogous to changes in what it represents). A sensory system has the function of producing inner states that are both reliably caused by, and analogs of, their referents. To break ties between candidate contents she invokes a **distality principle**: a visual system responds to proximal features (retinal patterns) *because* doing so historically enabled detecting distal ones (prey), and that explanatory asymmetry fixes the distal cause as the content.

## Dretske's Indicator-Function Account

Fred Dretske supplies the theory's information-theoretic ancestor. In *Explaining Behavior: Reasons in a World of Causes* (MIT Press, 1988) he argues that an internal state acquires the **function of indicating** a condition when a consumer system is recruited—through learning or selection—to use that state in controlling behaviour. Natural information (nomic dependence: the state would not be present unless the condition obtained) supplies the raw correlation; recruitment for behavioural control supplies the function; content is *what the state has the function to indicate*.

Dretske's ambition later widened. In *Naturalizing the Mind* (MIT Press, 1995) he extended representationalism to *phenomenal* character itself, arguing that qualia are the represented external properties—that what it is like to have an experience is exhausted by what the experience non-conceptually represents. This makes Dretske the most ambitious figure for the Map's purposes, since he aims to naturalise experience as well as aboutness, bringing the account into direct contact with the Map's [[hard-problem-of-consciousness|hard problem]] and [[explanatory-gap|explanatory gap]] reasoning.

## Papineau's Success-Semantic Version

David Papineau, in *Philosophical Naturalism* (Blackwell, 1993), gives an output-side account tied to **success semantics**. A desire's satisfaction condition is the effect it is the desire's biological purpose to produce; a belief is true when, combined with desires, it would tend to yield the success the underlying states were selected to deliver. Truth and satisfaction conditions are thus read off the selection history of behaviour-guiding states. Papineau is also notable for the frankest concession-and-reframe on the Swampman objection, discussed [below](#swampman).

## The Objection Battery and Its Best Replies

A genuine steelman must field the standard objections *with* the replies teleosemanticists actually give. Presenting the objections alone would smuggle in a refutation.

### The Disjunction Problem (the Founding Success)

Jerry Fodor's disjunction problem is the difficulty teleosemantics is standardly credited with solving. On a pure causal theory, a belief-state caused by cats and, on dark nights, by small foxes means *cat-or-small-fox*—so the theory cannot say the fox-triggered tokening is an *error* rather than a correct representation of a disjunction. Teleosemantics blocks this: if the consumer (or detector) was selected for its role relative to *cats*, then a fox-triggered tokening is a malfunction, not part of a disjunctive content. Misrepresentation is recovered. This is widely regarded as teleosemantics' strongest card, and the debate has moved on from *whether* function can distinguish error to *which* condition function picks out.

### The Frog Case (Neander versus Fodor)

That "which condition" question is sharpest in the frog. A frog's prey-detector was selected because it responded to small dark moving things, *and* because that enabled catching prey, *and* because prey yielded nutrition. So does the inner state represent *fly*, *frog food*, *nutrition*, or *small dark moving thing*? Fodor presses that selection is silent among locally co-instantiated candidates, so content is indeterminate and teleosemantics fails to deliver a definite answer. Neander's reply is the producer-side one: content is what the system was selected to be *causally sensitive* to, and a frog's visual system cannot be causally sensitive to *nutritional value* or to *dipteran taxonomy*—only to visible motion and shape. So the content is determinately *small, dark, moving thing*. The frog case remains the flagship test, still actively litigated, and a principal reason producer-side informational teleosemantics has gained ground on benefit-based versions.

### Swampman

Donald Davidson's [[swampman|Swampman]] is a lightning-formed molecular duplicate of a person, assembled by cosmic accident with no evolutionary or learning history. Having no selection history, Swampman has no proper functions—so teleosemantics entails his inner states have no content, which many find plainly false. Teleosemanticists reply in two main ways. The **bite-the-bullet** strategy (Millikan; Neander) holds that "intentionality" rigidly designates a historical-selectional kind, much as "water" designates H₂O: the intuition that Swampman thinks is a mistaken pre-theoretic intuition, like insisting water needn't be H₂O. The **actual-world-reduction** strategy (Papineau 2001) grants that teleosemantics reduces the *actual* property of belief, so a merely possible Swampman with content is no counterexample to an actual-world identity claim—Papineau reports being moved off pure bullet-biting partly by the ethical discomfort of denying that a Swamp-person has a mind. Both replies keep the theory intact at the price of a commitment about what content *is*. Papineau has since argued (2022) that selectional history is built into the kind essence of mental representation, narrowing the gap between the two strategies.

### The Indeterminacy of Function (Pietroski's *kimu*)

Paul Pietroski's *kimu* thought experiment (1992) targets *benefit*-based versions specifically. Imagine colour-blind creatures whose brain-state B, keyed to a certain wavelength, spread because it led them uphill toward safety from a dawn predator (the *snorf*)—not because it tracked *red*. A benefit-based teleosemantics that reads content off downstream advantage seems forced to say B means *snorf-free space*, when intuitively B represents *red*. The pressure generalises: selection acts on whole complexes of co-implicated properties (Neander's own antelope example), so function may underdetermine content. Millikan's response distinguishes *represented* from merely *causing* properties: a state can represent an invisible property (safety, nutrition) that is caused by visible features. And crucially, *kimu* is fuel for the producer-side turn: tying content to the distal *cause* the system detects, rather than the *benefit* it confers, sidesteps the case—one reason informational teleosemantics is now the more defended wing.

## Relation to Site Perspective

Teleosemantics is the most credible reductive rival to the Map's first tenet, **Dualism** (the irreducibility of the mental). Its appeal is exactly that it seems to derive normativity—correctness, error, misrepresentation—from mind-independent selection facts, with no appeal to consciousness. If it succeeds, aboutness is naturalised and one of the Map's central claims is undercut. So the Map owes it a fair hearing, which the sections above supply, before dissenting.

The Map's dissent is a claim about *two normativities*. Proper function yields **biological** normativity: a trait can malfunction relative to what selection favoured. Content requires **semantic-rational** normativity: a state can be *false*, and a thinker *ought*, given evidence, to judge one way rather than another. The Map's contention is that the first does not constitute the second. A malfunctioning heart is not *mistaken about* anything; it fails a selection-relative standard without thereby entering the space of reasons. Reading the "ought" of representing-truly off the "ought" of having-been-selected trades a rational relation for a historical-statistical one, and the two come apart—not over what *fixes* correctness, which is world-involving on any theory including the Map's, but over what earns a state *answerability to evidence*, which selection-relative malfunction never amounts to. This is a sibling of the Map's [[consciousness-and-the-normativity-of-reason|normativity-of-reason]] argument, where the prescriptive force of inference likewise resists reduction to what brains were selected to do.

This dissent is deliberately kept distinct from the standing argument the Map already holds against naturalised content, Hutto and Myin's [[the-naturalisation-failure-for-content|Hard Problem of Content]], which it cross-references rather than repeats. The HPC presses the *intensionality* gap—that even granting proper function, biology cannot fix the *guise* under which a state represents (as *food* versus as *nutritionally-optimal-nearby-item*)—and the naturalisation-failure topic develops that dilemma as a stand-alone parallel to the phenomenal hard problem. The present objection is upstream of it: it questions whether *selection-relative malfunction* is the *right kind* of normativity to begin with, before intensionality is even reached. Where the Map's [[intentionality|phenomenal intentionality]] tradition locates genuine aboutness in consciousness, teleosemantics locates it in history—and history, the Map holds, is the wrong place to find an *ought*.

The strongest reply to this dissent from inside the programme comes from Stephen Mann and Ross Pain (2022). Answering Hutto and Myin, they separate two criteria a theory of content might be asked to meet: *truth-evaluability* (the state can be true or false) and *intensionality* (content can differ while reference holds fixed). Teleosemantics, they argue, meets the first through its consumer-function machinery and is not required to meet the second: following Richard Heck's Russellian reply to Frege puzzles and Millikan's own theory of concepts, the explanatory work modes of presentation were meant to do is done by beliefs that differ in vehicle while sharing one purely referential content. A parallel move — the Map's construction, not the paper's — applies to the Map's dissent: answerability to evidence can be treated as a personal-level notion, so a theory of sub-personal content owes only selection-relative correctness and was never in the business of delivering rational normativity. The Map's answer is that the split concedes the point at issue rather than dissolving it. Personal-level content—the belief that can be false, the judgement one *ought* to revise—is what naturalising aboutness was supposed to deliver; relocating it outside the theory's remit grants that proper function does not reach it. The dissent then moves to the seam the split creates: whatever bridges sub-personal correctness to personal-level answerability is where, on the Map's view, consciousness does the work that selection cannot. The naturalisation-failure topic reads Mann and Pain's handling of intensionality the same way, and the two objections meet here. A sub-personal state that is neither intensional nor answerable to evidence is a well-behaved *tracker*, and the content-vocabulary applied to it is a summary of its dynamics rather than a report of its aboutness (compare [[content-vocabulary-as-derived-feature|the derived-feature calibration]] for predictive processing).

Three honest limitations. First, the bite-the-bullet reply is principled: a teleosemanticist can simply insist that "content" *names* the historical kind, so the Map's appeal to a rational normativity beyond selection begs the question against the reduction. The disagreement is over what content is, and neither side refutes the other by stipulation. Second, teleosemantics is a live and improving research programme; producer-side informational versions have answered several objections that sank cruder ancestors, and the Map's dissent is a reasoned bet about the limits of reduction ([[tenets|Occam's razor has limits]]), not a demonstration. Third, the level-split is not costless for the Map either: a teleosemanticist can hold that personal-level content is *constructed* from sub-personal content plus learning and sociocultural scaffolding—the route Hutto and Myin themselves take for contentful cognition—so that nothing extra is needed at the seam. The Map's claim that consciousness enters there is a bet on where that construction fails, and the burden of showing it fails remains the Map's. What the Map claims is that the crossing from covariation to aboutness, even with proper function added, stops one step short of the normativity that content-bearing thought requires—and that the missing step is where consciousness enters.
