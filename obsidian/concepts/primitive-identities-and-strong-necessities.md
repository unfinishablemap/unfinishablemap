**Primitive identities** and **strong necessities** are the two costs David Chalmers says Type-B physicalism must pay. His verdict in "Consciousness and its Place in Nature" (2003) is that the only remotely viable materialist options are type-A and type-B, at the cost of "denying the manifest explanandum in the first case, and embracing primitive identities or strong necessities in the second case". A primitive identity, in his sense, is an identity that is *epistemically* primitive: true, if the physicalist is right, but "not deducible from the complete physical truth". A strong necessity is "an a posteriori necessity with a necessary primary intension" (Chalmers 1999): unlike "water is H₂O", it comes out true at every metaphysically possible world considered as actual, though not at every conceivable one. The two costs match the two ways of stating Type-B, as an identity between phenomenal and physical properties or as a necessitation of the phenomenal by the physical. Chalmers's A/B/C taxonomy, and which Map reply reaches which type, are set out in [[type-a-type-b-and-type-c-physicalism]].

The Map's reading of the exchange is that Type-B grants the non-derivability on which the cost rests and disputes that it is a cost. Its explanatory structure, Chalmers and Frank Jackson write, "is just like the explanatory structure of property dualism" (2001): a physical component plus a bridge that cannot be derived from it. What remains in dispute is whether that bridge is an identity or a law, and the hinge is their distinction: "Identities are ontologically primitive, but they are not epistemically primitive" ([[#Ontological and Epistemic Primitiveness|explained below]]). The Map's evidential tier against Type-B stays at *compatible*, because the cost at most removes a defeater and raises no tier ([[#What the Cost Buys the Map|explained below]]).

The phrase "primitive identity" has a second use on the Map. Martine Nida-Rümelin's primitive identity of conscious subjects, discussed in [[consciousness-and-the-metaphysics-of-individuation]], concerns which subject survives fission. This page concerns the identity of phenomenal properties with physical ones.

## Ontological and Epistemic Primitiveness

Type-B's first answer to the cost is that identities never need explaining. David Papineau put it bluntly: "If they really are the same thing, then we can't explain why they are the same thing" (1993, p. 180); later, "Identities need no explanation. Identities are necessary. They could not have been otherwise" (2011, p. 9; compare 1998, p. 379). Of Mark Twain and Samuel Clemens, Ned Block and Robert Stalnaker write: "Identities don't have explanations (though of course there are explanations of how the two terms can denote the same thing)" (1999, p. 24). An identity earns its place, on their account, by inference to the best explanation, since identities "allow a transfer of explanatory and causal force not allowed by mere correlations".

Chalmers and Jackson (2001) reply that this "seems to conflate ontological and epistemological matters". That a thing is itself needs no explanation, and in that ontological sense every identity is primitive. An ordinary scientific identity, they hold, is still epistemically derivable. From the full base of truths, which for Chalmers and Jackson includes phenomenal, "that's all" and indexical truths as well as physical ones, "water is H₂O" follows a priori: water is whatever plays the water role, and chemistry shows that H₂O plays it. No such derivation, they argue, reaches the psychophysical identity. In its 1993 form the no-explanation slogan answers the ontological question and leaves the epistemic one untouched. Papineau's 2011 statement takes up the epistemic one: identities "can be evidenced more directly" than by a priori derivation, as when "the two kinds at issue co-occur" (p. 10), and identities involving names or indexicals are no more derivable, yet leave nothing feeling unexplained (p. 9); he traces the felt gap to an implicit commitment to dualism (p. 8). Chalmers's (2010) audit takes up names and demonstratives ([[#Is There Any Other Case?|below]]).

Joseph Levine, whom Chalmers lists among type-B materialists, draws the same contrast in his own vocabulary. He writes, "I generally endorse the claim that pure identities are not suitable candidates for explanation" (2001, p. 81), yet some identity claims are *gappy*, a term he introduces at p. 84: "a gappy identity claim is one for which a request for explanation is intelligible" (p. 163). For water he grants that "there is no a priori route from the 'H2O'-described facts to the 'water'-described facts" (p. 91), and still counts it non-gappy; the psychophysical identity is gappy. Asking whether this gives the anti-materialist more ammunition, he answers: "I think so, but not quite enough to deal materialism a death blow" (p. 86).

Three Map pages already answer the no-explanation reply, and their counters stack. Saul Kripke's point, run in [[type-identity-theory]], shows why pain cannot be treated like heat: pain has no appearance distinct from itself to absorb the felt contingency. The Chalmers–Jackson distinction, run in [[the-relocation-objection#rival-readings|the relocation objection]], says what kind of explanation is missing. The law-mark premise, next, says what the missing derivation implies.

## A Law Under Another Name

The cost argument proper turns on what an epistemically primitive connection is. Chalmers holds that the type-B materialist "must hold that the identification between consciousness and physical or functional states is epistemically primitive", and so "recognizes a principle that has the epistemic status of a fundamental law, but gives it the ontological status of an identity" (2003). The premise doing the work is Chalmers and Jackson's account of lawhood: "what it is to be a fundamental law of nature is precisely to be an objective, epistemically primitive counterfactual-supporting regularity" (2001, §7). Since "fundamental laws always connect distinct properties" (2003), a primitive identity looks like a relabelled law. "Unless there is an independent case for primitive identities," Chalmers concludes, "the suggestion will seem at best ad hoc and mysterious, and at worst incoherent."

Type-B grants the premise of the parity half and contests its significance. Psychophysical identities, Chalmers and Jackson observe, "are inferred from regularities between brain processes and consciousness, in order to systematize and explain those regularities"; "Ontologically, these identities may differ from laws. But epistemically, they are just like laws." Papineau and Block and Stalnaker reply that water's identity is evidenced the same way. Chalmers argues that type-B "will (in effect) accept my conclusions about explanation" (1999); Levine, a type-B author, grants as much in his own terms by conceding gappiness. Call this common ground the **parity result**: on the explanation side, Type-B and property dualism each posit a physical component plus an epistemically primitive bridge. Whether ordinary science posits such bridges too is the question of the sections below. In Chalmers's summary, the view "may preserve the letter of materialism; but by requiring primitive bridging principles, it sacrifices much of materialism's spirit" (2003).

Parity leaves the ontological question open. A physicalist can accept that the bridge behaves epistemically like a law and still hold that it is an identity, so the dispute moves to whether anything else behaves this way ([[#Is There Any Other Case?|below]]).

## Strong Necessities

The necessitation formulation of Type-B claims only that the complete physical truth necessitates the phenomenal truth, a posteriori. Chalmers (2003) notes that type-B theorists model this on Kripke, "though it should be noted that Kripke himself denies this claim"; Philip Goff, a panpsychist critic of physicalism, and Papineau (2014) agree that Kripke rejected the psychophysical necessity but read him as open to strong necessities involving "radically opaque" terms, so that his reasons were not two-dimensional. The Map leaves the reading of Kripke's reasons undecided.

The label is Chalmers's. His 1999 reply to critics introduced it, shortening the "strong metaphysical necessities" he reports using in *The Conscious Mind* (1996): "Let us call these a posteriori necessities not explicable by the 2-D framework strong necessities." Kripke's examples are **weak** necessities. "Water is H₂O" is necessary, but its primary intension, what it says if a given world is treated as actual, is contingent: a world where the watery stuff is XYZ verifies "water is not H₂O". A strong necessity has a necessary primary intension as well, so no metaphysically possible world, however considered, verifies its negation, though some conceivable world does: strong necessities "require a distinction between conceivability and possibility at the level of worlds" (1999), and the two-dimensional explanation of Kripke's cases has nothing to work with. The *Stanford Encyclopedia* puts the contrast without that machinery, as a derivation versus a non-derivation view of the necessary a posteriori, and concludes that "if one wants to defend a posteriori physicalism, one will have to defend the non-derivation view of the necessary a posteriori. However, the non-derivation view is controversial" (Stoljar 2026).

Chalmers's charges (1999, §3.3) are that strong necessities have no analogue, that they amount to "an ad hoc proliferation of modalities", that they are "brute and inexplicable", and that "the only motivation to postulate such necessities is the desire to save materialism". Admitting them is "modal dualism", which "requires two modal primitives" (§3.5).

One equivalence makes this half of the cost the same dispute as the two-dimensional argument. Chalmers (2010): "It is easy to see that CP- is equivalent to the thesis that there are no strong necessities." CP- is the negative conceivability–possibility thesis: roughly, whatever cannot be ruled out a priori holds at some possible world considered as actual. Denying strong necessities is accepting that thesis. In the two-dimensional argument, denying that whatever is conceivable is verified by some world is, Chalmers says, "the only hope for the type-B materialist" (2003), and that denial is the same disagreement seen from the other side. The argument itself runs on [[zombie-master-argument]] and [[conceivability-possibility-inference]]. It does not turn on the coincidence of primary and secondary intensions for phenomenal concepts: Chalmers calls that coincidence "inessential to the argument" (1999) and "not required for the argument to go through" (2003).

## Is There Any Other Case?

The cost argument rests on an analogy: elsewhere, "objective (non-indexical) epistemically primitive regularities among natural phenomena" are fundamental laws (Chalmers & Jackson 2001), and every other identity is derivable. Chalmers (2010) audits fifteen candidate strong necessities, from Cicero = Tully through necessary laws of nature and a necessary God to unknowable mathematical truths. "Perhaps the most serious challenges come from mathematical cases such as the Continuum Hypothesis and from metamodal cases," he judges, and even these are cases "where the initial situation is unclear, rather than cases where there is a clear counterexample". Neither is a physical identity.

Block and Stalnaker deny that the psychophysical case is special: "the facts about water are not a priori entailed by the microphysical facts either" (1999, p. 29). Chalmers and Jackson answer that the entailment base includes phenomenal and indexical truths, so water's identity is derivable "modulo phenomenology"; Levine, as above, grants no a priori route for water and still finds it non-gappy. Jonathan Schaffer (2017) generalises the physicalist answer: gaps "are pervasive, lurking in the transition from the physical to the chemical and in every concrete transition from more to less fundamental", and are "unproblematic, so long as they are bridged by substantive principles of metaphysical grounding". On that view the psychophysical bridge is one such principle among many.

The strongest physicalist reply grants the uniqueness and denies that it is evidence. By Chalmers's report, "Loar accepts explicitly that there are no other counterexamples to the 2-D model: he thinks that psychophysical necessities are sui generis" (1999). Loar's own challenge goes to the law-mark premise: "it is fair of the physicalist to request a justification of the assumption that conceptually distinct concepts must express metaphysically distinct properties" (1999, p. 467, as quoted by Kirk 2023). If phenomenal concepts are unlike all other concepts, as the [[phenomenal-concepts-strategy]] holds, a unique gap is what physicalism predicts. Goff and Papineau add that the case-by-case debate is "inevitably inconclusive": "Why shouldn't it still be open to a posteriori physicalists to hold that mind-brain necessities are an exception?" On this reply the absence of other cases is expected on Type-B, and the finding carries little weight unless Chalmers's (2007) master argument against the phenomenal-concepts strategy shows that no concept-based explanation can work.

## Where the Dispute Bottoms Out

Two disputes remain once parity is granted, and neither is settled.

The first is whether the uniqueness can be explained without dualism. Against cognitive explanations of zombie conceivability, Hill and McLaughlin's among them, Chalmers objects that they explain only the conceivability ("There will always be a cognitive explanation of a modal intuition!"), while a real explanation "has to show us why a state-of-affairs should be conceivable while at the same time being impossible" (1999, §3.4). Chalmers's (2007) master argument against the phenomenal-concepts strategy turns this into a dilemma for every concept-based account; [[phenomenal-concepts-strategy]] assesses it.

The second is the epistemology of modality. If metaphysical modality floats free of conceivability, Chalmers asks how conceivability could guide us to it: "Why should not there be just one metaphysically possible world, or 37?" (2010, §10). Papineau's half of the joint paper answers that "metaphysical modality has nothing to do with conceivability" and is "grounded in counterfactual thinking" (Goff & Papineau 2014). Chalmers himself grants that the type-B position "remains at least formally open" (1999, §3.5).

## What the Cost Buys the Map

The parity result, if Chalmers and Jackson are right that water's identity is derivable, removes a defeater: the claim that physicalism enjoys, for consciousness, the explanatory advantage it enjoys for water, heat and genes. Under the Map's rule that removing a defeater never raises an evidential tier ([[positions/methodology-and-calibration#^p-m1|P-M1]]), Type-B stays at *compatible*.

With parity granted, three considerations could decide between identity and law:

1. **Analogy.** On Chalmers and Jackson's account, every other objective, non-indexical epistemically primitive regularity among natural phenomena is a law, a premise Block and Stalnaker contest for water and Schaffer for every transition from more to less fundamental. This is the Map's strongest consideration, and a conditional one: Type-B predicts the disanalogy, so analogy discriminates only if Chalmers's (2007) master argument against the phenomenal-concepts strategy succeeds.
2. **Parsimony, on both sides.** The identity posits one property where the law posits two and a law between them; Block and Stalnaker justify the psychophysical identity "By using the kinds of methodological consideration sketched in our discussion of simplicity above" (1999, p. 29). Chalmers's case against modal dualism has a simplicity strand too: one modal primitive rather than two, and grounding metaphysical in logical modality "yields a simple explanation, and a simple epistemology" (2010, §10). [[tenets#^occams-limits|Tenet 5]] discounts both.
3. **The epistemology of modality.** The question of one world or 37 concerns access, not economy, so it survives Tenet 5. It is a priori, though, so it bears on plausibility rather than tier, and the joint paper answers it twice: Papineau grounds modality in counterfactual thinking, and Goff's "stress-free modal dualism" keeps conceivability as the guide while admitting strong necessities, which turns the question into whether phenomenal concepts are transparent.

Block and Stalnaker's appeal to the "transfer of explanatory and causal force" has two halves. The explanatory half is the no-explanation reply, answered [[#Ontological and Epistemic Primitiveness|above]]; the causal half rests on the closure of the physical. [[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Tenet 2]] denies closure at quantum indeterminacy ([[causal-closure]]), which removes a further defeater and adds no evidence.

None of this lifts the Map's case to *suggestive*. Tenet 5 disables the parsimony tiebreak that would otherwise favour the identity reading, which leaves a tie on the explanation side, and nothing empirical has entered. The cost argument is an input to the second-order comparison of explanations that [[type-a-type-b-and-type-c-physicalism]] lists as not yet run: it supplies one criterion on dualism's side, and the predicted-uniqueness reply is Type-B's answer to it. That comparison has still to be run.

The cost does not show:

- **That the identity is false.** Chalmers grants that the position "remains at least formally open".
- **Dualism rather than type-F.** Ruling out primitive identities and strong necessities yields "a disjunction" (Chalmers 2003) of type-D dualism, type-E epiphenomenalism and type-F [[russellian-monism]], and type-F can be read as a kind of materialism.
- **Anything specific to interactionism.** The interactionist's [[psychophysical-laws|psychophysical law]] is just as epistemically primitive, and [[parsimony-case-for-interactionist-dualism]] concedes it as "a genuine explanatory cost". Interactionism also needs selection laws running from experience to physical outcome, a second primitive bridge that Type-B and type-E do without, so on the cost argument's own ledger it carries more than either. The cost argument favours D, E and F alike.
- **Independent support for dualism.** Because denying strong necessities is equivalent to CP-, this half of the cost belongs to the conceivability cluster that [[positions/arguments-for-dualism#^p-d1|P-D1]] treats as premise-sharing. The identity half reaches the same dispute through the law-mark premise, a second route to one disputed point that adds no second piece of evidence.
- **That identities need explaining in the ontological sense.** Chalmers and Jackson grant that they do not.
- **Anything empirical.**

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]].** Against Type-B, Tenet 1 stands as a commitment rather than a result. On Chalmers and Jackson's reading, Type-B pays property dualism's explanatory price while keeping physicalism's name; whether the name is wrong turns on whether the bridge is a law or an identity, which the phenomenal-concepts dispute and the epistemology of modality must decide.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]].** Tenet 5 cuts both ways. It discounts Block and Stalnaker's simplicity case for the identity and, by the same rule, the simplicity strand of Chalmers's case against modal dualism. Of that case, only the argument from the epistemology of modality survives the tenet; it is a priori, and Papineau and Goff each answer it.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]] and [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]].** The cost argument gives these tenets no specific support: the dualist's psychophysical law is as primitive as Type-B's identity, and the selection laws Tenet 3 requires are a further primitive that Type-B does not need. Their contribution is defensive: denying closure answers the causal half of Block and Stalnaker's case for the identity.
