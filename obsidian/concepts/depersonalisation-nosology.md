Nosology asks where a disorder belongs in a classification, and for depersonalisation the manuals have never agreed. ICD-10 (1992) files *depersonalization-derealization syndrome* as F48.1 under "Other neurotic disorders". DSM-5 (2013), like DSM-III-R and DSM-IV before it, lists *depersonalization/derealization disorder* among the dissociative disorders, and ICD-11 (6B66; clinical text 2024) places it there too. A cognitive-behavioural model from the Maudsley group (Hunter, Phillips, Chalder, Sierra & David 2003) reads the condition as closer to the anxiety disorders, and the same group's later data (Sierra, Medford, Wyatt & David 2012), with German work (Michal et al. 2009, 2016), press a third option: an independent condition. This page sets out the three placements ([[#What the Manuals Say|explained below]]), the anxiety-spectrum model and its in-house qualification ([[#The Anxiety-Spectrum Model and Its In-House Qualification|below]]) and the substance and medical exclusions ([[#The Substance and Medical Exclusions|below]]) without adjudicating between them. One criterion survives every placement — retained insight — and it is the bridge to the Map's home page for the datum, [[depersonalisation]], which sets an affective reading of what comes apart against the reading it adopts; this debate shows that rival to be one party to a clinical dispute rather than its consensus ([[#Bearing on the Affective Reading|explained below]]).

## What the Manuals Say

### ICD-10 (1992): F48.1 under "Other neurotic disorders"

The Blue Book places the syndrome in block F48, apart from the F44 dissociative [conversion] disorders, and records that "The complaint of loss of emotions is the most frequent among these varied phenomena" (WHO 1992, pp. 171–172). The guidelines require depersonalisation or derealisation symptoms, or both, plus "(c) an acceptance that this is a subjective and spontaneous change, not imposed by outside forces or other people (i.e. insight)" and "(d) a clear sensorium and absence of toxic confusional state or epilepsy". The differential diagnosis separates the syndrome from schizophrenia, early dementia and "dissociative disorders (where awareness of change is lacking)". ICD-10 has no distress-or-impairment criterion and no substance exclusion beyond (d).

### ICD-11 (CDDR, 2024): 6B66 among the dissociative disorders

The *Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements* (WHO 2024, pp. 391–394) file 6B66 in the dissociative grouping with five essential features: persistent or recurrent depersonalisation or derealisation; that "reality testing remains intact"; that the symptoms are not better accounted for by another disorder, an anxiety or fear-related disorder among those named; that they "are not due to the effects of a substance or medication on the central nervous system (including withdrawal effects)" nor to neurological disease, head trauma or another medical condition; and that they cause significant distress or impairment. It also records anxiety-camp observations: "Catastrophic cognitions (e.g. frequent fears of “going crazy”)" appear among the additional features, panic attacks are a named boundary, and for childhood interpersonal trauma "the association is not as strong as for other dissociative disorders".

### DSM-5 (2013): Criteria A–E

The Map has not read the DSM-5 or DSM-5-TR text; what follows is as transcribed by secondary sources. Criterion A gives persistent or recurrent depersonalisation or derealisation; B requires intact reality testing; C requires clinically significant distress or impairment; D excludes the physiological effects of a substance or another medical condition; E excludes better explanation by another disorder. The substance exclusion is therefore Criterion D, not Criterion C as sometimes stated. DSM-5-TR (2022) is reported by secondary sources to carry the criteria unchanged.

One requirement is constant across all three: the patient knows the change is subjective. ICD-10 calls it insight, DSM-5 and ICD-11 call it intact reality testing, and it is this criterion that Medford, Sierra, Baker & David (2005) turned against the dissociative placement.

## The Anxiety-Spectrum Model and Its In-House Qualification

Hunter, Phillips, Chalder, Sierra & David (2003) proposed a cognitive-behavioural conceptualisation whose abstract claims "compelling evidence to link DPD with the anxiety disorders, particularly panic", and whose central proposal is "that it is the catastrophic appraisal of the normally transient symptoms of DP/DR that results in the development of a chronic disorder". If symptoms are "misinterpreted as indicative of severe mental illness or brain dysfunction, a vicious cycle of increasing anxiety and consequently increased DP/DR symptoms will result", maintained by "specific avoidances, 'safety behaviours' and cognitive biases". The full text was not reachable; the model is quoted from its abstract alone, and a widely circulated sentence about symptoms misread as signs of deteriorating mental health is a secondary summary's wording, not the paper's.

Medford, Sierra, Baker & David (2005), reviewing the model, report that it rests on the idea "that anxiety and depersonalisation are intimately related, and that depersonalisation is best conceptualised as related to anxiety disorders rather than to dissociative conditions" — their gloss, not a Hunter 2003 quotation. The same review states the nosological complaint: ICD-10 and DSM-IV "betray uncertainty as to the nosological status of depersonalisation", the dissociative listing being "an equally problematic classification, as the hallmark of true dissociation is a lack of subjective awareness of change", whereas "sufferers from depersonalisation are all too aware of a disturbing change". In the unit's 204-case series "Depersonalisation symptom scores correlated with both anxiety and depression" while "'Dissociative amnesia' was not prominent" (Baker et al. 2003), and in an open treatment study of 21 patients "29% of participants no longer met criteria for DPD" after cognitive-behavioural therapy (Hunter, Baker, Phillips, Sierra & David 2005).

The qualification came from the same unit. Sierra, Medford, Wyatt & David (2012), in 291 cases, found a low but significant anxiety–depersonalisation association "only apparent in those patients with low intensity depersonalization", a "'statistical dissociation'" which "adds further evidence in favour of depersonalization disorder being an independent condition and suggests that its association with anxiety has been overemphasized". Michal and colleagues concur from outside it: in the German general population depersonalisation "can not be reduced to a negligible variant of depression or anxiety" (Michal et al. 2009), and in a 223-patient ICD-10 F48.1 series, despite comparable depression and anxiety burden, "the clinical picture and course of both patient groups differed strongly" (Michal et al. 2016).

The debate is three-cornered, and one research group occupies two of its corners: Sierra, Medford and David are authors on both the 2003–2005 papers and the 2012 qualification.

## The Substance and Medical Exclusions

The CDDR states that "Psychoactive substance use, especially of marijuana or hallucinogens, is a common precipitant of depersonalization and derealization symptoms", but that the diagnosis "can only be assigned if the symptoms persist beyond the period of intoxication or withdrawal" (WHO 2024, p. 393). Intoxication-bound depersonalisation is excluded; drug-precipitated chronic depersonalisation is admitted. Among 164 chronic cases, 40 "related the onset of symptoms to an episode of illicit drug use"; once matched for age and sex "differences between groups largely disappeared", and the authors conclude that "Drug-induced DP does not appear to represent a distinct clinical syndrome" (Medford et al. 2003). Hürlimann, Kupferschmid & Simon (2012) describe six cases of "persistent depersonalization disorder in adolescence after consuming cannabis", an association they call "widely neglected". The manuals the Map has read name cannabis and hallucinogens; ketamine appears as a precipitant only in secondary clinical references.

On the medical side, Lambert, Sierra, Phillips & David (2002), reviewing 47 published and four new cases, found that "Epilepsy and migraine appear to be the disorders most commonly associated with depersonalization", noted that their "secondary status precludes a DSM-IV diagnosis", and proposed "an organic subtype". ICD-10 flags the "preictal aura of temporal lobe epilepsy".

## Prevalence and Responsivity: The 2023–2024 Update

Yang, Millman, David & Hunter (2023), from 23 papers, report prevalence "from 0% to 1.9% amongst the general population, 5-20% in outpatients and 17.5-41.9% in inpatients", and conclude that the rate "is around 1% in the general population, consistent with previous findings" — an update of, not a correction to, the "in the region of 1-2%" of Hunter, Sierra & David (2004). Their anxiety-sample figures are comorbidity rates, not trigger rates. Millman, Huang, Wainipitapong, Medford & Pick (2024) found "Some evidence for diminished subjective responsivity to aversive images and sounds, and hyperactivation in neurocircuits associated with emotional regulation when viewing aversive images emerged, corroborating neurobiological models of DDD", alongside inconsistent results elsewhere, and "Common confounds included small sample sizes, medication, and comorbidities".

## Bearing on the Affective Reading

The hub page sets out [[depersonalisation#The Affective Reading|an affective reading]] as the neurobiological rival to the reading the Map adopts from Billon: on the accounts it cites, what goes missing is emotional colouring, and no for-me-ness component is posited. Three things bear on that rival, not all one way. ICD-10's "loss of emotions is the most frequent" sentence is primary-text support that the *presenting* subtraction is affective, and it presses on the Map's reading rather than on the rival. The Hunter model supplies the suppression story with an entry route and a maintenance loop — anxiety and catastrophic appraisal carry a transient subtraction into a chronic one — which is how the anxiety-spectrum placement and the affective reading fit together. The 2012 result cuts against both: if the anxiety association holds only at low symptom intensity, anxiety does not scale with the subtraction in the severe cases, which are where the hub's architecture-tier interest lies. The honest summary is that the anxiety-spectrum account explains onset and chronicity better than it explains the severe, anxiety-decoupled core, and that the affective reading is one party to this dispute rather than its settled outcome — which leaves the rival's descriptive claim standing and the Map's reading no better evidenced than before.

What the nosology does not touch is the hub's first structural fact, and two predicates differ here: "dissociative" in a chapter heading records a classificatory kinship with amnesia and identity disorder, whereas the hub's claim that something *comes apart* within experience describes the episode itself. Insight and much ordinary perception, memory and action persist under every placement, and the hub's architecture claim holds whether the syndrome is filed as neurotic, dissociative or independent. The experimental sense-of-agency literature of 2023–2024, which bears on the one-way ownership/agency separation, is a separate question and belongs with [[phenomenology-of-agency-vs-passivity]].

The architecture tier is indifferent to the chapter heading. At the significance tier the result stays null. The anxiety-spectrum model, the trauma framing behind the DSM-5 placement (Spiegel et al. 2011, who cite "a growing body of evidence linking the dissociative disorders to a trauma history, and to specific neural mechanisms") and the independent-condition reading are all physicalist clinical frames; none discriminates Tenet 1 from its rivals, and none of their authors — Hunter, Medford, Sierra, David, Michal, Yang — is enlisted here for dualism. Drug-precipitated cases are likewise compatible with the interface reading and with every rival; compatible evidence is not support. Nothing here concedes epiphenomenalism: a chapter heading says where a syndrome is filed, not whether experience does causal work.
