**Nondualistic interactionism** is David Ray Griffin's name for the mind–body doctrine of his Whiteheadian panexperientialism: the mind is an actuality numerically distinct from the brain and interacts with it in both directions, but it is not different in ontological kind from the neurons composing the brain, because those neurons are themselves low-grade subjects of experience (Griffin 1998). It is the closest non-dualist rival the Map has: it treats consciousness as irreducible and causally efficacious, denies that physics gives a complete account of bodily motion, and has two numerically distinct relata, so every test the positions register names for selecting the Map's dualism from among the irreducibility-respecting views lets it through ([explained below](#relation-to-site-perspective)). The Map parts company with it on the claim that mind differs from matter *in kind* rather than in degree, which the [[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]] tenet's stated neutrality does not supply on its own; on the [[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]] tenet's small, quantum-located, conservation-respecting interface, which Griffin neither needs nor accepts; and on the Map's commitment to a persisting subject not simply generated by the brain. The view is not refuted here. The disagreement sits at the kind axis and at Tenet 2, and the Map's strongest objection, the composition problem, is one its own account of the subject must also answer.

## The Two Theses

Griffin's definition splits what "dualism" usually bundles. Dualism, he writes, "contains a double thesis: (1) that the mind is an actuality numerically distinct from the brain (the quantitative or numerical thesis) and (2) that it is ontologically different in kind from the entities of which the brain consists (the qualitative or ontological thesis)" (Griffin 1998, p. 47). Cartesian dualism affirms both; materialism denies both; nondualistic interactionism affirms the numerical thesis and denies the ontological one. Treating "interactionism" as a synonym for dualistic interactionism, he complains, hides this option, since such usages "imply that there could not be a nondualistic interactionism" (p. 48).

The Map's tenet text makes the move Griffin objects to. The Dualism tenet says that irreducibility, "combined with the Bidirectional Interaction tenet below", commits the Map to [[interactionist-dualism]], "the position that mind and body are distinct yet causally connected" ([[tenets]]). Griffin's view is distinct-yet-causally-connected without any difference in kind. It is a worked counterexample to the inference from "distinct and interacting" to "different in kind", so the kind claim has to be argued separately ([explained below](#relation-to-site-perspective)).

The distinctness Griffin affirms is thinner than Descartes's: the mind is not "numerically one through time", a picture that "exaggerates its distinctness from the brain and the rest of the world" (p. 172), but a serially ordered society of momentary occasions, numerically distinct from "the highest-level enduring individuals (the neurons) constituting the brain as such" (p. 172), a further actual entity presiding over them rather than a soul-substance apart from them.

## Compound Individuals and Dominant Occasions

The mechanism that makes a distinct-but-same-kind mind possible is a distinction between two sorts of society of occasions. Griffin takes the term from Hartshorne, "which Hartshorne calls a 'compound individual'" (p. 82).

A **compound individual** is a society of experiencing members with a dominant member whose experience unifies the whole's response and action: "These higher-level experiences can be called presiding, regnant, or dominant, because they exercise greater power over the society as a whole than do any of the other members" (p. 186). Animals with central nervous systems are "the most obvious examples" (p. 186); cells are others, by hypothesis. An **aggregational society** has no such member: sticks and stones are "aggregational societies devoid of a dominant member" (p. 181), and the small spontaneities of their constituents average out under the law of large numbers, which is why rocks behave as if nothing in them experienced.

The human mind is the dominant member of the human compound individual: "Out of the activities of billions of neurons arises that temporal society of dominant occasions of experience that is the mind or soul of the animal" (p. 186). Each neuron is itself a compound individual; the mind is a higher-level society presiding over them. "There is, to be sure, no ontological dualism. But there is an organizational duality" (p. 188). What is new at the human level is not a new kind of stuff, nor strictly a new kind of causal relation, but "more precisely, an extreme exemplification of a kind of causal relation that had become increasingly important in the evolution of the higher animals" (p. 193); "The difference is, nevertheless, one of degree, not of kind" (p. 241).

Interaction runs through [[prehension]], the Whiteheadian relation by which one occasion takes up aspects of another. The mind's occasions prehend the neuronal occasions, which is how the brain influences the mind; neuronal occasions prehend the mind's appetitions, which is how the mind influences the brain, "just as our brain cells derive guidance from the mind by prehending its appetitions" (p. 205). The mind's [[subjective-aim|subjective aim]] is partly self-determined in each moment, and because a dominant member is not washed out by the law of large numbers, compound individuals, "especially humans and other higher animals" (p. 188), are where that self-determination makes a difference to what a body does.

## Entity-Domain Closure Kept, Microphysical Sufficiency Denied

Griffin's final chapter re-labels the position "panexperientialist physicalism", resting the label on an enlarged sense of "physical": its physical entities are "physical-mental entities" (p. 237). Four theses need separating. He keeps closure over entities, which now include minds: "The closure of the system of physical causes to influence from nonphysical causes does not, accordingly, exclude human minds from the universal causal nexus" (p. 237). He rejects the materialist reading, on which "all causal efficacy must be exerted by the level of physical entities studied by physics" (p. 236), and with it microphysical sufficiency, since the mind acts downward on the body. His physicalism also "rejects Kim's contrary principle" (p. 237), on which a cause with physical and mental aspects acts only by virtue of the physical one. Completeness is "yet another divergence" (p. 237): "The idea that physics by itself could predict, or even causally explain, all the movements of living human bodies is a pure pipe dream" (p. 238). [[causal-exclusion-argument|Kim's]] own verdict (1999) is that panexperientialism is "inconsistent with each of the three doctrines (understood in their proper intended sense)" central to physicalism, [[causal-closure|closure]] among them. Griffin keeps closure only in his enlarged sense.

Two published responses mark the pressure points. Jaegwon Kim (1999) rejects the label, "I am inclined to think it misleading to regard panexperientialism as any form of physicalism", and finds Griffin's foundational commitments reminiscent of "a metaphysical wish list". More substantively, Kim asks for a composition principle: "an independently motivated and defended principle" that says how a compound individual made of experiencing members in a given structural configuration comes to have mentality of a specific kind. Without one, the dominant occasion names the thing to be explained.

David Chalmers (2003) lists Griffin 1998 among the "Versions of type-F monism", the Russellian family. In the same section, discussing type-F monists who hope to avoid the [[combination-problem]] by placing phenomenal properties at a high level, Chalmers writes that the only way to hold that position "would seem to be in combination with a denial of microphysical causal closure, holding that there are fundamental dispositions above the microphysical level, which have phenomenal properties as their grounds", and that "such a view would be indistinguishable from type-D dualism". Chalmers does not say this of Griffin. The Map's reading is that it applies, by Chalmers's own criterion. His footnote to the passage says "The main difference between the type-D view and the type-F view is that the type-D view involves fundamental causation above the microphysical level", and type-D, on his definition, "is also compatible with a property dualism, on which there is just one sort of substance or entity". Griffin's dominant occasion is a high-level subject with its own causal relation, and the microphysical sufficiency he rejects is the closure type-D denies. The footnote's further remark, that such a view "might have the advantage of avoiding the combination problem", concerns type-D views in general; on the Map's reading Griffin's version gains it only in part, since the problem returns as Kim's composition demand. By Chalmers's causal-structural criterion the interactionist variant sits with type-D; by Griffin's entity-kind criterion it is not dualism. The disagreement is over whether causal structure or entity kind settles the label, and a type-D classification would not by itself supply the Map's claim of a difference in kind.

## Where It Sits Among Neighbours

| View | Mind numerically distinct | Different in kind | Interaction | Microphysics causally closed? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Map's [[interactionist-dualism]] | yes | yes (a further claim, see below) | two-way, quantum-located | no |
| **Nondualistic interactionism** | **yes** | **no** (degree only) | **two-way, by prehension** | **no** |
| [[emergent-dualism]] (Hasker) | yes (emergent individual) | yes | two-way | no |
| Type-F / [[russellian-monism]] | not as such | no | none distinct from the physical | yes |
| Analytic [[idealism]] | no physical relatum | n/a | intra-mental | n/a |

Against Cartesian dualism, Griffin keeps distinctness and two-way causation but removes heterogeneity, so the "how can unlike act on unlike" half of the [[pairing-problem|interaction problem]] lapses: like acts on like, by prehension. Against dual-aspect views he asks "why is a two-aspect view of the relation of mind to brain not adequate?" (p. 172) and answers from unity and agency: the mind is a further entity, not the brain known from within. Against [[emergent-dualism]], both views have the mind emerge from neural activity and act downward; Hasker's emergent self differs in kind and Griffin's does not. On the dependence-versus-independence axis the Map uses against Hasker, Griffin sits with Hasker on origin, since the dominant occasions arise out of neuronal activity, though his book on parapsychology treats the nondualistic mind–brain distinction as making life after death conceivable (Griffin 1997).

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Map interprets nondualistic interactionism as the rival that makes visible what each tenet does and does not deliver.

**Dualism (Tenet 1).** As worded, the tenet asks for irreducibility and is "neutral between substance and property dualism". Griffin satisfies that, since on his view physics captures only the outer pole of occasions. The difference in kind is the Map's further claim. Tenet 1 alone does not say "in kind", and Griffin shows that pairing it with Tenet 3 does not supply the claim either. The Map argues the kind claim elsewhere: through its refusal of [[panpsychism]] on [[panpsychisms-combination-problem|combination-problem]] grounds, and through the persisting-subject and independence-with-coupling commitments recorded in [[where-the-substance-commitment-enters]]. Those premises belong to the agency cluster, which Tenet 1 states it does not entail.

**The positions register.** [[positions/arguments-for-dualism|P-D2]] says convergence earns irreducibility and Bidirectional Interaction then "selects dualism" from the irreducibility-respecting rivals, the selection needing only the existential claim that theorising consciousness is efficacious. [[positions/arguments-for-mental-causation|P-MC3]] supplies the exclusion criterion: a theory escapes epiphenomenalism only by denying that physical events always have sufficient physical causes and naming the point where consciousness makes a difference. Griffin's consciousness is not inert; he denies completeness; and he names the difference-making point, the dominant occasion's partly self-determined appetitions, prehended by neurons. The two-relata argument that excludes idealism does not reach him either, since neurons are physical in every sense physics uses. So, on the register's own terms, Tenet 3 selects *interactionism*, and this view is inside the selection. P-MC3's remark that panpsychism "inherits the problem at the macro level through the combination problem" describes identity panpsychism, not this variant, where the combination problem returns as Kim's composition demand rather than as inertness. The Map speculates that the clean repair is to read "selects dualism" as "selects interactionism", with the step to dualism-in-kind resting on the premises above; the register's counting rule, that the two earnings do not compound, is untouched.

**Minimal Quantum Interaction (Tenet 2).** The dominant occasion's influence is neither minimal nor quantum-located, and where Tenet 2's rules-out clause forbids conservation-law violation outright, Griffin discounts the law. The conservation objection "has been greatly exaggerated" (p. 51); "we have more reason to be confident of our belief in freedom than we do of the absolute and universal truth of the conservation laws" (p. 214); and if energy must be conserved, the concept can be enlarged to include the mind's own, "the latest in a long string of enlargements that have been necessary to preserve the principle of conservation" (p. 155). Quantum indeterminacy appears in the book chiefly as room for a mental pole in elementary particles. The mind's reach into the body's particles gets one footnote, which borrows Walter Elsasser's point that quantum laws "do not uniquely determine the happenings on the microscopic level" to show that the reach breaks no physical law (p. 191n); no mechanism is given. The Map's path through [[conservation-laws-and-mental-causation]] keeps the law and routes influence through outcome-selection within the Born distribution. A Whiteheadian could graft a quantum locus onto Griffin's view, as [[subjective-aim]] and [[process-philosophy]] record Stapp attempting, contestedly, for Whitehead; Griffin goes no further than that footnote, so the two frameworks differ in risk tolerance rather than in logical compatibility.

**Bidirectional Interaction (Tenet 3).** Satisfied on the headline and on both the existential and universal readings. The tenet's definition goes further, to a mechanism of outcome-selection, which Griffin meets only on the graft above; that clause is mechanism content graded through the quantum-interface register, not part of what Tenet 3 argues for.

**No Many Worlds (Tenet 4).** Compatible and unaddressed: concrescence actualises one outcome per occasion, and Griffin's libertarian freedom presupposes real alternatives with one realised, so the view is a natural single-world ally.

**Occam's Razor Has Limits (Tenet 5).** The tenet cuts both ways. Griffin's second charge against dualism, after the interaction of unlike things, is that it "violates the principle of continuity in a particularly egregious way" (p. 51), a uniformity heuristic of the kind Tenet 5 treats as defeasible while knowledge is incomplete. Symmetrically, the Map cannot call panexperientialism unparsimonious, and Kim's "wish list" charge applies with equal force to the Map's own posits. Griffin's appeal to hard-core common-sense notions as non-negotiable is close kin to the Map's [[self-stultification]] reasoning.

**What the Map should not claim.** The composition demand is the Map's best line against this view, and it is not a refutation: the Map's own account of how neural activity comes to host or couple to a single subject faces a structurally related question, as [[emergent-dualism]] records in its Rickabaugh discussion. Griffin's treatment of parapsychological data (ch. 5) is outside this page's scope and is not imported as support for either side.
