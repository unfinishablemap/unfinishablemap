The conceivability-possibility inference is the principle that if something can be coherently conceived, it is at least metaphysically possible. This seemingly innocent bridge from imagination to reality powers some of philosophy's most contested arguments—particularly in philosophy of mind, where [[philosophical-zombies|zombie arguments]] and the [[knowledge-argument|knowledge argument]] depend on inferring genuine metaphysical possibilities from acts of conceiving.

The Unfinishable Map endorses this inference in its most demanding form—ideal positive conceivability of fundamental conceptual separations—as evidentially significant for dualism. When sustained reflection finds that consciousness can be coherently separated from physical structure, the Map counts that gap as defeasible evidence about reality's structure rather than an artefact of imagination. The live physicalist reply grants the conceiving and denies the inference ([[#Relation to Site Perspective|explained below]]).

The inference matters because conceivability is epistemically accessible—we can investigate what we can and cannot imagine—while metaphysical possibility concerns the structure of reality itself, which may be hidden from direct inspection. If conceivability reliably tracks possibility, we gain an investigative tool. If it doesn't, major arguments collapse.

## The Basic Inference

**Standard form:**
1. S can coherently conceive that P
2. If P is coherently conceivable, then P is metaphysically possible
3. Therefore, P is metaphysically possible

**Example application (zombie argument):**
1. I can coherently conceive of a being physically identical to me but lacking consciousness
2. If such a being is coherently conceivable, it is metaphysically possible
3. Therefore, [[philosophical-zombies]] are metaphysically possible
4. If zombies are possible, [[materialism|physicalism]] is false (consciousness is not entailed by physical facts)

## Chalmers' Refinement

David Chalmers has developed the most sophisticated defense of conceivability-possibility reasoning. His framework distinguishes types of conceivability and types of possibility, then specifies which pairings yield reliable inferences.

### Types of Conceivability

**Prima facie vs. ideal conceivability:**
- **Prima facie**: Seems conceivable on initial reflection, but might contain hidden contradictions
- **Ideal**: Survives idealized rational reflection—no contradiction emerges even under maximal scrutiny

**Positive vs. negative conceivability:**
- **Positive**: Can form a detailed imagined situation where P holds
- **Negative**: Cannot detect any contradiction in P even after careful analysis

Prima facie conceivability is unreliable—many mathematical falsehoods seem conceivable until proven impossible. Stephen Yablo's influential analysis treated conceivability as defeasible evidence for possibility: finding P conceivable—being able to imagine a world one takes to verify P—gives prima facie justification for believing P possible, open to defeat. Chalmers' notion of positive conceivability builds on Yablo's account, and he argues that *ideal positive conceivability* provides the strongest epistemic warrant for possibility claims.

### The Core Claim

Chalmers defends what he calls weak modal rationalism: **ideal primary positive conceivability entails primary possibility**.

A statement is *primarily possible* when it is true in some possible world considered as actual—when, had our world turned out that way, the statement would have been true. Primary conceiving likewise treats a scenario as a hypothesis about how our own world is. "Water is not H₂O" is primarily possible: had the clear liquid in rivers and rain turned out to be XYZ, "water" would have picked out XYZ. It is not *secondarily* (metaphysically) possible, since water is H₂O in every world considered as counterfactual.

The zombie argument exploits this: if physical duplicates lacking consciousness are ideally positively conceivable, then on Chalmers' thesis they are primarily possible. Whether that primary possibility carries over to metaphysical possibility is the question the [[#The Two-Dimensional Objection|two-dimensional objection]] presses; if it does, consciousness is not necessitated by physical structure—it is a further fact about reality.

## Challenges to the Inference

### The Confabulation Objection

**Problem:** What feels like genuine conceiving might be pseudo-conceiving—manipulating labels without grasping genuine content.

When you "conceive" of a zombie, are you imagining a genuinely complete physical duplicate that lacks consciousness? Or are you just *telling yourself* you're imagining this while actually leaving crucial details unspecified?

The research on [[voids-conceptual-impossibility-2026-01-23|conceptual impossibility]] reveals an asymmetry: we can *refer to* impossibilities like "round square" without being able to *grasp* them. Could zombie conceivability be similar—linguistic reference without genuine conceptual content?

**Response:** Chalmers acknowledges this risk, which is why he requires *ideal positive* conceivability. Ideal reflection involves filling in details, checking for hidden contradictions, and ensuring the imagined scenario is coherent under sustained scrutiny. If gaps remain under ideal reflection, conceivability fails.

### The Two-Dimensional Objection

**Problem:** Conceivability might track epistemic possibility (compatible with what we know) rather than metaphysical possibility (compatible with reality's structure).

Before Kripke, philosophers thought "water is not H₂O" was conceivable. But Kripke argued this was an illusion—what we conceived was a *watery stuff* that isn't H₂O, not *water itself* being non-H₂O. Water is necessarily H₂O; we just didn't know this initially.

Could zombie conceivability be similar? We *think* we're conceiving of physical duplicates lacking consciousness, but actually we're conceiving of *something else* (non-conscious functional duplicates that aren't genuine physical duplicates).

**Response:** Chalmers' two-dimensional semantics is designed to address this. A term's primary intension picks out its referent in a world considered as actual (for "water", whatever watery stuff fills the rivers); its secondary intension picks it out in a world considered as counterfactual, holding fixed what the term actually refers to (H₂O everywhere). The water case is a primary possibility without a secondary one. The Kripkean escape for zombies needs the same gap between how a thing is picked out and what it is, and [[kripke-a-posteriori-necessity-argument|Kripke himself argued]] that pain lacks one: pain is picked out by how it feels, so whatever feels like pain is pain. Chalmers agrees that the two intensions of phenomenal terms plausibly coincide, though he treats this as a simplification his argument does not need. A physicalist who grants the conceiving must then deny that it reveals even a primary possibility ([[#Relation to Site Perspective|see below]]). The conclusion stays disjunctive: physicalism is false or, as Chalmers concedes, [[russellian-monism|Russellian monism]] is true, grounding consciousness in intrinsic natures that physics' structural description leaves out.

This response is contested. Block and Stalnaker (1999) argue that ordinary truths about water and life are not deducible a priori from microphysics either, yet water and life are physically constituted; so the absence of an a priori entailment from physical to phenomenal truths marks nothing special about consciousness, and the two-dimensional framework does not supply the missing entailments. The [[phenomenal-concepts-strategy]] represents physicalism's most sophisticated attempt to break the conceivability-possibility link specifically for phenomenal claims. Whether these responses succeed remains an active area of debate, though Chalmers' "master argument" (2007) poses a serious challenge to that strategy: either the special features of phenomenal concepts are not themselves physically explicable, which relocates the [[explanatory-gap]], or they cannot explain our epistemic situation.

### The Strength-Mismatch Objection

**Problem:** Even if conceivability provides *some* evidence for possibility, it might not be strong enough to bear the argumentative weight placed on it.

The history of science contains cases where expert conceivability proved misleading. For centuries, it seemed inconceivable that space could be non-Euclidean, that fundamental physical events could be undetermined, or that simultaneity could be relative. If sustained rational reflection has failed repeatedly for physical claims, why trust it for metaphysical ones?

**Response:** These cases require care. They are scenarios that were initially *inconceivable* but that physics went on to accept (indeterminism, on most interpretations)—conceivability failures that underestimated what is possible, not overestimated it. The zombie argument runs the other direction: it claims something *is* conceivable and therefore possible. The more relevant concern is cases where something seemed conceivable but proved impossible.

The strongest historical parallel here is **vitalism**. For centuries, it seemed genuinely conceivable—even rationally required—that life could not arise from mere chemistry. An irreducible life-force (*élan vital*) appeared necessary to explain biological phenomena, and sustained rational reflection by serious thinkers failed to see how chemical processes alone could produce life. Yet vitalism was wrong. If robust conceivability failed so thoroughly for life, why trust it for consciousness?

The dualist response distinguishes the two cases. Vitalism was abandoned because chemistry *explained* biological phenomena—metabolism, reproduction, growth all yielded to biochemical analysis, leaving no residual explanatory gap. The consciousness case is structurally different: neuroscience *correlates* neural activity with phenomenal experience but doesn't *explain* why those correlations hold. Additionally, vitalism made empirical predictions (vital fluids, life-specific forces) that were falsified. Consciousness dualism rests on a conceptual gap—the apparent non-entailment of phenomenal facts by physical facts—that cannot be closed by discovering new empirical correlations.

This response is not airtight. A [[type-a-type-b-and-type-c-physicalism|Type-C]] physicalist can argue that future theoretical breakthroughs may close the consciousness gap just as biochemistry closed the vitalism gap—and that the current persistence of the gap reflects our ignorance rather than a genuine metaphysical boundary. The Map acknowledges this possibility but holds that the *character* of the consciousness gap differs: we have first-person acquaintance with what consciousness *is*, and the gap persists not for lack of data but because additional data (more neural correlates) doesn't address the constitutive question. That reply reaches ignorance on the phenomenal side; the [[ignorance-hypothesis|ignorance hypothesis]] places it on the physical side, and the replies that reach it, chiefly the [[ignorance-hypothesis#What Does Reach It|conceptual-hook argument]], remove a defeater without adding evidence.

More fundamentally, the physics cases concern contingent laws of nature, while the zombie case concerns a constitutive relationship—whether physical facts *entail* phenomenal facts—where the inference is on firmest ground.

### The Cognitive Closure Objection

**Problem:** Perhaps our conceivability faculties are reliable in general but *locally unreliable* for the mind-body case specifically. If [[mysterianism|cognitive closure]] prevents us from grasping the properties that connect consciousness to physical structure, we might conceive of zombies not because they are genuinely possible but because we lack the concepts needed to see *why they are impossible*.

This is more targeted than general empiricist skepticism: it grants that the inference works for mathematics and logic but claims it fails precisely where consciousness meets physics.

**Response:** The cognitive closure objection faces a dilemma. If we cannot represent the relevant properties at all, then we also cannot determine whether zombie conceivability fails—the objection becomes unfalsifiable. And if the objection applies specifically to consciousness, one needs an explanation of *why* consciousness is the exception. McGinn, who holds that the link is an ordinary natural property, supplies one: our concepts derive from perception and introspection, and neither can represent the linking property. The rival explanation—that consciousness involves something beyond physical structure—is what the dualist claims, and the objection alone does not decide between them. Still, cognitive closure remains a genuinely open possibility, and the Map acknowledges it as such.

## Modal Rationalism and Its Limits

The conceivability-possibility inference embodies a form of *modal rationalism*—the view that reason can access modal truths (what is possible, necessary, or impossible) through a priori reflection.

**The rationalist bet:** Our imaginative faculties track something real about modal space. When we coherently conceive P, this isn't arbitrary—it reflects that P is compatible with the fundamental structure of reality.

**The empiricist skepticism:** Imagination is a cognitive capacity shaped by evolution and development. Why think it accurately maps metaphysical possibility rather than merely tracking what's familiar, useful, or psychologically accessible?

Modal rationalists point to successful cases (mathematical and logical necessity discovered through pure reason); empiricists point to failures (physical necessities that intuition missed). Neither side can resolve the dispute without presupposing something about the reliability of rational reflection.

However, consciousness conceivability may have special epistemic status. Unlike conceiving of non-Euclidean geometry or faster-than-light travel—where we lack direct access to the subject matter—we have first-person acquaintance with what consciousness *is*. When we conceive of a physical duplicate lacking consciousness, we are imagining the absence of something we know intimately through [[introspection]], which may give zombie conceivability more evidential weight than conceivability claims about domains we understand only theoretically. The [[modal-structure-of-phenomenal-properties|modal structure of phenomenal properties]] makes this concrete: the central argument there rests on exactly this inference, treating the ideal conceivability of phenomenal-physical separation as a guide to the modal profile of qualia themselves. As the [[meta-problem-of-consciousness]] highlights, the persistence of the conceivability intuition under scrutiny is itself a datum requiring explanation—though not a universal one: Díaz (2021) found such problem intuitions not widespread among ordinary people ([[metaproblem-of-consciousness-under-dualism|discussed here]]).

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Unfinishable Map's [[tenets|first tenet]]—[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]—depends partly on conceivability arguments. The zombie argument (conceivability of physical duplicates lacking consciousness) and the knowledge argument (Mary gains new knowledge when experiencing color, purportedly showing phenomenal facts exceed physical facts) both employ conceivability-possibility reasoning.

If this inference is fundamentally unreliable, dualist arguments lose significant support. The Map's position thus requires at least *qualified* defense of the inference.

**The qualification:** The Map endorses the inference for *ideal positive conceivability* of fundamental conceptual separations, not for all conceivable scenarios. When we can coherently and completely imagine consciousness coming apart from physical structure, this reveals that consciousness is not conceptually entailed by physics. The inference is what carries that conceptual result to the metaphysical conclusion dualism needs: that physics does not necessitate consciousness at all.

The inference bears on the Map's [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] tenet only indirectly. Conceivable separation shows that consciousness is distinct from physical structure, not what it does, so it leaves room for [[interactionist-dualism]] and [[concepts/epiphenomenalism|epiphenomenalism]] alike. The fully specified zombie—a physical duplicate behaving exactly as we do—sits uneasily with interactionism, which denies that physics alone fixes that behaviour; [[philosophical-zombies#The Interactionist Escape|the zombie article]] concedes that the argument then becomes conditional. The case for causally efficacious consciousness, and for [[free-will|libertarian free will]], rests on other arguments, chiefly the [[self-stultification]] problem facing epiphenomenalism.

This aligns with the [[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]] tenet. The parsimony objection to dualism often assumes that conceivability arguments are mere "intuitions" to be overridden by simplicity. But if conceivability provides genuine epistemic access to modal structure, then the apparent simplicity of physicalism might reflect ignorance rather than insight—we fail to grasp the additional facts about consciousness because we're looking only at physical structure.

Dismissing a conceptual gap that survives sustained, rigorous reflection requires showing the conceiving is pseudo-conceiving (contains hidden contradictions), arguing that the inference fails locally for the phenomenal case, or rejecting modal rationalism entirely (abandoning reason as a guide to possibility).

Each move carries costs. The first requires demonstrating where zombie conceiving goes wrong—and decades of scrutiny haven't produced consensus that it does. The second is the route of [[type-a-type-b-and-type-c-physicalism|Type-B physicalism]], the Map's live opponent, which grants that zombies are conceivable and denies that they are possible. It must posit [[primitive-identities-and-strong-necessities#Strong Necessities|strong necessities]]—metaphysical necessities that, unlike "water is H₂O", no two-dimensional analysis explains. Cognitive closure takes the same route at the price already noted [[#The Cognitive Closure Objection|above]]. These costs bear on Type-B's plausibility; they do not make the evidence favour dualism over it, and the Map's case against Type-B rests on such costs rather than on any observation Type-B fails to predict. The third move abandons a priori modal reasoning altogether, making necessity and possibility empirically inaccessible—a radical skepticism that undermines much of philosophy and mathematics.
