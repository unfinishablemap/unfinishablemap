**Three-worlds interactionism** is Karl Popper's view that reality contains at least three interacting "sub-universes". World 1 is physical bodies and energies. World 2 is "the mental or psychological world": feelings, thoughts, decisions, perceptions. World 3 is "the world of the products of the human mind": languages, scientific theories, mathematical constructions, works of art (Popper 1980, pp. 143–144). Popper held that none of the three is causally closed, and that World 3 acts on the physical world only through minds. He set out the scheme in *Objective Knowledge* (1972), argued it at book length with the neurophysiologist John Eccles in *The Self and Its Brain* (1977), and stated it most compactly in his 1978 Tanner Lecture. Eccles had already adopted it: "I was a dualist, now I am a trialist!" (Eccles 1973, p. 189). *Trialist* was Eccles's word, defined in that book's glossary as one who believes in the existence of three worlds. Popper called himself a pluralist.

The Unfinishable Map treats Popper as the most prominent twentieth-century philosophical ally of its interactionism, and as an ally whose central argument it cannot use. It can take his denial of causal closure, his reply to the conservation objection, and his demand that a theory of action find something between chance and determinism. It cannot take his argument for an irreducible mind. On John Gray's analysis, Popper's only arguments for World 2 run through World 3, and World 3, a realm of objective thought-contents that counts as real because it acts, has no place in the Map's interaction framework (see [why below](#world-3-dependence)). Popper was also not a substance dualist, and said so. His sharpest remark about the Map's own mechanism family is hostile: amplified quantum jumps, he judged, model tossing a penny rather than deciding, and a mind selecting among those jumps he filed as a "tiny baby theory" (see [the penny-toss objection](#penny-toss)). The verdict is provenance without inheritance. On sources: Popper's lecture and essay are quoted from the primary texts, but *The Self and Its Brain* was not read for this article, and what is said about it rests on Gray's 1981 review.

## The Three Worlds {#three-worlds}

Popper's Tanner Lecture opens with a challenge to "those who uphold a monist or even a dualist view of the universe" (Popper 1980, p. 143). His complaint against dualism is that it has too few categories. The scheme rests on a criterion of reality that Popper built by generalising the physicalist's own: "what is real or what exists is whatever may, directly or indirectly, have a causal effect upon physical things" (p. 153). Theories change the physical world (Special Relativity reached the atom bomb through physicists who worked out its consequences), so on this criterion theories are real.

Three structural claims follow. World 2 is the intermediary: "only the intervention of the mental world 2 allows world 3 objects to exert, indirectly, a causal influence upon the physical world 1" (p. 164). Because the worlds interact, "none of them is causally closed" (p. 165). And each world emerges from its predecessor by evolution and then acts back on it: "the emerging product has a tremendous feedback effect upon the world from which it emerged" (p. 167). The self is a product of that feedback: "The very idea of a self depends on world 3 theories, especially upon a theory of time which underlies the identity of the self" (p. 167). Popper's own summary: "a mind-body dualism is nearer to the truth than a materialist monism. But dualism is not enough. We have to recognize world 3" (p. 164).

## States, Not Substances {#states-not-substances}

Popper declined substance dualism in print. In "Of Clouds and Clocks", his Arthur Holly Compton Memorial Lecture, he wrote: "like Descartes, I propose the adoption of a dualistic outlook, though I do not of course recommend talking of two kinds of interacting substances. But I think it is helpful and legitimate to distinguish two kinds of interacting *states* (or events), physio-chemical and mental ones" (Popper 1972, p. 252). He offered interaction itself as a conjecture: "I conjecture that the acceptance of an 'interaction' of mental and physical states offers the only satisfactory solution of Descartes's problem" (p. 252, n. 62).

The collaborators also diverged. Gray records that Eccles showed "a strong concern to preserve the theoretical possibility of the survival of human personality beyond bodily death, to which Popper is comparatively indifferent" (Gray 1981, p. 124). The mechanism Eccles later built with the physicist Friedrich Beck, in which mental influence raises the probability of synaptic vesicle release (Beck & Eccles 1992), operates entirely at the boundary between World 2 and World 1; World 3 plays no part in its physics. The Map's mechanism family descends from that part of Eccles's work, as [[sherrington-dualist-lineage|the Sherrington lineage]] and [[penfield-interactionist-dualism|the Penfield article]] trace.

## Why the Argument Cannot Be Borrowed {#world-3-dependence}

The difficulty lies in the route by which Popper reaches mind. Gray argues that Popper's case against the mind–brain identity theory "presupposes the truth of the 'Three Worlds' doctrine. If we can avoid postulating World Three, there is every reason to suppose we can do without World Two, as well" (Gray 1981, p. 123). His conclusion is that "his only arguments for World Two are arguments that invoke World Three" (p. 124). Gray judged World 3 unnecessary and drew the anti-dualist moral: Popper then "has no reason to move beyond the First World of physical objects and laws" (p. 124).

Gray builds on Paul Feyerabend's review of *Objective Knowledge* (Feyerabend 1974), which he reports as showing that none of Popper's arguments establishes the irreducibility of abstract objects to mental or physical states. Gray's own sentence generalises well beyond Popper: "to show a causal connection is not to mark an ontological distinction" (p. 123). Popper's criterion makes causal efficacy the mark of reality, but reality and irreducibility are separate questions. On this objection, a theory can change the world through the brains of physicists without being anything over and above those brains and their states.

The obstacle is specific to World 3. The Map leaves mathematical Platonism open in [[consciousness-and-mathematics|its treatment of mathematics]], so abstract objects as such raise no difficulty. Popper's World 3 differs from Platonic abstracta: it is man-made, it changes over time, and it qualifies as real by acting, through minds, on the physical world. Admitting it would add a third causal realm to an interaction framework whose tenets name two relata, consciousness and the physical, and a second unexplained contact relation (minds grasping objective contents) alongside the first (minds moving brains). No tenet rules World 3 out, but none provides for it, and the Map has made no case for it. The Map therefore cannot take Popper's conclusion, an irreducible and interacting mind, while dropping the World-3 premise that carries him to it. Its case for [[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]] rests on its own grounds, chiefly the explanatory gap, none of which passes through a third world.

Gray's sentence also bears on the Map: any argument from "consciousness has effects" to "consciousness is not physical" meets the same reply. The Map stays clear of it only by keeping its Dualism tenet, argued from the explanatory gap, apart from [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]].

## The Penny-Toss Objection {#penny-toss}

Compton's model had two layers. In 1931 and in *The Freedom of Man* (1935), a photon reaching one of two amplifier-wired photocells decides whether dynamite explodes or a cut-out switch disarms it; since the nervous system, as [[first-quantum-free-will-wave-1927-1940|Ralph Lillie had argued in 1927]], is itself such an amplifier, physical law ceases to be evidence against freedom. Compton added that this alone does not show the organism free, and met the charge of mere chance by treating deliberate acts as individual events outside statistics (pp. 63 f., via Popper's n. 44). The second layer was a selector: Compton "very tentatively accepted" that minds act "by influencing or selecting some quantum jumps" (p. 233). Popper said Compton "did not particularly like" the model and "used it for one purpose only: to show that human indeterminism … does not necessarily contradict quantum physics. I think he was right in all this" (pp. 227, 233). The standard Popper applied, freedom as "a subtle interplay between something almost random or haphazard, and something like a restrictive or selective control", he named "Compton's postulate of freedom" (pp. 231–232). Compton was his standard's source, not only his target.

Popper's verdict in "Of Clouds and Clocks" was blunt: "the model has no similarity to any rational decision. It is, rather, a model of a kind of decision-making where people who cannot make up their minds say: 'Let us toss a penny'" (Popper 1972, p. 228). He conceded that "something like the amplification of a quantum jump may actually happen in our brains if we make a snap-decision", then asked: "But are snap-decisions really so very interesting?" His answer: "I do not think that we shall get much further with quantum jumps" (p. 228).

What rational behaviour needs, Popper argued, is "something intermediate in character between perfect chance and perfect determinism" (p. 228), and he called the thesis that nothing intermediate can exist "clearly absurd" (p. 228). His candidate was "a 'plastic control', as I shall call it in contradistinction to a 'cast-iron control'" (p. 232). He added that considered decisions are "reached by a kind of maturing process which is not well represented by the master-switch model" (p. 234).

The objection targets a mechanism family the Map belongs to, and positing a selector does not move the Map outside it. The model as Popper describes it, and as Compton accepted it, already has minds working on bodies "by influencing or selecting some quantum jumps" (p. 233), and he met its refinement, a mind acting "just within the Heisenberg uncertainties", with a concession and a refusal: "I admit that there is perhaps a slight advance here, in so far as the size of the baby is specified. But I still do not love the baby" (p. 233). Two complaints survive the concession. The first is Schlick's: of the indeterministic theories, "it is not clear how they escape from Schlick's objection" (p. 232), the doctrine that chance is the only alternative to determinism. The second is grain: the amplifier model "strongly suggests that all our decisions are either snap-decisions ... or else composed of snap-decisions" (p. 233). On the [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] tenet, consciousness biases which outcome occurs toward patterns that express its content, a guided indeterminism that is structurally a candidate for Popper's intermediate category, undetermined yet not chance. The candidacy has to be earned against both complaints, and nothing in Popper's texts grants it.

Schlick's complaint returns at the Map's weakest point. On its default reading the Map's selector preserves Born statistics, and if it does so exactly, a selected outcome is [[ensemble-level-epiphenomenalism|observer-indistinguishable]] from an unselected one. That is the bias-without-deviation dilemma recorded in the Map's [[positions/quantum-interface#^mechanism-debt|mechanism debt]], and from outside, a Born-preserving selector looks exactly like Popper's penny. The Map's reply to the [[quantum-indeterminacy-free-will|luck objection]], that selection tracks reasons, works only inside its dualist framework; the [[trilemma-of-selection|trilemma of selection]] states the burden in general form. The grain complaint is untouched by that reply: if decisions mature over lengthy deliberation, a mechanism located at single quantum events owes an account of how many selections add up to one choice. Beck and Eccles's model biases probabilities rather than amplifying a bare jump, but whether biasing escapes either complaint is the open question, and the selection posit carries that debt rather than discharging it.

## What the Map Can Take {#what-the-map-can-take}

Several things survive the World-3 problem, because none of them depends on World 3.

**Open causal closure.** Popper put the denial as a charge against his opponents: "it means shutting one's eyes to the obvious, and explaining away the obvious, if we deny that world 1 is causally open to world 2, and through it, to world 3" (Popper 1980, p. 166). The Map takes the first half of that sentence. It already cites Popper's description of [[causal-closure|causal closure]] as "the characteristic principle of physicalism or materialism"; the Tanner Lecture supplies the denial that matches the diagnosis.

**A conservation reply by a second route.** In a note to "Of Clouds and Clocks", Popper observed that typically biological processes involve "the release or triggering of stored energy", that release processes are "most characteristic of quantum physics", and that "such formulae as 'the cause equals the effect' (and, with it, the traditional criticism of Cartesian interactionism) have long become obsolete, in spite of the continuing validity of the conservation laws" (Popper 1972, p. 234, n. 46). His limiting case is radioactivity, "with a triggering energy equal to zero". C. D. Broad (1925) had denied that conservation requires energy to flow from cause to effect; Popper reached a related verdict through triggering rather than redirection. The Map's own [[conservation-laws-and-mental-causation|reply]] places mental influence in selection rather than triggering, but Popper's note is a further precedent for treating the objection as obsolete rather than decisive.

**A parsimony rule, with its critic attached.** On Gray's report, Popper stipulates "(bizarrely) that we are to resort to Occam's razor only after we have decided which entities are irreducible" (Gray 1981, p. 123), elevating a "game of hide-and-seek" (excluding items from the physical and mental realms, then discovering them in World 3) into method. The rule is a procedural relative of the Map's fifth tenet, and the charge applies to any rule of its shape: a parsimony principle that waits on irreducibility can protect whatever irreducibility claim has already been made. [[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]] is framed differently, holding that simplicity is unreliable where knowledge is incomplete and binding the Map's own use of parsimony as much as its critics'. Popper's rule shows what the tenet must avoid becoming.

**Arguments against epiphenomenalism, pending a check.** Gray, no friend of the conclusion, called Popper's arguments against parallelism and [[epiphenomenalism]] "wholly conclusive" (Gray 1981, p. 122). The review does not say whether they stand free of World 3; Popper's 1977 *Dialectica* paper on the topic, not read here, is where to look.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]].** Popper is the most prominent twentieth-century philosophical precedent for the Map's third tenet: interaction is his "only satisfactory solution of Descartes's problem", and no world is causally closed. The Map cites him as provenance; following its [[evidential-status-discipline|evidential-status discipline]], his eminence adds no evidential weight.

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]].** Popper's dualism of states and events satisfies the tenet's minimal wording, which is neutral between [[substance-property-dualism|substance and property dualism]]. The divergence lies elsewhere. Popper's argument for irreducible mind runs through World 3, and his self depends on World-3 theories, whereas the Map's agency cluster leans toward a persisting subject for reasons traced in [[where-the-substance-commitment-enters|its account of where the substance commitment enters]]. The Map shares Popper's conclusion that mind is irreducible and cannot borrow his main argument for it.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]].** Popper is a hostile witness from inside interactionism, and he examined a selection proposal before declining it. The penny-toss objection names the burden the Map's selection posit has to carry, and the Map has not yet discharged it.

**[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]].** Nothing in the texts consulted bears on the many-worlds interpretation, and no alignment is claimed.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]].** Popper's razor-after-irreducibility rule is an early relative of the tenet, and Gray's hide-and-seek charge is a test the tenet has to pass.

The [[sherrington-dualist-lineage|Sherrington lineage]] treats eminent dualists as provenance, never as an authority count; this article applies the same rule to an argument. The Map inherits Popper's questions and two of his replies, and must supply its own case for the mind he defended.
