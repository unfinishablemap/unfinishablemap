Self-blindness, in Sydney Shoemaker's sense, would be the condition of a creature that has the concepts of mental states but no first-person access to its own: it "could frame and understand ascriptions to itself of various mental states, but would be incapable of knowing by self-acquaintance whether such self-ascriptions were true" (Shoemaker 1988; 1996 reprint, p. 31). Shoemaker argued that self-blindness is conceptually impossible for a subject of normal rationality and conceptual competence. The thesis concerns the *capacity* for first-person access to a whole *kind* of state, and Shoemaker himself allows local failures of access. It is contested: Amy Kind, Brie Gertler and Kevin Kimble deny that the argument reaches the inner-sense view it targets.

The Unfinishable Map's verdict is that its clinical record supplies no counterexample. Anton syndrome, anosognosia, inter-identity amnesia in dissociative identity disorder (DID), thought insertion and alexithymia each fall outside the thesis on at least two of [[#clinical|five conditions]] (explained below) that the Map draws from Shoemaker's texts, and the same scope stops the record from confirming it. What stays open is whether "rational" can be specified without mentioning the access failures it is meant to exclude. Shoemaker is a functionalist who rejects Cartesian dualism, and both sides of the debate are compatible with physicalism, so the topic fits the Map's [[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]] tenet without evidencing it.

## Shoemaker's Argument {#argument}

Shoemaker developed the argument in "On Knowing One's Own Mind" (1988) and the second of his 1994 Royce Lectures. Years below identify the essay; pages are to the 1996 collection that reprints them. His target is the *broad perceptual model* of introspection (p. 224). Its causal condition holds that our beliefs about our mental states "are caused by those mental states, via a reliable belief-producing mechanism"; its independence condition, that the existence of those states "is independent of their being known in this way". On this model introspection could fail as sight fails, leaving the states and the subject's rationality intact. Only if self-blindness were a conceptual possibility, Shoemaker writes, "would it be appropriate to think of the capacity for self-acquaintance as a quasi-perceptual capacity" (p. 31). He is "equally opposed to Cartesianism" (p. 225), and on that page lists self-deception, confabulation and blindsight as data against Cartesian transparency, not against his own thesis.

**The George reductio.** George is a "self-blind man who has human intelligence and conceptual capacity" (p. 39). Rationality makes him appreciate "the logical impropriety of affirming something while denying that one believes it" (p. 237), so he avoids Moore-paradoxical assertions (*it is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining*). Nothing in his behaviour would "give away the fact that he lacks self-acquaintance" (p. 36). Rather than conclude that self-acquaintance adds nothing, Shoemaker takes this as a reductio of the supposition that self-blindness is possible.

**Pain and perception.** Self-blindness about pain "seems to be an impossibility" (p. 229), since removing pain's link to introspective belief would "take away a lot else" (p. 228). For perception Shoemaker makes the move that matters most clinically: a person might have "states that play some of the functional role of perceptual experiences" without access to them, but she would not have "perceptual experiences in the sense we do" (p. 231). The unaccessed state is reclassified, so it never counts as self-blindness.

**The agent and the agnostic.** For a body whose purposive behaviour comes with professions of ignorance (pp. 234–235), Shoemaker offers two readings. If two persons share it, "the blindness is not self-blindness, and not introspective blindness" (p. 234). What would make it reasonable to call them one person is that the agent's doings fit with, and are "'rationalized' by, beliefs and desire that can independently be ascribed to the agnostic" (p. 234).

## What the Thesis Permits {#scope}

- **Error is granted.** Shoemaker assumes, "at least for the sake of discussion", that "it is logically possible for a person to be mistaken about his own mental states, of whatever kind" (1988, p. 25).
- **Local failures go with failures of rationality.** "Failures of rationality, such as those involved in self-deception, can bring with them failures in access"; only supposing such failure "the rule rather than the exception" oversteps "the bounds of intelligibility" (1990, p. 71).
- **Some beliefs are unavailable.** A person may sincerely deny a belief she has, since "lots of the beliefs that one can be said to have at a time are not available to one at that time" (1995, p. 80).
- **Full self-intimation is an idealisation**, true "in some conceptually central class of cases, ones blessed with more rationality than is usual in human affairs" (p. 93).

Matthew Parrott, who tends to agree that a self-blind person is impossible, states the scope: "We all lack that kind of access to some of our beliefs some of the time, but none of us are self-blind" (2017, p. 6). The thesis rules out one thing: a subject of normal rationality and conceptual competence who lacks the capacity for first-person access to a whole kind of state.

## Critiques and Rival Explanations {#critiques}

**Against the argument.** On the evidence of her abstract, Kind (2003) argues that Shoemaker runs together introspective awareness and self-knowledge, and that third-person evidence available to a self-blind subject could explain the Moore's-paradox behaviour. Kimble (2013), also known here by abstract, defends the broad perceptual model. Gertler (2011, ch. 5), as her own encyclopedia entry reports, holds that an inner-sense theorist can stipulate that no self-blind creature counts as rational. The claim that no rational creature is self-blind then holds *de dicto*, by definition, and inner sense can accept it; Shoemaker needs the *de re* claim that the capacity for self-knowledge is a condition of being rational at all.

**Accepting the impossibility.** Shoemaker's explanation is functionalist: in a rational creature the causal role defining a mental state also suffices for the belief that one is in it (as Gertler 2021 summarises him), spelled out through his distinction between a state's "core" and "total" realization (p. 242). Parrott (2017) keeps a person's states "wholly distinct from her beliefs about them" and argues that his account is "better placed to explain our fallibility and ignorance" than constitutive theories. Christopher Peacocke (1998), as Jeff Speaks's teaching notes report him, derives the impossibility from what possessing the concept of belief requires.

**The clinical strategy.** Shaun Nichols and Stephen Stich (2003) posit a self-monitoring mechanism that dissociates from mindreading: defective mindreading with intact monitoring in autism, the reverse in certain first-rank symptoms of schizophrenia. Schwitzgebel's (2024) encyclopedia entry cites them as arguing that schizophrenia shows introspection breaking down, the possibility inner-sense views imply and Shoemaker denies.

## The Clinical Record {#clinical}

A clinical case would tell against the thesis only if it met five conditions, which the Map draws from Shoemaker's texts; he does not list them.

1. **Omission.** A state is present without first-person access; a false self-ascription does not qualify, since Shoemaker grants error.
2. **Object.** The state is a belief, desire, intention, pain or full perceptual experience, not a bodily condition or a capacity.
3. **Scope.** The failure is capacity-level, across a kind.
4. **Rationality.** Normal rationality is shown by markers independent of the failure.
5. **One subject.** The case does not divide into two "occupants" (p. 234).

| Case | Conditions failed | Why |
|---|---|---|
| [[anton-syndrome-and-the-sincere-report-of-seeing\|Anton syndrome]] | 1, 4 | A false positive ("I can see"); usually impaired on independent markers |
| [[blindsight\|Blindsight]], inverse Anton | 2, 3 | Presence of visual experience contested; confined to part of the field |
| [[anosognosia-and-the-reversible-self-monitoring-channel\|Anosognosia for hemiplegia]] | 2, 3, 4 | Error about the body; implicit registration not shown to be an available belief; reversible; impaired belief evaluation on two-factor accounts |
| DID inter-identity amnesia | 2, 3, 5 | Retained traces plus a false belief about one's memory; identity-relative; one subject or two contested |
| [[thought-insertion\|Thought insertion]] | 1, 3 | The thought is accessed; its authorship is disowned |
| [[alexithymia\|Alexithymia]] | 2, 3 | Graded difficulty classifying emotions, which are not among Shoemaker's paradigm kinds |

**Anton syndrome bears on a different restriction.** A blind patient who sincerely says "I can see" makes a false positive, where self-blindness is an omission. The case presses instead on David Chalmers's (1995) clause that no rational system "whose cognitive mechanisms are unimpaired can be so mistaken" about its experiences, which the Map's Anton article weighs against independent markers of impairment.

**Blindsight shows the price of the thesis's protection.** Here the error runs in the omission direction, and Shoemaker names blindsight himself (p. 225). His perceptual-experience move absorbs it, which shields the thesis from clinical counter-evidence and equally stops clinical data from confirming it. The move is principled only if "in the sense we do" can be spelled out without mentioning introspective access.

**DID is the most informative case.** Identities who report no memory of each other's material perform like healthy controls on feigning-resistant tests (Huntjens et al. 2003), though the meta-analytic picture is disputed ([[metacognitive-access-versus-retention]]). On the Map's reading, Shoemaker's agent/agnostic dilemma applies directly. If the identities are distinct occupants, the blindness is by his own words not self-blindness. If the patient is one person, the professed ignorance is a local, motivated failure of availability, which he allows. Since no general impairment of reasoning is part of the diagnosis, the only irrationality Shoemaker can cite is the motivated non-search, identified through the very failure it is meant to explain.

**Alexithymia** is the Map's strongest case of impaired self-knowledge in a rational subject, being a personality trait rather than a disorder of reasoning. Whether the affect is present but inaccessible is unresolved; even if it is, the trait is a graded difficulty identifying emotions, not a lack of access to whether a state occurs, and emotions are not among Shoemaker's paradigm kinds. It is measured mainly by the self-report Toronto Alexithymia Scale (Bagby, Parker & Taylor 1994), so a self-knowledge deficit is measured through self-report. Alexithymia shows that rationality does not guarantee accurate self-classification; total blindness is untouched.

**What the record shows** is local, domain-specific failure of first-person access, some of it in subjects with intact general cognition and some reversible. Monitoring accounts expect that pattern, but it does not discriminate, because Shoemaker's allowance for local failure absorbs it. The searches behind this article found no published paper using Anton syndrome, anosognosia or DID against the thesis.

## Chalmers's Restriction: Same Shape, Different Thesis {#chalmers}

Chalmers and Shoemaker both confine first-person reliability to rational, unimpaired subjects, but their theses differ. Chalmers guards against commission about experience (his fading-qualia subject judges that he has experiences he lacks) and makes a plausibility claim, granting that "Fading Qualia may be logically possible". Shoemaker guards against omission, chiefly about attitudes, and claims a conceptual impossibility.

The clauses share one liability: each avoids circularity only so far as impairment can be identified independently of the error. Chalmers names such markers, among them "any systematic psychiatric disorder"; Shoemaker's local failures are identified largely through the failures themselves, and he concedes that his account of them "puts a rather heavy burden on the concept of rationality" (1988, p. 49).

## The Open Question: Specifying "Rational" {#open-question}

The live issue is whether "rational" can be specified independently of the failures it is meant to exclude. Gertler's de dicto reply turns on it, and her encyclopedia entry states the dilemma for all rationalist accounts: "if a conception of rationality is too demanding, then it is questionable whether it applies to cognitively flawed creatures like us" (Gertler 2021, §3.6), while a realistic conception may not exclude self-blindness. The Map's Anton article meets the same question for Chalmers's clause and answers it with markers independent of the error. DID and alexithymia, where general cognition is intact, are where the clinical record could bear on it.

What follows from the thesis alone is a limit on what clinical cases can show. Only Shoemaker's constitutive explanation adds that first-person access "is less than perfect because our rationality is less than perfect" (1990, p. 71). Parrott accepts the thesis while arguing that a non-constitutive account explains ordinary fallibility better.

## Self-Blindness and Presentation {#presentation}

Self-blindness is orthogonal to the rule, set out in [[evidential-status-discipline]], that a first-person presentation does not certify the fact it presents: that rule governs the step from how a state seems to what it presents, while Shoemaker's thesis governs whether a state must be available for self-ascription at all. Shoemaker grants error (p. 25), and his account of attitude self-knowledge involves no perception-like presentation: there is no "organ of introspection" (p. 207).

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]].** Self-blindness fits the tenet without evidencing it. Both live accounts are compatible with physicalism: if self-blindness is impossible, a functionalist explains that through the causal roles of attitudes, and if it is possible, the monitoring mechanism is a physical detector. Neither outcome bears on whether consciousness is irreducible. Shoemaker holds that "there are lots of good reasons for rejecting Cartesian dualism" (p. 24). His thesis lends no support to the subject the Map posits, to its dualism, or to the near-incorrigibility the Map gives the judgement that experience is occurring, which rests on [[self-stultification]] rather than on his argument.

**What the Map takes from it.** The thesis bears on how much weight first-person reports of belief, desire and intention can carry, which the layered authority of [[phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence]], framed for phenomenal states, does not yet address. If true, it guarantees a rational subject the capacity for access to its attitudes, not the accuracy of any report.

**A tension, recorded.** Shoemaker's reclassification of unaccessed perceptual states runs against the Map's openness to phenomenal states without report, as in the overflow reading in [[introspection]] and in [[covert-consciousness-and-cognitive-motor-dissociation|covert consciousness]]. The disagreement sits at the framework boundary; the clinical data settle it for neither side.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]].** Compatible, without support. Shoemaker explains reliable attitude self-ascription through functional constitution, without phenomenal causation; that concerns attitudes, not reports about experience, and does not show experience to be causally idle. The Map's argument from reports about consciousness stays where the tenets place it: suggestive, not decisive, and confined to unimpaired systems.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]].** The tenet binds the Map's own reasoning: parsimony licenses neither the constitutive account for positing no extra mechanism nor the monitoring account for explaining clinical dissociation more economically.

[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]] and [[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]] are not engaged.
