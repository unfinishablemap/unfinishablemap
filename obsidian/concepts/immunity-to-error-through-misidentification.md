Immunity to error through misidentification (IEM) is a property some first-person judgements have relative to the grounds they rest on. When I judge "I feel pain" because I feel it, I cannot be right that someone feels pain and wrong only about whether that someone is me. Sydney Shoemaker named the property in 1968, after Wittgenstein's distinction between the use of "I" "as subject" and "as object"; Gareth Evans explained it by the absence of any self-identification. That introspective self-ascription is immune is the field's standard view; its scope beyond introspection is contested, and the Stanford Encyclopedia's survey holds that [[#counterexamples|thought insertion]] (explained below), the most discussed counterexample, leaves it standing, against a live dissent.

For The Unfinishable Map the verdict is narrow. IEM is compatible with the Map's posited subject and gives it no support, because every account of the first person predicts it: an embodied human person, Anscombe's non-referring "I", Kant's formal "I" and a Cartesian ego alike. A datum every rival predicts discriminates nothing, so IEM neither removes an objection to the Map's subject nor adds evidence for it. It does defeat one kind of argument: any inference from criterionless self-reference, or from the felt sense of *whose* experience this is, to a non-physical subject. The [[#three-readings|three main readings]] (explained below) treat that inference as the source of the Cartesian picture of the self, and the Map's [[kants-paralogisms-and-the-maps-subject|reading of Kant's Paralogisms]] lists it as the first point where its subject is exposed. IEM is no evidence that the subject is non-physical.

## The Thesis and Its Variants {#variants}

In the *Blue Book* (dictated 1933–34), Wittgenstein distinguished "two different cases in the use of the word 'I' (or 'my') which I might call 'the use as object' and 'the use as subject'" (1958, p. 66). In the first, error about the person is possible: "I should feel a pain in my arm, see a broken arm at my side, and think it is mine, when really it is my neighbour's" (p. 67). In the second it is not: "To ask 'are you sure that it's you who have pains?' would be nonsensical" (p. 67). On the standard reading the passage states the immunity thesis. Rachael Wiseman (2019) reads it as a warning against the distinction; William Child (2026) defends the standard reading.

Shoemaker (1968) gave the thesis its name and canonical form. One cannot be mistaken in saying "I feel pain" because, "although I do know of someone that feels pain, I am mistaken in thinking that person to be myself" (p. 8). The reason is that no identification takes place: my use of "I" "is not due to my having identified as myself something" of which the predicate holds (p. 9), and self-awareness "does not involve what I have called 'being presented to oneself as an object'" (p. 15). He also distinguished "absolute" from merely "circumstantial" immunity (p. 8).

Memory is the contested case. Shoemaker (1970) allowed memory judgements only "de facto immunity to error through misidentification" (p. 46). Shoemaker's quasi-memory, a memory-like state whose source could in principle be another person's experience, shows why: the immunity holds only because our memories in fact derive from our own pasts. Strawson had counted "directly remembered" states among those self-ascribed without criteria (1966, p. 165), so the two disagree over whether the immunity reaches across time. For the non-contingent kind, Annalisa Coliva (2002) speaks of self-ascriptions that are "logically IEM".

James Pryor (1999) distinguished several sorts of misidentification. In paraphrase: *de re* misidentification runs through a false identity belief about a particular object ("that person is me"); *wh*-misidentification runs through knowing only that someone or other is F and wrongly taking oneself to be the one. Since Pryor, the Stanford Encyclopedia reports, immunity to wh-misidentification has been treated as the more fundamental notion, entailing ordinary IEM but not conversely (Smith 2024).

The usual explanation is Evans's (1982, ch. 7), as the Stanford Encyclopedia reconstructs it. A judgement is *identification-dependent* when knowing it rests on knowing that *b* is F plus an identity, *a* = *b*, and *identification-free* when no identity enters. Introspection, bodily awareness and self-locating perception are, for Evans, channels of identification-free self-thought, so he models "I" on "here" rather than "this". Such "I"-thoughts can still fail to refer, if their channels derive from different objects (§7.6).

Two cautions follow. IEM "is always relative, then, to the grounds on which a judgement is based" (Smith 2024): a judgement about my arm made from how it feels can be immune, while the same judgement made from seeing an arm at my side is open to Wittgenstein's accident. And the immunity covers only the step from grounds to subject; the predicate stays fallible.

## Thought Insertion: The Standard Counterexample {#counterexamples}

The Stanford Encyclopedia lists the proposed counterexamples: thought insertion, alien limb, anarchic hand, "anonymous memory" and "anonymous vision" (Smith 2024). The most discussed is [[thought-insertion]], in which a patient introspects a thought, locates it in her own mind, and denies that it is hers.

John Campbell (1999), as Coliva's (2002) abstract reports him, held that the patient "is introspectively aware of a certain thought and yet she is wrong in identifying whose thought it is". Lane and Liang (2011) go further. Their abstract states: "we deny that this relationship is tautological; instead, we treat Shoemaker's principle (IEM) as a hypothesis", and concludes that "not only can the what of conscious experience be misrepresented, so too can the who."

The reply turns on a distinction. IEM, "as typically understood, concerns an immunity to self-misidentification" (Smith 2024): it excludes judging oneself F by wrongly taking someone else to be oneself. On this reply the patient makes the opposite error, if she errs at all: she withholds "I think that *p*", or attributes her own thought to another, which is *other*-misidentification. Coliva concludes that thought insertion "is not a counterexample to the view that introspection-based mental self-ascriptions are logically IEM" (2002, abstract), and Shaun Gallagher (2000) also resists the counterexample. The Stanford Encyclopedia's verdict on the whole family of cases is that "none obviously challenge the claim that when these various information sources do ground self-ascriptions, those first-personal thoughts are IEM" (Smith 2024). On the survey's account the harder challenges are imagined cases such as quasi-proprioception, which turn on contested conditions for *de re* thought.

Manuel García-Carpintero (2024) reframes the question. His abstract defends "a Shoemaker-like line" and holds that the "all-inclusive term 'thought' should be avoided", since the answer varies with the kind of thought at issue. The debate is open.

On the self/other distinction the case shows a separation. Mineness, a thought's presenting as one's own, can be disturbed while the patient's "I" goes unmisidentified. This fits the split between *mineness* and *me-ness* that the Map's [[thought-insertion]] article takes from Marie Guillot, where the case's five readings are set out. Ownership and self-identification are separate questions, and IEM answers only the second.

## Three Readings of What the Immunity Shows {#three-readings}

Granting that introspective self-ascription is immune, what follows about the subject? Three readings dominate, and none of them takes the immunity as evidence for a soul.

### The Cartesian Genealogy

On the first reading IEM explains why the Cartesian picture of the self tempts us, and so cannot confirm it. Because we do not use "I" as subject by recognising a person from bodily characteristics, Wittgenstein wrote, "this creates the illusion that we use this word to refer to something bodiless, which, however, has its seat in our body" (1958, p. 69). Strawson called criterionless self-ascription "the fact that lies at the root of the Cartesian illusion" (1966, pp. 164–165): when a subject ascribes a current or directly remembered experience to herself, "no use whatever of any criteria of personal identity is required" (p. 165). "I" still refers, because its links with public criteria "are not in practice severed" (p. 165). Abstracted from those links, it produces "the illusion of a purely inner and yet subject-referring use for 'I'", whose referent then seems "an object of singular purity and simplicity - a pure, individual, immaterial substance" (pp. 165–166). The Stanford Encyclopedia reports the same diagnosis: identification-freedom "gives rise to the idea that they pick out a private object, a soul" (Smith 2024).

Child (2026, §3) supplies the control case. Some physical self-ascriptions are also made as subject, without recognising anyone. When I judge that my legs are crossed, the property ascribed is "a property of an embodied person; an immaterial ego could not have crossed legs". In that case "it is evident that the subject of the ascription is an embodied human being, even though no recognition of a person by their bodily characteristics is involved." Identification-freedom thus occurs where the subject is plainly a body, so the feature that tempts the Cartesian inference does not discriminate in its favour. For Child both uses of "I" refer to "the same kind of thing: a human person."

### Anscombe: No Reference at All

Elizabeth Anscombe (1975) accepted the immunity and drew a dilemma from it. If "I" refers with guaranteed reference, she argued, only one referent will do: "Thus we discover that if 'I' is a referring expression, then Descartes was right about what the referent was" (1975/1981, pp. 30–32). This horn is the strongest case for reading IEM as support for a Cartesian subject, and Anscombe states its limits: "Nothing but a Cartesian Ego will serve. Or, rather, a stretch of one." Nor could the thinker tell one ego from many: "How do I know that 'I' is not ten thinkers thinking in unison?"

She takes the other horn. "Getting hold of the wrong object is excluded, and that makes us think that getting hold of the right object is guaranteed. But the reason is that there is no getting hold of an object at all." Her conclusion is that "'I' is neither a name nor another kind of expression whose logical role is to make a reference, at all" (pp. 30–32). Among the positions in a dispute she calls "endless", she lists a move close to the Map's: "Others in effect treat selves as postulated objects for 'I' to be names of in different people's mouths."

Evans's "here" model rejects the dilemma's premise: on the Stanford Encyclopedia's reconstruction it answers Anscombe's sensory-deprivation case, so a referring "I" need not pick out a Cartesian ego.

### The Kantian Reading

Strawson gave Kant the credit: "it is because Kant recognized this truth that his treatment of the subject is so greatly superior to Hume's" (1966, p. 165). Kant's insight, he adds, "explains why that notion, so used, is really quite empty of content" (p. 166). Béatrice Longuenesse (2017) reads the theme back into the Paralogisms. Her fifth chapter's abstract states: "That the first-person standpoint gives us no access to any knowledge of the kind of entity we are, is, it is argued, Kant's groundbreaking claim in the Paralogisms of Pure Reason."

The three readings disagree about whether "I" refers and to what. They agree that the immunity follows from how first-person thought works, whatever the subject turns out to be.

## The Kant Article's Three Exposures in IEM Terms {#three-exposures}

From here the mapping is the Map's own reconstruction; none of the authors above discusses the Map.

The Map's [[kants-paralogisms-and-the-maps-subject|Kant article]] finds its posited subject exposed at three points, wherever the corpus argues for the subject instead of positing it. IEM gives each a precise form.

**Felt mine-ness used as evidence.** Strawson states the mechanism. Because current experience is self-ascribed without criteria, "I" seems to refer to something purely inner, simple and immaterial. An argument from the felt *whose*-ness of experience to a non-physical owner runs the inference that mechanism produces, and Child's crossed-legs case shows identification-freedom where the owner is plainly a body. Felt [[mine-ness]] is compatible with the Map's subject and gives it no support over rivals that predict the same datum. The [[positions/individuation-and-subjecthood|individuation register]] already grades felt this-ness accordingly: "no independent evidence discriminates".

**Unity read as simplicity.** Anscombe's "ten thinkers thinking in unison" is Kant's collective-bearer objection to the second paralogism (A352–353) put in the first person. Guaranteed self-reference, like the unity of a thought, cannot by itself show whether one thinker is in view or many thinking together.

**The diachronic clause.** Because quasi-memory leaves memory only de facto immune, the immunity cannot certify that a remembering subject is the one who had the remembered experience. The Cartesian horn of Anscombe's dilemma yields at most "a stretch of" an ego. Neither reaches the persistence the Map's [[tenets/background-commitments|first background posit]] asserts.

The Kant article's verdict stands: the Map *posits* its subject rather than inferring it, and IEM bears only on inferences. What IEM adds is a precise list of the arguments the posit must forgo.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]].** Tenet 1 is untouched in its bare form, since its case for irreducibility does not rest on first-person reference. IEM is compatible with the Map's posited subject and gives it no support, because an embodied person, a non-referring "I", Kant's formal "I" and a Cartesian ego all predict it. It does defeat one argument: the inference from criterionless self-reference, or from the absence of *whose* in physical description, to a non-physical owner. The [[consciousness-and-the-ownership-problem|ownership problem]] article records that caveat where it argues from that absence.

**[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]].** Anscombe's reading gives a specific form to the deflation of the [[vertiginous-question|vertiginous question]]. If first-person thought makes no identification, "why am I *this* one?" asks for an identification that "I" never makes. On the Map's reading this is one more way of deflating the non-deflationary "I" that Tenet 4's indexical objection presupposes. The individuation register's P-I2 already records that dependency, holding that Tenet 4 "is stated unconditionally but argued conditionally", so the deflation adds no new concession. IEM also bears only partly on P-I1's clause that closed individualism would shift if felt this-ness proved a perspectival artefact: it explains an appearance about reference, not the felt this-ness itself.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]].** No bearing. IEM concerns how first-person judgements are justified and what "I" picks out, not whether conscious states make a difference to physical outcomes.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]].** Because every reading predicts the immunity, the datum cannot choose among them. Tenet 5 stops the deflationary readings' economy from settling that choice against the posit, and gives the posit no support of its own.
