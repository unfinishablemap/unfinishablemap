The dissociative subtype of post-traumatic stress disorder is a DSM-5 specifier, "with dissociative symptoms", for people who meet full PTSD criteria and also have persistent or recurrent depersonalisation or derealisation. ICD-11 has no such subtype. Its leading mechanistic account is the overmodulation model, proposed in a 2010 review by Lanius and colleagues. It treats ordinary re-experiencing PTSD as emotional *under*modulation, "mediated by failure of prefrontal inhibition of limbic regions", and the dissociative form as *over*modulation "mediated by midline prefrontal inhibition of the same limbic regions".

The Unfinishable Map's verdict on the model runs against the Map's own trauma reading. The model is the best-specified production account of trauma-provoked depersonalisation: a brain-only mechanism with a state-provocation paradigm, a within-trauma control group and an evolutionary rationale in passive defence. [[clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence]] left the interface reading one role in trauma, explaining why trauma-linked dissociation looks selective and protective. The model reaches that datum from its core, so for depersonalisation the point now belongs to the rival. Only the bare Dualism thesis is untouched, because nothing in the model explains why inhibited limbic output should feel unreal. The model's specific circuitry is also weaker than its 2010 statement: there is no reliable autonomic signature, the implicated regions vary across studies, and widespread hyperconnectivity appears where localised inhibition was expected (see [[#ledger|what held and what did not]], below).

## The Specifier and Prevalence

DSM-5 describes depersonalisation as "persistent or recurrent experiences of feeling detached from, and as if one were an outside observer of, one's mental processes or body", and derealisation as "persistent or recurrent experiences of unreality of surroundings" (APA 2013, as transcribed in Eder-Moreau et al. 2022). Brewin and colleagues' 2025 narrative review records the divergence between the manuals: "In contrast to the DSM-5, the ICD-11 does not include a dissociative subtype of PTSD or complex PTSD."

Prevalence depends mostly on method. In the World Mental Health Surveys (25,018 respondents in 16 countries), 14.4% of people with 12-month DSM-IV PTSD had dissociative symptoms (Stein et al. 2013). A meta-analysis of 49 studies (N = 8,214) estimated 38.1% overall, 22.8% in latent class and profile samples and 48.1% under strict DSM-5 criteria, all "characterised by high levels of heterogeneity" (White et al. 2022). A diagnostic count and a statistical class are different objects.

## The Overmodulation Model and Its Evidence

The model began in the scanner. Pilot work with script-driven trauma imagery, reported by Lanius et al. (2002), found that "Approximately 70% of patients relived their traumatic experience" with a rise in heart rate, while "The other 30% of patients had a dissociative response with no concomitant increase in heart rate". Their fMRI experiment then found more medial frontal and anterior cingulate activation in 7 dissociative patients than in 10 controls. Felmingham et al. (2008), comparing 12 dissociative with 11 non-dissociative patients, found "enhanced activation in the ventral prefrontal cortex for conscious fear", a regulatory strategy that "appears to function only during conscious processing of threat".

The strongest support came later. Resting-state dynamic causal modelling in 155 participants found "predominant bottom-up connectivity from the amygdala to the vmPFC" in PTSD and "predominant top-down connectivity between all node pairs" in the subtype (Nicholson et al. 2017). A prospective study scanned 145 survivors in the AURORA cohort two weeks after trauma: "Derealization was associated with increased ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) activation" (Lebois et al. 2022).

The model then acquired a reason. A narrative review from the model's own laboratory holds that PTSD and its subtype "are thought to differ in their proclivity to elicit active (i.e., fight, flight) versus passive (i.e., tonic immobility, emotional shutdown) defensive responses", with the periaqueductal grey in a unifying role (Terpou et al. 2019). In the evolved defence cascade, passive immobility is the last resort against inescapable threat (Kozlowska et al. 2015). Laurin et al. (2026), a theoretical model, give the [[predictive-processing]] form, in which "emotional over-inhibition and anterior insula hypoactivity weaken priors and interoception, while exteroceptive inputs dominate". This is the trauma form of the active-inference account the Map engages for [[depersonalisation]]. Like that account it is a model of brain function, and the Map treats it as a rival.

## What Held and What Did Not {#ledger}

**The class held.** Brewin et al. (2025) count "Close to 20 studies" using latent class or profile analysis in support, and in one model comparison "the subtype structure provided the best fit to the data".

**The prefrontal signal held.** Raised medial prefrontal activity or top-down coupling recurs from the 2002 study to the AURORA cohort.

**The autonomic signature did not.** Passive defence predicts hypoarousal, but a systematic review of 28 studies found that "No clear trend exists across all measured physiological markers in trauma-related dissociation", "questioning the validity of distinct psychophysiological profiles in PTSD" (Beutler et al. 2022).

**Specific circuitry did not.** Brewin et al. (2025) report that "the implicated brain regions and the nature of these alterations have varied greatly across studies". Because most studies measure trait dissociation, the alterations "may reflect a vulnerability or proclivity to dissociative experiences rather than the neurobiology of an active dissociative experience". A whole-brain resting-state study of 197 participants, from the model's home laboratory, found in the subtype "widespread small-scale and large-scale functional hyperconnectivity, compared to controls" (Shaw et al. 2023; preprint wording).

**The treatment difference did not.** A meta-analysis of 21 psychotherapy trials with 1,714 patients found "Pre-treatment dissociation was not related to treatment effectiveness" (Hoeboer et al. 2020). Brewin et al. (2025) still say the subtype "may respond less well"; for psychotherapy the effect is small or null.

**The mirror image failed, and the dynamic version survived.** If the dissociative state were the overmodulated mirror image of re-experiencing, the dissociative class should re-experience less. It re-experiences more. Dissociation is "associated with high counts of re-experiencing symptoms" (Stein et al. 2013), and a dissociative latent class "was also marked by higher reexperiencing symptoms" (Burton et al. 2018). The 2010 paper had already said the two modulation types "lead to alternating symptom profiles in PTSD". Chiba et al. (2021), drawing on behavioural and neuroimaging meta-analyses, model reciprocal inhibition between amygdala and vmPFC with alternation within the same patients, though their overmodulated pole is avoidance rather than dissociation. Read that way, the subtype is a disposition to spend more time in one state of a single bistable circuit.

None of these failures helps the interface reading. They concern how the state is implemented, not whether a brain-only account of it exists, and a less tidy rival is still a rival.

## Relation to the Depersonalisation Readings

The Map engages two production families for depersonalisation ([[depersonalisation#Four Readings of What Comes Apart|the four readings]]): corticolimbic inhibition and active inference. The subtype model is not a third. Sierra and Berrios (1998), a theoretical model, proposed that "a left-sided prefrontal mechanism would inhibit the amygdala"; Lanius et al. (2010) cite that paper and propose midline prefrontal inhibition of limbic regions. The nodes differ and the architecture is the same. Laurin et al. (2026) transpose the active-inference family to trauma.

The subtype literature adds four things the depersonalisation-disorder literature lacks:

1. **A within-trauma control.** Trauma exposure and PTSD diagnosis are held fixed and only dissociation varies (Felmingham et al. 2008; Nicholson et al. 2017).
2. **State provocation.** Script-driven imagery evokes the state on demand in the scanner (Lanius et al. 2002).
3. **A functional rationale.** The defence cascade explains why a regulatory system would have a setting for detachment under inescapable threat. As the Map presents it, the corticolimbic family has a mechanism without a reason.
4. **Single-dial symmetry.** One balance, tipped either way, yields hyperarousal or detachment. Any reading that counts the two as separate channels faces this single-mechanism deflation.

It also changes two things. First, its own psychometrics press on its affect-first premise: a factor analysis of 557 participants extracted "Emotional Numbing/Anhedonia" and "Dissociation" as separate factors (Frewen et al. 2015). The post-traumatic-numbing point that [[depersonalisation]] credits to Knappik (2022), who cites Frewen et al., comes from this field, and it presses on Lanius as hard as on Sierra and Berrios. Second, conditions that share the symptom do not share a neural signature. A systematic review of 33 MRI studies found that DID and dissociative PTSD "showed more overlap in brain activation than each group showed with depersonalization/derealization disorder", and concluded that "dissociative processing cannot be localized to a few distinctive brain regions" (Lotfinia et al. 2020). That favours Knappik's type-specific reading at the architecture tier. It also removes any support the Map's single self-model channel might have drawn from a shared signature, in line with what [[logic-of-dissociation-inference|dissociations license]] about components.

The subtype says nothing new about *what* comes apart: the standoff among Billon, Zahavi and Knappik over [[mine-ness]] is untouched.

## The Hippocampal Question

The subtype literature also supplies the comparison groups behind a caveat the Map already carries. [[metacognitive-access-versus-retention]] cites smaller hippocampal and CA1 volumes in DID (Dimitrova et al. 2023) as a group-level structural difference, noting that the patients all had PTSD. That rung stands; its specificity does not. In Dimitrova's sample "Twenty-nine volunteers with DID had a co-morbid diagnosis of PTSD and three had PTSD in remission", and controls were excluded for "a history of trauma". Among trauma-exposed groups, PTSD-only patients also had smaller hippocampi, findings their authors read as support for "a childhood trauma-related etiology for abnormal hippocampal morphology in both PTSD and DID" (Chalavi et al. 2015). The one group with dissociative amnesia or DID *without* PTSD (n = 13) "displayed normal amygdala and hippocampal size" (Weniger et al. 2008).

The published exchange has two sides. Huntjens et al. (2022) argued that "the results equally favor the interpretation that PTSD and hippocampal CA1 volume reduction are linked", and pressed the multiple-testing problem. Reinders et al. (2022) rejected the first point and accepted the second ("Here, we agree"); on a priori regions the CA1–amnesia correlation held (r = −0.398). Reinders et al. recommend the study that would test the PTSD confound, PTSD with and without dissociative features; none was found. The DID-specific residue is a correlation with *self-reported* amnesia, which reaches report rather than retention.

## What the Model Does to the Interface Reading {#interface-reading}

The flagship's [[clinical-dissociation-as-systematic-evidence#The Trauma Connection|trauma section]] names the *apparent clinical selectivity* of trauma-linked dissociation as what "stands in need of explanation". It sets "consciousness withdrawing from specific channels to protect itself" against what it calls an affective-salience physicalism, and leaves the choice open. For depersonalisation and derealisation, the subtype literature tips it against the Map. On the rival's account, the protectiveness is the passive end of an evolved defence cascade. The selectivity is what prefrontal regulation of threat circuitry does when threat is inescapable. The purposiveness is inherited from evolution rather than chosen by a withdrawing subject. Both readings' "protective" is a claim about the moment. The [[peritraumatic-dissociation|peritraumatic literature]] finds dissociation at the time of trauma a marker of worse later outcome, and the direct tests the Map has found show no relief of felt distress. The protective half of the datum is weaker for the rival and the interface reading alike; the comparative verdict on selectivity stands.

The rival reaches the datum from its core, with a mechanism, a provocation paradigm and a partner state at the circuit's other setting. The protective-withdrawal reading reaches it only by positing a subject's purpose and a channel structure it does not independently specify. Calling the datum merely compatible with both would understate the result: the rival explains it at least as well, and with a mechanism, which counts against the interface reading's comparative standing on this point. Whether the defence cascade extends to dissociative amnesia and DID is not examined here.

On the [[architecture-vs-significance-two-tier-discount|two tiers]], the subtype is a robust class at the architecture tier and supplies nothing at the significance tier. On the [[phenomenal-output-causal-machinery-dissociation|four-level dissociation chain]] it acts at mechanistic independence and does not reach irreducibility.

Three readings would co-opt the literature for the Map, and each fails:

1. **"Top-down" is a direction within the brain.** Prefrontal-to-limbic regulation is one region modulating another. Citing it for mind-to-brain causation slides from access-level to phenomenal causation.
2. **"Only during conscious processing" is an access-level finding.** Felmingham's manipulation was backward masking, which changes global availability, and workspace theories predict that a regulatory loop engages on globally available content.
3. **Inhibition is more coupling, not disconnection.** As in [[conversion-disorder-as-consciousness-side-fault|conversion disorder]], whose symptom is actively generated, trauma-related depersonalisation looks like increased coupling (top-down overregulation, widespread hyperconnectivity) rather than a channel falling silent. The flagship's "selective disconnections" fits it poorly.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]**: compatible with, not evidence for, and on one point the balance moves away from the Map. The model answers the flagship's open question, why trauma-linked depersonalisation looks selective and protective, with a mechanism whose purposiveness evolution supplied. The Map's reply is limited. The model says when, and through which circuitry, the sense of unreality arises; it does not say why inhibited limbic output should feel unreal, so the bare thesis that consciousness is not reducible to physical processes is untouched. Trauma-related depersonalisation constrains the interface reading's protective-withdrawal gloss without establishing physicalism. The weaknesses in the model's circuitry give the Map nothing: they make the rival messier, not absent.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]**: not engaged. The model's "top-down" runs from prefrontal cortex to limbic regions inside the brain and supports at most the access-level reading of mental causation.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]]** and **[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]]**: not engaged.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]**: the one-dial, two-setting model was the rival's own simplicity bet, and co-elevated re-experiencing, within-patient alternation and hyperconnectivity have complicated it. The tenet's warning applies to the rival's tidiness as much as to the Map's single-channel phrasing, and the complication transfers no credit to the interface reading.
