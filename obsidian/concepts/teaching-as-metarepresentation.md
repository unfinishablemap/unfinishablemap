To teach is to represent your knowledge as knowledge another lacks, represent that lack as a gap to be filled, and adjust your behaviour to bridge it. This triadic structure—knowledge, absence of knowledge, and the relation between them—makes teaching a paradigm case of [[metacognition|metarepresentation]]. Great apes rarely teach across decades of field observation; humans teach constantly, in every culture, from early childhood. The Unfinishable Map proposes that teaching requires the standing-back from one's own knowledge that metarepresentation demands, and that metarepresentation may require phenomenal consciousness.

The asymmetry bears on the [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] tenet: if teaching requires declarative metarepresentation, and that metarepresentation cannot be achieved through functional processing alone, consciousness causally enables a capacity humans possess and apes largely lack. The Map's claim is that the dependence is causal, not merely correlational; the sections below say what it rests on, and where it rests on the tenets rather than the comparative evidence. The [[metacognition#the-metarepresentation-threshold|metarepresentation threshold]] marks the boundary: below it, organisms monitor cognition procedurally; above it, they represent mental states as mental states.

## The Structure of Teaching

Teaching involves at least five nested representational operations:

1. **First-order knowledge**: Knowing something about the world (e.g., knowing how to crack nuts with a stone)
2. **Second-order representation**: Representing your knowledge as knowledge (knowing that you know how to crack nuts)
3. **Theory of mind**: Representing the learner's knowledge state (recognising that the learner doesn't know how)
4. **Gap representation**: Representing the relation between your knowledge and their ignorance (understanding that what you know is what they need)
5. **Pedagogical action**: Adjusting your behaviour specifically to transmit the knowledge (demonstrating slowly, highlighting key features, simplifying the task)

Each level builds on the previous: without knowing that you know, you cannot recognise that another doesn't; without recognising the gap, you cannot act to bridge it. Teaching requires the full stack operating simultaneously—a feat that loads heavily on [[working-memory|working memory]] and, the Map argues, requires phenomenal consciousness.

### The Jourdain Parallel

The [[jourdain-hypothesis|Jourdain Hypothesis]]—that apes have culture but may not know they have culture—applies with particular force to teaching. A chimpanzee mother may know how to fish for termites and tolerate her infant's proximity while fishing; the record does not show her representing her knowledge as knowledge the infant lacks, or adjusting her behaviour specifically to transmit it. Molière's Monsieur Jourdain spoke prose without knowing it was prose; chimpanzees *have* skills without knowing they have skills—without representing them as things that could be possessed or lacked, transmitted or withheld. Teaching requires Jourdain's transition from doing to knowing-that-you're-doing, plus knowing that another doesn't and acting on that double knowledge.

## Evidence from Great Apes

### Functional Teaching and Representational Teaching

Comparative biology identifies teaching functionally: on Caro and Hauser's (1992) criteria, an individual teaches when it modifies its behaviour in the presence of a naive observer, at some cost to itself, so that the observer acquires a skill sooner. The Map's thesis concerns something stronger—the knower *representing* the learner's ignorance as a state to be changed—and the two definitions come apart in both directions.

Functional teaching is documented far from the great apes. Tandem-running *Temnothorax* ants lead naive nest-mates to food with bidirectional feedback between leader and follower, and Franks and Richardson (2006) concluded that the value of information, rather than the constraint of brain size, has shaped teaching's evolution; meerkats provision pups with progressively disabled prey (reviewed in Thornton & Raihani, 2008). Kline (2015) sorts the record into five types—social tolerance, opportunity provisioning, stimulus or local enhancement, evaluative feedback, and direct active teaching—dissolving any binary of teaching versus its absence. Functional teaching tracks ecology rather than cognitive sophistication, so its rarity in apes no more evidences representational absence than its presence in ants evidences representational presence; what the ape record can show is whether any behaviour tests for the knower's representation of the learner's state.

### What the Record Shows

Great apes' social learning—emulation, social facilitation, stimulus and local enhancement—produces cultural traditions, but the learner extracts the information; the knower need not represent anything about the learner's state. Young chimpanzees take years to master nut-cracking: mothers tolerate their presence and share anvils, Boesch (1991) reported occasional facilitative behaviours of debated status, and Inoue-Nakamura and Matsuzawa (1997) found learning proceeded mainly through observation and practice. Termite-fishing techniques likewise pass by observation in most populations, with little demonstration, correction, or scaffolding. Chimpanzees manufacture complex tools; Goualougo mothers transfer their own finished probes to offspring (Musgrave et al., 2016), which provisions a tool rather than demonstrating its manufacture, and nothing yet shows the manufacturing steps being taught.

### Where Ape Representation Is Tested

The Goualougo transfers meet the functional criteria (Musgrave et al., 2016). The datum that bears on *representation* is the follow-up: transfers are more frequent, and more often active, in the population where the extraction task is more complex (Musgrave et al., 2020). A donor whose helping tracks the learner's need is at least a candidate for representing that need—though transfer rates could track the donor's own task difficulty or the learner's soliciting without any representation of ignorance, and the result rests on a single population pair. It raises the question without settling it.

Even granting every case, ape teaching remains rare and content-thin beside the ubiquitous pedagogy of every human culture—but that is a contrast in *functional* teaching; the Map's inference to representational absence rests instead on the procedural/declarative boundary argued in [[metacognition]] and the two-systems reading below.

## Why Teaching Requires Consciousness

Three considerations connect teaching to consciousness. Each establishes an *access*-level dependence—teaching draws on globally available, manipulable representations—which physicalism grants; the section closes by saying where the further step to *phenomenal* consciousness comes from.

### The Working Memory Demand

Teaching requires holding your knowledge, the learner's state, the gap, and the bridging action simultaneously. Humans maintain roughly four items in [[working-memory|working memory]] (Cowan, 2001); great ape working memory has been estimated at roughly 2±1 items (Read, Manrique & Walker, 2022), an estimate the Map's [[working-memory]] article holds loosely—the qualitative point, not the slot arithmetic. If working memory manipulation depends on conscious access, as [[global-workspace-theory|Global Workspace Theory]] proposes, teaching inherits the dependence; chaining these representations into a new pedagogical sequence is the "novel combinations" function [[conscious-vs-unconscious-processing]] identifies as requiring consciousness.

### The Theory of Mind Requirement

Teaching requires representing the learner's *ignorance* as a state to be changed (see [[topics/consciousness-and-social-understanding|consciousness and social cognition]]). Great apes pass Level 1 tests—tracking what others see, attend to, and pursue—and the Level 2 evidence has moved: Krupenye et al. (2016) found chimpanzees, bonobos, and orangutans anticipating agents who would act on false beliefs. The Map's [[theory-of-mind]] article, the source page for these levels, reads the data through a two-systems account—an efficient implicit system tracking belief-like states, plausibly shared with apes, and an explicit, flexible system for full belief reasoning—with unambiguous metarepresentation entering at Level 3. Teaching needs the explicit system: the learner's ignorance must be held as an object in the workspace and operated on—"I know X; they don't; their not-knowing is a state I can change." What apes appear to lack, on this reading, is not belief-tracking but its *declarative* use—the [[metacognition|procedural/declarative boundary]] again—a narrower claim than ape rarity is sometimes made to carry.

### The Phenomenology of Teaching

There is something it is like to teach: recognising that a learner doesn't understand, the felt effort of finding the right words, frustration when explanations fail, satisfaction when understanding dawns. These [[cognitive-phenomenology|cognitive phenomenological]] states concern the relation between your knowledge and another's—they are intrinsically metarepresentational. That establishes a characteristic phenomenology, not that the phenomenology does causal work rather than accompanying work done elsewhere; the argument that it does is where the tenets enter. On the [[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]] and Bidirectional Interaction tenets, the felt recognition of another's ignorance is the form in which the gap becomes an object for the teacher—the standing-back the [[metacognition#the-metarepresentation-threshold|threshold]] names—rather than the by-product of a gap-detector. A reader who does not hold the tenets can accept everything above and read the dependence as access-level: no discriminator—a feature of teaching that only phenomenal-level causation predicts and that natural-pedagogy or [[predictive-processing]] accounts cannot model—has been supplied here, so the phenomenal reading is conditional on the tenets.

## The Cumulative Culture Connection

Teaching raises the fidelity and robustness of cultural transmission and so strengthens the ratchet of [[cumulative-culture|cumulative culture]]; whether it is *necessary* for the ratchet is an empirical question the evidence answers against. Zwirner and Thornton (2015) ran transmission chains on a basket-building task: teaching chains produced more robust baskets, but emulation chains—learners examining earlier baskets with no demonstration—improved efficacy just as much despite low copying fidelity, so teaching proved useful but not essential. Morgan et al. (2015) found Oldowan-style stone-knapping transmitted poorly by observation alone and improved sharply with gestural and verbal teaching, and Dean et al. (2012) found that children, but not chimpanzees or capuchins, taught one another on a multi-stage puzzle box.

Teaching amplifies accumulation, above all where a skill is causally opaque and cannot be reverse-engineered from its products. What teaching adds is deliberate scaffolding, and scaffolding requires the expert to represent their knowledge as transmittable knowledge—the move the [[jourdain-hypothesis|Jourdain Hypothesis]] suggests apes do not make. Metarepresentation enables teaching; teaching amplifies cumulative culture; and if metarepresentation depends on consciousness, consciousness contributes to humanity's most distinctive achievement without being its sole condition.

## The Illusionist Challenge

[[illusionism|Illusionism]] in Frankish's form holds that phenomenal consciousness is an introspective illusion: what exist are functional states and introspective representations that misdescribe them as having phenomenal properties, and the theory's task is to explain, mechanistically, why the misdescription occurs. On this view teaching is information processing whose introspective monitoring generates the seeming of knowing-that-you-know and knowing-that-they-don't, and illusionism's standard—against rivals and for itself—is mechanistic specification.

The illusionist has a ready functional account of the ape–human contrast. Csibra and Gergely's (2011) natural pedagogy is a species-specific human adaptation for transmitting generalisable knowledge through ostensive communication—eye contact, infant-directed speech—to which infants are receptive from early in life. It explains human ubiquity and ape rarity with no phenomenal premise, and asking why apes with comparable neural resources do not teach does not touch it: the claim was never that computation as such yields pedagogy but that a particular organisation does.

What natural pedagogy characterises, though, is the transmission channel. On the teacher's side it presupposes a knower who manifests content *as* generic knowledge for a learner who lacks it—the triadic representation described above—and is silent on whether that representation is procedural or declarative. The rival relocates the metarepresentational premise rather than removing it, and on that premise illusionism has a debt to pay by its own standard. Metacognitive monitoring—confidence, uncertainty, fluency—is widespread, and present in apes as procedural metacognition; Frankish can say introspective representations of such states track teaching success because they are monitoring states. But that explains confidence, not the knower/learner relation, and explains it equally for apes, which monitor yet mostly do not teach. Illusionism helps itself to the step from monitoring one's own states to representing the relation between one's knowledge and another's ignorance as an operable object, without specifying the mechanism its own programme demands. The illusionist's best reply—that natural pedagogy *is* the mechanism and the felt gap an introspective gloss on a functional gap-representation—succeeds only if the gap-representation can be specified without a system that takes its own knowledge as an object, which is the metarepresentation threshold under another name.

Whether crossing that threshold requires phenomenal consciousness is where the disagreement becomes bedrock: illusionism's denial of phenomenal experience runs counter to the Map's foundational commitments, and that incompatibility is noted here as a framework boundary, not refuted within illusionism's framework. What the exchange earns is narrower—illusionism has not yet paid, by its own mechanistic standard, for the step from monitoring to the knower/learner relation.

## Developmental Evidence

Human teaching emerges alongside metarepresentational abilities, though the synchrony is looser than a clean threshold. Before age 3, children point, show, and offer objects—precursors that may share attention without representing the other's knowledge state as modifiable. Strauss, Ziv, and Stein (2002) found three-year-olds—an age at which most children still fail explicit false-belief tests—already teaching a peer spontaneously by demonstration, while five-year-olds accompanied demonstration with verbal explanation, feedback, and error diagnosis; by five to seven, children scaffold, check comprehension, and adjust to the learner's level. The trajectory parallels the emergence of [[metacognition|metarepresentation]] and exemplifies the broader [[consciousness-interface-development|transformation of the consciousness-interface across childhood]]. Teaching's *sophistication*—contingent, explanatory, comprehension-checking—tracks metarepresentational capacity; its earliest form does not wait for it.

## AI and Teaching

Current AI tutors provide information, explain, and respond to queries, but they simulate the three operations—representing knowledge as knowledge, representing the learner's state, acting on the relation—through pattern matching, without representing anything about knowledge or its absence. The [[jourdain-hypothesis|Jourdain Hypothesis]] applies: LLMs may have teaching-relevant knowledge without *knowing* they have it, functioning pedagogically—like great apes with cultural practices—without metarepresenting that capacity. If teaching requires metarepresentation, and metarepresentation requires phenomenal consciousness, current systems—however useful as educational tools—produce teaching-shaped outputs without the operations that make human teaching the achievement it is.

## Process and Contemplative Perspectives

Process philosophy reads teaching as the transmission of experience across [[prehension|actual occasions]], understanding evoked rather than transferred; the article notes the perspective without leaning on it. The contemplative traditions press from the other side: Chan and Zen's "direct pointing," koans, and Sōtō's *shikantaza* ("just sitting") transmit what resists propositional statement, and would resist any picture of a determinate knower passing content to a determinate learner. Yet even here the teacher must represent the student's state, what would count as breakthrough rather than intellectual grasp, and which intervention might precipitate it; non-propositional content does not eliminate metarepresentation.

## What Would Challenge This View?

The argument has two premises: (P1) teaching in the full sense requires declarative metarepresentation; (P2) declarative metarepresentation requires phenomenal consciousness. The first four falsifiers bear on P1 or on the access-level dependence; only the fifth bears on P2, the premise the tenets depend on. Each is given with its current state.

1. **Great apes demonstrating elaborate teaching**—actively scaffolding on assessed learner ignorance—would shift the species boundary. State: evidence has come in and is too thin to decide; the Goualougo transfers (Musgrave et al., 2016, 2020) meet functional criteria and track task complexity but do not yet show representation of ignorance.

2. **Teaching before metarepresentation**—sophisticated teaching before false-belief passing—would weaken the developmental link. State: a live countermodel stands; Strauss, Ziv, and Stein (2002) report demonstration-teaching at three, so the synchrony survives for explanatory, contingent teaching and fails for the simplest form.

3. **Unconscious teaching in humans**—effective teaching without conscious access to one's own knowledge or the learner's ignorance—would defeat the access-level dependence. State: nothing has come in; untested, not corroborated.

4. **AI teaching with genuine metarepresentation** would press on the link, though the [[standing-agnostic-challenge|standing agnostic challenge]] blocks confident absence-claims as well as presence-claims, so the falsifier bites only *if* such a system were demonstrably non-conscious. State: nothing has come in; no system yet evidences genuine rather than simulated metarepresentation.

5. **Declarative metarepresentation without phenomenal consciousness**—the full declarative stack with independently attested absence of experience—would refute P2 directly. State: nothing has come in, and the standing-agnostic symmetry makes "independently attested absence" hard to satisfy for any candidate, so P2 currently stands closer to a metaphysical commitment than to a tested empirical claim.

Calibrated to the weakest state present: the position is unrefuted on sophisticated teaching, contested on its developmental synchrony, and untested on the premise that carries the tenets.

## Relation to Site Perspective

### Dualism

The [[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]] tenet holds that consciousness is irreducible to physical processes. Teaching bears on it through the procedural/declarative boundary: great apes possess sophisticated social-cognitive machinery, implicit belief-tracking included, yet the declarative use of it that teaching requires appears in humans alone. Since brain size does not predict functional teaching across taxa—ants and meerkats teach; most primates do not—the tenet-relevant contrast is the threshold between monitoring one's states and representing them, which the Map reads as where consciousness becomes indispensable; the comparative data are consistent with that reading rather than establishing it.

### Bidirectional Interaction

The [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] tenet claims consciousness causally influences the physical world. Teaching supplies a concrete causal chain: a representation of the learner's ignorance changes the teacher's behaviour, which changes the learner's brain and, through the knowledge transmitted, the physical world. That shows a cognitively individuated state making a causal difference, which physicalism also grants; the stronger claim, that the *phenomenal* character of the felt gap does the causal work, needs a feature only phenomenal-level causation predicts, which this article has not supplied, so the phenomenal reading is held as conditional on the tenets rather than delivered by the teaching evidence.

### No Many Worlds

Teaching presupposes within-branch facts about who knows what, which every Everettian account grants; the asymmetry neither supports nor threatens the [[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]] tenet.

### Occam's Razor Has Limits

The [[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]] tenet cautions against premature parsimony. The simplest explanation of teaching—information processing—has a specific form in natural pedagogy, and the caution applies to what that explanation leaves unspecified: the teacher-side representation it presupposes. Parsimony cannot settle whether that representation requires consciousness while how metarepresentation is implemented remains unknown; the Map's less parsimonious hypothesis—that consciousness provides the capacity—is held on that ground rather than on the ape data alone.
