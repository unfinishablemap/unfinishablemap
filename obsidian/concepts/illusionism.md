Illusionism is the most radical physicalist response to the [[hard-problem-of-consciousness|hard problem]]. Rather than explaining how [[phenomenal-consciousness]] arises from physical processes, illusionists deny that it exists *as traditionally conceived*: the "what it's like" aspect of experience—the redness of red, the painfulness of pain—is, on this view, an introspective illusion, though the discriminating, reacting and reporting are real.

This matters for The Unfinishable Map because illusionism is the most direct challenge to its starting point: if illusionists are right there is no [[explanatory-gap]], nothing non-physical to explain, and the [[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]] tenet defends something that doesn't exist. The Map rejects illusionism ([[#Where the Functional Account Falls Short|argued below]]): the mechanism the view owes by its own standard is not yet built, the illusionist declines to grant what the Map takes as a datum, that experience is what we know most directly, and functional roles underdetermine experience. Past these the disagreement is a boundary between frameworks, not a refutation.

Illusionism is the eliminativist strand of physicalism targeting phenomenal consciousness. Where the Churchlands' classical [[eliminative-materialism|eliminativism]] denies the reality of propositional attitudes, illusionism denies the reality of qualia. The two strands are independent, but Frankish (2016) grants that we might call illusionism "eliminativism about phenomenal consciousness"—though he sets the label aside in his next sentence, as suggesting a wider rejection of folk psychology not essential to the approach; he proposes "illusionism" to block that inheritance. Placing it there anyway is the Map's move, and it matters: illusionism inherits eliminativism's dialectical history, including the [[self-stultification|self-refutation]] arguments that arise whenever a theory denies the reality of its own categories of assertion.

## The Core Claim

Keith Frankish and Daniel Dennett, illusionism's leading defenders, hold that we *seem* to have experiences with special qualitative properties ([[qualia]]), but this seeming is itself a misrepresentation. Frankish is careful that illusionism denies phenomenal consciousness *as traditionally conceived* (his wording is "as usually conceived")—experience bearing qualitative, what-it-is-like properties, "whether physical or non-physical"—not consciousness in every functional or everyday sense; the illusionist still grants that we discriminate, react, and report. Dennett's [[heterophenomenology]]—treating consciousness reports as third-person data rather than privileged first-person access—provides the methodological foundation.

Frankish (2012) distinguishes three conceptions of qualia: **classic qualia** (introspectable qualitative properties that are intrinsic, ineffable, and subjective), **diet qualia** (the phenomenal characters of experience, "what-it-is-likenesses", with the classic commitments stripped away), and **zero qualia** (the properties of experiences that dispose us to judge that they have classic qualia). Diet qualia are the common ground many realists and physicalists assume; Frankish argues the diet notion has no distinctive content, so that once classic qualia are rejected only zero qualia remain to be explained. Illusionists accept zero qualia and reject classic and diet qualia alike. The anti-illusionist's reply is that diet qualia are exactly the explanandum, and that denying them any content beyond zero qualia assumes the functional analysis at issue.

## The Illusion Problem

Illusionism trades the hard problem for what Frankish calls the "illusion problem": explaining why we so powerfully believe we're phenomenally conscious when we're not. This splits into two questions—why the illusion arises, and why it resists dissolution. The second is critical: even philosophers who intellectually accept illusionism report that experiences *still seem* phenomenal, a persistence that makes the illusion problem potentially as hard as the hard problem itself.

## The Illusionist Spectrum: Hard to Subtle

Graziano (2024) frames illusionism as "a general philosophical framework in which specific theories of consciousness can be constructed", and proposes six hypotheses within it: the magical mind, hard illusionism, illusionist versions of integrated information theory and global workspace theory, [[attention-schema-theory|Attention Schema Theory]], and deep illusionism. He diagnoses the framework as underrated because "scholars tend to think only of the most extreme forms and miss the range of possibilities".

At the extreme end, **hard illusionism** holds that consciousness has no real referent at all—the entire category is empty. Graziano's own contrast term is **subtle illusionism**: the brain models something real, but models it schematically, so what introspection presents diverges from what is there. The distinction carries dialectical weight, because objections calibrated against the hard end do not automatically reach the subtle end.

## The Neuroscientific Mechanism: Attention Schema Theory

Graziano's [[attention-schema-theory|Attention Schema Theory]] (AST) is the most developed neuroscientific implementation of illusionism: the brain models its own [[attention-as-interface|attention]] schematically, representing it as having experiential qualities because that simplification usefully manages attentional resources.

AST sits at the subtle rather than the hard end of that spectrum—it is Graziano's fifth hypothesis, and he prefers "caricature" to "illusion", since a caricature implies something real being represented, just simplified. The bare regress objection ("if the attention schema *seems* to be consciousness, something must be doing the seeming") therefore fails twice over: it assumes the seeming is itself phenomenal, which illusionists deny, and it targets an eliminative reading AST does not hold. Engagement inside AST's own framework can only mark a step the framework has not earned: the central identification, that a system's *modelling X as phenomenal* constitutes *X being phenomenal*, is asserted rather than derived. AST explains why we *report* being conscious, not why there's anything it's like to be us; neither side closes the case against the other inside the rival's framework. See [[attention-and-the-consciousness-interface|the Map's engagement with AST]] for the full treatment.

## Major Objections

### The Self-Evidence Objection

Tallis (2024) puts it thus: "How can I be wrong about the existence of something for which I have inescapable evidence?" An experience need not be *veridical* to exist: the belief that I'm seeing a pink elephant may be erroneous, but not that I'm having the experience. Galen Strawson (2018) calls the denial of consciousness's existence "the silliest claim ever made", naming Dennett among the deniers—though not Frankish, whose restriction to phenomenal consciousness *as traditionally conceived* the charge leaves unaddressed.

### The Infinite Regress Problem

Raymond Tallis (2024) argues illusionism compounds rather than solves the hard problem: "misrepresentation presupposes presentation", presentation requires a subject conscious of what is presented, and so "all illusions presuppose experience". The "regress" label is the literature's (Frankish and Pereboom answer it under that name), not Tallis's.

But the bare regress assumes "seeming" is itself phenomenal—precisely what illusionists deny. Frankish replies ([[#The Illusionist Response Functional Seeming|below]]) that a system can represent itself as phenomenally conscious without any phenomenal consciousness existing, and he is right that the bare regress proves nothing: a representational system need not instantiate what it represents. The substantive engagement is therefore the [[#The Meta-Representational Bridge to Felt Unity|meta-representational bridge]] Frankish offers in the regress's place, and whether it can be built where the represented and the representing coincide—which the [[#Where the Functional Account Falls Short|five objections]] press.

### The Moorean Argument

Chalmers (2018), who holds strong illusionism "not incoherent" but "empirically false", presents a Moorean argument: people sometimes feel pain; if strong illusionism is true, no one does. Loginov (2024) presses against it, arguing the key premise—that pain has phenomenal character—lacks genuine common-sense status and smuggles in philosophical jargon; Kammerer (2022) that defending it begs the question. Morozov (2025) defends a reformulation trading on the bare experiencing of pain rather than its theory-laden description. The debate stays live, but the core intuition—that denying pain's reality is harder to believe than any theory motivating the denial—continues to exert force.

### The Meta-Problem Cuts Both Ways

Chalmers's [[meta-problem-of-consciousness|meta-problem]]—explaining why we think consciousness is hard to explain—is no objection to illusionism. Chalmers (2018) holds that the best arguments for the view are debunking arguments resting on a solution that explains our beliefs about consciousness without invoking it; none yet commands consensus. Realists in turn must say why consciousness is not idle in producing their reports, which the [[metaproblem-of-consciousness-under-dualism|interactionist response]] takes up.

## The Illusionist Response: Functional Seeming

The strongest illusionist defence is [[functional-seeming]] (see that page for the full treatment and the Map's objections): the *seeming* of phenomenal properties is functional, with no phenomenal properties behind it. Where dualists hold the redness of red to be intrinsic and irreducible to functional description, Frankish (2016, 2023) contends that seeming to have phenomenal properties consists in being disposed to judge, report, and react as if one had them: "seeming need not be phenomenal seeming" (2023).

The mechanism is **quasi-phenomenal properties**: non-phenomenal physical properties of inner states that introspection typically misrepresents as phenomenal (Frankish 2016). Gorbachev and Frankish (2025) name the governing claim the **distortion thesis** and leave the distortion's exact nature open. On one reading introspective *access* is real but introspective *interpretation* is flawed, like pre-scientific theories positing phlogiston; Shabasson (2022) gives a "false inference" version. Kammerer (2022b) argues the illusion is "rich": the cognitively impenetrable activation of an incorrect representation, not merely an incomplete one from which false judgments are drawn.

### The Meta-Representational Bridge to Felt Unity

In the 2016 *Journal of Consciousness Studies* target article that gave illusionism its name, Frankish sets the explanatory target himself: "how the illusion of phenomenality arises and why it is so powerful", with the content of introspective representations explained "in broadly functional terms". He argues for a research programme, not a detailed theory. The Map calls the account owed a meta-representational bridge, from machinery that models first-order states to the *impression* of a unified field of felt qualities. Frankish's wager is that it need not be regress-vulnerable: one can introspectively represent oneself as having an experience without having it, and the illusion needs no "unified audience" smaller than the organism, so the demand "but what experiences the unity?" never arises. In the Map's gloss, introspective self-models stand to phenomenal character as a map stands to the mountain it charts without being mountainous. This is a more demanding move than the [[#The Infinite Regress Problem|regress reply]], and the dialectic requires engaging it on its own terms.

The programme is alive on this question into the 2020s. Frankish, Graziano, Kammerer, and Shabasson have continued to develop illusionist accounts, Frankish (2016) and Pereboom (2016) answering the regress directly and Kammerer (2025) arguing that supposedly innocent definitions of phenomenal consciousness are implicitly loaded. Dennett (1991, 2017) supplies the best-known version, the "user-illusion" of a unified Cartesian Theatre. The Map's verdict that the account falls short is therefore a verdict on a live programme, not a defeated one.

### Where the Functional Account Falls Short

The functional-seeming move, in its meta-representational form, is the best version of illusionism available. Even so, it faces problems the access/interpretation distinction does not resolve—problems tracing to the gap the Map's [[tenets#^dualism|Dualism tenet]] identifies between what experience *does* and what it *is*. The five objections below operate in different registers. Four press Frankish using commitments his own framework endorses (mechanism, evidence, representational structure); the intrinsic-quality objection runs through dualism instead, and is honestly noted as framework-boundary disagreement rather than dressed as refutation inside materialism. The [[functional-seeming]] article develops the first three in detail.

**The vantage-point problem.** The access/interpretation reading analogises introspective error to phlogiston-style theoretical error. But combustion scientists had independent evidence (oxygen experiments) to reveal the distortion. For consciousness, no independent vantage point exists—the seeming itself is all the evidence available. Whether neuroscience describes the phenomenon introspection accesses, or merely its physical correlates, is what is at issue.

**The zombie convergence.** Frankish accepts that a [[philosophical-zombies|philosophical zombie]] would have identical quasi-phenomenal properties and functional seeming. The illusionist's own reasoning about consciousness is therefore causally identical to the zombie's—the framework cannot distinguish genuine philosophical insight from its empty mechanical echo.

**The self-representation problem.** This is where the meta-representational bridge does its real work and where, on illusionism's own standard, it has not yet been built. The map analogy holds for externally directed representation. But "I am phenomenally conscious" targets the representing state itself, and at that point the gap the analogy trades on closes: the state representing itself as experiential *is* the candidate for being experiential—unlike combustion, indifferent to being called "phlogiston." So the bridge owes a mechanism the analogy cannot supply: the two-part explanandum Frankish (2016) himself sets, now with the represented and the representing coinciding. The illusionist can reply that the coincidence is no obstacle—the self-model is still a model—but that asserts the disanalogy rather than deriving it, and mechanistic specification is precisely the standard illusionism presses against its rivals. (The Map names this the [[constitutive-vs-referring-observation|constitutive vs referring]] distinction: illusionism needs introspection to be *referring*—seeming and being come apart—rather than *constitutive*.)

**The seeming/processing gap.** Solving Kammerer's (2017) illusion meta-problem—why illusionism seems so absurd—would *explain away* our resistance. But phenomenal seeming's persistence under full intellectual acceptance is equally well explained by its being veridical: the "rich illusion" and "real experience" hypotheses predict identical phenomenology, and neither can appeal to the evidence to rule out the other. If the functional account strips all experiential character from "seeming," what distinguishes a functional disposition to judge pain from mere information processing? By driving a wedge between representing and experiencing, Frankish explains our *reports*; on the Map's view reports are not what makes the hard problem hard, and the underdetermination leaves that view standing without establishing it.

**The intrinsic-quality problem.** Frankish's core move—replacing intrinsic phenomenal qualities with functional roles—works only if functional roles exhaust what needs explaining. But the dualist challenge is that they don't. A complete functional description of colour vision specifies which wavelengths are discriminated, what behavioural dispositions result, and how internal states relate causally—but not *what redness is like*. Frankish treats this residual question as confused—a product of introspective misrepresentation. The Map holds it is the question that matters most. If functional roles were all there was to experience, the [[knowledge-argument|knowledge argument]] would have no force: Mary would learn nothing new on seeing red, because she already knew every functional fact. That she learns something—a qualitative character not captured by functional description—is, for the dualist, evidence that phenomenal properties are intrinsic in exactly the sense Frankish denies. Illusionists must instead say Mary acquires a *representation* misrepresenting her brain state as phenomenal—leaving open why evolution would produce so elaborate a misrepresentation, with the specific character of *qualitative experience*.

## What Would Challenge This View

Illusionism would be undermined if:

1. **The illusion problem proves as hard as the hard problem.** If each attempt to specify the mechanism turns out to need what it denies—shown case by case, which the bare regress does not do—illusionism has only relocated the mystery.

2. **Contemplative practice deepens rather than dissolves phenomenal access.** Meditators report that training refines it rather than revealing experience as empty. This is first-person phenomenological pressure rather than independent evidence—heterophenomenology treats reports as third-person data, so the illusionist absorbs what practitioners say about altered access without conceding intrinsic phenomenality. It would reverse into support for illusionism if practitioners reported the opposite.

3. **Neuroscience reveals qualia-specific mechanisms.** If neural research discovers mechanisms best explained as generating qualitative experience rather than mere representations *about* it.

The converse test applies to the Map: a mechanism meeting Frankish's two-part explanandum where the represented and the representing coincide would remove its main in-framework objection, leaving only the boundary disagreement.

## Structural Convergence with Epiphenomenalism

Illusionism and [[concepts/epiphenomenalism|epiphenomenalism]] present themselves as opposites—one denies phenomenal consciousness exists, the other grants it but denies it causes anything—yet both converge: phenomenal properties play no causal role in producing our reports about them. Under epiphenomenalism the brain state causes both experience and report, leaving the experience idle; under illusionism it causes a quasi-phenomenal representation plus the report. The illusionist has a reply: the representational and functional work *is* the causal contribution, so no further "what it's like" is left over to be rendered idle. On the illusionist's own terms the convergence dissolves, and the Map presses past this only by holding the residue Frankish relabels is real—which the convergence argument assumes rather than establishes. Like the [[functional-seeming]] page, it earns its force only given prior phenomenal-realist commitments.

The [[self-stultification-as-master-argument|self-stultification argument]] applies on the same proviso: granting phenomenal realism, the illusionist cannot claim their position is *informed by* careful attention to the character of experience, since on their view experience has no such character. Frankish denies the proviso—what introspection attends to is quasi-phenomenal—so the charge marks where the Map and the illusionist part company rather than refuting him within his framework. Granted the residue is real, the causal picture is the same either way: illusionism has renamed the hard problem rather than dissolving it.

## Structural Parallel with Content Irrealism

Placing illusionism in the eliminativist tradition has teeth because both programmes deny the reality of a class of states their critics take to be obviously real, so arguments accumulated against propositional-content irrealism bear on illusionism by analogy. Hutto and Myin's [[the-naturalisation-failure-for-content|Hard Problem of Content]] argues that no naturalistic theory delivers genuine semantic content for basic minds; Boghossian's [[status-of-content|self-refutation argument]] presses from the other side, that arguments for content irrealism presuppose the contentful states they deny. If both succeed they form a pincer, and each arm has an analogue here. Illusionism needs introspective states that *misrepresent*, and specifying *when* a state is tokened does not deliver what it *says*—the worry Hutto and Myin press against teleosemantics. And the felt obviousness behind Strawson's charge and Tallis's argument is the phenomenal-state counterpart of Boghossian's presupposition, though it binds only someone who grants that conviction is phenomenal.

**Honest limitation**: the parallel is structural, not an identity of position. Hutto and Myin do not endorse illusionism, and Boghossian targets eliminativism about propositional attitudes specifically; Frankish holds phenomenal-realism is separable from content-realism. The weaker structural claim still stands—a debt for vivid, dissolution-resistant phenomenal seeming—and whether it is dischargeable is what the [[functional-seeming]] objections press.

## Relation to Site Perspective

Illusionism directly challenges the Map's [[tenets]]:

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]**: Frankish's functional-seeming response is the strongest version of the challenge, arguing that what dualists call "intrinsic qualities" are mischaracterisations of functional states. The Map's response, from a commitment it chooses rather than derives, is that functional roles underdetermine experience: the gap between what a state *does* and what it *is like* is the very phenomenon dualism exists to explain, not a failure of introspection. The self-representation and intrinsic-quality objections above mark where the Map holds the functional account has not yet closed this gap.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]**: The Map's framework treats conscious effort and attention as causally efficacious; illusionism asks us to deny the phenomenal character of the very experiences that make mind-matter interaction seem evident, so the phenomenology of trying, attending, and deciding—central to the [[mental-effort]] model—would be mere appearance.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]**: Illusionism might seem parsimonious—no new ontological categories needed. But [[parsimony-epistemology|parsimony arbitrates between theories of equal explanatory power]], and whether illusionism has that power is what the illusion problem leaves open: its [[epistemological-limits-of-occams-razor|simplicity]] is earned only once the mechanism it owes is built.
