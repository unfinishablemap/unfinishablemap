**Sentientism is the view that the capacity for valenced experience — experience that feels good or bad to its subject — is both necessary and sufficient for moral status.** That is the school-neutral formulation the debate works with, and describing the position that way commits nobody to holding it. Its canonical statement is Bentham's: "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" Its modern development is Singer's extension of equal consideration of interests to every being that has interests at all.

The Unfinishable Map holds sentientism, but in a specific and contestable form: **phenomenal sentientism**, on which the criterion is the presence of felt experience rather than the presence of a functional role that pain and pleasure play in an information-processing system. That qualification is the Map's own, licensed by the [[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]] tenet and registered as [[positions/moral-status|P-MS1]], and the encyclopedia literature explicitly declines to make it. Two other things this article covers are largely absent from the wider Map: **speciesism**, Singer's charge against the anthropocentric alternative, and **biocentrism**, sentientism's hardest opponent — harder than ecocentrism, because the biocentrist concedes that moral status attaches to individuals and disputes only the experience requirement.

## The Criterion and Its Two Directions of Attack

Sentientism sits between two rival extensions of the moral circle and is attacked from both at once.

**Anthropocentrism** restricts moral status to humans. Sentientism's reply is the argument from arbitrariness, which Singer named **speciesism**: privileging *Homo sapiens* as such is a preference for members of one's own group over relevantly similar outsiders, structurally like the preferences that racism and sexism encode. The charge has teeth because of the insuperable-line problem. Any cognitive threshold proposed to mark the human/non-human boundary — rationality, language, autobiographical memory — excludes some humans (infants, the severely cognitively impaired, the comatose) whose protection nobody is willing to withdraw. Either the threshold is lowered until it admits many non-human animals, or it is retained and some humans lose their standing. Species membership cannot save the boundary without conceding that a biological classification does moral work no argument supports.

**Biocentrism and ecocentrism** press the opposite complaint: sentience is an arbitrarily *narrow* threshold, discriminating against organisms that lack nervous systems but have goods of their own. The Map's reply to Rolston's ecocentrism — that "adaptive value" is either functional description, which is not yet normative, or a genuine normative property, which needs the experiential grounding it lacks — is already worked out in [[phenomenal-normativity-environmental-ethics#Against Ecocentrism|the treatment of environmental ethics]]. Biocentrism is the harder case, and the [[#biocentrism|biocentric challenge]] is set out below.

Being squeezed from both directions settles nothing on its own; an unprincipled compromise would attract the same pair of complaints. Both objections turn on whether the experience requirement is principled, which is what the rest of this article is about.

## Biocentrism: The Harder Opponent {#biocentrism}

Ecocentrism locates value in wholes — species, ecosystems, evolutionary processes — and so must explain how a system with no experiencer in it comes to contain normative properties at all. Biocentrism gives that objection nothing to grip.

Paul Taylor's 1981 and 1986 statements are the rigorous version. The Stanford Encyclopedia's environmental-ethics entry summarises the position: each individual living thing in nature — animal, plant, or micro-organism — is a "teleological-center-of-life" having a good or well-being of its own which can be enhanced or damaged. On Taylor's account every such entity has equal inherent worth, generating a prima facie duty to preserve or promote the goods of wild living things as ends in themselves. Attfield's 1987 hierarchical variant keeps the ground (having a good) while denying the equality (all beings with a good of their own have intrinsic value, but some, persons among them, have it to a greater extent, and conflicting goods are balanced consequentially). Varner's 1998 "biocentric individualism" develops the individualist commitment with both consequentialist and deontological affinities. Schweitzer's reverence for life is the tradition's precursor.

The argument does not depend on any mysticism about nature. A sunflower turning toward light, a bacterium repairing its membrane — each is a system with a direction, conditions under which it does well and others under which it does badly. Sentience adds nothing to that good; it adds only someone to whom it is apparent. Why should the moral weight attach to the awareness rather than to the good itself?

### The Sentientist Reply: Interests Require a Subject

The sentientist's standard reply is **Joel Feinberg's interests-criterion** (1974) — the legal philosopher, not Todd Feinberg the neuroscientist the Map cites elsewhere. Feinberg's claim is that only entities with interests can have legal and, by the same reasoning, moral standing, and that "interests must be compounded somehow out of conations" — a mere thing, "however valuable to others, has no good of its own." The biocentrist will object that a plant is no mere thing, having exactly the "latent tendencies, directions of growth, and natural fulfillments" Feinberg's list denies to stones. But those yield interests only as compounded with the conative, and conation — wanting rather than merely tending — is what requires a subject.

That does not refute biocentrism, since the conation requirement is exactly what the biocentrist denies: his position is that tending suffices. It marks a foundational commitment — that a system with a direction but no perspective offers a description to be reported rather than a perspective to be represented — and locates the disagreement rather than settling it. The next argument carries the anti-biocentrist weight.

The biocentrist's key move — "a good of its own" — does more work than it can bear. The phrase runs together two things: *biological* good (the conditions under which a system persists and reproduces) and *moral* good (a state of affairs there is reason to bring about). The Stanford entry presses the point, following Williams and O'Neill, with an example the biocentrist must answer: HIV has a good of its own. Its replication conditions are as determinate as a sunflower's. If having a good of one's own generates a prima facie duty to promote it, the duty extends to the virus. The entry puts this conditionally rather than as a reductio — even if HIV has a good of its own, that does not mean any positive moral weight attaches to realising it — and the biocentrist has machinery for exactly this case. Taylor gives a chapter of *Respect for Nature* to priority principles for competing claims (1986, ch. 6), the first of which licenses self-defence against organisms dangerous to humans. That restricts which goods generate enforceable duties without appealing to mattering-to-someone, so the sentientist cannot claim that every principled restriction must borrow her ground. What it does not supply is an account of why the virus generated a duty needing to be overridden, and the restrictions on offer tend either to leave that open or to answer it in terms that look like mattering to someone.

The Map holds that the gap is not incidental but the structural feature of value that [[consciousness-value-connection|the consciousness-value connection]] identifies. Descriptive teleology is everywhere in biology. Normative teleology requires an experiencer for whom the trajectory is good or bad, because the goodness and badness are features of how the state is felt. Korsgaard's observation that "it is a pain to be in pain. And that is not a trivial fact" states the datum, though she grounds it in a Kantian account of the reflexive structure of valuing rather than in the phenomenal realism the Map defends — the datum is shared; the explanation of it is not.

The requirement must also say *which* experiencer, since every theory that grounds standing in mattering-to owes that statement ([[no-self-objection-to-phenomenal-value#implications|two layers]]). The Map's answer — unrefuted and contested, not tested — is that the demand is minimal: a momentary experiential locus, whoever this pain is bad for now, suffices for standing, and a persisting subject is a further claim ([[no-self-objection-to-phenomenal-value#maps-reply|a locus, not a career]]). Sentientism's diachronic stakes, thwarted preferences and the deprivation death inflicts, draw on that further claim, which the Map makes on agency grounds rather than on value.

## Phenomenal Sentientism, Not Functional Sentientism

The distinction the wider literature leaves unmade is the one the Map's position turns on.

The Stanford Encyclopedia's "Grounds of Moral Status" entry treats sentience among rudimentary cognitive capacities and reports the utilitarian view that the protection and promotion of interests, understood as presupposing consciousness, is morality's central subject matter. Throughout, "consciousness" and "sentience" are used interchangeably with no technical differentiation between phenomenal consciousness and access or functional consciousness. Applied work inherits the elision: behavioural and neural indicators are treated as if constitutive of the criterion rather than as evidence for it.

Two consequences follow if sentience is read functionally.

**First, the criterion inherits whatever indeterminacy the functional analysis has.** If sentience just is a certain organisation of nociception, aversive learning, motivational trade-off, and behavioural flexibility, then "sentient" is a cluster term whose borderline cases are settled by where the thresholds are set. The functionalist need concede only *semantic* indeterminacy here: she can hold the underlying functional facts perfectly determinate and merely unknown, with the vagueness in the term and resolvable by precisification. That much is coherent, and no more is needed. But a precisification is a decision, so "is this insect sentient?" becomes "have we counted enough indicators, on which sharpening?", and what moral status waits on is partly stipulated. The Map's [[consciousness-in-simple-organisms|case-by-case treatments]] read those cases the other way: they can be assessments of genuine uncertainty about a real boundary only if there is a real boundary to be uncertain about.

**Second, functional sentientism survives illusionism but loses its rationale.** [[illusionism|Illusionists]] hold that phenomenal consciousness is an introspective misrepresentation of non-phenomenal properties — that nothing has the felt character introspection attributes to it. Kammerer's work on why the illusion is rich and why appeals to its obviousness beg the question against illusionism sharpens that position. Grant illusionism, and the functionalist's criterion is left standing: the aversive-motivational organisation it picks out is exactly what the illusionist grants. What is not left standing is the reason for caring about it. The case for the criterion has always run through felt badness — Bentham asks whether they can *suffer*, not whether they can learn aversively — so once nothing feels like anything, why that organisation rather than any other generates obligations is reopened rather than answered. Kammerer takes up that burden himself in "Ethics Without Sentience" (2022c), arguing that if materialism is true phenomenal consciousness is probably not normatively significant and ethics should be rebuilt on non-phenomenal ground. The Map's reason for doubting such reconstructions is the one it presses against ecocentrism above — a functional description is not yet a normative property — and that is a commitment of the Map's framework rather than a defect in Kammerer's project on its own terms.

The Map's [[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]] tenet dissolves the first consequence and takes a side on the second. If phenomenal experience is irreducible, then valence is a real property of a real subject, the sentience boundary tracks a feature of the world rather than a threshold in a gradient of information processing, and the criterion cannot be eliminated by a correct functional analysis because it was never a functional claim.

The exposure this brings is worth stating plainly. Illusionism costs the functionalist a rationale; it would cost phenomenal sentientism its subject matter, since if no state ever actually feels bad then "capacity for valenced experience" names no property and the Map's criterion picks out nothing at all. No in-framework refutation of illusionism is on offer here: Dualism denies it, and the denial is a foundational commitment rather than a result. Phenomenal sentientism stands or falls with the tenet, and does not claim independent support from the applied literature that leaves the distinction unmade. What the metaphysics buys is not a settled verdict in any hard case but a different account of what the uncertainty there is — ignorance about a real boundary rather than indecision about where to draw one, which is why precaution rather than stipulation is the response it calls for.

## Disputed Implications

Some consequences follow from the neutral criterion alone; others only from the Map's phenomenal reading.

**From the criterion alone: overinclusiveness.** The standard objection recorded in the encyclopedia literature is that lowering the standard to sentience puts most animals on a par with most humans, conflicting with common moral judgement. This bites against hedonic sentientism most directly.

**From the phenomenal reading: a reply to overinclusiveness, and to disenhancement.** The Map's [[topics/phenomenal-value-realism#Beyond Hedonism|phenomenal value pluralism]] holds that hedonic valence is one value-bearing feature of experience among several — aesthetic quality, felt meaning, agency, understanding, connection. Sentience is then the entry ticket to moral status, not the whole measure of what is at stake in a life, which is why a being with the full range of value-bearing phenomenal features has more at stake than one with valence alone, without species membership doing any of the work.

That grading collides with the argument from marginal cases used above, and the collision has to be met. The humans that argument protects — infants, the severely cognitively impaired, the comatose — are exactly those in whom the wider features are absent or in abeyance, so the ranking does imply that a typical adult mammal can have more at stake than a permanently comatose human. Two things bound that without dissolving it. Status and stakes come apart: sentience remains the entry ticket, and equal consideration of *equal* interests is untouched by the grading, so suffering of a given intensity counts the same whoever bears it. And much of what those humans are owed rests on trajectory, history, and standing relationships rather than on present capacity. The residue the Map accepts knowingly, as Singer and McMahan accept it by other routes. Marginal cases show that species membership cannot ground the boundary; they never showed that every human sits at the top of the ranking.

The same resource bears on the **disenhancement objection**, which the Map has not previously addressed — though it answers only one version. If suffering is the only bad, a hedonic sentientist appears committed to accepting the genetic removal of farmed animals' capacity to suffer as an alternative to removing the causes of their suffering. Against wholesale removal of valence, value pluralism replies directly: the capacity for valenced experience is also the capacity for positively valenced experience, so eliminating it destroys a bearer of value rather than merely a bearer of disvalue.

The proposal actually in the literature is narrower, and that reply misses it. Shriver's "Knocking Out Pain in Livestock" (2009) targets only the *affective* dimension of pain — its unpleasantness, how much the animal minds it — on the model of cingulotomy and morphine, which relieve suffering while leaving nociceptive detection intact. Positive valence is untouched, as is every other feature on the pluralist's list, so value pluralism counts *for* the intervention rather than against it. The line the Map thinks available is narrower still, and is a direction rather than a settled reply: what negative valence does that no other listed feature does is register damage *to the subject as its own*, the mode in which a creature's welfare shows up to it from the inside. Removing it removes the capacity to be harmed in the first person while leaving the harms in place — the loss of an evaluative capacity, not merely of a disvaluable episode. Whether that outweighs the suffering prevented the Map leaves open.

**From the criterion alone: the criterion does not supply a count.** Saying what has moral standing is not yet saying how many bearers of it a given system contains, and the two questions come apart most sharply where individuation is unsettled — a GPU cluster, a tray of organoids, a split brain. On the Map's commitments the count is a determinate fact that no physical or behavioural inspection can be shown to settle, so applied arguments turning on numbers of patients inherit an assumption the framework cannot discharge ([[moral-census-opacity|moral census opacity]]).

**Under uncertainty: precaution.** [[birch-edge-of-sentience-and-the-five-tier-scale|Birch's *The Edge of Sentience*]] (2024) defines sentience as the capacity for valenced experience and argues that sentience is sufficient for moral consideration, supplying an action layer for cases where the metaphysics is unsettled — proportionate precaution triggered by credible possibility rather than by proof. The framework is deliberately metaphysics-neutral, and Birch himself assigns non-materialist views low credence; the Map adopts the action layer without claiming him as an ally on the grounding question.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** is what makes the Map's sentientism phenomenal rather than functional. Under materialism, "sentience" ultimately names some functional or physical organisation; the criterion inherits that analysis's indeterminacy, and under illusionism it survives while its rationale does not. Under Dualism the criterion names an irreducible property and the moral boundary is discovered rather than stipulated — at the price that the criterion is only as secure as the tenet, since illusionism, if true, would leave it naming nothing.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]** matters for whether the criterion picks out anything that does work in the world. If valence were epiphenomenal, an animal's suffering would be a passenger on the behaviour rather than its driver, and the sentientist's claim that suffering constitutes a reason would be harder to sustain. Because consciousness genuinely influences physical outcomes, the felt badness is part of the causal story of avoidance, not a commentary on it.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]** governs the precautionary layer. Dismissing sentience in unfamiliar architectures — invertebrates, and any future artificial system — on the grounds that "mere mechanism" is the simpler hypothesis is exactly the false parsimony the tenet rejects. The asymmetry of costs under uncertainty runs the other way.

**Acknowledged tension.** Sentientism's implications are not comfortable. Wild animal suffering, on this view, is a genuine moral concern at a scale that dwarfs human suffering, and the Map has no worked account of what follows practically. The [[ethics-of-possible-ai-consciousness|question of artificial sentience]] is harder still, because the behavioural evidence that grounds attributions in animals is exactly the evidence a trained system can produce without the underlying property (see [[gaming-problem|the gaming problem]]).
