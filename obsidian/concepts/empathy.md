**Empathy**, in the minimal sense every camp accepts, is the capacity to grasp what another subject is experiencing—their fear, grief or intention—*as theirs*. It is directed at another's experience and keeps a difference between the one who empathises and the one empathised with; beyond that, everything is disputed. Some say empathy works by inference from behaviour, some by simulating the other in oneself, and some by directly perceiving the mind in the body. The phenomenological tradition of Edith Stein, Max Scheler, Edmund Husserl and, today, Dan Zahavi rejects both inference and projection. It describes empathy as its own kind of experience: I am really given the other's experience, but I am never given it as the other lives it. The Unfinishable Map's reading, set out [below](#the-maps-reading-empathy-as-non-coincidence), treats this *non-coincidence* as the phenomenological form of the gap between minds. That reading is *coherent with* the Map's individual [[tenets#^dualism|dualism]] but does not prove it. A physicalist direct-perception theorist can accept the same phenomenology.

## What the Word Covers

The English "empathy" was coined in the early twentieth century to translate *Einfühlung*, "feeling-into." That was Theodor Lipps's term for the process by which we understand others and aesthetic objects. On his account we inwardly imitate another's expression and project the resulting feeling back onto them. The phenomenologists took over the word and rejected the mechanism.

Several phenomena that ordinary usage runs together need separating. Scheler's book on sympathy (1913; enlarged in 1923 as *Wesen und Formen der Sympathie*) distinguishes:

- *vicarious feeling* (*Nachfühlen*): grasping another's feeling without feeling it yourself;
- *fellow feeling* (*Mitgefühl*): feeling *for* the other, where their state and mine stay distinct;
- *emotional contagion*: being swept into a mood without registering whose it is;
- *identification* (*Einsfühlung*): a limit case in which the difference between self and other lapses.

Stein (1917) draws similar lines. She separates empathy from sympathy, from contagion, from "feeling-one-with" and from shared feeling in a "we." Contemporary psychology uses "empathy" more broadly. Frans de Waal's (2008) layered model places emotional contagion at the evolutionary core, with sympathetic concern and perspective-taking built on top. When the Map's articles speak of empathy without qualification, they mean the narrower, other-directed act the phenomenologists isolated. That act involves the self–other distinction that contagion lacks.

## The Phenomenological Account

### Scheler Against Inference

The classical rival is the *argument from analogy*, usually credited to John Stuart Mill. I observe that my own behaviour is caused by my mental states, I observe similar behaviour in another, and I infer similar states behind it. As Zahavi (2001) reconstructs his critique, Scheler raised four objections to this:

1. The inference is too complex a cognitive feat to explain belief in other minds in infants and animals.
2. My body as I feel it proprioceptively does not resemble your body as I see it, so there is no analogy to draw.
3. We empathise with creatures whose bodies do not resemble ours at all.
4. At best, the argument licenses the conclusion that the other body is linked to *my* mind.

Scheler also rejected the argument's two premises: that self-knowledge is purely mental and comes first, and that we perceive only bodies and must reason our way to minds. In our encounters, he held, we believe ourselves directly acquainted with another's joy *in* their laughter and their shame *in* their blushing. To anyone who objects that this cannot be perception, he replied that they should "address himself to the phenomenological facts" (Scheler, as quoted in Zahavi 2001, 152). Scheler also rejected Lipps's projection model. Of the classical phenomenologists, the SEP entry on empathy suggests he probably went furthest in abandoning the Cartesian picture of the mind as an interior hidden behind the body.

### Stein: An Originary Act With Non-Originary Content

Edith Stein's doctoral dissertation, *Zum Problem der Einfühlung* (1917), written under Husserl, gives the most detailed early analysis. For Stein, empathy is a *sui generis* kind of intentional experience. It cannot be built out of perception, memory or imagination, although it shares features with each. Its distinctive structure is this: the empathic act is *my* present experience, lived through first-personally, but what it presents is not lived through by me. The other's grief is "originary" for them and given to me only non-originarily. Stein stresses that this non-originary content is someone else's originary experience, given to me *as* someone else's, rather than a defective copy of my own. Empathy therefore always keeps a self–other difference, and the empathiser is aware of that difference.

Stein describes a process with three possible stages. First, the other's experience emerges directly in their expression. Second, I may "explicate" it, following it through as though alongside the other. Third, I may take it up again as an object of reflection. Even in the second stage she insists that I am *with* the other, never one with them. Against Lipps she argues that putting oneself in the other's place while suppressing the fact that there is another subject would yield only a "surrogate" of empathy (Stein 1917, via the SEP entry on Stein). Her basic stage is close to what is now called direct perception. On her account, what we perceive is the unity of expression and experience. The distress is co-given in the distressed face, not inferred from it.

### Husserl: Appresentation and Pairing

In the Fifth of his *Cartesian Meditations* (1931), Husserl calls the structure *appresentation*. When I perceive a house, its hidden back side is co-intended along with its visible front. In a similar way, the other's experiential life is co-given with their perceived body without ever being presented in person. The link is an "analogizing apprehension," or *pairing*, between my lived body and theirs. Husserl stresses that this is not an inference by analogy. The key point for everything that follows is that if I had the same access to the other's experience as to my own, the other would not be another at all. They would be a part of me. The other is given precisely as the one whose experience I cannot live through. As Zahavi puts Husserl's point, it is this inaccessibility itself that I experience (Zahavi 2001, 153). The Map's [[intersubjectivity]] article treats this at length.

### Zahavi: Direct Social Perception, and Beyond Empathy

Dan Zahavi has done most to bring this tradition into the current debate. In "Empathy and Direct Social Perception" (2011) and *Self and Other* (2014), he defends a phenomenological proposal on which others' emotions and intentions can be directly perceived in their expressive behaviour. Perceiving them this way need not be mediated by inference or simulation. Such perception is also compatible with the other's experience being given differently to them than to me. With Søren Overgaard he argues for "empathy without isomorphism": empathising with your anger does not require me to be angry.

Zahavi's 2001 paper, which the Map's intersubjectivity cluster cites often, argues for something wider than direct perception. It accepts Scheler's critique of the argument from analogy. It then argues that the most far-reaching phenomenological analyses go *beyond* empathy. On those analyses, intersubjectivity is already at work in perception, tool use and self-awareness, before any thematic face-to-face encounter, and empathy discloses rather than establishes it. Citing Zahavi for the rejection of the inference model is accurate. Citing him for the view that empathy is the foundation of intersubjectivity would not be. Zahavi is not a dualist. His target is the Cartesian picture of minds sealed inside bodies, and the Map cites him as a rival on the metaphysics, not an ally.

## The Contemporary Three-Way Debate

Since the 1980s, analytic philosophy of mind has framed the question as a debate about "mindreading." There are three main positions.

**Theory-theory** holds that we understand others by applying a tacit folk-psychological theory: a body of generalisations linking perception, belief, desire and action. In the developmental version, children revise that theory much as scientists revise theories. Gopnik and Wellman (1992) argued that the child's theory of mind "really is a theory" in this sense.

**Simulation theory** holds that we use our own mind as a model. Robert Gordon (1986) proposed that we predict others by imaginatively taking up their situation and letting our own practical reasoning run on those pretend inputs. Alvin Goldman's *Simulating Minds* (2006) develops a hybrid. It combines low-level mirroring, which Goldman connects to mirror-neuron findings, with high-level imaginative simulation, and admits some theoretical inference. The SEP entry on empathy notes that Goldman ties his account to a neo-Cartesian view of mental concepts, while Gordon's later versions avoid that commitment. Lipps's inner imitation is often read as a forerunner of simulation theory.

**Direct perception** is the phenomenologists' heir. Shaun Gallagher (2008) and Zahavi (2011) argue that theory-theory and simulation share a hidden premise: that other minds are unobservable, so that something must bridge the gap between seen behaviour and unseen mind. Drop that premise, they argue, and much everyday understanding needs neither theory nor simulation, because the mind is perceptible in expressive, embodied action. Critics reply that the perceptual claim either overreaches or collapses into something like fast inference. Pierre Jacob (2011) gives a sustained critique of the direct-perception model of empathy. Much current work is pluralist. Different mechanisms may serve different tasks, with basic emotion recognition at one end and recursive belief attribution at the other; the latter is treated in [[theory-of-mind]].

This debate concerns *mechanism*; the phenomenological one concerns *givenness*, what the grasp is like and what it presents. The two do not map neatly onto each other. A simulation theorist can accept Stein's description of how the result is given, and a direct-perception theorist need not settle what the brain does.

## The Map's Reading: Empathy as Non-Coincidence

What follows is the Map's interpretation, not a thesis any of the authors above defend.

Across all three positions, and in every phenomenological account, one feature of empathy is left standing. The empathiser does not *live through* the empathised experience. Stein builds this into her definition as non-originary content. Husserl makes it a condition for the other being other at all. Zahavi keeps it explicitly in "empathy without isomorphism." Even a simulation theorist, who says I generate a pretend state in myself, grants that the pretend state is mine and not the other's. The Map calls this feature *non-coincidence*: however direct, rich or accurate empathy becomes, the empathiser's experience and the target's never coincide.

The Map proposes that non-coincidence is the form the gap between minds takes *from inside experience*. The [[voids-between-minds]] article describes that gap structurally. [[problem-of-other-minds|The problem of other minds]] describes it epistemically. Empathy is where a subject meets it in lived experience. Read this way, direct-perception accounts and the gap are compatible. What is directly perceived is the other's experience *as* the other's, given in a way that cannot turn into having it. This fits the Map's [[mine-ness]] analysis, on which every experience presents itself as belonging to a subject; empathy presents experiences whose for-me-ness is someone else's.

Two cautions keep this reading calibrated. First, none of the phenomenologists above argued for substance or property dualism. Scheler and Zahavi aimed their accounts *against* the Cartesian picture, and Husserl's framework is transcendental, not a mind–body metaphysics. The Map adopts their descriptions, not their metaphysical conclusions. Second, the direct-perception view is, if anything, a pressure on one of the Map's own habits. Where Map articles say we "infer" others' experience from their behaviour, the phenomenologists would say the article has quietly adopted the analogy model that Scheler refuted. The Map's reading therefore locates the gap in *coincidence*, which empathy never achieves, not in *access*, which empathy may well achieve directly.

## What Follows and What Does Not

The neutral definition gives only other-directedness and a self–other difference. The phenomenological account adds a further point. The inference model misdescribes ordinary social experience, and the unobservability premise behind theory-theory and simulation theory is open to challenge. This is a strong but contested result, and Jacob-style critiques keep it live.

What does *not* follow is dualism. A physicalist can accept all of Stein's descriptions. Two brains are two token systems, so one cannot undergo the other's states. On this view, non-coincidence is simply the numerical distinctness of two organisms, and direct perception is an efficient perceptual system tuned to expressive behaviour. Gallagher develops direct perception inside an enactive, non-dualist framework. The phenomenology is equally at home there.

The disagreement with that reading lies at the framework boundary; neither side refutes the other on the other's terms. The Map's claim is narrower. If experiences are ontologically individual, as the Dualism tenet holds, then the non-coincidence that empathy displays is what one would expect to find, as a feature of what experiences are and not a limit of our instruments. The physicalist expects it for a different reason. The Map holds that the dualist explanation fits the *character* of non-coincidence better: the other is given as a centre of experience I cannot occupy, not merely as a separate object I happen not to be. This is a judgement about fit, not a demonstration.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]**: Empathy's non-coincidence gives the Map's view of each consciousness as an irreducible, individually owned reality a phenomenological face. It does not establish that view; as argued [above](#what-follows-and-what-does-not), a physicalist accepts the same phenomenology and explains it by the distinctness of organisms.

**[[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]]**: Stein and Scheler describe experience as *expressed*. Grief shows in the face as a unity with the grief, not as a sign of it. If that description is right, experience shapes bodily comportment in ways others can perceive, which the Map reads as an everyday case of consciousness mattering physically. The rival reading is familiar: expression and experience share a neural cause, and the experience does no work of its own. As in [[consciousness-and-intersubjectivity]], the Map replies that this common-cause picture owes an account of why the *felt* state, not just its correlate, is what the empathiser perceives and responds to—a reply that marks where the views part rather than a refutation.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]**: Theory-theory and simulation theory can look like the parsimonious default. They posit one inferential or modelling mechanism and treat minds as hidden. The direct-perception critique shows that this simplicity rests on an assumption, the unobservability of minds, which the phenomenology puts in question. This illustrates the tenet on a small scale. The simplest mechanism can misdescribe the thing it was meant to explain.

**[[tenets#^minimal-quantum-interaction|Minimal Quantum Interaction]]** and **[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]]**: Neither is engaged here. Empathy's structure is independent of where or how consciousness acts on the brain, and within any branch the empathiser meets a determinate other.
