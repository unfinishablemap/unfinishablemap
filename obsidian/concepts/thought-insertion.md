Thought insertion is the experience, reported chiefly in schizophrenia, that a thought in one's own mind is not one's own: someone or something else is thinking it there. Kurt Schneider listed it among the first-rank symptoms in 1939, though descriptions date from 1810. All parties accept a minimal description: the patient has the thought available to introspection, locates it in her own mind, and denies that it is hers.

What is disputed is what has gone missing. Philosophers read the case [[#Five Readings of What Goes Wrong|five ways]] (explained below), and none describes a conscious experience stripped of first-personal givenness: on four the inserted thought is still presented to the patient alone, and on the fifth (Billon 2013) it is not phenomenally conscious at all. The contested feature is *mineness*, the thought's presenting as the patient's own; Marie Guillot's (2017) split between *for-me-ness*, *me-ness* and *mineness* (set out below) organises the field. The Unfinishable Map's other articles use the reading "ownership preserved, agency lost", which is one of the five and the most contested.

The Map holds that for-me-ness belongs to conscious experience in kind, whatever its degree (the [[self-and-self-consciousness#Constitutive as Kind, Not as Degree|kind-claim]]). Thought insertion leaves that claim standing without testing it: the claim's falsifier describes a case no patient could report, so it is conceptual, and its inference that every case is "reported from somewhere" faces an unanswered objection. For the Dualism tenet the case is merely compatible: a physicalist self-model account predicts the same profile.

## The Clinical Picture

In forty texts from 1810 to 1932, "Somatic passivity was most commonly noted, followed by thought insertion, thought withdrawal, and made actions" (Kendler & Mishara 2019). Narrowly defined, thought insertion "is reliably identified but not specific to schizophrenia", and whether it is "solely an abnormal belief (or may also be an experience)" remains open (Mullins & Spence 2003).

Henriksen, Parnas and Zahavi (2019, hereafter HPZ) call Mellor's (1970) and Frith's (1992) patients "Two of the most used and recycled examples". Mellor's patient described a man using the patient's mind as a screen, who "flashes his thoughts on to it" (as transcribed by HPZ and Guillot; transcriptions disagree). One of HPZ's own patients said: "Sometimes, I get thoughts about death that are not my own thoughts. But I can't say that they are yours or someone else's." HPZ warn that "clinicians should not take patients' reports at face value", and that the symptom belongs to a wider self-disturbance: "Putting it bluntly, there are no TI patients".

Reports come in two shapes: a missing feeling (a patient quoted by Guillot from Allison-Bolger 1999 said her own thoughts might say the same thing "but the feeling isn't the same") and something added, such as thoughts seen like subtitles.

## Five Readings of What Goes Wrong

### Agency Lost, Ownership Kept

Graham and Stephens (1994) introduced the dominant split: the inserted thought keeps its sense of subjectivity, occurring in the patient's mind, and loses its sense of agency. Shaun Gallagher (2000) gave the distinction a first-order, pre-reflective form, the sense of ownership being "the pre-reflective experience or sense that I am the subject of the thought or movement" (Gallagher 2007), and later noted that his was "not the same distinction found in Graham and Stephens (1994)". Guillot (2017) calls it the standard account.

The strongest objection is Billon's (2013). Obsessional intrusions also arrive without felt agency, sometimes irresistibly, yet are not disowned: "Even then, patients with OCD will acknowledge that the intrusive thoughts are really theirs" (p. 296). If lost agency made a thought alien, intrusions should feel inserted. Nor do the reports fit: "Patients do not say that, although they have no agency for the thought, it nevertheless seems theirs. They explicitly deny this!" (p. 301). Gallagher replies that for an unbidden thought "I know at a second-order level that this unbidden thought was generated by me" (2007). That moves the difference into higher-order attribution, conceding that first-order agency loss does not by itself pick out thought insertion.

### Ownership Lost

On the second family of readings ownership itself fails and agency loss follows. Metzinger (2003), on Zahavi and Kriegel's report, takes such cases "to demonstrate that phenomenological mineness is not a necessary component of experience" (Zahavi & Kriegel 2016, p. 42). Lane (2012), as they quote him, goes further: "Phenomenal consciousness does not entail self-awareness; it is not stamped with a meish quality; and, for-me-ness does not play a determining role in its constitution". Martin and Pacherie (2013) hold that "thought insertion primarily involves a disruption of the sense of ownership for thoughts", with agency loss its consequence, caused by failed context integration.

### Billon 2013: Neither Subjective nor Phenomenally Conscious

Henriksen and Parnas (2019) list Billon's paper among counterexamples to the principle that consciousness entails subjectivity, but the paper defends that principle, the "Cartesian principle": "If a thought is conscious then it is subjective" (p. 293). Taking reports at face value ("If the patient says that an occurrent thought is not his, then it is not subjective", p. 299), Billon concludes: "In a nutshell, inserted thoughts really lack subjectivity but they are not conscious either, at least in the usual sense of the term" (p. 294). The patient has "a good reflexive awareness" of thoughts that "lack phenomenality and subjectivity altogether", so the account stays "consistent with the Cartesian principle" (p. 306). Billon grants that it "would, however, benefit from an empirical confirmation" (p. 308).

### Something Added

Billon's view later moved. With Uriah Kriegel he proposed that thought insertion "might be better explained by an extra sense of disownership (explained, itself, by an extra mark of alienness) rather than by a missing sense of ownership" (Billon 2023, summarising Billon & Kriegel 2015). Zahavi and Kriegel (2016) allow that their view "is even consistent with there being nothing missing in the experience of thought-insertion patients" (p. 43). Ratcliffe and Wilkinson (2015) describe "experiencing thought contents as alien, rather than episodes of thinking", and find "the agency/ownership distinction is unhelpful here". On these readings the case adds a feature and removes none.

### Universalist Disturbance

The fifth reading denies that anything first-personal is lost. Inserted thoughts "cannot lack minimal ownership altogether, since the afflicted subject is aware that it is she herself rather than somebody else who is experiencing them" (Zahavi & Kriegel 2016, p. 43). For HPZ, for-me-ness is "neither lacking nor unaffected but disturbed", and their criterion for its survival is that "The epistemic asymmetry is preserved" (p. 7). Henriksen and Parnas (2019) argue that had it vanished, "the experience of thought insertion could simply not articulate itself" (p. 12).

## For-Me-Ness, Me-Ness and Mineness

Guillot (2017) argues that "for-me-ness" has covered three features, with "no conceptual implication from for-me-ness to me-ness or mineness". For-me-ness is the first-personal mode in which an experience is given; me-ness is awareness of oneself in having it; mineness is awareness of the experience as one's own. "I thus propose to describe thought insertion as a condition in which experience lacks mineness, but retains both for-me-ness and me-ness." Depersonalisation "lacks both mineness and me-ness, but retains for-me-ness".

Three consequences follow. First, the live dispute concerns mineness: even Lane, on Zahavi and Kriegel's report, "actually allows that there is an utterly trivial sense in which the first-person perspective is retained even in pathological cases" (2016, p. 44).

Second, the double dissociation the Map's articles cite, thought insertion keeping ownership and losing agency while [[depersonalisation]] does the reverse, holds only on the agency reading. On ownership-loss readings both conditions lose mineness; on Guillot's they differ in me-ness; on added-element readings thought insertion subtracts nothing.

Third, the Map needs Guillot's vocabulary. [[mine-ness]] glosses mine-ness as Zahavi's for-me-ness and treats it as separable, while [[self-and-self-consciousness]] holds that "no genuinely anonymous experiences exist". With one word for both features, Guillot's inserted thought counts against the second claim; with her three, the claims are consistent: for-me-ness universal, mineness general but able to lapse.

## Mechanisms and the Physicalist Reading

Frith's comparator account (1987, 1992) proposed that willed intentions "are not monitored correctly". Synofzik, Vosgerau and Newen (2008) argued that "the comparator model can neither be extended to account for the sense of ownership … nor for the sense of agency for thoughts", and Frith (2012) conceded that "we still do not understand the origin of the most mysterious symptom of all: thought insertion." Sterzer et al. (2016), admitting that "no plausible account has been proposed in terms of the predictive-coding framework", propose that imprecise context-dependent predictions lend thoughts aberrant salience.

The physicalist reading at full strength is [[self-model-theory-of-subjectivity|self-model theory]], Metzinger's naturalism, which recognises no subject beyond the model. Mineness is a representational tag the self-model attaches to contents, and an inserted thought is a content the model fails to tag as self-generated or self-owned. The account predicts a thin perspectival feature whenever the model runs and a content-specific tag that can drop out: the profile all five readings report. Its weakness is a missing mechanism, which its own authors concede; counting that gap as evidence for the Map would be a gap argument, which [[evidential-status-discipline]] rules out.

## What the Case Does to the Map's Kind-Claim

[[self-and-self-consciousness]] states the claim: "Clinical variation shows the asymmetry can attenuate, distort, or destabilise — never that it fully dissolves while phenomenal life continues." Its closest published statement is HPZ's "neither lacking nor unaffected but disturbed".

**It survives.** No reading describes phenomenal experience without first-personal givenness: Billon's inserted thought lacks phenomenality, Guillot's keeps for-me-ness, and Lane reportedly grants a trivial first-person perspective.

**It survives on uncontested ground.** Every party grants the thin feature the claim concerns; the counterexample authors deny mineness, which the Map already holds can lapse.

**Its falsifier cannot be met by any report.** The falsifier is "phenomenal life continuing without any residue of for-me-ness, no position from which the lack is registered". Nobody could report such a case, and clinical evidence consists of reports, so its absence from the record is guaranteed by how the record is gathered. The falsifier is conceptual, and the claim is unrefuted but untested. An empirical test would need independent markers of phenomenal presence and of first-personal access that could dissociate in the direction of presence without access; none exist.

**Its supporting inference faces an unanswered objection.** "Every reported case is reported from somewhere", an inference [[cotard-delusion]] shares, runs Henriksen and Parnas's transcendental argument. Billon (2013, §4.1) argues that a thought's apparent location in the patient's mind is "a good availability to reflexive awareness" (p. 303), a functional property that does not entail subjectivity. None of the three universalist texts the Map has read engages this. The Map's answer is that the objection defeats the inference and spares the claim: if inner location is only reflexive availability, "reported from somewhere" proves a reporting position and nothing phenomenal, so the kind-claim must rest on the tie between phenomenality and subjectivity that Billon's own solution preserves.

Billon's reading carries a further cost: if a thought can be reportable without being phenomenally conscious, a report of "my thought" does not certify phenomenality, which weakens arguments that lean on such reports ([[phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence]]).

## Relation to Site Perspective

**Dualism.** Compatible, neither suggestive nor discriminating: the Map's interface reading and the self-model reading predict the same profile. Billon's warning against trying to "reduce the phenomenal dimension to the spatial dimension" (2013, p. 311) is congenial at the [[architecture-vs-significance-two-tier-discount|architecture tier]], but Billon does not argue for non-physicality, and his 2023 chapter looks to neurophysiology to explain mineness.

**Bidirectional Interaction.** A caution. [[phenomenology-of-agency-vs-passivity]] reasons from the double dissociation toward genuine conscious selection, a premise that holds only on the agency reading. On that reading a thought the patient generated feels generated elsewhere, so felt agency is an unreliable guide to causal origin, and arguments from agency phenomenology to mental causation must allow for it.

**No Many Worlds.** Coherence only: "not mine" presupposes a determinate subject to whom the thought is given.

**Occam's Razor Has Limits.** The self-model account explains the profile with one structure, but the dispute concerns what the data are (reports at face value or in clinical context), which simplicity cannot settle.
