Semanticisation (in North American usage, semanticization) is the process by which a memory of a particular experienced event—episodic memory, with its contextual detail and its felt quality of re-experiencing—gradually becomes semantic knowledge: the retained fact that the event happened, without the reliving. You once re-inhabited your first day of school; decades later you know you attended, in the way you know Paris is the capital of France. The transformation itself is common ground across memory science: trace transformation theory treats consolidation as transformation rather than mere stabilisation (Winocur & Moscovitch 2011), and the hybrid forms the process produces have their own named category, personal semantics (Renoult et al. 2012). What the camps dispute is what the transformation shows.

The Unfinishable Map's stake in that dispute is explicit and on the record. The Map's treatment of memory phenomenology names semanticisation, studied phenomenologically, as the sharpest available test of its claim that autonoetic consciousness—the felt re-experiencing that marks a memory as one's own past—differs in kind from mere knowing. If felt pastness fades by degrees as a memory semanticises, that reading loses on its own terms. This article reports where the test stands as of 2026, and the standing is mixed: the categorical question has largely gone against the Map—the leading recent literature argues that episodic and semantic memory are not two kinds—while the phenomenological measurement the falsifier actually turns on has not been made, the strongest phenomenal evidence available points toward discreteness rather than grading, and [[#the-third-reading-fading-as-loss-of-access|a third reading]] on which fading is loss of access rather than transformation of the trace complicates the test's own logic. The wager stands open and contested—not won, and not yet lost.

## What Semanticisation Is

In the hours after an event, retrieval approaches re-experiencing: sensory detail, spatial layout, emotional tone. Over days and weeks, peripheral detail thins while the narrative core strengthens. Over months and years, recall increasingly delivers gist; over decades, for most memories, only the knowing remains. The Map's account of [[consciousness-and-memory|consciousness and memory]] describes this trajectory phenomenologically; the neuroscience attributes it to systems consolidation, in which cortical networks slowly extract statistical regularities from hippocampally bound episodes and—on the trace transformation view—change what is stored rather than merely relocating it.

Stated this way, semanticisation is neutral. Every position in the dispute—functionalist, eliminativist, dualist—accepts that content persists while episodic specificity and reported re-experiencing decline, that the decline spans years, and that hybrid forms proliferate along the way. The dispute begins when the transformation is asked to bear on what remembering is.

## The Wager the Map Placed on It

The Map's reading of memory, developed across [[episodic-memory]], [[semantic-memory]] and [[anoetic-noetic-autonoetic-consciousness]], holds that remembering-as-reliving and knowing-that differ in the mode of consciousness rather than the information carried—Tulving's autonoetic/noetic contrast (Tulving 1985) taken as a difference in kind. The episodic-memory article draws the consequence for semanticisation directly: a memory that semanticises loses autonoetic character while retaining content, which "makes consciousness constitutive of the *kind* of memory you have" rather than an accompaniment to it. That reading is the Map's proposal, not a neutral rendering of the science.

A claim of that strength owes the world a way of losing, and the Map's treatment of [[phenomenology-of-memory-and-the-self|memory phenomenology]] states one. Functionalist accounts of semanticisation are absorbed by the Map's standing reply—they specify how the state is produced, not why its output is experienced—so counting them as falsifiers would be empty. What survives as a genuine falsifier is phenomenological: "semanticisation studied phenomenologically rather than functionally—tracking whether felt pastness fades by degrees as an episodic memory becomes semantic—is the sharpest available test." If careful phenomenological report grades smoothly between remembering-as and knowing-that, the joint the Map draws is an artefact of coarse categories. The commitment is stated so it can be checked—this article is the check running.

## What the Literature Has Returned: The Category Has Graded

On the categorical limb the field has largely returned the adverse answer. A 2024 theme issue of *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* collects a coordinated case against the distinction. Gentry and Buckner (2024) argue from transitional gradation: episodic and semantic memory grade into each other—through gradual semanticisation in one direction and the semantic composition of episodic recall in the other—so densely that they are not two kinds, and the authors "recommend replacing the distinction with a dynamic life cycle of memory in which a variety of transitional forms will proliferate." In the same issue, Addis and Szpunar (2024) push past the continuum to a multidimensional model of mental representations, and De Brigard (2024) argues that autonoetic consciousness lacks construct validity and that episodic memory's phenomenology is a contingent feature of retrieval. The one in-issue defence of distinctness, Cheng (2024), argues for a mechanically and functionally distinct episodic subsystem—grounds that leave the phenomenology aside, so it is no ally of a constitutive reading either. Outside the issue, a widely cited review concludes the two systems are "inextricably intertwined, yet retain a measure of distinctiveness" (Renoult, Irish, Moscovitch & Rugg 2019)—a continuum claim with a residue clause—and Rubin (2022) replaces the hierarchy with a continuous three-dimensional conceptual space in which self-reference, the dimension nearest the Map's concerns, grades like the rest. The graded reading of the category is now mainstream.

The argument form matters for how the Map files it. Transitional gradation is a metaphysical argument about kind-individuation. It is not a functionalist mechanism story—it does not claim to explain why remembering is experienced—so the Map's standing reply cannot absorb it. Nor is it a phenomenological measurement, so it does not trigger the falsifier. The Map's earlier two-way sorting of semanticisation findings—functionalist or phenomenological—had no slot for it; this article adds the third category. What a kind-individuation argument attacks is whether "autonoetic" and "noetic" name kinds at all—and whether it succeeds turns on how the graded properties relate to the phenomenal one.

## Where the Phenomenal Limb Stands

The paper itself answers that question: Gentry and Buckner's full text, verified against the open-access publisher record in September 2026, reaches the phenomenal limb in two moves. First, it asserts grading: memories midway through abstraction "may exhibit properties of both forms of memory—even partially autonoetic components," instancing Neisser's "repisodic" memories of stereotyped repeated events. Second, it concedes the appearance of a joint and explains it away: "the phenomenal experience of remembering does offer apparently sharp boundaries in recollective experiences," which the authors argue is "best explained in terms of an error theory"—why subjects would mistakenly suppose there are two kinds of memory. Autonoesis as a criterion on episodic abilities they set aside as "empirically intractable."

Assertion plus error theory is argument, not measurement. The measurement the falsifier turns on—longitudinal tracking, within a single memory, of felt re-experiencing as that memory semanticises—has still not been made. But a September 2026 search under the vocabularies the August survey named as its ceiling found each half published separately. The longitudinal half exists at scale under flashbulb vocabulary—fifty-seven test–retest studies of memory for public events (Frinco et al. 2024)—and its model design cuts both ways. Rating the same memories at delays up to a year, Talarico and Rubin (2003; 2007) found everyday memories' reported recollection and vividness declining by degrees—the falsifier's adverse direction, at the report level, access uncontrolled—while flashbulb memories' reported recollection held high as their consistency decayed at the everyday rate. Flashbulb memories are "not special in their accuracy, as previously claimed, but only in their perceived accuracy" (Talarico & Rubin 2003): reported reliving dissociating from retained content, the manner/content independence the Map argues from. The remember-to-know shift literature tracks semanticisation directly (Conway et al. 1997; Dewhurst, Conway & Brandt 2009), and diary and cohort methods reach the timescales without the instrument: Thomsen and colleagues (2015) rated reliving once, at a 3.5-year follow-up; Frankenberg and colleagues (2022) watched the same healthy participants shift from specific toward general memories across a decade, as narrative category counts, not felt re-experiencing. The conjunction remains unfound—trauma-memory, bereavement and non-English literatures unswept—a hardening null at a higher stated ceiling. By the Map's [[evidential-status-discipline|evidential-status discipline]], the classification is unchanged in kind: nothing has come in with access controlled, though the fade and the dissociation now stand on the record; the serious live countermodel stands at the level of interpretation.

What has come in concerns the instrument the test would use. Zaman, Setton, Catmur and Russell (2024), factor-analysing subjective ratings from 342 participants, recovered a stable latent structure for autonoesis—the construct is measurable, against the invalidity charge—but found re-experiencing and mental time travel, not the remember/know contrast—itself long modelled as criterion placement on a continuous strength dimension (Wixted & Mickes 2010)—doing the identifying work. Folville, Simons, D'Argembeau and Bastin (2022) found that older adults overestimate the vividness of their remembering and rely less on retrieved detail when judging it, concluding that "the subjective experience of remembering is more than the reactivation of memory content"—a sentence close to the Map's thesis, from the same study showing that the self-report scales any fading test would use are miscalibrated across groups. Cooper and Ritchey (2022) found vividness tracking gist strongly and perceptual detail weakly, with participant clusters weighting the two differently—the decomposition experiment one step short of the residual test the parent article names. Coane, Umanath, Cimenian and Chang (2022), rating memory for public events across twelve years with no usage instructions, found unprompted definitions of remembering and knowing matching the theoretical categories, and remembered and known items not differing in subjective strength. [[microphenomenological-interview-method|Micro-phenomenological interviewing]], finally, exists as a published instrument pointed at remembering, though its reliability is under active dispute (Høffding, Martiny & Roepstorff 2021; Heimann et al. 2022). The Map's sharpest available test is unrun—with an instrument that exists.

## The Strongest Phenomenal Evidence Points Toward Discreteness

Severely deficient autobiographical memory (SDAM) is semanticisation's endpoint present from birth. Palombo, Alain, Söderlund, Khuu and Levine (2015) describe three healthy, high-functioning adults with a lifelong inability to relive their own past from the first person, corroborated by absent fMRI and event-related-potential signatures of episodic recollection: "their past is experienced in the absence of recollection." Autobiographical content intact; the manner of access missing entirely. It is content/manner independence in its cleanest available form; [[episodic-memory]] and [[anoetic-noetic-autonoetic-consciousness]] develop the case.

Two cautions keep the evidence honest. SDAM is a trait, not an observed transition: it shows the two states are separable without showing that the passage between them, when it happens across decades, is a jump rather than a slide. And the same case serves the other side: De Brigard can read SDAM as episodic memory functioning without autonoesis, which is precisely his thesis. Discreteness evidence it is; decisive it is not.

## The Third Reading: Fading as Loss of Access

The Map's dichotomy—graded phenomenology adverse to its reading, discrete phenomenology favourable—omitted a third possibility, the one the empirical record most directly supports. Sekeres and colleagues (2016), in a seven-day forgetting study, found peripheral details lost faster than central ones—and brief reminders reinstated them, so that "loss of details is not always permanent, and may reflect both a storage and retrieval deficit." Barry, Chadwick and Maguire (2018), tracking autobiographical memories longitudinally with fMRI, found ventromedial prefrontal engagement rising and falling around a peak at eight to twelve months before re-engaging for two- and five-year-old memories, replicated across independent memory sets: the neural time course of consolidation is not a smooth decay.

The Proustian phenomenon points the same way, and now in quantitative form: odour-cued autobiographical memories cluster in the first decade of life—the oldest, least-rehearsed, most thoroughly semanticised memories a person has—yet arrive with stronger feelings of being "brought back in time" than word- or picture-cued memories (Willander & Larsson 2006). On the transformation reading, such memories should have the least re-experiencing left to give; on the access reading, this is what success looks like when a rarely used route is finally exercised. Involuntary memories tell the same story from the sampling side, reaching specific episodes—with physical reaction and mood impact—more often than voluntary word-cued recall: "the way autobiographical memories are sampled greatly affects the findings" (Berntsen & Hall 2004), and a fade curve built from voluntary recall, the route of nearly every study above, may understate what remains reachable by other routes.

If fading is partly an access failure rather than a storage transformation, a smoothly graded report profile is compatible with an ungraded phenomenal property: the grading would live in retrieval accessibility, not in the quality retrieved. When access succeeds, re-experiencing returns whole; when it degrades, reports grade. The Map proposes this access reading as a live alternative rather than an established result—still resting on a small evidence base, and largely undiscussed as a distinct option in the philosophy-of-memory literature. The proposal also cut against the Map's own prose: [[consciousness-and-memory]]'s consolidation staging now flags that the time course beneath it is not a smooth decay. And it raises the bar for the falsifier's design—which can now be specified from published parts: sampling at encoding by diary or dual voluntary/involuntary capture (Thomsen et al. 2015; Berntsen & Hall 2004); a graded re-experiencing instrument rather than bare remember/know, whose binary format is categorical by construction—a graded within-memory fade and a discrete flip generate identical remember-to-know data—with Zaman et al. (2024) supplying the validated graded structure; one retest per delay group, because retrieval alters the object—one shift to an observer perspective reduces vividness, and the reduction predicts accuracy loss at later retrievals (Marcotti & St. Jacques 2018); and reinstatement probes—odour cues, reminders, involuntary capture—crossed with standard recall, so a fading report can be sorted into transformation (fails under every route) or access loss (returns under the right probe). Each component is published; the conjunction has never been run.

## What the Definition Settles and What It Does Not

From the neutral definition alone, this much follows: memories transform; hybrid forms such as personal semantics are real; and any boundary drawn in terms of content, structure or function will be graded, because those properties demonstrably grade. A functionalist can accept all of that and stop.

The further claims are the Map's, and follow only if its reading is accepted: that felt pastness is a unified phenomenal property rather than a bundle of cues; that its fate under semanticisation bears on whether consciousness is constitutive of memory kinds or an accompaniment; and that the access reading, if it holds, would protect a discrete quale behind a graded report. Whether the falsifier's eventual verdict carries metaphysical weight depends on that framing—which is why the Map states the framing as its own.

## Relation to Site Perspective

Semanticisation matters to The Unfinishable Map because it is where the Map's reading of memory phenomenology most directly exposes itself to evidence. The Dualism tenet motivates treating autonoetic re-experiencing as irreducible; the falsifier staked on semanticisation is what keeps that treatment a commitment rather than an immunised doctrine. Reporting its standing requires three verdicts at once. On the decisive phenomenological measurement, nothing has come in: the constitutive reading is unrefuted and untested, the weakest of the falsifier states, and the moderate confidence the parent article assigns should be read against that. On the kind question, a serious live countermodel stands: transitional gradation, with graded autonoesis asserted and the apparent sharpness of recollective experience explained away by an error theory. On the instrument question, the evidence that exists is too thin and too miscalibrated to decide.

The Occam's-limits tenet bears on how much the gradation argument can claim. Replacing a dichotomy with a single graded kind because transitional forms proliferate is a parsimony-driven revision, and with the decisive phenomenal measurement missing, the one-kind ontology is underdetermined at exactly the point in dispute. The Map declines to concede the phenomenal limb to an error theory in advance of measurement—while accepting, on the record, that if longitudinal phenomenological tracking ever shows felt pastness fading by degrees within a memory, with access effects controlled, the constitutive reading loses on its own terms. The remaining tenets are not implicated: nothing here bears on the Map's quantum-interaction commitments or its rejection of Many Worlds, in either direction.
