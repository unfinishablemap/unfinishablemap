The Galilean exclusion is the move, made systematic in one influential reconstruction of modern science, by which mathematical physics excluded sensible qualities from its explanatory primitives. Galileo's distinction between primary qualities (shape, size, motion, number — properties he could not conceive a body without, and which mathematics describes) and secondary qualities (colour, taste, warmth, sound-as-experienced — which he located in the sentient body) kept the first as the vocabulary of physics and relocated the second into the perceiving animal, where they were to be explained causally by the first. The passage was incidental to Galileo's own programme (Miller, 2025); Husserl and Whitehead later read it as founding. That reconstruction is interpretive, but the exclusion it names is real: it is one of the conditions of modern science's success, and it shaped the modern form of the [[hard-problem-of-consciousness|hard problem of consciousness]] without supplying the problem's force.

## The Distinction and Its Logic

In *The Assayer* (1623), Galileo argued that qualities like heat, colour, and taste do not belong to external objects but arise from the interaction between objects and perceiving subjects. A feather tickles, but ticklishness is not a property of the feather — it exists only in the person being touched. By the same reasoning, warmth is not in the fire; it is in the hand that feels the fire.

The vocabulary matters. In §48 of *Il Saggiatore* Galileo writes that he cannot conceive a corporeal substance without its being bounded, shaped, placed, moving or at rest, one or many — but feels no such necessity to conceive it as white or red, bitter or sweet, sonorous or mute. Hence, he says, "vo io pensando" — "I am inclined to think" — that tastes, odours and colours, on the side of the object in which they seem to reside, are "puri nomi" (mere names) and have their residence only in the "corpo sensitivo" (the sentient body), so that if the animal is removed they are "levate ed annichilate" (removed and annihilated); and we should not believe them truly and really distinct from the other "primi e reali accidenti" (first and real accidents) merely because we have given them different names (Galilei, 1623/1957, p. 274; *Opere* VI, 347–350). Drake's translation renders "corpo sensitivo" as "consciousness"; the Italian says *body*, and Finocchiaro's "sensitive body" is closer. Two features carry weight below: the passage is voiced tentatively, and it is ontological — the qualities are annihilated when the animal goes, and are contrasted with accidents that are real. Galileo is saying where the qualities are, not merely which variables physics should model.

What remains when the relocated qualities are set aside is the mathematical skeleton: particles in motion, describable by geometry and number. Galileo's insight was that this skeleton is sufficient for physics. You do not need to know what red looks like to predict the wavelength of light. You do not need to feel heat to calculate thermal dynamics. The subjective dimension is expendable — for physics. The relocation was also a research programme rather than a dismissal: the qualities now placed in the sentient body were to be explained by the motions and contacts of the primary ones, a programme Piccolino and Wade (2008) read as anticipating Helmholtz and present-day sensory neuroscience.

Descartes gave a related division a metaphysical architecture. His distinction of *res extensa* (extended, measurable substance) from *res cogitans* (thinking substance) placed experience outside physical description as a matter of ontology: physics governs *res extensa*; experience belongs to *res cogitans*. Cartesian dualism had its own roots — the method of doubt, the theory of ideas — and is not derivative of Galileo, but it supplied the two-substance framework within which the relocated qualities became a domain of their own. (Galileo already writes of "primi e reali accidenti"; the English terms "primary" and "secondary" qualities were introduced by Boyle and made canonical by Locke.) Galileo's own claim was thus already ontological rather than a methodological convenience — he *relocates* the qualities rather than merely omitting them — but it was a speculative posit, made in passing within an atomist account of heat, and it was Descartes who turned the relocation into a system. The [[primary-secondary-quality-boundary|boundary the exclusion presupposes]] is itself unstable — it admits no clean, non-circular criterion — which is the deeper reason the relocation never amounted to a genuine discovery about qualities.

## Why the Exclusion Worked

The Galilean exclusion was spectacularly productive. By restricting attention to observer-independent, quantifiable properties, physics achieved a universality that no prior knowledge system had managed. Chemistry, biology, and eventually neuroscience adopted the same template: describe the world in third-person terms, eliminate subjective variation, seek laws that hold regardless of who observes.

This success was not accidental. Subjectivity introduces noise. Different observers experience colour differently. Pain varies between individuals. Taste is culturally inflected. By excluding all of this, science gained precision, repeatability, and cumulative progress. The exclusion was a filter that separated signal from noise — where "signal" was defined as whatever could be mathematically described and experimentally controlled.

The filter's success reinforced itself. Each new scientific triumph confirmed that the observer-independent approach works. Over centuries, what Galileo had advanced as a tentative posit hardened into a default metaphysics, assumed rather than argued: the mathematically describable properties are not merely useful but *real*, while the relocated qualities are derivative, reducible, or illusory.

## The Problem When Consciousness Is the Target

The Galilean exclusion generates a distinctive difficulty when science turns to study consciousness itself. Two things need separating here. Galileo's secondary qualities were attributes ascribed to objects — the warmth of the fire, the colour of the body — which he denied were in the objects at all. [[qualia|Qualia]] — the redness of red, the painfulness of pain — are properties of the experience itself, the character of seeing or hurting rather than a feature ascribed to the thing seen. The two are not identical, but the relocation connects them: qualia are what the exclusion deposits in the perceiver, the residue that the primary-quality programme was to explain. They are what consciousness *consists of*. Asking a method whose primitives were chosen to leave that residue out to explain the residue creates a mismatch between the method and its target.

A natural objection: modern cognitive science has moved beyond Galileo. Neuroscientists routinely incorporate subjective reports as data, and [[concepts/functionalism]] maps mental states to functional roles rather than ignoring them. But this accommodation operates within the Galilean framework rather than transcending it. Subjective reports enter as third-person data *about* experience — verbal behaviour, button presses, ratings on scales — not as experience itself. The structural issue remains: the tools of description are observer-independent, even when the target of description is not.

The claim is best put as a conditional rather than an image. Galilean explanation is structural-functional: it accounts for a phenomenon by specifying the arrangement, dynamics and functional roles of its parts. If phenomenal character is not exhausted by structure and function, then an explanatory form confined to structure and function is incomplete for consciousness. The first premise is history. The second is the central disputed proposition of the philosophy of mind — the Map defends it in [[arguments-against-materialism|the arguments against materialism]] and in [[philosophical-zombies|the zombie literature]], and physicalists of most kinds deny it — and the history does not establish it. What the genealogy establishes is narrower and still worth having. It blocks the inference from the method's success to the completeness of its ontology: a method whose primitives were chosen to exclude quality cannot count its silence about quality as evidence that there is none. It does not establish that no expanded science, with different primitives or a disciplined first-person data channel, could explain experience; whether one can is what [[neurophenomenology-and-contemplative-neuroscience|neurophenomenology]] and the Map's own interface programme are testing, and the genealogy leaves that open. Whether the obstacle is peculiar to consciousness is a further question. The Map's [[emergence-as-universal-hard-problem|universalist reading of the hard problem]] argues that a remainder of the same shape survives every level transition, which would make consciousness the case where the residue becomes impossible to overlook rather than the only place it arises — a reading that article defends as well-motivated rather than forced.

The [[explanatory-gap]] between neural description and felt experience gains sharper diagnosis in this light. More than a hard empirical problem awaiting further research, the gap reflects the fact that the framework of physical explanation was assembled from primitives chosen to leave out the phenomenon now under investigation. Husserl made this argument in *The Crisis of European Sciences* (1936, §9), identifying what he called Galileo's "mathematization of nature" — the substitution of mathematical idealisations for the concrete world of lived experience — and arguing (§9h–i) that the life-world from which the idealisations were built becomes their forgotten meaning-fundament. Thompson builds on Husserl's critique of objectivism in *Mind in Life* (2007), and with Frank and Gleiser (2024) argues that modern science has come to treat mathematical-physical description as if it exhausted what is objectively real (a paraphrase of a theme running through both books, not a quotation; the Galileo-specific framing is chiefly the later book's). Everything excluded from that description — including experience — then becomes either reducible to physics or scientifically invisible.

Whitehead identified the same structural problem from a different angle, naming it the "bifurcation of nature" in *The Concept of Nature* (1920, ch. II, "Theories of the Bifurcation of Nature") — the splitting of reality into the scientifically respectable (mathematical structure) and the scientifically dismissed (experienced quality). For Whitehead, this bifurcation was not a discovery about nature but an artefact of the Galilean method, one that [[process-philosophy]] was designed to overcome.

## Not a Discovery but a Posit

A crucial distinction: Galileo did not *discover* that secondary qualities are unreal, and he did not merely *decide* to leave them out of physics either. He asserted — tentatively, in the vocabulary of accidents, in the course of an atomist account of heat — that they are mere names on the side of the object and real only in the sentient body. What warranted the assertion was not evidence about qualities. It was the success of a mathematical programme that had no use for them, and that success is what the posit has traded on ever since. The programme's productivity shows that mathematical physics can describe physical structure while setting consciousness aside. It does not show that consciousness is nothing beyond physical structure.

The slide from the method's silence to metaphysical absence underlies much of the consciousness debate. When materialists argue that everything real is physical, they often implicitly rely on the Galilean exclusion: if physics has never needed subjective qualities to explain anything, then subjective qualities must not be fundamental features of reality. But this reasoning is circular. Physics never needed subjective qualities because its primitives were chosen to do without them. The absence of consciousness from physical description reflects that choice of primitives, not an empirical finding.

This point cuts against more than one position. It tells the materialist that the absence of consciousness from physics does not prove consciousness is physical. It tells the dualist, symmetrically, that the method's failure to capture qualities is no evidence *for* their non-physicality: a method built to leave quality out would fail to capture it whether or not quality is physical. It tells the mysterian that the difficulty of explaining consciousness scientifically may reflect the method's structure rather than any intrinsic ineffability of the phenomenon.

A third position accepts the exclusion and denies there was ever a residue to exclude. [[ontic-structural-realism|Ontic structural realism]] holds that relational structure is what fundamentally exists, so the mathematical skeleton is reality itself rather than a filtered view of it. The contrast with Galileo is sharper than it first appears. Galileo's own account places secondary qualities inside the sentient body rather than in the objects causing them: he *relocates* the residue. Structural realism, on its strongest reading, *abolishes* it — but that reading extends a thesis about the ontology of physics to experience itself, and the extension is a separate commitment (phenomenal structuralism) rather than part of structural realism about physics. The circularity charge above does not reach a position that denies the remainder was ever a datum, so the argument against it has to be made on other ground; the Map's grounds, chiefly Newman's problem and the standing of phenomenal structuralism, are set out in [[ontic-structural-realism#the-choice|the structural-realism article]].

## The Exclusion in Contemporary Debates

The Galilean exclusion surfaces repeatedly in contemporary philosophy of mind, often without being named.

The [[hard-problem-of-consciousness|hard problem]] takes its modern form from the exclusion, though not its force. David Chalmers's formulation — why is physical processing accompanied by subjective experience? — rests on a conceptual and modal claim: physical description is structural and dynamical in principle, and no description of structure and dynamics entails phenomenal character. That claim would survive any history, and the genealogy does not ground it; Chalmers's premise gives the genealogy its bite, not the reverse. What the exclusion supplies is the shape of the modern problem — a physics whose primitives are structural, facing a residue it had placed in the perceiver — and the hard problem is less puzzling, though no less real, once one sees why the vocabulary of physics is structural: its primitives were chosen that way, and the choice was rewarded.

Theories of consciousness face a dilemma traceable to the exclusion. Approaches that remain within the Galilean framework (functionalism, computationalism, Global Workspace Theory) can describe the structural and functional correlates of consciousness with increasing precision. But they struggle to bridge the gap between structure and quality — precisely because the framework was built by excluding quality. Approaches that break from the framework (panpsychism, [[neurophenomenology-and-contemplative-neuroscience|neurophenomenology]], radical enactivism) gain resources for addressing experience directly, but risk losing the scientific rigour that the exclusion made possible.

The [[consciousness-and-the-authority-of-formal-systems|authority of formal systems]] debate also reflects the exclusion's legacy. Mathematics excels at describing the primary qualities Galileo kept. Its success with structure does not establish its sovereignty over the secondary qualities he relocated.

## Relation to Site Perspective

The Unfinishable Map's [[tenets]] engage with the Galilean exclusion at multiple points.

**Dualism**: The Map holds that consciousness is not reducible to physical processes. The Galilean exclusion provides historical context for why this claim is not merely stubborn resistance to science but a recognition of structural limits built into the scientific method. If the method was designed to work without subjectivity, the failure of that method to explain subjectivity is not surprising — it is expected.

**Bidirectional Interaction**: If consciousness causally influences the physical world, then the Galilean exclusion created an incomplete picture of physical causation. A science that excludes consciousness from its descriptions will miss any causal contributions consciousness makes. The Map's insistence on bidirectional interaction implies that the exclusion does not merely leave consciousness unexplained — it leaves *physics* incomplete in domains where consciousness acts.

**Occam's Razor Has Limits**: The apparent simplicity of materialist accounts of consciousness is purchased by the Galilean exclusion. Excluding the phenomenon and then declaring it reducible to what remains is simplicity through omission. Genuine parsimony requires accounting for all the phenomena, including the ones Galileo set aside.
