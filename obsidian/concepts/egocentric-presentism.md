Egocentric presentism is Caspar Hare's metaphysical thesis that, of all conscious beings, exactly one — oneself — has experiences that possess the irreducible, monadic, non-relational property of *presence*. Other people are genuinely conscious, but their experiences are simply *not present*. The view's structural core is an analogy with the A-theory of time: just as the A-theorist privileges *now* over past and future, egocentric presentism privileges *me* over other selves. The privileged "now" of time has a cousin — a privileged "me" — though Hare's own analogy is presentist and the moving-spotlight reading is the Map's (see [[#temporal-analogue|below]]).

The Unfinishable Map gives egocentric presentism a dedicated entry for one reason above all others: it is the leading *one-world* response to the [[vertiginous-question|vertiginous question]] ("why, of all conscious beings, am *I* this one?"). Christian List has shown that the family of theories surrounding this question forms a [[#the-quadrilemma|quadrilemma]] (explained below): four individually plausible claims — irreducible first-personal facts, more than one real subject, joint compossibility of a world's facts, and one world — are mutually inconsistent, so any theory keeps at most three. Irreducibility is a premise List defends separately; a deflationist about indexicals rejects it and so takes the first exit rather than escaping the choice. Egocentric presentism keeps **one world** while taking the first-person perspective seriously — exactly the horn that the resolution List tentatively prefers, a *many-worlds theory of consciousness*, abandons. That places egocentric presentism in direct structural alignment with the Map's [[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]] tenet, and makes List's many-worlds resolution the foil the Map rejects.

## The Thesis Stated

Hare introduced the view in "Self-Bias, Time-Bias, and the Metaphysics of Self and Time" (*Journal of Philosophy*, 2007) and developed it fully in the monograph *On Myself, and Other, Less Important Subjects* (Princeton University Press, 2009).

The 2007 paper begins not with metaphysics but with ethics. We are all, Hare observes, "mild egocentric hedonists": all else equal, we prefer that pleasure befall ourselves and pain befall others. Can this self-bias be rationally grounded? Hare names the difficulty the **Grounding Problem**: to think my comfort makes an especially weighty contribution to the value of a state of affairs, my picture of who I am must make me metaphysically unique in some way that bears on the intrinsic value of my suffering — yet, as he puts it, "I am mundane in all measurable respects." His tentative conclusion is that "perhaps my only hope is to believe that I am extraordinary in some immeasurable respect, that I am *metaphysically unique* in some way that bears upon the intrinsic value of my suffering."

### The Temporal Analogue {#temporal-analogue}

The metaphysical payoff arrives as a parity claim. The same move that makes the present *time* metaphysically privileged (the A-theory) can make the present *self* metaphysically privileged. Self-bias and time-bias are treated as two faces of one metaphysics. *Presence* is the property that does the work: for the A-theorist one moment is present and the others are not; in egocentric presentism only my experiences are present, indexed to the self rather than to time. Which A-theory is meant depends on the level of description. Hare's own analogy is presentist and runs at the level of subject worlds: only one exists, and "from his point of view" is an operator over worlds he does not believe in, an attitude "very like a temporal presentist's attitude toward the standard four-dimensionalist semantics for tense logic" (2007: 369). Within that single world other functionally sentient creatures exist and exactly one bears presence, which resembles tense realism — Hare's name for the eternalist A-theory, the moving spotlight — instead; that resemblance is the Map's reading, though it is the pairing Hare (2010) draws for the sibling view, perspectival realism. The Map takes the analogy as one structure recurring in two domains, not as an inference from time to selves. The time-bias half of the parity has its own literature, from Prior's "thank goodness that's over" argument to the case for temporal neutrality ([[time-bias-and-thank-goodness-thats-over]]).

Two features of the property matter. First, it is **monadic** — a one-place property, "present" full stop, not the two-place relation "present *to* x." Hare insists my experiences are present *simpliciter*, not present-relative-to-me, because a relational reading would make presence symmetric across subjects and dissolve the privilege. Second, it is **immeasurable** — no instrument, and no third-person description however complete, detects which subject is the present one. The privileged subject is *me*, and (Hare maintains) I am the only one whose point of view corresponds with the facts.

## Perspectival Realism (Hare's Sibling View) {#perspectival-realism}

Hare pairs egocentric presentism with a closely related view he calls **perspectival realism**. Other perspectives genuinely exist, but *my* perspective has a defining intrinsic, non-relational property: the world is present, the objects around me are *here*, *simpliciter* rather than relative to a point of view. Hare concedes it "commits me to a kind of solipsism", one on which "I am surrounded by a sphere of intrinsic privilege" rather than one on which only I exist (2010). Where egocentric presentism extends the privilege through time (events simply happening now, for me), perspectival realism concerns the spatial and perceptual perspective (the scene centred, intrinsically, on me). The Map ranks it the weaker and more defensible of the two because it claims less; that ranking is the Map's, not Hare's. In "Realism About Tense and Perspective" (2010) Hare makes perspectival realism his primary statement of the view and measures it against tense realism, not egocentric presentism, judging it "in many ways a better view than tense realism": the case for tense realism carries over, while its problems — a contested meta-ontology, and a simultaneity relation special relativity denies it — do not. The same paper adds facts about how things are *from another's point of view*, so that her belief can be correct from her standpoint while false simpliciter — "False – but rightly so." Whether such facts quietly reintroduce the relational structure the view was built to avoid is what the [[#the-communicability-objection|communicability objection]] (explained below) presses.

**Disambiguation — a critical one.** Hare's "perspectival realism" is unrelated to the philosophy-of-science doctrine of the *same name* associated with Ronald Giere and Michela Massimi, on which models, measurements and theoretical claims are made from a particular vantage of instruments and concepts while still latching onto a mind-independent world. That is a thesis about *scientific knowledge*, not the metaphysics of the self; the two share only a label. Throughout this article, "perspectival realism" means Hare's sense exclusively.

## The Quadrilemma {#the-quadrilemma}

Christian List has given the cleanest map of the logical space egocentric presentism inhabits. In "A Quadrilemma for Theories of Consciousness" (*The Philosophical Quarterly*, 2024/25), he isolates four individually plausible claims that cannot all be true together; at most three can hold:

1. **First-person realism** — there are irreducible first-personal facts: a fact about which subject is *the* present or lived one, not capturable by any third-person description.
2. **Non-solipsism** — more than one conscious subject is real. The sense of "conscious" matters here, and it is what lets List file Hare under the solipsist horn while Hare insists other people are conscious: on egocentric presentism "every functionally sentient creature is conscious", but only one *subject world* — a world in which exactly one creature's experiences bear presence — exists, and talk of other points of view is an operator over worlds Hare does not believe in (2007: 366–369). Non-solipsism, as List uses it, requires more than one subject world to be real, which Hare denies.
3. **Non-fragmentation** — the facts true in any single world are jointly compossible; no contradictory "centred" facts coexist in one world.
4. **One world** — reality is a single world, not many.

The quadrilemma is therefore a forced choice: every theory of consciousness that engages the first-person fact must sacrifice one claim. List names a theory for each exit: deflationists drop first-person realism; Hare's egocentric presentism drops non-solipsism; fragmentalists in Kit Fine's style drop non-fragmentation; a many-worlds theory of consciousness drops one world.

The deflationist exit deserves its strongest form, since everything that follows leans on the premise it rejects. Perry and Lewis showed that a complete impersonal description can leave an agent unable to act until she self-locates; they did not show that reality contains a further fact the description omits. Lewis analysed knowledge *de se* as self-ascription of a property, and Perry located the difference in the believer's belief state rather than in what is believed; on both accounts the indexical is indispensable to thought and action while the world's inventory stays impersonal ([[indexical-knowledge-and-identity]]). The step from epistemic indispensability to ontological irreducibility is one the Map has to earn, and against these accounts it has not. Its reason for taking the step is that the vertiginous datum does not present itself as a belief state: that *this* subject-world is the one being lived seems a feature of how things are, not of how a subject represents them. The deflationist can redescribe even that as self-ascription, and the Map has no argument that compels her to stop; the disagreement lies closer to bedrock than the quadrilemma's geometry suggests, and is recorded here as such rather than as a refutation.

Egocentric presentism, with its sibling perspectival realism, keeps first-person realism, non-fragmentation, and one world — by **dropping non-solipsism**. Only my experiences are present; others are conscious but not present. This is the "kind of solipsism" Hare avows: not the denial that other minds exist, but the denial that their experiences share the privileged property mine have. Of the two exits that keep both first-person realism and one world, it is the one that preserves a single coherent inventory of facts; the other is fragmentalism, whose costs are taken up [[#relation-to-site-perspective|below]].

List's own resolution, which he says he tentatively prefers but does not defend in the quadrilemma paper, drops **one world** instead. In a separate paper, "The Many-Worlds Theory of Consciousness" (*Noûs*, 2023), he associates each conscious subject with its own first-personally centred world — a distinct first-personal realiser of a shared third-personal world — and treats all these centred worlds as equally real, in a modal-realist register, with exactly one *present* for each subject. The first-person privilege the indexical intuition demands is represented by a primitive first-personal fact: my centred world is the one that is present. Whether that *explains* anything is a further question, and List is tentative about it. A primitive fact encodes the vertiginous datum without saying why this subject-world is the one being lived, much as "because it instantiates primitive presentness" restates rather than answers "why is this time present?" The Map's own primitive "I" within one world carries the same limitation and should be advertised the same way, as a refusal of further explanation; List and the Map differ on where the primitive is lodged, not on whether it explains.

These two are the sharpest contrast for the Map's purposes, though not the only live options. The structural cousin of this contrast — *this* outcome from many possibilities, *this* viewpoint from many subjects — is developed further at [[indexical-identity-quantum-measurement|indexical identity and quantum measurement]].

## The Communicability Objection

The standard objection is that the privileged property cannot be coherently stated. The worry: a non-relational "present, but present *to* no one in particular" seems either unintelligible or covertly relational. When I try to say what is special about my experiences, I say they are present *to me* — and that "to me" reintroduces a relation, restoring symmetry across subjects and dissolving the very privilege the view asserts. If presence is genuinely monadic, it appears unstatable; if it is statable, it appears relational and so universal. Ned Markosian, reviewing the book for *The Philosophical Review* (2014), argues that the evidence supports the relational reading — "presence is a two-place relation between an experience or an object and a conscious organism" — so that the inside/outside intuitions can be reconciled without privileging anyone, and he closes by asking what "metaphysical mechanism or backstory" could make presence attach to one subject's experiences "rather than someone else". Kris McDaniel, in a critical study of the book (2012), raises different objections. Presence does not track awareness: unnoticed parts and the electrons of a present table are present without being perceptual objects, so Hare's claim that presence lines up with what one is aware of fails. Presence might be only one *determinate* of a more general determinable, with one determinate per point of view, which would make every subject special in its own way and undercut Hare's axiological use of the privilege. And Hare's treatment of "I" as a "nonrigid definite description that refers to whoever it is that has present" perceptual objects is open to question.

Hare's reply is that monadic presence is a genuine property whose intelligibility does not require relational expressibility — the difficulty of saying it is a feature of any deeply first-personal fact, not evidence against its reality. The debate is live. Giovanni Merlo's "subjectivism" (2016) develops a near-neighbour position that differs precisely on the status of the privileged fact, and Vincent Conitzer's "personalized A-theory of time and perspective" (2020), which takes Hare's position as its explicit target, argues that A-theorists about time should also accept a metaphysically distinguished "I", because "key arguments in favor of the A-theory are more effective as arguments in favor of the resulting combined position, and key arguments against the A-theory are ineffective against the combined position" — which together show egocentric presentism is one member of a small family of "metaphysically privileged first-person" theories rather than an isolated curiosity.

## Relation to Site Perspective

Egocentric presentism is, for the Map, an exploratory ontological *fit* — a metaphysical rival that maps the same terrain the tenets occupy — rather than positive evidence for the Map's framework. Its structural alignment with the tenets is real and matters to the argument; its status as confirmation is not. The distinction is the [[project/evidential-status-discipline|evidential-status discipline]] applied: a congenial metaphysics that keeps one world and takes the self seriously removes a *defeater* (it shows the indexical privilege need not force many worlds), but it does not *upgrade* the evidence for dualism. The Map adopts the structure as a worked-out option, not as a result.

**[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]]** — the sharpest connection. Tenet 4 rejects the many-worlds interpretation primarily on the indexical objection: many-worlds dissolves "why am *I* this branch?" by making every branch equally real, so the question has no answer. List's many-worlds theory of consciousness is the harder case the Map must face directly, because it *keeps* the first-personal fact the vertiginous question turns on — by giving each subject a present centred world — rather than dissolving it. The Map shares List's diagnosis (the bare one-world picture struggles to say why *this* subject is mine) but rejects his remedy: his modal realism makes every other subject's centred world a *real* world, abandoning the global uniqueness Tenet 4 requires. That rejection is a framework-relative exclusion resting on the Map's prior refusal of modal realism, not a demonstration that List is wrong; egocentric presentism stands as the constructive one-world alternative. The Map's own settled position is more cautious still: it treats the privileged "I" as a genuine [[vertiginous-question|void]] — a primitive within one world that [[haecceity|haecceity]] marks but does not cross — rather than fully endorsing Hare's metaphysic. Egocentric presentism shows the one-world horn is *occupiable*; the Map occupies it without claiming the communicability objection is answered, and without Hare's weak solipsism. That has a price. If the Map's first-person realism is read in List's strong sense, keeping non-solipsism and one world together forces it onto the fragmentalist exit, as the [[vertiginous-question#the-quadrilemma|vertiginous-question article]] states. Hare's view is the one-world alternative that avoids fragmentation, and the Map declines it. That exit is not cheap, and the Map has so far named it rather than paid for it. Fine's fragmentalism privileges tensed facts "without privileging any particular temporal standpoint from which they obtain"; carried to subjects, it puts incompatible facts — my experiences are present, yours are present — into one reality and owes a compossibility relation saying which cohere with which, whose structure is disputed. The sharpest cost is distribution: Fine's view and Lipman's standpoint pluralism are *egalitarian*, privileging no fragment and so no subject, which leaves no uniquely present "I" and sits badly with the Map's primitive-"I" language, while a fragmentalism that privileges one fragment collapses back towards Hare. The debate is live — Merlo argues that "unstructured" fragmentalism faces a tensed-belief-explosion problem and is "at best unmotivated and at worst seriously flawed"; Loss replies that the explosion poses no problem; Lipman builds a pluralism on which neither a time nor a subject is privileged — and until the Map says which it inherits, its fragmentalist exit is a promissory note.

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** — *presence* is, on Hare's account, immeasurable: no instrument detects it, and no third-person description settles which subject bears it. That sits congenially alongside the Map's dualism, which holds that consciousness has features physical description cannot capture — but congeniality is as far as the inference goes. Immeasurability does not make presence non-physical: a property can be indexically inaccessible, private, or causally screened and still be physical, and concluding otherwise needs a criterion of the physical that neither Hare nor this article supplies. A further tension should be stated rather than smoothed over. [[tenets#^bidirectional-interaction|Bidirectional Interaction]] holds that consciousness makes a difference to physical outcomes; if presence has no possible third-personal footprint it does no work for that tenet — swap the privileged subject and nothing physical changes — and if it does make a difference, "immeasurable" weakens to "not yet measured". The Map has not settled which reading it takes, and does not settle it here. The fit requires care in a second way: Hare himself pairs egocentric presentism with a lean, broadly physicalist ontology, so the Map *repurposes* the structure under a dualist reading rather than enlisting Hare as an ally. He would not accept the Map's metaphysics, and the view is engaged as a rival whose structure the Map borrows, not as corroboration of dualism.

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]** — egocentric presentism posits a primitive, theoretically tidy-looking property (monadic presence) whose explanatory adequacy is contested. Anti-haecceitists will call it an unparsimonious addition to a world that qualitative facts already describe completely. By Tenet 5 the Map declines to let parsimony decide: the apparent simplicity of the deflationary view may reflect the inadequacy of our concepts for the first-person fact, not insight into it. The same discipline that blocks parsimony against dualism blocks it against a privileged self. The tenet earns only that much. Lewis's and Perry's accounts pay for the parsimony without adding facts to the world, so the razor cuts against first-person realism only if they succeed — the question at issue, not something the razor can presuppose. And blunting the razor gives no positive reason for a primitive first-personal fact; that reason, if the Map has one, lies in the [[#the-quadrilemma|datum above]], and is contested there.
