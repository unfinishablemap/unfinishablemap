Consistent Histories (CH), also called Decoherent Histories, is—on its standard, realist (Griffiths) reading—a single-world interpretation of quantum mechanics that removes measurement, observers, and wave-function collapse from the foundations. It treats quantum theory as a stochastic theory of *histories*—time-sequences of properties—and assigns ordinary, interference-free probabilities only to families of histories meeting a mathematical "consistency" condition. Presented on its own terms, CH is one of the most economical no-collapse interpretations that still keeps a single actual world: no many worlds, no hidden variables, no fundamental observer, and no added collapse dynamics.

The Map engages CH as a serious competitor rather than a resource. On Griffiths' single-world reading it is an *ally* against Many-Worlds—but that alliance is conditional, because treating *every* history in a consistent family as real can be argued to collapse back into many-worlds ([explained below](#relation-to-site-perspective)), so the payoff hangs on Griffiths' reading, not on CH as such. On every reading CH is a *rival* to consciousness-linked collapse: it needs no mind, no observer, nothing extra-physical to fix outcomes. One structural feature—framework-dependence, and the absence of any law selecting a preferred framework (the [Dowker–Kent set-selection gap](#the-dowker-kent-set-selection-critique), explained below)—raises a question the Map is entitled to ask but not to answer in its own favour: does that gap leave room for a consciousness-selected framework? CH proponents say no selection is *needed* at all. So a consciousness-selection reading is at most a *possible extension the Map might explore*—not a proof of dualism, and not something CH itself endorses.

## Histories as Sequences of Properties

The basic object of CH is a **history**: a temporal succession of events, each a property or event represented by a projection operator (a "projector"). Two related formal objects encode a history and should be kept apart. As a *history projector* it is a tensor product of the time-indexed projectors, defined on an enlarged "history Hilbert space" (one copy of the system's Hilbert space per time). As a *chain operator* (or class operator) it is the time-ordered product of those same projectors interleaved with the unitary propagators that carry the system between the times, acting on the ordinary system Hilbert space; the chain operator is what the Born-rule weight of the history is computed from. Where standard textbook quantum mechanics tracks a state evolving toward a measurement, CH tracks whole time-sequences of properties directly and asks how probable each sequence is.

A **framework** is the sample space for these probabilities. Technically it is a projective decomposition of the identity—a set of mutually orthogonal projectors that sum to the identity—extended across the relevant times. Griffiths (1984) generalized the standard transition-probability formula to yield conditional probabilities for such sequences of events, "consistent histories," applicable to closed systems, time-symmetric (past and future interchangeable), with no collapse and no reference to measurement. Because the framework is fixed before probabilities are assigned, CH is unusually explicit about a normally hidden step: *which* set of alternatives one is asking about.

## The Consistency (Decoherence) Condition

Not every family of histories can carry sensible probabilities. Because quantum amplitudes interfere, naively assigning probabilities to arbitrary history sets violates the classical additivity rule—the probability of "A or B" would not equal the sum of the parts. CH restricts probabilities to families where the interference cross-terms between distinct histories vanish (or are negligible). This is the **consistency condition**; Gell-Mann and Hartle's independently developed **decoherent histories** formulation states it via a *decoherence functional*, with medium decoherence implying consistency (Gell-Mann & Hartle 1990). When the cross-terms vanish, the Born rule delivers a genuine, classical probability distribution over the histories in that family.

[[decoherence|Decoherence]]—environmental entanglement that scrambles phase relations—is what typically makes macroscopic, "quasiclassical" families consistent. Gell-Mann and Hartle frame this cosmologically: the aim is to *derive* the everyday classical domain from a decoherence process among coarse-grained histories of a closed universe. This is an active and contested research programme rather than a finished result—whether a genuinely quasiclassical domain can be singled out from consistency and decoherence alone is exactly what the [Dowker–Kent critique](#the-dowker-kent-set-selection-critique) below disputes. On the intended reading, the classical world is not added by hand but emerges as the coarse-grained, decohered face of a quantum universe with no external observer.

The payoff CH claims is the dissolution of the [[measurement-problem|measurement problem]]. "Measurement" is just one physical interaction among many, analyzable inside a suitable framework; apparatus and observer are ordinary quantum systems. As the interpretation's own summary puts it, "There is no measurement problem, and under appropriate conditions one can discuss the microscopic properties revealed by measurements" (Griffiths 2014/2024). Wave-function collapse survives only as bookkeeping: on Griffiths' account, in the specific setting of reasoning about a measured subsystem, "there is no need to invoke wave function collapse except, perhaps, as a handy calculational tool," rather than as a physical event.

## Framework-Dependence and the Single-Framework Rule

CH's most distinctive—and most contested—feature is **framework-dependence**. Many mutually incompatible consistent families exist for the same system, and the theory offers no law selecting a preferred one. Griffiths organizes this with four principles: *Liberty* (multiple frameworks may be used), *Equality* (no framework is more fundamental than another), *Incompatibility* (incompatible frameworks are never combined into a single description), and *Utility* (the choice of framework is guided by the question asked). He calls this quantum "pluricity," contrasting it with the classical assumption of "unicity"—the tacit idea that there is one true, all-encompassing description.

The **single-framework rule** enforces the *Incompatibility* principle. Griffiths sets it out in *Consistent Quantum Theory* (Griffiths 2002, §16.1): any prediction must be confined to a single framework, and combining elements from incompatible frameworks yields quantum-mechanically meaningless statements. Hohenberg's (2010) introduction to the approach calls the rule "the precise mathematical statement of Bohr's complementarity." Within one framework, ordinary classical logic holds; different frameworks can license different conclusions, but the rule forbids stitching them together, which is how CH blocks the standard no-go paradoxes without adding structure. Omnès (1994) develops the same core into a reworked Copenhagen interpretation that keeps its empirical prescriptions, supplying logical rules for when a quantum statement counts as true or reliable within a framework and using decoherence to recover classical logic macroscopically.

Griffiths reads all this **realistically**: there is one world with definite, framework-relative properties, and the multiplicity of frameworks is like the multiplicity of coordinate systems, not a multiplicity of worlds. No framework is privileged, and on his view that is a feature, not a defect—demanding a privileged one merely imports the classical unicity assumption CH rejects.

## The Dowker–Kent Set-Selection Critique {#the-dowker-kent-set-selection-critique}

The canonical objection is Dowker and Kent (1996), which built on their earlier result (Dowker & Kent 1995) that the consistency criterion alone cannot recover the standard predictions and retrodictions of quasiclassical physics. Reviewing the Griffiths, Omnès, and Gell-Mann–Hartle formulations and classifying the consistent sets, they argue that the formalism supplies no criterion selecting a preferred family, so that some selection criterion "will have to be found to explain why we use particular sets." With no law of nature fixing the framework, there is no way to compute *unconditional* probabilities for specific histories—probabilities are always conditional on a framework chosen from outside the physics. They further show Omnès' characterization of "true" statements to be incorrect, and conclude that Gell-Mann and Hartle's explanation of the apparent persistence of quasiclassicality "relies on assumptions about an as yet unknown theory of experience." Their conclusion: CH must be *supplemented* by some set-selection principle before it can serve as a fundamental theory capable of unconditional predictions. This became Kent's **set-selection problem**.

The technical exchange sharpened from there. Kent (1997) showed that different consistent sets can license *contrary inferences*—orthogonal, mutually exclusive propositions each retrodicted with probability one from the same data—and Griffiths and Hartle (1998) replied that the single-framework rule forbids combining such inferences, with Kent responding in turn. Bassi and Ghirardi (1999) argued that four assumptions—three they deem necessary for any sound interpretation, plus one the approach's own supporters accept—jointly lead to a logical contradiction; Griffiths replied, and they answered again. More recently, Zampeli, Pavlou and Wallden (2025) show that the contrary-inferences problem persists even in the quasiclassical macroscopic limit, using the arrival time of a ball in an infinite square well as a worked case—evidence that the difficulty is not confined to contrived microscopic examples.

The disagreement is genuine and unresolved. Griffiths replies that framework-conditional probabilities are exactly what a quantum theory should deliver—probabilities are conditional on a chosen framework the way classical probabilities are conditional on a chosen sample space, and no "law of nature" need pick one. The dispute turns on a prior question: must a physical theory deliver framework-independent, unconditional probabilities, or are framework-conditional probabilities plus the single-framework rule enough? No consensus set-selection principle has been found, and CH remains a live minority interpretation. Everettians press the point one step further: Wallace (2012, pp. 98–99) argues that, given the Dowker–Kent multiplicity, single-world readings such as Griffiths' and Omnès' "fall short of conventional scientific realism," whereas Gell-Mann and Hartle's exploration of consistent histories, restricted to quasiclassical ones, "can be understood as an exploration of Everettian quantum mechanics."

Dowker and Kent also pressed whether the observer is smuggled back in. On their reading the predictive content of Griffiths' interpretation, too, "is meant to follow from an implicit theory of experience." They cast the open choice as a fork—complete CH with a criterion selecting the sets that describe observers' experiences, or with one selecting a single set to describe reality. They find it "awkward" that experience thereby "becomes entangled with the quantum formalism at a fundamental level," grant that this "could conceivably turn out to be unavoidable," and reject its claim to be the "natural null hypothesis" as "very clearly untenable." Griffiths treats framework choice as a harmless pragmatic step, like choosing coordinates; whether it is that innocent is precisely where CH's realist self-understanding is contested.

## Where CH Sits in the Landscape

CH occupies a distinctive corner of the interpretive map, surveyed more broadly in [[qm-interpretations-beyond-many-worlds|Quantum Interpretations Beyond Many-Worlds]]. Unlike Bohmian mechanics it adds no hidden variables; unlike GRW/CSL it adds no collapse dynamics; unlike Many-Worlds it keeps a single actual history and denies superpositions physical coexistence; unlike QBism and relational quantum mechanics it aims to be realist about a single world rather than agent- or observer-relative about states. It positions itself as "Copenhagen done right" (Griffiths 2014/2024): measurement loses fundamental status, quantum time-development is fundamentally probabilistic—unitary propagators describe the deterministic evolution between events while the Born rule fixes the probabilities of the events themselves—and collapse is demoted to bookkeeping. Its price is framework-dependence and the unsolved set-selection problem, which CH pays openly.

## Relation to Site Perspective

CH bears on all five of the Map's [[tenets|tenets]]; the interpretation's own claims must be kept separate from the Map's overlay.

**Tenet 4 (No Many-Worlds): ally only on the single-world reading.** The alliance is conditional, because the many-worlds reading of CH is a live one. Bigaj (2023) develops a *many-worlds* variant of CH on which each history in a given family describes a separate world, and Everettians such as Wallace (2012) read Gell-Mann and Hartle's quasiclassical histories as Everett's branches under another name ([above](#the-dowker-kent-set-selection-critique)). Gell-Mann and Hartle's own 2012 reformulation, which posits one "real" fine-grained history (their scare quotes) embedded in an ensemble of imagined ones, is a further sign that the decoherent-histories programme does not by itself single out one actual world. Only on Griffiths' *realist single-world* reading—one history in a consistent family realized, superpositions denied the status of physically coexisting branches—does CH reject [[concepts/many-worlds|Many-Worlds]], and it does so without paying either the hidden-variable price (Bohm) or the added-collapse price (GRW/CSL). That reading, which the Map leans on, shows that single-world realism is a live, technically serious option and not a nostalgic holdout.

**Tenet 2 (Minimal Quantum Interaction): rival.** CH removes any fundamental role for consciousness. Within a chosen framework, which history is realized is a matter of Born-rule chance, not of mental selection, and the framework itself is chosen for descriptive convenience. Griffiths is explicit that no mind is needed: on his account "quantum mechanics is compatible with the traditional idea of an independent physical reality whose fundamental properties can be discussed without needing to make reference to human observers or human consciousness" (Griffiths 2013). The Map must engage CH as a genuine competitor to consciousness-linked collapse, not fold it into dualism.

**Tenet 1 (Dualism): no support, no contradiction.** Standard CH is a physicalist interpretation: it locates everything in the quantum state and its histories and invokes nothing non-physical. It neither establishes nor refutes dualism; only the speculative consciousness-selection reading below, which CH itself disowns, could bear on Tenet 1. The Map records CH as a naturalistic competitor whose success would count against the *need* for dualism, not as evidence for it.

**Tenet 3 (Bidirectional Interaction): no bidirectional influence.** Standard CH provides no channel by which consciousness could act back on the physical: histories and their probabilities are fixed by the Born-rule dynamics, and on Griffiths' account the physicist's choice of framework "has not the slightest influence" on the system described (Griffiths 2013). CH is therefore structurally inhospitable to the Map's claim that mind causally influences the physical world; any bidirectional reading would have to be imported by the consciousness-selection extension.

**Tenet 5 (Occam's Razor Has Limits): cuts both ways.** CH is admirably economical—it adds nothing to standard quantum mechanics except an interpretive discipline. The Map does not treat that parsimony as decisive, precisely because Tenet 5 warns that simplicity is an unreliable guide when knowledge is incomplete. The measurement problem's persistence is itself a sign of such incompleteness.

The Map's distinctive question is whether the [Dowker–Kent set-selection gap](#the-dowker-kent-set-selection-critique) leaves room for a *consciousness-selected* framework. There is a structural resonance with the Map's recurring claim—developed in [[quantum-measurement-and-consciousness|the measurement problem as philosophical problem]]—that a complete third-person description can fail to fix a first-person fact. The resonance is a limited and contested *structural* analogy, and three distinct selections must be kept apart before it can be weighed:

- *Framework selection*—which sample space, or descriptive context, one adopts. This is what CH leaves open.
- *History selection*—which single history in a family is the actual one. CH treats this as a matter of Born-rule chance.
- *Outcome selection*—why this definite result rather than another. This is the measurement problem's demand, and what the Map's consciousness-linked reading is really about. The [[post-decoherence-selection|post-decoherence selection]] concept page sets these apart as four distinct loci—adding preferred-basis selection to the three above—and locates the Map's consciousness claim at outcome selection specifically.

The gap CH exhibits sits at the level of *framework* (context) selection, whereas the measurement-problem parallel the Map cares about is *outcome* selection. Because Griffiths holds that framework choice does not influence reality, even a consciousness-selected framework would be at most context-selection—which the Map's own [[tenets|tenets]] treatment subordinates to outcome selection. So the parallel is best read as a disanalogy: in the measurement problem an outcome *is* needed and unexplained, whereas CH claims no framework selection is needed at all. To turn the affinity into an argument, the Map would have to earn a foundational move CH denies—that some selection is needed and that consciousness supplies it. The first half has standing in the critical literature: Dowker and Kent argued that unconditional predictions need a set-selection criterion and named a theory of experience as one candidate ([above](#the-dowker-kent-set-selection-critique)). But their experience route picks which sets *describe* an observer's experience—framework selection again, not outcome selection—and they denied it default status, so it locates the Map's question without raising its evidential standing. The full move is not available inside CH's own framework, where the single-framework rule and framework-conditional probabilities coherently deny the need; the disagreement is a framework-boundary one, honestly noted as such rather than presented as a result CH underwrites. The Map registers the affinity, treats framework-dependence as an open seam rather than a hidden endorsement of dualism, and mirrors the cautious [[quantum-darwinism-and-consciousness|outcome-gap reading of quantum Darwinism]] without claiming CH shares its ontology.
