**Cognitive penetration** is the hypothesis that a subject's beliefs, desires, expectations, or other cognitive states can change the *content or character of perceptual experience itself*, not merely the judgements formed on its basis. The neutral core, which every side of the debate accepts as the question at issue, comes from Fiona Macpherson's framing: can "the phenomenal character of perceptual experience be altered by the states of one's cognitive system, for example, one's thoughts or beliefs?" (Macpherson 2012). Whether it happens, how often, and what it would mean for justification are all contested.

The concept matters to The Unfinishable Map because the Map treats first-person [[seemings]] as defeasible evidence (see [[phenomenal-conservatism]]) and also leans on seemings produced by years of doctrinally framed contemplative training. Susanna Siegel's argument that a seeming's causal history can lower its justificatory power turns those two commitments against each other. This article defines the phenomenon and the downgrade thesis, maps the Map's existing uses, and then takes up [the training case](#the-training-case-resolution-or-contamination) directly. Its verdict is that whether training *resolves* or *contaminates* a seeming is an empirical question that has to be answered one kind of seeming at a time. That training reveals structure rather than installing it is a live hypothesis the Map may hold, but it does not raise the evidential standing of what trained practitioners report.

## Definition and the Empirical Dispute

The idea descends from debates over modularity. Zenon Pylyshyn defended the view that "early vision" is "prohibited from accessing relevant expectations, knowledge, and utilities in determining the function it computes—in other words, it is cognitively impenetrable" (Pylyshyn 1999). He did not deny that cognition affects what a person ends up seeing. He placed its influence in two other stages: a "pre-perceptual attention-allocation stage" and a "post-perceptual evaluation, selection, and inference stage." That three-way division still organises the dispute. An alleged penetration effect counts only if it reaches the experience itself, rather than changing where attention goes beforehand or what the subject judges afterwards.

Macpherson (2012) defends one case she argues "cannot be explained away by the standard strategies": subjects' beliefs about the typical colour of objects appear to affect their colour experience. She proposes a "two-step mechanism of indirect cognitive penetration," in which a cognitive state first produces a perception-like state such as imagery, and that state then affects the experience.

The main counterweight is Chaz Firestone and Brian Scholl's survey in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*. They argue that none of the "hundreds of studies—either individually or collectively—provides compelling evidence for true top-down effects on perception, or 'cognitive penetrability'" (Firestone & Scholl 2016). A handful of pitfalls recur across the literature, among them effects driven by task demand, effects on judgement rather than perception, and effects mediated by shifts in where attention falls. They show that several celebrated effects actually are explained by these pitfalls "(in practice)," not merely that they could be. Their conclusion is a checklist rather than a flat denial: "without addressing these pitfalls, no such empirical report will license such exciting conclusions." Dustin Stokes (2018) replies that the attention pitfall "oversimplifies the possible roles that attention might play," and he sketches forms of "attention-mediated cognitive penetration." Visual penetration remains contested, with the sceptical case methodologically stronger for early vision.

One limit of scope matters for everything below. Firestone and Scholl's argument concerns visual perception. The seemings the Map relies on most are [[introspection|*introspective*]] seemings about one's own experience, and nothing in the modularity case for early vision shows that introspection is encapsulated in the same way. If anything, introspective judgement is the more exposed faculty, which is why the Map cannot borrow the vision sceptics' conclusion as a shield.

## The Epistemic Downgrade Thesis

Siegel moved the question from psychology to epistemology. In "Cognitive Penetrability and Perceptual Justification" (Siegel 2012) and at book length in *The Rationality of Perception* (Siegel 2017), she argues that if an experience is shaped by a belief, the experience can inherit an epistemic defect from that belief. Her central example, Angry-looking Jack, has Jill believing without justification that Jack is angry with her. When she sees him, her belief shapes the experience so that he *looks* angry, and the look then appears to confirm the belief. Siegel's claim is that the experience cannot give Jill the support it would give someone whose experience had arisen independently of the belief. Otherwise an unjustified belief could launder itself through perception. The 2017 book develops this into the stronger thesis that perceptual experiences can themselves be well or badly formed, and so open to rational assessment.

The downgrade thesis bears directly on [[phenomenal-conservatism]] (PC), on which a seeming justifies in virtue of its present content, whatever its provenance. On PC's surface reading, Jill's seeming justifies. The replies divide into three:

- **Accept and modify.** Matthew McGrath (2013) finds both PC and the "bad basis" counterexamples plausible, and uses the counterexamples to motivate a modification of the principle.
- **Deny the damage.** Luca Moretti (2020, ch. 3) examines the penetration-based cases from both the reliabilist and the internalist-inferentialist side and argues that cognitive penetrability does not create lethal or substantive difficulties for PC.
- **Deny the premise.** If Firestone and Scholl are right about vision, the cases that drive the downgrade thesis may rarely or never occur perceptually, and the epistemological debate becomes a debate about possibilities.

The phenomenal-conservatism article records that the objection's "standing is contested rather than decisive"—the right calibration, and the reason the Map owes an answer rather than an exemption.

## Three Ways the Map Already Uses the Idea

The Map invokes cognitive penetration in three directions: as a tool for exposing hidden construction, as an objection it must answer, and as something certain seemings are said to resist:

| Locus | Direction | What it claims |
|---|---|---|
| [[aspect-perception-void]] | Tool | If perception is penetrable, aspect-seeing is the rule, and the penetrating mechanism is hidden from the perceiver |
| [[predictive-construction-void]] | Tool | Penetration selects one reading of an ambiguous figure while remaining invisible |
| [[appetitive-void]] | Tool, hedged | Siegel's "wishful seeing" places desire below deliberation, and the article records Firestone and Scholl's dissent |
| [[phenomenal-transparency-opacity-spectrum]] | Tool, different sense | Transparency is "cognitively impenetrable to ordinary effort"; disciplined practice "may partially reduce it" |
| [[phenomenal-conservatism]] | Objection | Siegel's etiology-based downgrade is a standing objection to PC, and Moretti contests it |
| [[phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence]] | Objection | The seeming that pain hurts is unlikely to be penetrated. The seeming that it is *irreducible* is more exposed |
| [[phenomenal-acquaintance]] | Resistance | Direct acquaintance "limits the channels through which distortion can enter" |

Two cautions follow. First, the spectrum's use differs from Siegel's. "Impenetrable" there means that *attention cannot get through* the transparency of experience to its constructive processing. That is a limit on introspective access, not a claim about belief reaching into perception. The two senses meet only in the training case, where the claim is that sustained practice lets attention reach what ordinary attention cannot. Second, the tool direction and the objection direction are symmetric. A Map that uses penetration to argue that perception is invisibly constructed cannot then treat trained introspection as immune to the same mechanism. Whatever answer it gives about training has to be consistent with its voids.

Three further articles rely on trained introspection as revealing, without naming penetration: [[phenomenal-depth]] ("the layers are there, but accessing them requires trained attention"), [[contemplative-practice-as-philosophical-evidence]], and [[objectivity-and-consciousness]], whose sympathetic account of Varela's neurophenomenology treats trained introspective reports as "disciplined first-person data." These are the claims the next section tests.

## The Training Case: Resolution or Contamination

Years of practice inside a tradition that teaches what practitioners will find is a paradigm case of the kind of etiology Siegel's thesis targets. Two hypotheses fit the fact that trained practitioners report structure untrained people do not:

- **Resolution.** Training works like perceptual expertise. It raises discrimination, so that structure present all along becomes reportable. Phenomenal depth's claim is of this kind: each layer was "not added by closer attention."
- **Contamination.** Training installs doctrinal expectations that shape the seemings, or at least the reports, so that practitioners find what their tradition taught them to find.

Two clarifications follow. On Pylyshyn's stage model, much of what training does is *attentional* or *post-perceptual*, and a sceptic in the Firestone–Scholl mould would deny that it is cognitive penetration at all. That does not rescue the Map: an attentional or judgemental route to doctrine-shaped reports contaminates the evidence as surely, even if not the experience. The Map's question is therefore broader than Siegel's: does the whole pipeline from training to report track the experience or the doctrine? Second, the two hypotheses are not exclusive. Training can sharpen some discriminations and install some interpretations at the same time.

The Map's proposal, marked as the Map's own, is that the difference between the two is empirical and has identifiable markers. It builds on the formulation already in the transparency-opacity spectrum. That article observes that the contemplative claim "risks unfalsifiability" and requires a discriminating marker: genuine opacity, unlike a trained belief that perception is constructed, "should predict measurable changes in perceptual processing or report." Generalised, a trained seeming counts as resolution to the degree that it:

1. **Tracks an independent benchmark.** Fox et al. (2012) compared meditators' reports of tactile sensitivity across body regions with objective measures drawn from prior research and found that "long-term meditators provide more accurate introspective reports than novices." The design was cross-sectional and the authors call it a "preliminary exploration," so self-selection has not been excluded.
2. **Shows a change in sensitivity rather than in criterion.** Signal-detection measures separate a real gain in discrimination from a shift in willingness to report. Pylyshyn (1999) warns that a sensitivity gain does not by itself locate an effect in perception rather than attention, but for the Map's broader question either locus counts, since both track the stimulus rather than the doctrine. MacLean et al. (2010) randomised participants to three months of intensive attention training and found "improvements in visual discrimination that were linked to increases in perceptual sensitivity." This bears on the attentional capacities training builds, not on metaphysical content.
3. **Converges across doctrinally opposed traditions.** Doctrine-installed content should diverge where doctrines diverge. The contemplative-practice article concedes that on *metaphysical interpretation* the traditions do diverge (no observer, a permanent observer, dissolution into something larger), and it limits its convergence claim to structural findings. Convergence counts only once shared historical transmission is ruled out.
4. **Includes discriminations the tradition did not teach.** A finding a tradition had no doctrinal stake in is harder to explain as installed.

The markers do not settle the matter. Lutz, Slagter, Dunne and Davidson (2008) distinguish focused-attention from open-monitoring practice as training distinct attentional capacities. That makes "training" a family of interventions rather than one, and a marker established for one style does not transfer automatically. The structural neuroscience is less supportive than it once appeared: Kral et al. (2022), in two combined randomised trials, "found no evidence" that an eight-week MBSR course produced structural brain change. That result concerns novices in a short course, not long-term practice, but it removes one line of support sometimes cited for the resolution hypothesis.

The verdict is split. For *structural and discriminative* seemings, such as finer bodily discrimination and sustained-attention gains, there is modest evidence of resolution that is still open to confounds. For *interpretive and metaphysical* seemings, such as that the self is empty or that awareness is an eternal witness, the divergence across traditions makes contamination the better explanation, and the Map already concedes this. For the claims the Map most wants, that training discloses pre-existing depth or constructive process, "training reveals rather than installs" is a live hypothesis the evidence does not yet decide. It fits the tenets, but that fit cannot be counted as evidence.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]** (Tenet 5) is where the concept does its main work. The phenomenal-conservatism article argues that dislodging a robust seeming requires a *defeater* rather than a tidier theory without it. Cognitive penetration is the most serious candidate for such a defeater, and it is an etiological one: it concerns how the seeming came about, not whether a simpler ontology can do without it. Tenet 5 therefore commits the Map to more than resisting parsimony-based dismissals. It must say which etiologies defeat. The Map's answer is that an etiology defeats a seeming when the seeming's content covaries with the subject's background commitments rather than with an independent benchmark. The markers above are how that covariation is detected. The rule applies symmetrically. It downgrades a physicalist's theory-shaped seeming that consciousness is "nothing but" processing, and it equally downgrades a dualist's theory-shaped seeming of irreducibility.

For **[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** (Tenet 1) the consequence is a narrowing. The evidential base of the dualist argument should rest on seemings least exposed to penetration: the thin, near-constitutive seeming that an experience is occurring and has a felt character, which [[phenomenal-acquaintance]] explains and [[phenomenal-authority-and-first-person-evidence]] ranks as most secure. The thicker seeming that this character is *irreducible*, and any trained seeming whose content matches a practitioner's metaphysics, carry less weight until they pass the markers. This costs less than it might seem: the case for dualism already rests on the hard problem and the knowledge argument rather than on contemplative testimony, and the [[evidential-status-discipline|evidential-status discipline]] already forbids treating trained reports as proof. What changes is that the discount is now explicit and tied to a stated test. The [[illusionism|illusionist]] charge that even the thin seeming is produced by a misrepresenting introspective model is a different objection: universal rather than commitment-covarying, so this criterion neither answers it nor is weakened by it, and the Map meets it on other grounds.

The symmetry with the voids is the final constraint. The Map says perception and introspection are shaped by processes hidden from the subject. It must therefore accept that trained introspection is shaped too, and it should claim only that some shaping improves resolution, and only where the evidence shows it.
