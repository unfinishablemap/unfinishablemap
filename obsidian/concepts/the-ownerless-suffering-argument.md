The Ownerless Suffering Argument is the name now given to an inference at *Bodhicaryāvatāra* 8.101–102, where the eighth-century Buddhist monk Śāntideva argues from the non-existence of the sufferer to the impartial relief of all suffering. It has two steps: the person who would own a suffering does not exist, so no suffering belongs to anyone; and what remains — suffering, unowned — is to be prevented with no partiality as to whose it is. The argument is the no-self doctrine worked through into ethics. Where [[no-self-objection-to-phenomenal-value|the abstract no-self objection]] asks whom value could be good *for* once the self is gone and leaves the answer open, this argument makes ownerlessness the premise of a positive normative conclusion: universal benevolence.

The Map's finding here cuts against the Map's own reply to that objection, and corrects it without refuting the argument. [[no-self-objection-to-phenomenal-value]] judges the residual dispute — whether "bad for the momentary locus" adds anything to "this feeling-moment is bad" — "close to terminological" at the momentary grain. That verdict survives metaphysically and fails inside one argument, on one reading of it. Metaphysically, the abhidharma inventory the argument draws on keeps momentary suffering-events as ultimately real, close to what the Map's momentary experiential locus asserts. The finding is scope-relative ([explained below](#map-meets)). If "no sufferings belong to anyone" ranges over *any bearer*, a momentary locus falsifies it and the step to "they are to be warded off simply because they are suffering" loses its stated premise. If "anyone" ranges over *persons* — the partiality-grounding owners 8.94–99 are about — the locus leaves the premise true and the inference untouched. The normative conclusion, impartial benevolence, survives either way, and the Map endorses it through [[sentientism]]. The tradition also has a reply of its own ([the Parity Reply](#rivals)), and one prominent reading holds the verses are not an argument at all.

## The Argument in the Text {#argument}

The verses answer an objection raised a few lines earlier. Śāntideva's opening move at 8.94–96 is a similarity argument: suffering is disliked by me and by others equally, so what is special about me that I protect myself and not the other? The natural reply is that I stand in a relation to my own future self — personal identity — that I do not stand in to anyone else, and that this grounds a prudential asymmetry. Verses 8.97 and 8.99 press against that reply, and 8.101–102 close it off by denying the relation a term.

Two published translations, given because the wording matters and the numbering varies. Crosby and Skilton's (1995, Oxford World's Classics), as quoted in Goodman's *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry on Śāntideva:

> 101. The continuum of consciousness, like a queue, and the combination of constituents, like an army, are not real. The person who experiences suffering does not exist. To whom will that suffering belong?
>
> 102. Without exception, no sufferings belong to anyone. They must be warded off simply because they are suffering. Why is any limitation put on this?

And the translation in Garfield, Jenkins and Priest's chapter in the Cowherds' *Moonpaths*, which gives 102 as "Since all ownerless sufferings are / Without distinction, / They should be alleviated just because of being suffering, / What restriction can be made in that case?":

> VIII: 101 A continuum and collection, / Just like such things as a series or an army, are unreal. / The one for whom there is suffering does not exist. / Therefore to whom will that suffering belong?

The chapter is eight, although the *Śāntideva* entry's prose also says IX; its two encyclopaedia entries render 8.102 as "must be" and "are to be warded off", and which is the slip is unresolved. The name is recent and external to the text: Goodman and Schultz call it what "some scholars now call the Ownerless Suffering Argument".

The second translation is the more useful here because it renders the denial in the Map's own vocabulary with no gloss required: "The one *for whom* there is suffering does not exist" denies exactly the mattering-recipient that the second premise of the [[consciousness-value-connection|value chain]] asks for.

## What Carries the Inference {#asymmetry}

The step from 101 to 102 needs more than the unreality of persons: it needs suffering to survive an analysis persons do not. The canonical route, endorsed in Prajñākaramati's commentary as Garfield, Jenkins and Priest report and reconstruct it, is abhidharma metaphysics: composites are conventionally real, and momentary property-instantiations (*dharmas*) are ultimately real. In their words:

> Momentary experiences of suffering and happiness are dharmas. They are hence ultimately real. Suffering has the svalakṣaṇa of being unbearable, happiness the svalakṣaṇa of being desirable. Persons are composites; they are hence ultimately unreal, albeit conventionally real. They have no svalakṣaṇa at all.

The normative payoff follows directly: "The claim that each person has a special relation to his or her own pain fails, because there are literally no people."

That asymmetry — suffering ultimately real, bearers only conventionally real — is load-bearing in the strict sense. Remove it and 101 no longer reaches 102. Garfield, Jenkins and Priest register the instability themselves, in a footnote: 101 "says that persons are unreal. It does not say explicitly that dharmas are not. … Moreover, in that case, if the people do not exist, neither does the pain!"

Charles Goodman is the argument's leading modern expositor, and his statement of its reach is his own: "it's hard to see how the Ownerless Suffering Argument could support any moral view that is not some form of universalist consequentialism." His monograph *Consequences of Compassion* (2009) advances a consequentialist interpretation of Buddhist ethics — its fourth chapter is titled "Theravāda Ethics as Rule-Consequentialism". The classification is contested in any case. Bronwyn Finnigan's survey records four camps: "Keown (2001) and, later, Cooper & James (2005: 68) claim that Buddhist ethics is a type of virtue ethics. Siderits (2003, 2007) argues that it is unmistakably consequentialist, as do Williams (1998) and Goodman (2008). Velez (2004) and Clayton (2006) argue that Buddhist ethics is best understood as a combination of virtue ethics and utilitarianism whilst Harvey (2000: 49), though acknowledging the analogies, nonetheless maintains that a Buddhist ethical theory is significantly distinct." "Buddhist consequentialism" therefore names the reading on which the challenge to the experiencer requirement is sharpest, not the settled reading of the tradition.

## Where the Map's Reply Meets the Argument {#map-meets}

This section states the Map's own reading; no part of it is attributed to the sources above.

The Map's reply to the abstract no-self objection is that the value chain needs a *momentary experiential locus* rather than a persisting self: momentariness does not remove the for-whom, it multiplies it. Read against 8.101–102, that reply has a consequence the abstract treatment does not surface, because Śāntideva's argument does more than offer an escape route to someone who wants to keep value while dropping owners; its conclusion depends on there being no owner at the grain at which the suffering occurs.

Grant the momentary locus and 101's two clauses come apart. "The person who experiences suffering does not exist" stays true on the Map's reading, since the Map asserts no *ātman* and its Dualism tenet requires irreducibility rather than permanence ([[buddhism-and-dualism]]). Whether "no sufferings belong to anyone" also stays true depends on the scope of "anyone". Read wide, as any bearer whatever, it becomes false: a momentary locus is a someone at the moment of the suffering, and the suffering is its. Read narrow, as any person — the owner the objector at 8.97–99 needs to ground partiality — it stays true, because a momentary locus has no future toward which to be partial, and the foot-and-hand analogy still bites against *diachronic* self-concern. The dialectical setting favours the narrow reading, and the Map has no textual argument for the wide one. So the Map's reply removes the tradition's stated bridge from ownerlessness to impartiality only on the wide reading; on the narrow reading, "belongs to no one who could be favoured" does the same work in the premise's own terms. Either way impartiality stands, and the Map reaches it by the similarity argument of 8.94–96 or by what all loci have in common, the route its [[sentientism]] takes.

That is why the sibling article's "close to terminological" verdict needs scoping rather than reversing. At the momentary grain, "this feeling-moment is bad" and "this feeling-moment is bad for its locus" may well be one fact under two descriptions — on the pattern [[positions/value-in-selection|P-VS3]] sets for felt badness and badness, which is the identity that entry actually asserts. The two are interchangeable for the *valuation* — neither makes the moment worse — but not for the *ontology*, since the second entails a bearer and the first does not. On its wide reading Śāntideva's premise quantifies over bearers rather than over badness, and that is how a distinction idle within a metaphysics can matter within an argument ranging over what the metaphysics posits. On the narrow reading it does not matter there either.

## Ownerless, or Merely Unranked? {#unranked}

Goodman's own gloss on what the argument delivers speaks of beneficiaries:

> From the perspective of this argument, your suffering has no greater significance than that of anyone else, but it also has no less. You are one of the many sentient beings whose welfare is to be promoted.

The welfare-talk concedes nothing: on the two-truths reading [set out above](#asymmetry), conventionally real persons have welfare, and a reductionist says exactly this. What the gloss shows is that the argument's delivered conclusion is about *ranking* — no one's suffering counts for more for being theirs — and that conclusion is compatible with the Map's reply: loci exist, and none is weighted above another for being this one. The distinction between ownerless and unranked is the Map's, not Goodman's.

## Rivals at Full Strength {#rivals}

### The Parity Reply

The strongest response comes from inside the tradition, and Garfield, Jenkins and Priest state it in their own voice:

> For a Mādhyamika, from the fact that persons are not ultimately real, it does not follow that they are not real in any sense. And since action is conventional, and is undertaken by conventional agents, there is no reason to think that merely conventional distinctions are irrelevant to action and its goals. Indeed, if it were the case that we could disregard things that exist merely conventionally, then we could disregard suffering, since this has no ultimate existence either!

The two halves pull opposite ways. The second cuts against the reductionist: on Madhyamaka all things share one ontological status, and Śāntideva was a Mādhyamika rather than an ābhidharmika, so the asymmetry the argument needs is unavailable to its own author, and any deflation reaching the bearer reaches the badness with it. The first is the harder half here: if merely conventional distinctions are action-relevant, impartial benevolence can be rebuilt on conventionally real streams with no appeal to ownerlessness, and the finding above constrains the abhidharma route only. The symmetry also cuts against the Map: the analysis that dissolves the person may dissolve an imputed subject-pole too. They record that the Cowherds disagree about the force of this objection and direct readers to Mark Siderits's chapter for a contrary view.

### The verses may not be an argument

Stephen Harris holds that the reconstructions of these verses as a proof fail. Goodman reports the position: Harris "regards the Ownerless Suffering Argument as itself a mere upāya. It must be an upāya, Harris says, since it relies on the Abhidharma understanding of the doctrine of no self, whereas Śāntideva's actual view belongs to a very different form of Buddhist philosophy, the Madhyamaka."

This dissolves the challenge rather than answering it, and the Map should say so plainly rather than take the comfort. The concession has a limit, though, which the naming point above marks: the "Ownerless Suffering Argument" is a modern label for a reconstruction. What dissolves if Harris is right is the *exegetical* target — that Śāntideva himself grounded impartiality on ownerlessness. The reconstruction survives him, because Goodman advances it in his own voice, and it is that inference the finding above constrains. Harris's abstract fixes the scope: the reconstructions he rejects are those of Williams, Siderits and Pettit.

### The experiencer requirement, pressed from inside Buddhist studies

Paul Williams's *Altruism and Reality* (1998) and his 2000 reply to Siderits's review are the reference points for the objection that suffering makes no sense with nothing to own it. Garfield, Jenkins and Priest note that the central argument "is at least prima facie fallacious from a Madhyamaka standpoint, as Williams and others note". The apparent tension — Finnigan's survey groups Williams *with* the consequentialist interpreters, which sits oddly against reading him as the argument's critic — resolves inside the same chapter: Garfield, Jenkins and Priest credit the abhidharma reading of the passage to Williams, then cite him among those who find that reading fallacious. He supplies the reading and doubts it. The bibliographic point stands independently, and it is why this subject is more than a quibble: the requirement the Map states in a paragraph has a named adversarial literature inside Buddhist studies.

### Two ways the conventionalist exit narrows

Finnigan's *Philosophy East and West* paper takes up the justificatory grounds of Madhyamaka moral judgments directly, pressing from inside Buddhist studies on the "conventionally real streams" route the sibling article credits to Siderits. And Monima Chadha reads the Abhidharma as eliminativist about persons rather than merely reductionist, a characterisation [[eastern-philosophy-consciousness]] already carries. If persons are eliminated, the conventionalist exit narrows, though eliminating the person-concept need not eliminate the causal series it was imputed onto; to the extent it does, the question returns to the abhidharma asymmetry.

Siderits's chapter, Williams's two texts, Harris's full paper and Goodman's 2009 monograph were not consulted; each is reported only through the sources quoted, and no formulation is attributed to them beyond those reports and chapter titles.

## What Follows, and What Only on the Map's Reading {#implications}

Four things follow from the argument alone, for anyone. The premise's force depends on whether "anyone" ranges over bearers or persons. Any ethics grounding obligation in suffering owes an account of whether ownerlessness is doing ontological or normative work, because the two licence different conclusions. An impartiality argument routed through ownerlessness is hostage to the metaphysics of bearers where a similarity argument is not — a reason to prefer 8.94–96 to 8.101–102 if impartiality is the goal. And the abhidharma and Madhyamaka readings of the same verses yield different ethics, so "the Buddhist view" underdetermines the normative claim.

Two things follow only on the Map's reading: read wide, the ownerless premise is false at the momentary grain, because the locus is a genuine subject-pole not reducible to the mechanism realising it; and on that reading the tradition's route to impartial benevolence needs replacing rather than adopting.

What would move this article: a textual case for the wide reading of 102's "anyone"; Williams 1998 or 2000, which would replace a reported objection with a formulation; Siderits's *Moonpaths* chapter, the best available answer to the Parity Reply; or a reading of 101 on which "the one for whom there is suffering does not exist" denies the momentary locus and not merely the person — the reading Chadha's eliminativism and Metzinger's [[self-model-theory-of-subjectivity|self-model theory]] make live, and which [[no-self-objection-to-phenomenal-value#rivals|the sibling]] treats as the objection's strongest form.

## Relation to Site Perspective

**[[tenets#^occams-limits|Occam's Razor Has Limits]]** governs this article in both directions, which is unusual. Against the Map: the ownerless reading is the more parsimonious one — drop bearers, keep suffering — and the Map declines to count that parsimony against itself, a choice the tenet permits where knowledge is incomplete. For the Map: the Parity Reply's second half shows what parsimony pressed consistently costs the reductionist, since a deflation reaching persons reaches the suffering too. Neither side may stop deflating where it suits them.

**[[tenets#^dualism|Dualism]]** supplies the momentary locus's *irreducibility*, not its existence. Tenet 1 is neutral on bearers: abhidharma dharma-realism, with irreducible suffering-moments and no owner, satisfies it too, so the tenet does not decide 8.101. The locus's existence rests on the minimal-self structure borrowed in [[no-self-objection-to-phenomenal-value#maps-reply|the sibling's reply]], and how a moment comes to be someone's is not introspectable, as the [[mine-ness#the-ownership-void|ownership void]] records. The Map asserts a bearer on that commitment rather than on a finding, which is why the finding above corrects a verdict and does not defeat the argument.

**[[tenets#^no-many-worlds|No Many Worlds]]** is deliberately not drawn on. The persisting subject that tenet needs would reopen the diachronic half of this dispute, and the [[no-self-objection-to-phenomenal-value#maps-reply|sibling article]] keeps the value chain insulated from it so that an account of why suffering is bad does not fall with an account of why this branch is actual. The same isolation holds here.
